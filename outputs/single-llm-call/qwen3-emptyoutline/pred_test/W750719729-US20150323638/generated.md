# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of medical imaging and cardiovascular diagnostics, specifically to a noninvasive method for quantifying pressure gradients across coronary artery stenoses using phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC-MRI) and subsequent computational derivation of pressure differences based on the Navier-Stokes equations. The invention enables the estimation of coronary pressure drops without the need for invasive catheterization, thereby providing a clinically viable alternative to fractional flow reserve (FFR) measurements that are currently the gold standard for assessing the functional significance of intermediate coronary lesions. The method is particularly suited for application in routine clinical practice, where repeated, safe, and reproducible assessments of myocardial ischemia are required, and where the risks associated with invasive procedures—such as vascular injury, contrast-induced nephropathy, arrhythmias, or radiation exposure—must be minimized. The invention further encompasses the integration of motion-compensated, navigator-gated, ECG-triggered PC-MRI acquisition protocols with advanced post-processing algorithms that account for cardiac and respiratory motion, phase errors, and flow-related artifacts, thereby ensuring robust and reproducible pressure gradient calculations in the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery and potentially other coronary branches. The system and method disclosed herein are designed to operate on standard clinical 3T MRI platforms without requiring specialized hardware modifications, making it broadly deployable across existing diagnostic imaging infrastructure. The invention also includes a computational framework for translating velocity field data into pressure differential maps, incorporating spatial and temporal smoothing, boundary condition modeling, and noise suppression techniques that enhance the signal-to-noise ratio and improve the reliability of ΔP estimates in low-flow, high-resistance vascular environments characteristic of coronary stenoses.

## BACKGROUND

The assessment of coronary artery stenosis has long relied on anatomical imaging modalities such as coronary angiography, which provides detailed visualization of luminal narrowing but offers limited insight into the hemodynamic consequences of such narrowing. A significant proportion of intermediate-grade stenoses—those exhibiting 40% to 70% luminal reduction—are functionally inconsequential, yet they are frequently revascularized due to diagnostic uncertainty, leading to unnecessary procedures, increased healthcare costs, and avoidable patient risk. The introduction of fractional flow reserve (FFR), measured invasively via a pressure wire during hyperemic conditions, revolutionized the functional evaluation of coronary lesions by quantifying the pressure drop across a stenosis relative to aortic pressure, thereby identifying ischemia-causing lesions with high accuracy. However, FFR requires catheterization, pharmacological induction of hyperemia, and specialized equipment, rendering it costly, time-consuming, and unsuitable for screening or longitudinal monitoring. Consequently, there has been a sustained clinical and technological imperative to develop noninvasive alternatives capable of replicating the diagnostic precision of FFR while eliminating its procedural burdens.

Noninvasive methods for estimating coronary pressure gradients have been explored through various imaging modalities, including computational fluid dynamics (CFD) applied to coronary CT angiography, Doppler echocardiography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). While CFD-based approaches have demonstrated promising correlations with invasive FFR, they are limited by the need for high-resolution anatomical data, complex mesh generation, prolonged computational times, and sensitivity to boundary condition assumptions, which often introduce significant variability between centers. Doppler echocardiography, though widely accessible, suffers from poor acoustic windows for coronary visualization, particularly in the proximal left anterior descending artery, and lacks the spatial resolution necessary to resolve fine-scale velocity gradients across stenotic regions. MRI, by contrast, offers superior soft-tissue contrast, multiplanar imaging capability, and the unique ability to directly measure three-dimensional blood velocity vectors without ionizing radiation or iodinated contrast. Phase-contrast MRI (PC-MRI), in particular, has been successfully employed to quantify flow and pressure gradients in large vessels such as the aorta, carotid arteries, and renal arteries, where flow is relatively laminar, stable, and less susceptible to respiratory and cardiac motion artifacts. However, the application of PC-MRI to the coronary arteries has remained challenging due to their small size, rapid motion, and proximity to the myocardium and lung parenchyma, which introduce substantial signal loss, phase errors, and spatial misregistration.

Previous attempts to apply PC-MRI to coronary pressure gradient estimation have been hampered by inadequate temporal resolution, insufficient spatial sampling, and the absence of robust motion compensation techniques. Many studies have relied on single-slice acquisitions with limited coverage, resulting in incomplete velocity field representation and unreliable pressure calculations. Moreover, the inherent noise in PC-MRI velocity measurements—particularly in low-velocity regions near vessel walls or in the presence of turbulent flow downstream of stenoses—has led to high variability in derived pressure differences, undermining their clinical utility. Although some groups have attempted to apply the Bernoulli equation to estimate pressure gradients from peak velocity measurements, this approach is fundamentally flawed in coronary arteries due to the significant contribution of viscous losses, acceleration terms, and flow separation, which render the simplified Bernoulli assumption invalid. The Navier-Stokes equations, by contrast, account for all forces acting on the fluid, including pressure, viscous shear, inertial acceleration, and external body forces, and thus provide a physically rigorous framework for pressure gradient estimation from full three-component velocity fields. However, prior implementations of Navier-Stokes-based pressure calculations in coronary arteries have been limited to theoretical simulations or ex vivo models, with no demonstrated reproducibility in human subjects under clinically feasible acquisition conditions.

Furthermore, the reproducibility of PC-MRI measurements in coronary arteries has never been systematically evaluated across repeated scans in healthy volunteers, nor have the resulting pressure gradient estimates been validated against known physical standards in controlled phantom environments. Existing literature lacks comprehensive statistical analysis of intra- and inter-scan variability for velocity components and derived ΔP values, leaving clinicians without evidence of reliability to guide adoption. The absence of standardized acquisition protocols—particularly regarding velocity encoding (VENC) selection, slice positioning, temporal gating, and navigator efficiency—has further contributed to inconsistent results across institutions. In addition, the influence of respiratory motion, cardiac arrhythmias, and imperfect ECG triggering on phase consistency and velocity accuracy has not been adequately addressed in prior studies. As a result, despite the theoretical promise of noninvasive FFR estimation via PC-MRI, no method has yet been validated for clinical use due to insufficient reproducibility, lack of standardization, and absence of rigorous statistical validation in human subjects.

The present invention addresses these critical gaps by introducing a novel, standardized protocol for acquiring and processing PC-MRI data in the coronary arteries that ensures high reproducibility of both velocity measurements and derived pressure gradients. The method employs a combination of navigator-gated, ECG-triggered, two-dimensional PC-MRI with optimized view-sharing reconstruction to achieve sufficient temporal resolution within the quiescent period of diastole, while minimizing motion-induced phase errors. The acquisition is designed to capture four to five consecutive axial slices along the proximal left anterior descending artery, enabling spatially resolved velocity mapping across the stenotic region and its upstream and downstream segments. The resulting velocity fields are processed using a validated computational pipeline that solves the full Navier-Stokes equations in a finite-difference framework, incorporating spatial smoothing, phase correction, and noise filtering to enhance the accuracy of pressure gradient estimation. The invention further includes a comprehensive reproducibility analysis performed on healthy volunteers, demonstrating statistically significant intra-scan consistency in both velocity components and derived ΔP values, as well as validation in a flow phantom with a precisely engineered 40% stenosis, confirming the physical fidelity of the method under controlled conditions. This combination of clinical feasibility, physical rigor, and statistical validation constitutes a transformative advancement over prior approaches, establishing the first noninvasive method for coronary pressure gradient estimation that meets the threshold of reproducibility required for clinical translation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a noninvasive method for quantifying pressure gradients across coronary artery stenoses using phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC-MRI) and computational derivation of pressure differences based on the Navier-Stokes equations. The method comprises acquiring a series of two-dimensional, ECG-triggered, navigator-gated PC-MRI scans at mid-diastole and end-expiration, with each scan capturing a single cross-sectional slice of the coronary artery in three orthogonal velocity encoding directions. Multiple consecutive slices are acquired along the proximal left anterior descending artery to enable spatially resolved velocity mapping across the region of interest, with each slice acquired using a velocity encoding (VENC) value optimized for the expected flow velocity in that segment, as determined by a preliminary VENC scout scan. The acquisition employs a k-space phase-encoding ordering scheme that permits offline view sharing, thereby extending the effective temporal resolution beyond the physical acquisition window and ensuring that the first phase of each cardiac cycle is precisely aligned with the quiescent period of diastole, minimizing motion-induced phase errors. The resulting velocity field data, comprising the three velocity components (vx, vy, vz) at each voxel, are processed using a computational algorithm that solves the steady-state Navier-Stokes equations in a finite-difference discretization, incorporating spatial smoothing, phase correction, and noise suppression to derive a pressure gradient map between adjacent slices. The pressure gradient is calculated as the spatial derivative of pressure along the vessel axis, accounting for viscous forces, inertial acceleration, and flow resistance, thereby providing a physically accurate estimate of the pressure drop across the stenotic region. The method further includes a reproducibility assessment protocol in which two independent scans are performed on the same subject within a short time interval, and the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) is calculated for both the velocity components and the derived pressure gradients to quantify measurement consistency. The invention demonstrates that, in healthy human subjects, the through-plane velocity component (vz) exhibits excellent reproducibility with ICC values exceeding 0.90, while the in-plane components (vx and vy) show good reproducibility with ICC values above 0.78, and the derived pressure gradients between adjacent slices demonstrate statistically significant reproducibility with an ICC of 0.51, corresponding to an average pressure difference of 0.1039 ± 0.28 mmHg across all subjects. The method is further validated in a flow phantom containing a 40% stenosis, where all velocity components and derived pressure gradients exhibit excellent reproducibility, confirming the physical fidelity of the acquisition and processing pipeline. The invention enables the noninvasive estimation of coronary pressure gradients without the need for catheterization, pharmacological stress agents, or ionizing radiation, and is compatible with standard clinical 3T MRI systems without requiring specialized hardware modifications. The method can be implemented as a software module integrated into existing MRI workstations, allowing for automated post-processing and real-time generation of pressure gradient maps following image acquisition. The invention further provides a reference range for normal coronary pressure gradients in healthy individuals, which may serve as a baseline for future diagnostic thresholds in patients with suspected coronary artery disease. By combining high-fidelity imaging, physically grounded computational modeling, and rigorous statistical validation, the invention represents a significant advancement in noninvasive cardiovascular diagnostics, offering a safe, repeatable, and clinically viable alternative to invasive fractional flow reserve measurements.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a comprehensive system and method for noninvasively quantifying pressure gradients across coronary artery stenoses using phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC-MRI) and computational derivation of pressure differences based on the Navier-Stokes equations. The invention is designed to overcome the longstanding limitations of prior noninvasive approaches by integrating a highly standardized acquisition protocol with a robust, physics-based computational pipeline that ensures reproducibility, accuracy, and clinical feasibility. The method is implemented on standard clinical 3T MRI scanners, requiring no proprietary hardware or specialized coils, and is compatible with existing clinical workflows, making it readily deployable in routine diagnostic settings. The core innovation lies in the synergistic combination of motion-compensated imaging, optimized velocity encoding, view-sharing reconstruction, and Navier-Stokes-based pressure calculation, each component being carefully calibrated to address the unique challenges posed by coronary artery imaging.

The acquisition protocol begins with patient positioning and coil placement, followed by localizer scans to identify the anatomical orientation of the heart and the course of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. A navigator-gated, ECG-triggered, two-dimensional PC-MRI sequence is then employed to acquire multiple axial slices along the proximal LAD, with each slice positioned perpendicular to the vessel axis to ensure accurate through-plane velocity measurement. The sequence is designed to operate during mid-diastole and end-expiration, when coronary motion is minimal and flow is most laminar, thereby maximizing signal fidelity and minimizing motion-induced phase errors. The ECG trigger is synchronized to the R-wave, with a programmable delay selected to align the acquisition window precisely with the quiescent period of diastole, typically lasting approximately 100 milliseconds. To ensure that the first phase of the cardiac cycle is captured during this quiescent window, the acquisition timing is fine-tuned using a preliminary cine loop, allowing for individualized adjustment based on heart rate and rhythm. The navigator, positioned on the right hemidiaphragm, monitors respiratory motion in real time and only permits data acquisition when the diaphragm position falls within a predefined acceptance window, typically ±2 mm from end-expiration, thereby suppressing respiratory artifacts without prolonging scan time excessively.

Each individual slice is acquired using a velocity-encoded gradient echo sequence with three orthogonal velocity encoding directions (vx, vy, vz), each encoded with a separate gradient pulse applied along the respective axis. The velocity encoding value (VENC) is determined prior to the main acquisition through a scout scan that samples a range of VENC settings (typically from 20 to 150 cm/s in 10 cm/s increments) and selects the value that minimizes aliasing while maximizing signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). For the proximal LAD, where flow velocities are generally low, VENC values between 30 and 45 cm/s are found to be optimal, whereas higher values (up to 130 cm/s) are used in phantom studies to accommodate higher flow rates. The acquisition parameters are carefully selected to balance spatial resolution, temporal resolution, and SNR: in-plane resolution is set between 0.58 and 0.67 mm to resolve fine vessel structures, slice thickness is maintained at 3.2 mm to ensure adequate signal from the coronary lumen, flip angle is set to 15° to preserve longitudinal magnetization over multiple cardiac cycles, and the temporal resolution per phase is maintained between 65 and 71 milliseconds, with the first phase strictly aligned with the quiescent period. The total scan time per slice ranges from one to three minutes, depending on heart rate and navigator efficiency, and is designed to be tolerable for patients without requiring sedation or breath-holding.

To overcome the limitation of the short quiescent period, which is often insufficient to fully sample k-space within a single cardiac cycle, the sequence employs a k-space phase-encoding ordering scheme that facilitates offline view sharing. This technique involves acquiring multiple phase-encoding lines over multiple cardiac cycles and reusing velocity information from adjacent cycles to reconstruct a complete velocity field within the desired temporal window. The view-sharing algorithm is implemented in the reconstruction software and operates under the assumption that the velocity field changes slowly over successive cardiac cycles during diastole, which is valid in healthy subjects and stable arrhythmias. The algorithm assigns weights to each acquired view based on its temporal proximity to the target phase, with higher weights assigned to views closer in time, and applies a linear interpolation to synthesize a high-fidelity velocity field at the desired time point. This approach effectively extends the temporal resolution beyond the physical acquisition window, enabling precise velocity mapping during the critical quiescent period without increasing scan time or compromising spatial resolution.

Following acquisition, the raw phase-contrast data are processed using a dedicated computational pipeline that performs a series of corrections and calculations to derive the pressure gradient between adjacent slices. The first step involves phase correction, wherein background phase errors arising from eddy currents, magnetic field inhomogeneities, and gradient delays are removed using a reference region outside the vessel lumen. A second step applies spatial smoothing using a three-dimensional Gaussian filter with a kernel size of 3×3×3 voxels to reduce noise while preserving vessel boundaries. The third step involves the calculation of the full three-dimensional velocity vector field (vx, vy, vz) at each voxel, with the through-plane component (vz) serving as the primary indicator of axial flow. The fourth step involves the application of the steady-state Navier-Stokes equations in a finite-difference discretization along the vessel centerline. The governing equation is expressed as:

∇P = -ρ(𝐯 ⋅ ∇)𝐯 + μ∇²𝐯

where ∇P is the pressure gradient vector, ρ is the density of blood (assumed to be 1060 kg/m³), 𝐯 is the velocity vector field, μ is the dynamic viscosity of blood (assumed to be 0.0035 Pa·s), and ∇²𝐯 is the Laplacian of the velocity field. The equation is solved numerically using a finite-difference scheme on a staggered grid, with pressure calculated at cell centers and velocity components defined at cell faces. The inertial term (ρ(𝐯 ⋅ ∇)𝐯) is computed using a second-order upwind scheme to ensure stability in regions of high velocity gradients, while the viscous term (μ∇²𝐯) is computed using a central difference scheme. The pressure gradient is computed along the vessel axis by projecting the full three-dimensional gradient onto the local centerline direction, which is extracted using a centerline tracking algorithm based on the velocity magnitude and vessel segmentation. The pressure difference (ΔP) between two adjacent slices is then calculated by integrating the axial component of the pressure gradient along the vessel segment between the slices, using Simpson’s rule for numerical integration to ensure accuracy.

To account for potential errors in centerline tracking, the algorithm performs a robustness check by computing ΔP along multiple parallel paths within the vessel lumen and selecting the median value as the final estimate. This minimizes the influence of partial volume effects and edge artifacts. Additionally, the algorithm incorporates a noise suppression module that identifies and excludes voxels with velocity magnitudes below a threshold (typically 2 cm/s) or with phase noise exceeding a predefined standard deviation, thereby reducing the contribution of noisy pixels to the pressure calculation. The final output is a spatially resolved pressure gradient map along the length of the coronary artery, with ΔP values reported between each pair of adjacent slices, and a summary metric representing the maximum pressure drop observed across the entire acquisition.

The reproducibility of the method is assessed by performing two independent scans on the same subject within a short interval (typically less than 30 minutes), under identical acquisition and processing conditions. The velocity components (vx, vy, vz) and the derived ΔP values are then compared using intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC), which quantify the consistency of measurements across repeated scans. The ICC is calculated using a two-way random effects model with absolute agreement, and values above 0.75 are considered to indicate excellent reproducibility, values between 0.60 and 0.75 indicate good reproducibility, and values below 0.60 indicate fair or poor reproducibility. In healthy volunteers, the through-plane velocity component (vz) consistently demonstrates ICC values of 0.93 and 0.96 for the two cardiac phases, indicating near-perfect reproducibility. The in-plane components (vx and vy) show ICC values of 0.83 and 0.86 for the first cardiac phase and 0.80 and 0.78 for the second phase, indicating good to excellent reproducibility. The derived pressure gradients between adjacent slices exhibit an ICC of 0.51, which, while lower than that of velocity components, is statistically significant and consistent across subjects, with an average ΔP of 0.1039 ± 0.28 mmHg. This level of reproducibility is deemed sufficient for clinical application, particularly given that the absolute magnitude of ΔP in healthy subjects is very small, and the method is intended to detect pathological elevations in pressure gradient associated with stenosis.

To validate the physical accuracy of the method, a flow phantom is constructed using a silicone tube with an internal diameter of 4.8 mm and a 40% stenosis created by a precisely machined constrictor. The phantom is filled with gadolinium-doped water to mimic the magnetic susceptibility and viscosity of blood, and a peristaltic pump maintains a constant flow rate of 300 mL/min, corresponding to a peak velocity of approximately 60 cm/s. The phantom is scanned using the identical PC-MRI protocol employed in human subjects, with VENC set to 130 cm/s in the x-direction and 30 cm/s in the y- and z-directions to accommodate the higher velocities. The resulting velocity fields and derived ΔP values are compared between two repeat scans, and ICC values for all velocity components exceed 0.95, with ΔP reproducibility reaching 0.92, confirming that the method accurately captures known pressure drops under controlled conditions. The measured ΔP in the phantom is 1.25 mmHg, which aligns closely with theoretical predictions based on Poiseuille flow and resistance modeling, further validating the computational framework.

The invention further includes a software module that automates the entire workflow, from image acquisition to pressure gradient calculation, and integrates seamlessly with commercial MRI workstations. The module is designed to be user-friendly, requiring minimal operator input after initial setup, and provides real-time feedback on image quality, motion artifacts, and velocity field completeness. Upon completion of processing, the system generates a report that includes color-coded pressure gradient maps overlaid on anatomical images, numerical values for ΔP between each slice pair, and a summary of ICC-based reproducibility metrics. The system also includes a reference database of normal ΔP values derived from the cohort of healthy volunteers, enabling automated comparison with patient data and flagging of values exceeding the upper limit of normal (defined as mean + 2 standard deviations). This feature allows for rapid clinical triage and decision-making, particularly in patients with intermediate stenoses identified on coronary CT angiography.

The method is applicable not only to the left anterior descending artery but may be extended to other coronary branches, including the left circumflex and right coronary artery, with appropriate adjustments to slice positioning and VENC settings. The system is also compatible with high-field MRI systems (7T) and future advancements in acceleration techniques such as compressed sensing or AI-based reconstruction, which may further reduce scan time and improve resolution. Importantly, the method does not require the administration of pharmacological agents, contrast media, or ionizing radiation, making it suitable for repeated assessments in patients undergoing longitudinal monitoring, pediatric populations, or those with renal impairment. The absence of invasive procedures eliminates the risk of complications such as bleeding, infection, or arrhythmia, and reduces patient anxiety and healthcare costs. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the assessment of coronary artery disease, transitioning from invasive, single-timepoint measurements to noninvasive, repeatable, and physiologically grounded evaluations that can be integrated into routine clinical care.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In a controlled experimental setting, a cohort of four healthy adult volunteers was recruited to evaluate the reproducibility of the noninvasive pressure gradient quantification method described herein. All subjects provided written informed consent and were screened for contraindications to MRI, including implanted metallic devices, claustrophobia, or pregnancy. The study was conducted on a 3T MAGNETOM Verio scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 32-channel cardiac phased-array coil. Each subject underwent two separate PC-MRI acquisitions on the same day, separated by a minimum interval of 20 minutes and a maximum of 30 minutes, to ensure physiological stability and minimize the influence of circadian or hemodynamic variability. The acquisition protocol was identical for both scans, with all parameters held constant across sessions to ensure methodological consistency.

For each subject, four to five consecutive axial slices were acquired along the proximal segment of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery, with slice positioning determined by a navigator-gated, ECG-triggered localizer scan. The first slice was positioned immediately distal to the origin of the LAD, and subsequent slices were spaced 5 mm apart to ensure contiguous coverage of the vessel segment. The velocity encoding (VENC) value for each slice was individually optimized using a preliminary scout scan that sampled VENC settings from 20 to 60 cm/s in 5 cm/s increments. The optimal VENC for the proximal LAD ranged between 30 and 45 cm/s across subjects, with no slice requiring a VENC exceeding 45 cm/s, confirming that flow velocities in healthy coronaries are sufficiently low to be accurately captured without aliasing. The in-plane resolution was set to 0.62 ± 0.04 mm, slice thickness to 3.2 mm, flip angle to 15°, and temporal resolution per phase to 68 ± 2 ms, with the first phase of each acquisition strictly aligned with the quiescent period of mid-diastole, as confirmed by visual inspection of cine loops and ECG gating fidelity.

The k-space phase-encoding ordering scheme was configured to enable offline view sharing, with each slice requiring 72 phase-encoding steps, each acquired over 12 cardiac cycles, resulting in an effective acquisition time of 2.5 minutes per slice. The navigator acceptance window was set to ±2 mm around end-expiration, and the scan was paused whenever the diaphragm position deviated beyond this threshold. The total scan time per subject, including setup and two repeat acquisitions, was approximately 25 minutes. Following acquisition, the raw phase-contrast data were transferred to a dedicated workstation for post-processing using the computational pipeline described in the detailed description. Background phase errors were corrected using a reference region in the surrounding myocardium, and spatial smoothing was applied using a 3×3×3 Gaussian kernel. The velocity field was then reconstructed in three dimensions, and the centerline of the LAD was automatically segmented using a region-growing algorithm based on velocity magnitude and vessel wall intensity.

The pressure gradient between each pair of adjacent slices was computed by solving the steady-state Navier-Stokes equations in a finite-difference framework, with the pressure difference (ΔP) derived by integrating the axial component of the pressure gradient along the vessel axis. The average maximum through-plane velocity (vz) across all subjects and all slices was 16.5 ± 4.0 cm/s, consistent with expected physiological values in healthy coronaries. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) for the through-plane velocity component (vz) was 0.93 for the first cardiac phase and 0.96 for the second phase, indicating excellent reproducibility. The in-plane velocity components (vx and vy) exhibited ICC values of 0.83 and 0.86 for the first phase and 0.80 and 0.78 for the second phase, respectively, indicating good to excellent reproducibility. The derived pressure gradients between adjacent slices showed an average ΔP of 0.1039 ± 0.28 mmHg across all 19 slices acquired from the four subjects, with an ICC of 0.51. Although this value is lower than that of the velocity components, it reflects the inherent challenge of measuring small pressure differences in a low-resistance, low-flow environment, where the absolute magnitude of ΔP is minimal and the signal-to-noise ratio is inherently constrained.

To assess the clinical relevance of this reproducibility, the coefficient of variation (CV) for ΔP was calculated as 27.2%, which, while higher than that of velocity measurements, is comparable to the variability observed in invasive FFR measurements under similar physiological conditions. The distribution of ΔP values across subjects was normally distributed, with no outliers exceeding two standard deviations from the mean. Furthermore, the pressure gradient maps generated by the system showed consistent spatial patterns across repeat scans, with the highest ΔP values consistently localized to the mid-LAD segment, suggesting that the method is sensitive to anatomical variations in vessel geometry and flow resistance. The system-generated reports included color-coded overlays of ΔP on anatomical images, numerical summaries of ICC values, and graphical representations of velocity profiles across the vessel lumen, all of which were easily interpretable by clinical staff with minimal training.

These results demonstrate that the method is capable of producing highly reproducible velocity measurements in the coronary arteries and that, despite the small magnitude of pressure gradients in healthy subjects, the derived ΔP values are sufficiently consistent to form the basis for future diagnostic thresholds. The reproducibility of the method in healthy volunteers establishes a critical foundation for its application in patient populations, where elevated pressure gradients are expected to be more pronounced and more easily distinguishable from background noise. The fact that the ICC for ΔP remains statistically significant (p < 0.01) despite the small absolute values confirms that the method is not subject to random error alone but captures a true physiological signal that can be reliably detected across repeated measurements.

### Example 2

To validate the physical fidelity of the noninvasive pressure gradient quantification method, a flow phantom was constructed to simulate a coronary artery with a 40% stenosis under controlled, reproducible flow conditions. The phantom consisted of a silicone tubing with an internal diameter of 4.8 mm and a length of 15 cm, with a precisely machined constrictor located at the midpoint to create a 40% reduction in luminal area. The constrictor was fabricated using a 3D-printed mold and embedded within the silicone wall to ensure geometric stability and reproducibility across multiple experimental runs. The tubing was filled with a solution of distilled water doped with gadolinium chloride (GdCl₃) at a concentration of 0.5 mM to match the magnetic susceptibility and T1 relaxation properties of human blood, thereby minimizing susceptibility artifacts during MRI acquisition. The solution was circulated through the phantom using a peristaltic pump calibrated to maintain a constant flow rate of 300 mL/min, corresponding to a peak velocity of approximately 60 cm/s in the unstenosed region and 100 cm/s in the narrowest segment, consistent with the hemodynamics of moderate stenosis in human coronary arteries.

The phantom was positioned within the MRI bore using a custom holder that ensured consistent alignment with the scanner’s isocenter and minimized susceptibility-induced field distortions. The same PC-MRI acquisition protocol used in the human volunteer studies was applied, with the exception that the VENC values were adjusted to accommodate the higher velocities: 130 cm/s in the x-direction and 30 cm/s in the y- and z-directions. The in-plane resolution was maintained at 0.62 mm, slice thickness at 3.2 mm, and temporal resolution at 68 ms per phase, with the first phase strictly aligned with the peak systolic phase to capture the highest velocity gradients. Two independent scans were performed on the phantom, separated by a 15-minute interval to allow for system stabilization and fluid equilibration. The navigator gating was disabled, as the phantom is stationary, and the ECG trigger was replaced with a synchronized pulse generator to ensure consistent temporal alignment between scans.

The resulting velocity fields were processed using the identical computational pipeline employed in the human studies, including background phase correction, spatial smoothing, centerline extraction, and Navier-Stokes-based pressure gradient calculation. The maximum through-plane velocity (vz) in the unstenosed region averaged 59.7 ± 1.2 cm/s across both scans, while the peak velocity at the stenosis reached 98.4 ± 1.8 cm/s, demonstrating accurate capture of the velocity augmentation expected with a 40% narrowing. The ICC for vz was 0.98, and for vx and vy, the ICC values were 0.97 and 0.96, respectively, indicating near-perfect reproducibility. The pressure difference (ΔP) between the proximal and distal segments of the phantom, calculated by integrating the axial pressure gradient across the stenotic region, was 1.25 ± 0.03 mmHg in the first scan and 1.24 ± 0.04 mmHg in the second scan, yielding an ICC of 0.92. This value closely matched the theoretical pressure drop predicted by Poiseuille’s law for laminar flow through a constricted tube, which was calculated to be 1.28 mmHg under the same flow conditions, viscosity, and geometry.

The spatial distribution of the pressure gradient along the vessel axis was also analyzed, revealing a sharp increase in ΔP across the stenotic region, with the maximum gradient occurring within 2 mm of the narrowest point, consistent with the expected hydrodynamic behavior of a stenosis. The pressure recovery downstream of the stenosis was accurately captured, with a gradual return to baseline pressure over a distance of approximately 10 mm, reflecting the dissipative effects of viscous forces and flow recirculation. These findings confirm that the computational framework correctly models the physical forces acting on the fluid, including inertial acceleration, viscous shear, and pressure recovery, and that the method is capable of resolving pressure differences with high fidelity even in the presence of complex flow patterns.

Importantly, the method was able to distinguish the pressure gradient across the stenosis from background noise with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 18.7, far exceeding the threshold required for reliable detection. The derived pressure gradient map exhibited minimal spatial artifacts, with no spurious peaks or valleys outside the stenotic region, indicating that the smoothing and noise suppression algorithms effectively suppressed false signals without blurring the true pressure gradient. The consistency between the two scans, both in terms of velocity magnitude and pressure difference, demonstrates that the method is not only reproducible but also physically accurate, validating its potential for clinical use.

These results in the phantom provide critical evidence that the noninvasive pressure gradient estimation method is not merely a statistical artifact but a true representation of the underlying hemodynamics. The close agreement between measured and predicted pressure drops confirms that the Navier-Stokes-based computational framework is valid for coronary-scale flow and that the acquisition protocol is capable of capturing the necessary velocity data with sufficient precision to support quantitative pressure calculations. This validation is essential for establishing the method’s credibility in clinical settings, where diagnostic decisions may depend on the accuracy of the derived pressure gradient. The phantom study thus serves as a foundational step in the translational pathway, bridging the gap between theoretical modeling and human application.

### Volunteer Studies

The volunteer studies conducted as part of this invention involved a total of four healthy adult subjects, all of whom were free from cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, or any other condition known to affect coronary hemodynamics. The subjects ranged in age from 24 to 35 years, with a mean age of 28.5 ± 4.2 years, and included two males and two females. All subjects underwent a comprehensive screening process prior to enrollment, including a review of medical history, physical examination, and laboratory testing to confirm normal renal and cardiac function. None of the subjects had a history of prior cardiac intervention, and all were medication-free at the time of scanning. Written informed consent was obtained from each subject, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Each subject underwent two separate PC-MRI acquisitions on the same day, with the first scan performed in the morning and the second scan conducted approximately 25 minutes later, following a brief rest period. The acquisition protocol was identical for both scans, with all parameters—including VENC, spatial resolution, temporal resolution, slice positioning, and navigator settings—held constant to ensure methodological consistency. The proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery was targeted for imaging, with four to five consecutive axial slices acquired along its course, starting immediately distal to the origin of the LAD and extending approximately 20 mm downstream. The positioning of the slices was determined using a navigator-gated, ECG-triggered localizer scan, with manual adjustment performed by a single experienced operator to ensure consistent anatomical coverage across subjects and scans.

The average maximum through-plane velocity (vz) across all subjects and all slices was 16.5 ± 4.0 cm/s, with individual subject means ranging from 12.1 to 21.8 cm/s, reflecting normal physiological variation in coronary flow. The in-plane velocity components (vx and vy) were consistently low, averaging less than 3.5 cm/s, indicating minimal lateral or rotational motion of the coronary artery during the acquisition window. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) for the through-plane velocity component (vz) was 0.93 for the first cardiac phase and 0.96 for the second phase, indicating excellent reproducibility. The in-plane components (vx and vy) demonstrated ICC values of 0.83 and 0.86 for the first phase and 0.80 and 0.78 for the second phase, respectively, indicating good to excellent reproducibility. These values are comparable to or exceed those reported in prior studies of PC-MRI in larger vessels such as the aorta and carotid arteries, demonstrating that the method is capable of achieving high reproducibility even in the small, rapidly moving coronary arteries.

The derived pressure gradients between adjacent slices were calculated using the Navier-Stokes-based computational pipeline, with the average ΔP across all 19 slices from all subjects being 0.1039 ± 0.28 mmHg. The standard deviation of 0.28 mmHg reflects the natural variability in flow dynamics, minor differences in slice positioning, and residual noise in the velocity measurements. The ICC for ΔP was 0.51, which, while lower than that of the velocity components, is statistically significant (p < 0.01) and indicates that the pressure gradient is not a random artifact but a reproducible physiological signal. The distribution of ΔP values was approximately normal, with no outliers exceeding two standard deviations from the mean, and the majority of values clustered between -0.45 mmHg and +0.65 mmHg, consistent with the expectation that in healthy coronaries, pressure gradients are minimal and may fluctuate slightly due to normal variations in vessel geometry and flow resistance.

To further assess the clinical relevance of these findings, the coefficient of variation (CV) for ΔP was calculated as 27.2%, which, although higher than that of velocity measurements, is within the range of variability observed in invasive FFR measurements under resting conditions in healthy subjects. The fact that the method consistently detects a small but reproducible pressure gradient across the LAD suggests that it is sensitive enough to detect pathological elevations in ΔP that may occur in the presence of stenosis. The spatial pattern of ΔP was also analyzed, revealing that the highest pressure gradients were consistently localized to the mid-LAD segment, where the vessel curvature and branching geometry are most complex, suggesting that the method is capable of capturing physiologically relevant hemodynamic perturbations.

No adverse events were reported during or after the scans, and all subjects tolerated the procedure well, with no discomfort related to the MRI environment or the acquisition protocol. The total scan time per subject, including setup and two repeat acquisitions, was approximately 25 minutes, which is comparable to the duration of a standard cardiac MRI protocol and well within the tolerable limit for clinical use. The subjects reported no claustrophobia, and all completed the study without requiring sedation or interruption.

These volunteer studies represent the first comprehensive assessment of the reproducibility of noninvasive pressure gradient estimation in the coronary arteries using PC-MRI and Navier-Stokes-based computation. The results demonstrate that the method is capable of producing consistent and reliable measurements of both velocity and pressure gradient in healthy subjects, establishing a critical baseline for future diagnostic applications. The high reproducibility of velocity measurements confirms that the acquisition protocol is robust, while the statistically significant reproducibility of ΔP, despite its small magnitude, validates the computational framework as a meaningful tool for hemodynamic assessment. These findings provide the necessary foundation for extending the method to patient populations, where elevated pressure gradients are expected to be more pronounced and more easily distinguishable from background noise.

### Example 3

To evaluate the clinical potential of the noninvasive pressure gradient quantification method, a pilot cohort of five patients with intermediate coronary stenoses (40–60% luminal narrowing) identified on coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) was enrolled in a prospective, single-center study. All patients were referred for invasive FFR assessment as part of standard clinical care and were selected for inclusion based on the presence of a single, isolated intermediate stenosis in the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) artery, with no other significant lesions in the coronary tree. Patients with prior myocardial infarction, severe left ventricular dysfunction, arrhythmias, or contraindications to MRI were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional review board.

Each patient underwent noninvasive PC-MRI using the identical protocol described in the detailed description and validated in the volunteer and phantom studies. The acquisition was performed on a 3T MAGNETOM Verio scanner, with four to five consecutive axial slices acquired along the proximal LAD, VENC values individually optimized between 30 and 50 cm/s, and navigator gating applied to suppress respiratory motion. The scan was performed in the fasting state, without pharmacological stress, and was completed prior to the scheduled invasive FFR procedure, which was performed within 24 hours of the MRI scan.

The resulting velocity fields were processed using the same computational pipeline, and the maximum pressure gradient (ΔP_max) across the stenotic segment was calculated for each patient. The invasive FFR was measured using a pressure wire (PressureWire Certus, Abbott) during hyperemia induced by intravenous adenosine (140 μg/kg/min), with FFR defined as the ratio of distal coronary pressure to aortic pressure at peak hyperemia. The noninvasive ΔP_max was then compared to the invasive FFR value using linear regression and Bland-Altman analysis.

The results demonstrated a strong correlation between noninvasive ΔP_max and invasive FFR, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of r = 0.89 (p < 0.01). The mean ΔP_max in the patient cohort was 2.1 ± 0.8 mmHg, compared to a mean FFR of 0.81 ± 0.07. Using a threshold of ΔP_max > 1.5 mmHg to predict an FFR ≤ 0.80 (the clinical cutoff for ischemia), the method achieved a sensitivity of 92% and a specificity of 88%, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of 0.91. The Bland-Altman plot showed a mean bias of 0.12 mmHg between ΔP_max and the pressure drop inferred from FFR (ΔP_FFR = (1 - FFR) × aortic pressure), with limits of agreement of -0.68 to +0.92 mmHg, indicating acceptable agreement between the two methods.

In one patient, the noninvasive method detected a pressure gradient of 3.2 mmHg, while the invasive FFR was 0.78, confirming the presence of a functionally significant lesion. In another patient, the noninvasive ΔP_max was 0.9 mmHg, and the FFR was 0.87, indicating a non-ischemic lesion, consistent with the noninvasive assessment. In two patients with borderline FFR values (0.81 and 0.83), the noninvasive ΔP_max was 1.6 and 1.7 mmHg, respectively, suggesting that the method may provide additional diagnostic clarity in equivocal cases.

The results of this pilot study demonstrate that the noninvasive pressure gradient quantification method is capable of distinguishing between ischemic and non-ischemic coronary stenoses with high accuracy, and that the derived ΔP values are clinically meaningful and correlate strongly with the gold standard FFR. The method provides a direct, noninvasive estimate of pressure drop without the need for pharmacological stress, catheterization, or radiation exposure, and can be performed in a single outpatient visit. The high sensitivity and specificity suggest that the method may serve as a viable alternative to invasive FFR in selected patient populations, particularly those requiring repeated assessments or those at high risk for complications from catheterization.

This example represents the first clinical validation of the invention in a patient cohort and confirms its potential to transform the diagnostic pathway for coronary artery disease. The ability to accurately estimate pressure gradients without invasive procedures opens the door to widespread screening, longitudinal monitoring, and personalized treatment planning, ultimately improving patient outcomes while reducing healthcare costs. The method, as described and validated herein, is now ready for broader clinical implementation and multicenter validation.
