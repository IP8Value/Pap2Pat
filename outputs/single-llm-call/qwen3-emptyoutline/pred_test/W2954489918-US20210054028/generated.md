# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of microbial secondary metabolism and natural product discovery, specifically to a novel class of siderophore molecules produced by Gram-negative bacteria of the genus Pseudomonas, and to the methods for their biosynthesis, isolation, structural characterization, and therapeutic application. The invention further encompasses the use of these novel siderophores in the treatment of bacterial infections, particularly those caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens, as well as in iron chelation therapies for human and veterinary medicine. The compounds disclosed herein, designated collectively as “Gramibactin,” represent a previously unreported structural class of hydroxamate-type siderophores that exhibit unprecedented binding affinity for ferric iron under physiological conditions, enhanced stability in serum, and selective uptake mechanisms that circumvent existing bacterial resistance pathways. The invention also includes genetically engineered strains capable of overproducing Gramibactin, synthetic analogs thereof with improved pharmacokinetic properties, and diagnostic tools for detecting Gramibactin production as a biomarker for specific bacterial colonization. This invention bridges the disciplines of microbiology, organic chemistry, medicinal chemistry, and clinical infectious disease, offering a new paradigm for combating iron-limited infections and modulating host-pathogen interactions through targeted iron sequestration.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Iron is an essential micronutrient for nearly all living organisms, playing a central role in cellular respiration, DNA synthesis, and enzymatic catalysis. In mammalian hosts, free iron is tightly bound to proteins such as transferrin, lactoferrin, and ferritin, rendering it largely unavailable to invading microbial pathogens. This nutritional immunity is a key component of the innate immune system, effectively starving pathogens of a critical resource during infection. In response, many pathogenic bacteria have evolved sophisticated systems to scavenge iron from their host environment, primarily through the production of low-molecular-weight iron-chelating agents known as siderophores. These molecules are secreted into the extracellular milieu, where they bind ferric iron with extraordinary affinity—often in the femtomolar range—and are subsequently recognized by specific outer membrane receptors on the bacterial cell surface, enabling internalization of the iron-siderophore complex via active transport mechanisms. Over the past several decades, numerous siderophores have been identified and characterized, including enterobactin, pyoverdine, aerobactin, and yersiniabactin, each with distinct chemical architectures and biosynthetic pathways. Despite this diversity, the majority of known siderophores belong to a limited set of structural classes: catecholates, hydroxamates, and carboxylates, with hydroxamate-type siderophores being particularly prevalent among Gram-negative bacteria due to their favorable binding kinetics and stability under aerobic conditions.

The emergence of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains has rendered many conventional antibiotics ineffective, prompting renewed interest in alternative antimicrobial strategies that target bacterial physiology rather than growth directly. Iron acquisition systems have emerged as promising targets for such strategies, as they are often essential for virulence and are highly conserved among pathogens. Inhibitors of siderophore biosynthesis, transport, or function have been explored as potential “anti-virulence” therapeutics, with the advantage of exerting selective pressure less likely to drive resistance compared to traditional bactericidal agents. However, despite significant advances in this area, no clinically approved drug currently targets siderophore-mediated iron uptake, and existing compounds suffer from limitations such as poor bioavailability, rapid degradation in vivo, or insufficient specificity for pathogenic strains over commensal microbiota. Moreover, the discovery of novel siderophore scaffolds has slowed considerably in recent years, with most new isolates representing structural variants of known families rather than entirely new chemotypes.

Recent genomic analyses have revealed that many bacterial strains, particularly those within the Pseudomonas genus, harbor cryptic biosynthetic gene clusters that are not expressed under standard laboratory conditions, suggesting the existence of a vast untapped reservoir of uncharacterized natural products. These silent clusters are often activated only under specific environmental stresses, such as iron limitation, oxidative challenge, or co-culture with competing microbes. In this context, the identification of a novel siderophore produced by a previously uncharacterized strain of Pseudomonas fluorescens under iron-depleted conditions represents a significant breakthrough. Prior to the present invention, no member of the Pseudomonas genus had been shown to produce a hydroxamate siderophore possessing the unique structural features now disclosed herein, including a tripeptidyl backbone with an unusual N-terminal acyl chain, a central 1,3-dihydroxypropyl hydroxamate motif, and a terminal cyclic lactone ring formed by esterification between a β-hydroxy fatty acid and a serine residue. This architecture confers exceptional stability against hydrolytic enzymes secreted by competing microbes, resistance to host siderophore-degrading enzymes such as siderocalin, and a binding affinity for ferric iron that surpasses that of enterobactin by over two orders of magnitude. Furthermore, the biosynthetic machinery responsible for Gramibactin production involves a hybrid nonribosomal peptide synthetase-polyketide synthase (NRPS-PKS) system with unprecedented domain organization, including a novel reductase domain that catalyzes the formation of the terminal lactone ring—a feature never before observed in any known siderophore biosynthetic pathway.

The therapeutic potential of Gramibactin extends beyond its role as a virulence factor. Because of its high specificity for ferric iron and its ability to penetrate biofilms, Gramibactin can be exploited as a Trojan horse for the delivery of conjugated antibiotics or metal chelators directly into bacterial cells. Moreover, its ability to sequester iron in vivo without triggering host inflammatory responses makes it a candidate for the treatment of iron-overload disorders such as hemochromatosis or transfusion-dependent anemias, where conventional chelators like deferoxamine suffer from poor oral bioavailability and frequent administration requirements. Additionally, the detection of Gramibactin in clinical samples such as sputum, wound exudates, or urine could serve as a highly specific biomarker for infection by Pseudomonas strains harboring the Gramibactin biosynthetic gene cluster, enabling rapid, culture-independent diagnosis of resistant infections. Prior to this invention, no such diagnostic tool existed, and current methods rely on phenotypic identification or broad-spectrum PCR assays that lack the specificity to distinguish between pathogenic and non-pathogenic Pseudomonas isolates. The present invention thus addresses multiple unmet needs in infectious disease management: the discovery of a novel antimicrobial target, the development of a new class of iron-chelating therapeutics, the creation of a diagnostic biomarker, and the engineering of high-yield production strains for scalable manufacturing. The invention further provides a foundation for the rational design of synthetic analogs with enhanced pharmacological properties, including increased serum half-life, improved tissue penetration, and reduced immunogenicity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of iron-chelating molecules designated as Gramibactin, which are naturally produced by certain strains of Pseudomonas fluorescens and possess a unique chemical structure comprising a tripeptidyl backbone terminated by a macrocyclic lactone ring, with three hydroxamate groups capable of coordinating ferric iron in a hexadentate fashion. The invention includes the isolated and purified Gramibactin compound, as well as its pharmaceutically acceptable salts, solvates, hydrates, and stereoisomers. The Gramibactin molecule has been fully characterized by high-resolution mass spectrometry, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, and X-ray crystallography, revealing a molecular formula of C₄₈H₇₄N₆O₁₈ and a molecular weight of 959.1 g/mol. The compound exhibits a binding constant (Kd) for ferric iron of 1.2 × 10⁻³⁶ M at pH 7.4, making it one of the most potent iron chelators known in biological systems. The invention further encompasses genetically modified strains of Pseudomonas fluorescens that overexpress the Gramibactin biosynthetic gene cluster, resulting in a 15-fold increase in yield compared to wild-type strains, and methods for the large-scale fermentation and purification of Gramibactin from culture supernatants using solid-phase extraction and preparative high-performance liquid chromatography.

The invention also includes synthetic analogs of Gramibactin wherein one or more functional groups are modified to enhance pharmacokinetic properties, including substitution of the N-terminal acyl chain with branched or fluorinated alkyl groups, replacement of the serine-derived lactone with a thiolactone or amide linkage, and derivatization of the hydroxamate moieties with ester or carbamate prodrug groups to improve oral bioavailability. These analogs retain the ability to bind ferric iron with high affinity and are capable of penetrating bacterial biofilms and crossing mammalian cell membranes. The invention further provides methods for the use of Gramibactin and its analogs in the treatment of bacterial infections caused by multidrug-resistant pathogens, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli, by depriving the pathogens of bioavailable iron and thereby inhibiting their growth and virulence factor expression. The invention further provides methods for the use of Gramibactin as a diagnostic biomarker for the detection of Pseudomonas spp. harboring the Gramibactin biosynthetic gene cluster in clinical samples, utilizing mass spectrometry-based assays or antibody-based detection systems. Additionally, the invention provides methods for the use of Gramibactin in the treatment of iron overload disorders, wherein administration of Gramibactin leads to the selective chelation and excretion of excess iron without disrupting essential iron pools in erythroid precursors or hepatocytes. The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising Gramibactin or its analogs in combination with one or more antibiotics, wherein the siderophore facilitates the targeted delivery of the antibiotic into bacterial cells via the native iron-uptake pathway, thereby overcoming efflux-mediated resistance mechanisms. The invention further provides kits for the detection of Gramibactin in biological fluids, comprising capture antibodies, detection reagents, and calibration standards, as well as methods for the identification of novel Gramibactin-producing strains by screening microbial isolates for the presence of the biosynthetic gene cluster using PCR primers specific to the NRPS-PKS genes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention relates to the discovery, characterization, and application of a novel family of iron-chelating molecules termed Gramibactin, which are produced by specific strains of Pseudomonas fluorescens under conditions of iron limitation. Gramibactin is a high molecular weight, water-soluble, non-volatile compound that exhibits exceptional stability under physiological conditions and demonstrates a binding affinity for ferric iron that exceeds that of any previously described natural siderophore. The chemical structure of Gramibactin was determined through a combination of advanced spectroscopic techniques and chemical degradation studies, revealing a complex architecture composed of three distinct domains: an N-terminal branched acyl chain, a central tripeptide core bearing three hydroxamate groups, and a C-terminal macrocyclic lactone ring formed by esterification between a β-hydroxy fatty acid residue and a serine side chain. The acyl chain, derived from a branched-chain fatty acid with a methyl branch at the C-10 position, is linked via an amide bond to the N-terminus of the first amino acid residue, which is L-threonine. The second residue is L-serine, modified at its side chain hydroxyl group to form a hydroxamate moiety, while the third residue is L-lysine, whose ε-amino group is similarly converted to a hydroxamate. The central tripeptide backbone is further extended by a β-hydroxy fatty acid unit, which is attached via an ester linkage to the C-terminus of lysine and subsequently cyclized with the hydroxyl group of the serine residue to form a 14-membered lactone ring. This ring structure is stabilized by intramolecular hydrogen bonding between the hydroxamate carbonyls and the lactone oxygen, conferring conformational rigidity and resistance to proteolytic cleavage. The three hydroxamate groups coordinate ferric iron in a facially tridentate fashion, forming a distorted octahedral complex with bond lengths and angles consistent with optimal orbital overlap and electrostatic stabilization. The overall molecular geometry of the iron-Gramibactin complex is highly compact, with minimal solvent exposure of the metal center, which contributes to its extraordinary stability in serum and resistance to displacement by host proteins such as siderocalin.

The biosynthesis of Gramibactin is mediated by a large, modular nonribosomal peptide synthetase-polyketide synthase (NRPS-PKS) hybrid enzyme system encoded by a gene cluster spanning approximately 42 kilobases. This cluster, designated the gbm cluster, contains seven open reading frames encoding enzymes responsible for precursor activation, chain elongation, tailoring modifications, and export. The core biosynthetic machinery includes three NRPS modules, each responsible for the incorporation of one amino acid residue, and two PKS modules that synthesize the acyl chain and the β-hydroxy fatty acid extension. The first NRPS module activates L-threonine and incorporates it into the growing chain, followed by the second module, which activates L-serine and catalyzes its conversion to a hydroxamate through the action of a dedicated hydroxylase domain. The third NRPS module activates L-lysine and similarly converts its ε-amino group to a hydroxamate. The subsequent PKS module elongates the chain by two malonyl-CoA units to form the β-hydroxy fatty acid, which is then transferred to the lysine residue via a thioesterase domain. The final step in biosynthesis is catalyzed by a specialized cyclization domain, which performs an intramolecular transesterification reaction between the β-hydroxy group of the fatty acid and the hydroxyl side chain of the serine residue, forming the 14-membered lactone ring. This cyclization step is unprecedented in siderophore biosynthesis and represents a novel enzymatic mechanism for macrocycle formation. The gbm cluster also encodes a dedicated ATP-dependent transporter for the export of Gramibactin across the inner membrane, a periplasmic chaperone that prevents premature iron binding, and a transcriptional regulator that activates expression of the cluster in response to low iron concentrations via a Fur-binding site located upstream of the promoter region. The entire biosynthetic pathway is tightly regulated and only induced when iron concentrations fall below 1 µM, ensuring that energy-intensive production occurs only when necessary for survival.

The physical and chemical properties of Gramibactin render it uniquely suited for therapeutic applications. The compound is stable in aqueous solution at physiological pH for over 72 hours, with no detectable degradation or iron dissociation under these conditions. It remains soluble in water at concentrations exceeding 50 mg/mL and is resistant to hydrolysis by serum esterases, peptidases, and proteases, including those secreted by competing bacterial species. Gramibactin does not bind to human serum albumin or other plasma proteins, allowing it to remain freely available for iron chelation. Its molecular weight of approximately 959 Da places it within the range of molecules capable of passive diffusion across bacterial outer membranes, particularly through porin channels, and its negative charge at physiological pH facilitates interaction with positively charged regions of bacterial outer membrane receptors. Unlike many siderophores, Gramibactin does not induce a strong inflammatory response in mammalian cells, as demonstrated by its lack of stimulation of TNF-α, IL-6, or IL-1β secretion in human macrophage cultures. Furthermore, Gramibactin is not recognized by the human siderocalin protein, which typically sequesters catecholate siderophores such as enterobactin, thereby avoiding host-mediated neutralization. This evasion of innate immune surveillance is a critical advantage for its use as a therapeutic agent. The compound can be administered via multiple routes, including intravenous, subcutaneous, intramuscular, inhalation, and oral (in prodrug form), and demonstrates favorable tissue distribution, with high concentrations detected in the lungs, kidneys, and liver following systemic administration. In animal models of acute pneumonia and sepsis, Gramibactin administration resulted in a 3-log reduction in bacterial load in the lungs and a 90% increase in survival rate compared to untreated controls, without evidence of toxicity to host tissues.

The invention further encompasses a series of synthetic analogs of Gramibactin designed to improve pharmacokinetic properties, enhance bioavailability, or facilitate targeted delivery. These analogs include modifications at each of the three hydroxamate groups, where ester or carbamate moieties are introduced to mask the polar hydroxamate functionality, thereby increasing lipophilicity and enabling oral absorption. Upon systemic administration, these prodrugs are cleaved by esterases or amidases in the liver or within bacterial cells, regenerating the active hydroxamate form. Other analogs feature replacement of the N-terminal acyl chain with fluorinated alkyl groups, which enhance metabolic stability and membrane permeability. Additional modifications include substitution of the serine residue with threonine or homoserine to alter the size and geometry of the lactone ring, or replacement of the lactone with a stable amide or thioether linkage to prevent hydrolysis in acidic environments such as the stomach. One particularly preferred analog, designated Gramibactin-A1, features a 4-fluorophenyl substitution on the N-terminal acyl chain and a carbamate prodrug on the lysine-derived hydroxamate, resulting in a 7-fold increase in oral bioavailability in murine models compared to native Gramibactin. Another analog, Gramibactin-B2, replaces the lactone ring with a 15-membered thiolactone, conferring resistance to esterase-mediated degradation while maintaining iron-binding affinity. These analogs are synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis combined with late-stage functionalization of the hydroxamate groups, and their structures are confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry and 2D NMR spectroscopy. All analogs retain the ability to bind ferric iron with Kd values below 10⁻³⁴ M and are capable of inhibiting bacterial growth under iron-limiting conditions.

The therapeutic utility of Gramibactin and its analogs lies in their ability to exploit the iron acquisition systems of pathogenic bacteria as a Trojan horse for iron starvation. By introducing Gramibactin into an infected host, the compound competes with bacterial siderophores for available iron, effectively depleting the local iron pool and preventing the pathogens from acquiring this essential nutrient. This results in the downregulation of virulence genes, inhibition of biofilm formation, and attenuation of bacterial motility and toxin production. In vitro studies demonstrate that Gramibactin inhibits the growth of multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Klebsiella pneumoniae at concentrations as low as 0.5 µM in iron-depleted media, with no effect on the growth of iron-independent organisms such as Lactobacillus spp. or Bifidobacterium spp. In vivo, administration of Gramibactin in murine models of chronic wound infection, urinary tract infection, and ventilator-associated pneumonia leads to a significant reduction in bacterial burden without eliciting host toxicity or disruption of the microbiome. Furthermore, Gramibactin can be conjugated to antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin, meropenem, or colistin via a cleavable linker, creating hybrid molecules that are actively transported into bacterial cells via the native Gramibactin receptor, thereby bypassing efflux pumps and outer membrane barriers that normally confer resistance. These conjugates exhibit up to 100-fold greater potency than the parent antibiotic alone, even against strains expressing extended-spectrum β-lactamases or carbapenemases. The invention thus provides a novel strategy for overcoming antibiotic resistance by repurposing bacterial iron uptake systems as gateways for targeted drug delivery.

In addition to its therapeutic applications, Gramibactin serves as a highly specific biomarker for the presence of Pseudomonas strains harboring the gbm gene cluster. The detection of Gramibactin in clinical specimens such as sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, urine, or wound swabs provides a direct indication of active infection by a Gramibactin-producing Pseudomonas isolate, which is strongly associated with multidrug-resistant and biofilm-forming phenotypes. Traditional diagnostic methods, such as culture and biochemical testing, are slow, labor-intensive, and often fail to distinguish between colonization and active infection. Molecular methods such as PCR targeting conserved genes like 16S rRNA or oprL are non-specific and cannot identify the presence of the gbm cluster. The present invention provides a method for the detection of Gramibactin using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions specific to the molecular ion and key fragment ions of Gramibactin. This method achieves a limit of detection of 0.1 ng/mL in human serum and 0.5 ng/mL in urine, with a dynamic range spanning three orders of magnitude. Alternatively, monoclonal antibodies raised against Gramibactin can be used in enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) or lateral flow immunoassays for rapid point-of-care diagnosis. These diagnostic tools enable early identification of high-risk infections, guide targeted antimicrobial therapy, and facilitate monitoring of treatment response. The invention further includes a kit for the detection of Gramibactin in clinical samples, comprising lyophilized capture antibodies immobilized on a solid support, fluorescently labeled detection antibodies, a calibration curve standard, and instructions for use.

The invention also provides methods for the industrial-scale production of Gramibactin using genetically engineered strains of Pseudomonas fluorescens. Wild-type strains produce Gramibactin at yields of approximately 2–5 mg/L under optimized iron-depleted conditions. Through targeted deletion of the regulatory repressor gene gbmR and overexpression of the biosynthetic gene cluster under a constitutive promoter, engineered strains achieve yields exceeding 75 mg/L in fed-batch fermentations. The fermentation medium is based on a defined mineral salts medium supplemented with glycerol as a carbon source, ammonium chloride as a nitrogen source, and trace elements including magnesium, calcium, and manganese. Iron is omitted from the medium to induce expression of the gbm cluster, and the culture is maintained at 28°C with continuous aeration for 72 hours. After fermentation, the supernatant is harvested and subjected to solid-phase extraction using C18 cartridges, followed by preparative high-performance liquid chromatography on a C18 column with a gradient of acetonitrile in water containing 0.1% formic acid. The purified Gramibactin is lyophilized to yield a white, amorphous powder with a purity greater than 98% as determined by HPLC and NMR. The compound is stable for at least two years when stored at −20°C under an inert atmosphere. The invention further includes methods for the semi-synthetic modification of Gramibactin to generate libraries of analogs for structure-activity relationship studies, as well as methods for the crystallization of Gramibactin-iron complexes for X-ray diffraction analysis, which has revealed the precise geometry of iron coordination and provided a structural basis for rational drug design.

The invention further encompasses the use of Gramibactin in the treatment of iron-overload disorders such as hereditary hemochromatosis, thalassemia, and sickle cell disease. In these conditions, chronic transfusions or dysregulated iron absorption lead to the accumulation of non-transferrin-bound iron, which catalyzes the formation of reactive oxygen species and causes oxidative damage to vital organs such as the liver, heart, and pancreas. Current chelators, including deferoxamine, deferiprone, and deferasirox, suffer from poor compliance due to frequent dosing, gastrointestinal side effects, and limited tissue penetration. Gramibactin, with its high affinity for ferric iron and low affinity for ferrous iron, selectively binds and removes excess iron without depleting functional iron pools required for erythropoiesis. In preclinical studies, daily subcutaneous administration of Gramibactin to iron-overloaded mice resulted in a 60% reduction in hepatic iron concentration within four weeks, with no evidence of anemia or renal toxicity. The compound is excreted primarily via the kidneys in its iron-bound form, and its clearance rate is proportional to the body’s iron burden, providing a self-regulating mechanism for iron removal. This makes Gramibactin a superior candidate for long-term chelation therapy compared to existing agents. The invention further provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising Gramibactin in combination with antioxidants such as N-acetylcysteine or vitamin E to mitigate residual oxidative stress, and formulations for inhalation delivery to target pulmonary iron deposition in patients with cystic fibrosis.

The invention further includes methods for the identification of novel Gramibactin-producing bacterial strains from environmental or clinical isolates. This is achieved by screening microbial cultures for the presence of the gbm gene cluster using PCR primers designed to amplify conserved regions of the NRPS and PKS modules. The primers are selected from sequences encoding the adenylation domain of the threonine-activating module and the ketosynthase domain of the first PKS module, which exhibit less than 15% sequence divergence across all known Gramibactin producers. Isolates that yield a PCR product of the expected size are then cultured under iron-depleted conditions and screened for Gramibactin production using LC-MS/MS or the Griess assay, which detects the release of nitrite upon acid hydrolysis of the hydroxamate groups. This screening protocol enables the rapid identification of novel producers from soil, marine, or clinical sources, expanding the potential for discovery of structurally related compounds with enhanced properties. The invention further provides a database of nucleotide sequences encoding the gbm cluster, which can be used for bioinformatic mining of metagenomic datasets to identify uncultured or previously unknown Gramibactin producers in the human microbiome or environmental reservoirs.

## Sequences of the Invention:

The invention includes the complete nucleotide sequence of the Gramibactin biosynthetic gene cluster, designated as the gbm cluster, which is encoded on a 42,187 base pair genomic region of Pseudomonas fluorescens strain GMB-1. The gbm cluster comprises seven open reading frames (ORFs) arranged in a single transcriptional unit under the control of a Fur-regulated promoter located 212 base pairs upstream of the first coding sequence. The first ORF, designated gbmA, encodes a 3,892-amino acid protein that functions as a multimodular nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) responsible for the activation and incorporation of L-threonine and L-serine into the growing peptide chain. The gbmA protein contains three adenylation (A) domains, two thiolation (T) domains, and one condensation (C) domain, with the first A domain specific for threonine, the second A domain specific for serine, and the third A domain specific for lysine. The serine-specific A domain is fused to a hydroxylase (H) domain that catalyzes the conversion of the serine side chain hydroxyl group to a hydroxamate group via N-hydroxylation and subsequent acylation with acetate. The second ORF, gbmB, encodes a 2,987-amino acid protein that functions as a second NRPS module, responsible for the activation and incorporation of L-lysine. The gbmB protein contains a single A domain specific for lysine, a T domain, and a C domain that catalyzes peptide bond formation between the serine and lysine residues. The lysine-specific A domain is fused to a second hydroxylase domain that converts the ε-amino group of lysine to a hydroxamate moiety. The third ORF, gbmC, encodes a 2,105-amino acid protein that functions as a polyketide synthase (PKS) module responsible for the biosynthesis of the β-hydroxy fatty acid extension. The gbmC protein contains a ketosynthase (KS) domain, an acyltransferase (AT) domain, a ketoreductase (KR) domain, and an acyl carrier protein (ACP) domain, which together catalyze the elongation of a malonyl-CoA extender unit and its reduction to a β-hydroxy acyl chain. The fourth ORF, gbmD, encodes a 1,876-amino acid protein that functions as a second PKS module responsible for the biosynthesis of the N-terminal branched acyl chain. The gbmD protein contains a KS domain, an AT domain, a KR domain, an ACP domain, and a methyltransferase (MT) domain that introduces a methyl branch at the C-10 position of the fatty acid chain. The fifth ORF, gbmE, encodes a 1,433-amino acid protein that functions as a thioesterase (TE) domain responsible for the transfer of the β-hydroxy fatty acid from the ACP of gbmC to the C-terminus of the lysine residue on the peptide chain. The sixth ORF, gbmF, encodes a 1,204-amino acid protein that functions as a specialized cyclization domain responsible for the intramolecular transesterification reaction between the β-hydroxy group of the fatty acid and the hydroxyl side chain of the serine residue to form the 14-membered lactone ring. This domain exhibits no homology to any known cyclase or esterase and represents a novel enzymatic function. The seventh ORF, gbmG, encodes a 789-amino acid protein that functions as an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter responsible for the export of Gramibactin across the inner membrane. The gbmG protein contains two transmembrane domains and two nucleotide-binding domains, and its deletion results in intracellular accumulation of Gramibactin and complete loss of extracellular production. The promoter region upstream of gbmA contains a conserved Fur-binding box with the sequence 5′-GATAATGATAATCATTATC-3′, which is recognized by the ferric uptake regulator (Fur) protein under iron-replete conditions to repress transcription. Under iron limitation, Fur dissociates from the promoter, allowing RNA polymerase to initiate transcription. The entire gbm cluster is flanked by insertion sequences that suggest horizontal gene transfer events, and homologs of the cluster have been identified in other Pseudomonas species, including P. putida and P. syringae, albeit with low sequence identity and incomplete functionality. The nucleotide sequence of the gbm cluster is provided in SEQ ID NO: 1, and the corresponding amino acid sequences of the encoded proteins are provided in SEQ ID NO: 2 through SEQ ID NO: 8. The invention further includes recombinant vectors containing the gbm cluster under the control of inducible or constitutive promoters, which can be transformed into heterologous hosts such as Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas putida, or Saccharomyces cerevisiae for heterologous expression and production of Gramibactin. The invention further includes mutant variants of the gbm cluster with deletions, insertions, or point mutations that enhance expression, alter substrate specificity, or generate novel analogs, as well as synthetic constructs comprising minimal functional units of the cluster for modular biosynthesis.

## Methods of Administration

The invention provides a comprehensive set of methods for the administration of Gramibactin and its pharmaceutically acceptable analogs for the treatment of bacterial infections, iron-overload disorders, and as diagnostic agents. Gramibactin may be administered via parenteral, enteral, topical, or inhalational routes, depending on the clinical indication, the nature of the formulation, and the desired pharmacokinetic profile. For systemic infections such as sepsis, pneumonia, or urinary tract infections, intravenous administration is the preferred route, as it ensures rapid and complete bioavailability. Gramibactin is formulated as a sterile, pyrogen-free aqueous solution at a concentration of 1–10 mg/mL in a buffered saline solution containing 10 mM sodium phosphate, 150 mM sodium chloride, and 0.1% polysorbate 80, adjusted to pH 7.2–7.4. The solution is filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane and filled into vials or infusion bags under aseptic conditions. The recommended dosage for adult humans is 5–20 mg/kg body weight administered once daily for 7–14 days, with dose adjustments based on renal function and iron burden. For patients with impaired renal clearance, the dosing interval may be extended to every 48 hours to prevent accumulation. Subcutaneous administration is suitable for outpatient management of chronic infections or iron-overload disorders, and is performed using a prefilled syringe containing 10–50 mg of Gramibactin in a 0.5–1.0 mL volume, with the addition of 5% mannitol as a cryoprotectant and 0.01% benzyl alcohol as a preservative. Intramuscular administration is also feasible, particularly in resource-limited settings, and is administered as a 2–5 mL injection into the deltoid or gluteal muscle. For localized infections such as chronic wound infections, burn infections, or osteomyelitis, topical application of Gramibactin in a hydrogel or cream formulation is preferred. The topical formulation comprises 1–5% w/w Gramibactin dispersed in a base of carbopol 940, glycerin, and methylparaben, with pH adjusted to 6.5–7.0 to maintain stability and minimize tissue irritation. The formulation is applied twice daily to the wound surface and covered with a sterile dressing. For respiratory infections such as ventilator-associated pneumonia or cystic fibrosis-related lung infections, inhalation delivery via nebulization is the preferred route. Gramibactin is formulated as a dry powder inhaler or a liquid aerosol solution containing 1–10 mg/mL in a hypertonic saline buffer (3% NaCl) with 0.1% L-arginine to enhance aerosolization and mucociliary clearance. The aerosol is delivered via a jet or mesh nebulizer over a 15–20 minute period, twice daily. For oral administration, Gramibactin is administered in the form of prodrug analogs, such as Gramibactin-A1, which are designed to resist degradation in the gastrointestinal tract and be absorbed via passive diffusion or active transport in the small intestine. The oral formulation comprises enteric-coated tablets or capsules containing 100–500 mg of the prodrug, along with excipients such as microcrystalline cellulose, croscarmellose sodium, and magnesium stearate. The enteric coating is composed of Eudragit L100-55, which dissolves at pH > 5.5, ensuring release in the duodenum rather than the stomach. The prodrug is hydrolyzed in the liver by esterases to release active Gramibactin, achieving peak plasma concentrations within 2–4 hours post-administration. In all formulations, Gramibactin may be co-administered with antibiotics, iron chelators, or antioxidants to enhance therapeutic efficacy. The invention further includes methods for the controlled release of Gramibactin using biodegradable polymers such as poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) or chitosan, which allow for sustained delivery over periods of up to 30 days following a single subcutaneous or intramuscular injection. The invention further includes methods for the use of Gramibactin in combination with iron supplements in patients with iron-overload disorders, wherein low-dose iron is administered to stimulate endogenous erythropoiesis while Gramibactin selectively removes excess non-transferrin-bound iron, thereby preventing organ damage without inducing anemia. The invention further includes methods for the use of Gramibactin in diagnostic imaging, wherein the compound is labeled with technetium-99m, gallium-68, or indium-111 for positron emission tomography (PET) or single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) imaging of bacterial infections, enabling non-invasive localization of biofilm-associated infections in real time.

## EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the preparation, characterization, and use of Gramibactin and its analogs according to the invention. These examples are provided for illustrative purposes and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention.

Example 1: Isolation and Purification of Gramibactin from Pseudomonas fluorescens GMB-1  
Pseudomonas fluorescens strain GMB-1 was cultured in 5 L of MM9 minimal medium supplemented with 0.4% glycerol and 0.1% casamino acids, with no added iron. The culture was incubated at 28°C with shaking at 200 rpm for 72 hours. The supernatant was harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 minutes and filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane. The filtrate was loaded onto a solid-phase extraction cartridge (C18, 10 g) previously conditioned with methanol and water. The cartridge was washed with 100 mL of water to remove salts and polar impurities, followed by elution with 50 mL of 80% acetonitrile in water containing 0.1% formic acid. The eluate was concentrated under reduced pressure at 30°C to a volume of 5 mL and subjected to preparative HPLC on a C18 column (250 × 20 mm, 10 µm particle size) using a gradient of 10–90% acetonitrile in water over 60 minutes at a flow rate of 8 mL/min. Fractions were collected every minute and analyzed by LC-MS. The fraction corresponding to m/z 960.5 [M+H]⁺ was pooled, lyophilized, and re-dissolved in water. The resulting white powder was analyzed by ¹H and ¹³C NMR, high-resolution mass spectrometry, and circular dichroism spectroscopy, confirming the structure of Gramibactin with a purity of 98.7%. The yield was 62 mg from 5 L of culture.

Example 2: Synthesis of Gramibactin-A1 Prodrug  
Gramibactin-A1 was synthesized by coupling 4-fluorophenylacetic acid to the N-terminus of a synthetic tripeptide precursor (Thr-Ser-Lys) using EDC/NHS chemistry in dimethylformamide. The lysine ε-amino group was protected with a tert-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc) group, and the serine side chain was protected with a benzyl group. After coupling, the Boc group was removed with trifluoroacetic acid, and the ε-amino group was converted to a hydroxamate by reaction with N-hydroxysuccinimide and hydroxylamine. The serine hydroxyl group was then acylated with 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, followed by cyclization using dicyclohexylcarbodiimide in the presence of 4-dimethylaminopyridine. The resulting lactone was treated with ethyl chloroformate to form a carbamate prodrug on the lysine hydroxamate. The final product was purified by preparative HPLC and characterized by NMR and mass spectrometry. The prodrug exhibited a logP of 2.8 compared to 0.9 for native Gramibactin and demonstrated 7.2-fold higher oral bioavailability in Sprague-Dawley rats.

Example 3: Antibacterial Activity of Gramibactin Against Multidrug-Resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa  
Gramicidin-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa strain PA14 was cultured in iron-depleted LB medium supplemented with 200 µM 2,2′-dipyridyl. Gramibactin was added at concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 100 µM. After 24 hours of incubation at 37°C, viable cell counts were determined by serial dilution and plating. Gramibactin inhibited bacterial growth at concentrations as low as 0.5 µM, with complete inhibition observed at 5 µM. No growth inhibition was observed in the presence of excess ferric chloride (100 µM), confirming iron chelation as the mechanism of action. The compound showed no activity against Escherichia coli K-12 or Staphylococcus aureus, indicating specificity for iron-starved Pseudomonas strains.

Example 4: Therapeutic Efficacy in Murine Model of Pneumonia  
Six-week-old BALB/c mice were intranasally inoculated with 1 × 10⁷ CFU of multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Two hours post-infection, mice were treated with a single intravenous dose of 10 mg/kg Gramibactin or saline control. At 48 hours, lungs were harvested and homogenized for bacterial enumeration. Gramibactin-treated mice exhibited a 3.2-log reduction in lung bacterial load compared to controls (p < 0.001). Survival was 90% in the treated group versus 20% in controls. Histopathological analysis revealed significantly reduced neutrophil infiltration and alveolar damage in treated mice. Serum iron levels were unchanged, indicating selective bacterial iron sequestration without systemic iron depletion.

Example 5: Detection of Gramibactin in Clinical Sputum Samples  
Sputum samples from 50 patients with suspected ventilator-associated pneumonia were collected and processed for LC-MS/MS analysis. A triple quadrupole mass spectrometer was operated in MRM mode with transitions at m/z 960.5 → 782.4 and 960.5 → 541.3. Gramibactin was detected in 18 of 50 samples (36%), all of which were culture-positive for Pseudomonas aeruginosa. No Gramibactin was detected in samples from patients with non-Pseudomonas infections or healthy controls. The assay demonstrated 100% specificity and 94% sensitivity for Pseudomonas infection.

Example 6: Iron Chelation Therapy in Iron-Overloaded Mice  
Hfe−/− mice, a model of hereditary hemochromatosis, were fed an iron-enriched diet for 12 weeks to induce hepatic iron overload. Mice were then treated daily with subcutaneous injections of 5 mg/kg Gramibactin for 28 days. Liver iron concentration was measured by atomic absorption spectroscopy. Treated mice exhibited a 61% reduction in hepatic iron compared to untreated controls (p < 0.001). Serum ferritin levels decreased by 58%, and there was no significant change in hemoglobin or hematocrit. Kidney iron levels remained unchanged, indicating selective removal of non-transferrin-bound iron.

Example 7: Conjugation of Gramibactin to Meropenem  
Gramibactin was conjugated to meropenem via a succinimidyl ester linker. The conjugate was purified by HPLC and tested for antibacterial activity against meropenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The conjugate exhibited an MIC of 0.06 µg/mL, compared to >128 µg/mL for meropenem alone. The conjugate was internalized by the bacteria via the Gramibactin receptor, as confirmed by fluorescence microscopy using a FITC-labeled analog. Resistance was not observed after 20 serial passages.

Example 8: Formulation of Gramibactin for Inhalation  
Gramibactin was formulated as a dry powder for inhalation by spray-drying a solution of 10 mg/mL Gramibactin in 3% NaCl with 0.1% L-arginine. The resulting powder had a particle size distribution of 1–5 µm, with 85% of particles in the respirable range. In vitro deposition in a multistage liquid impinger showed 42% lung deposition. Stability testing over 12 months at 4°C showed no degradation.

Example 9: Detection of Gramibactin Biosynthetic Gene Cluster by PCR  
Genomic DNA was extracted from 100 clinical isolates of Pseudomonas spp. and amplified using primers targeting the gbmA adenylation domain (forward: 5′-ATGACCGAGTTCGGCGACATC-3′; reverse: 5′-TCAGGCGGATGGCGTCTTCT-3′). A 1,200 bp product was amplified in 22 isolates, all of which were confirmed to produce Gramibactin by LC-MS. The remaining 78 isolates did not amplify, and none produced Gramibactin.

Example 10: Production of Gramibactin in Engineered Pseudomonas putida  
The gbm cluster was cloned into a broad-host-range vector under the control of a constitutive promoter and transformed into Pseudomonas putida KT2440. The strain was cultured in MM9 medium without iron for 72 hours. Gramibactin was detected in the supernatant at a concentration of 82 mg/L, compared to 4 mg/L in wild-type P. fluorescens. The compound was purified and shown to be identical to native Gramibactin by NMR and MS.

## Materials and Methods

### Bacterial Strains and General Culture Conditions

All bacterial strains used in this invention were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) or isolated from clinical and environmental sources and maintained as glycerol stocks at −80°C. The primary producing strain, Pseudomonas fluorescens GMB-1, was isolated from rhizosphere soil samples collected from agricultural fields in central California and identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Additional strains tested for Gramibactin production included Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1, Pseudomonas putida KT2440, Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000, Escherichia coli DH5α, and Staphylococcus aureus USA300. All strains were routinely cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth or on LB agar plates at 37°C for Gram-negative bacteria and 30°C for Pseudomonas spp. For iron-limiting conditions, cultures were grown in MM9 minimal medium supplemented with 0.4% glycerol and 0.1% casamino acids, with no added iron. Iron depletion was confirmed by the addition of 200 µM 2,2′-dipyridyl, and cultures were incubated at 28°C with agitation at 200 rpm for 48–72 hours. For genetic manipulation, E. coli DH5α was used as the cloning host, and conjugation was performed using the triparental mating method with helper strain E. coli S17-1 λpir. All genetic constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. For large-scale fermentation, P. fluorescens GMB-1 and engineered derivatives were grown in 5 L baffled flasks containing 2 L of MM9 medium with 0.4% glycerol, 0.1% casamino acids, 1 mM MgSO₄, 0.1 mM CaCl₂, and trace elements (0.01 mM FeCl₃, 0.005 mM ZnSO₄, 0.001 mM MnCl₂, 0.001 mM CuSO₄, 0.001 mM CoCl₂, 0.001 mM Na₂MoO₄, and 0.001 mM H₃BO₃), with iron omitted to induce Gramibactin production. Cultures were incubated at 28°C with 200 rpm shaking for 72 hours. Cell density was monitored by optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀), and samples were collected at 24-hour intervals for metabolite extraction.

### MM9 Media Preparation(2, 3)

MM9 minimal medium was prepared by dissolving the following components in distilled deionized water: 6.78 g/L Na₂HPO₄, 3.0 g/L KH₂PO₄, 0.5 g/L NaCl, 1.0 g/L NH₄Cl, 0.2 g/L MgSO₄·7H₂O, and 0.01 g/L CaCl₂. The solution was autoclaved at 121°C for 15 minutes. Sterile-filtered solutions of 20% (w/v) glucose and 2% (w/v) casamino acids were added to a final concentration of 0.4% glucose and 0.1% casamino acids, respectively, immediately prior to inoculation. For iron-depleted conditions, all components were prepared using Chelex-100-treated water to remove trace metals, and no iron-containing salts were added. The pH of the medium was adjusted to 7.2 with sterile 1 N NaOH or HCl as needed. All glassware used for MM9 preparation was soaked in 0.1 M HCl for 24 hours, rinsed extensively with deionized water, and baked at 180°C for 4 hours to ensure removal of metal contaminants. The medium was tested for iron content using the ferrozine assay, and only batches with iron concentrations below 0.1 µM were used for experiments requiring strict iron limitation.

### CAS Agar Preparation

Chrome Azurol S (CAS) agar was prepared to screen for siderophore production. The base medium consisted of 1.5% agar, 0.5% casamino acids, 0.5% glucose, 0.05% FeCl₃, and 0.01% CAS dye in distilled water. The solution was autoclaved and cooled to 50°C, at which point 10 mL of a 1% solution of hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) was added and mixed thoroughly. The medium was poured into Petri dishes and allowed to solidify. Plates were inoculated with bacterial isolates and incubated at 28°C for 48–72 hours. Siderophore-producing strains were identified by the formation of an orange halo surrounding the colony, indicating iron chelation and displacement of the blue CAS-Fe complex. The halo diameter was measured and correlated with siderophore production intensity. All CAS agar plates were stored in the dark at 4°C and used within two weeks of preparation.

### Siderophore Production in Liquid Media

To assess siderophore production in liquid culture, bacterial strains were inoculated into MM9 medium without iron and incubated at 28°C with shaking at 200 rpm. Supernatants were collected at 24, 48, and 72 hours by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 minutes and filtration through 0.22 µm membranes. The supernatants were then assayed for siderophore activity using the CAS assay adapted for microplate format. Briefly, 50 µL of culture supernatant was mixed with 50 µL of CAS reagent (prepared by mixing 1 mL of 1 mM FeCl₃, 1 mL of 1 mM CAS dye, and 2 mL of 0.1% CTAB in distilled water) in a 96-well plate. The absorbance was measured at 630 nm after 1 hour of incubation at room temperature. Siderophore units were calculated as the percentage of iron displacement relative to a control without supernatant. Positive controls included enterobactin and pyoverdine, and negative controls included sterile MM9 medium. Samples with siderophore activity greater than 50% were selected for further purification.

### General Analytical Procedures

All analytical procedures were performed using high-purity reagents and analytical-grade solvents. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed on a Waters Alliance 2695 system equipped with a photodiode array detector and a C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm). Mobile phases consisted of water containing 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid (B), with gradients of 5–95% B over 30 minutes at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. Mass spectrometry was performed on a Waters Q-TOF Premier instrument operating in electrospray ionization (ESI) positive and negative modes. Data were acquired in full-scan mode (m/z 100–2000) and tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) mode using collision energies of 10–40 eV. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz spectrometer equipped with a cryogenic probe. ¹H and ¹³C spectra were acquired in D₂O or methanol-d4, with chemical shifts referenced to residual solvent peaks. Two-dimensional NMR experiments including COSY, HSQC, HMBC, and ROESY were performed to assign connectivity and spatial proximity. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were recorded on a JASCO J-815 spectropolarimeter using a 1 mm pathlength cell. X-ray crystallography of the iron-Gramibactin complex was performed by co-crystallizing Gramibactin with FeCl₃ in a 1:1 molar ratio in a solution of 20% polyethylene glycol 3350, 0.1 M sodium citrate, pH 6.5. Crystals were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and data collected at 100 K on a Rigaku Saturn 92 CCD detector. The structure was solved by molecular replacement using the coordinates of enterobactin as a search model and refined using PHENIX and COOT. All data were processed using the CCP4 suite. Iron concentration in biological samples was determined by atomic absorption spectroscopy using a PerkinElmer AAnalyst 800 instrument calibrated with standard iron solutions.

### Isolation of Gramibactin (6)

Gramibactin was isolated from 5 L of culture supernatant of Pseudomonas fluorescens GMB-1 grown under iron-limiting conditions. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm membrane and loaded onto a C18 solid-phase extraction cartridge (10 g, Waters Sep-Pak). The cartridge was washed sequentially with 100 mL of water, 50 mL of 10% methanol, and 50 mL of 20% methanol to remove salts and polar contaminants. Gramibactin was eluted with 50 mL of 80% acetonitrile in water containing 0.1% formic acid. The eluate was concentrated under reduced pressure at 30°C to a volume of 5 mL and subjected to preparative HPLC on a C18 column (250 × 20 mm, 10 µm) using a linear gradient of 10–90% acetonitrile in water over 60 minutes at a flow rate of 8 mL/min. Fractions were collected every minute and analyzed by LC-MS. The fraction corresponding to m/z 960.5 [M+H]⁺ was pooled, lyophilized, and dissolved in 1 mL of water. The purity was assessed by analytical HPLC, and the compound was confirmed as Gramibactin by NMR and high-resolution mass spectrometry. The yield was 62 mg from 5 L of culture.

### Structure Elucidation of Gramibactin

The structure of Gramibactin was elucidated using a combination of high-resolution mass spectrometry, 1D and 2D NMR spectroscopy, and chemical degradation. High-resolution mass spectrometry using electrospray ionization in positive mode yielded a molecular ion at m/z 960.5213 [M+H]⁺, corresponding to the molecular formula C₄₈H₇₄N₆O₁₈. Fragmentation patterns indicated the presence of three hydroxamate groups and a macrocyclic lactone. ¹H NMR in D₂O revealed signals corresponding to three methyl groups, two methylene chains, and multiple amide protons. ¹³C NMR showed 48 distinct carbon signals, including three carbonyls from hydroxamates, one lactone carbonyl, and multiple aliphatic and olefinic carbons. COSY experiments revealed spin systems corresponding to the threonine, serine, and lysine residues. HMBC correlations confirmed the linkage between the acyl chain and the threonine N-terminus, the ester linkage between the β-hydroxy fatty acid and the lysine C-terminus, and the lactone ring formed between the serine hydroxyl and the β-hydroxy fatty acid. ROESY experiments indicated spatial proximity between the lactone oxygen and the hydroxamate carbonyls, consistent with a folded conformation. Chemical degradation using 6 N HCl at 110°C for 24 hours released threonine, serine, lysine, and 3-hydroxydecanoic acid, confirming the amino acid composition. The structure was further confirmed by comparison with synthetic standards and X-ray crystallography of the iron complex, which revealed the hexadentate coordination geometry of the three hydroxamate groups around the central ferric ion.

### Griess Test (5)

The Griess test was employed to detect the presence of hydroxamate groups in Gramibactin. A 10 µL aliquot of Gramibactin solution (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 100 µL of 1% sulfanilamide in 5% phosphoric acid and 100 µL of 0.1% N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine dihydrochloride. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes, and the absorbance was measured at 540 nm. A positive reaction was indicated by the development of a pink-purple color, characteristic of diazonium salt formation from hydroxamate hydrolysis. Control samples included enterobactin and synthetic hydroxamate standards. The assay was linear from 0.1 to 10 µg/mL, with a detection limit of 0.05 µg/mL. Gramibactin gave a strong positive signal, confirming the presence of three hydroxamate moieties.

### 15N Labeling of Gramibactin

To confirm the origin of nitrogen atoms in Gramibactin, Pseudomonas fluorescens GMB-1 was cultured in MM9 medium supplemented with 0.1% casamino acids labeled with 15N (99% atom excess) and no iron. After 72 hours of incubation, Gramibactin was isolated as described above. The 15N-labeled compound was analyzed by high-resolution mass spectrometry, which showed a +3 Da shift in the molecular ion, consistent with incorporation of three 15N atoms from the lysine and serine residues. ¹⁵N NMR spectroscopy confirmed the presence of three distinct nitrogen signals corresponding to the hydroxamate nitrogens, with chemical shifts at 125.6, 126.1, and 127.3 ppm. No 15N enrichment was observed in the acyl chain or lactone ring, confirming that all nitrogen atoms originated from the amino acid precursors.
