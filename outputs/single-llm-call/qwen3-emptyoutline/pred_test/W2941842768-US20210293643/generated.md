# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of prosthetic limb systems, specifically to advanced tactile and proprioceptive sensory feedback mechanisms for upper limb prostheses. More particularly, the invention encompasses a multi-modal fingertip sensor system that integrates infrared proximity detection, barometric pressure sensing, and elastomeric force transduction into a single, compact, and robust anatomical interface designed for integration into prosthetic hands. This sensor system is engineered to provide continuous, high-fidelity, spatially resolved, and angularly discriminative feedback regarding object proximity, initial contact, force magnitude, spatial location of contact, and angle of force incidence—information critical for enabling natural, dexterous, and intuitive manipulation of objects by upper limb amputees. The invention further encompasses the associated signal processing architecture, sensor fusion algorithms, and communication protocols that enable real-time transmission of multi-dimensional sensory data to external controllers or neural interfaces, thereby closing the sensory loop in myoelectric prosthetic systems. The technology is particularly suited for integration with peripheral nerve stimulation systems, where the rich, structured sensory data generated by the sensor can be mapped to physiologically plausible or novel sensory percepts, significantly enhancing embodiment, object discrimination, and manipulation precision beyond the capabilities of existing prosthetic systems that rely solely on binary contact or uniaxial force measurements. The invention addresses a critical gap in current prosthetic technology by providing a sensor capable of distinguishing not only the magnitude of applied force but also its precise location and orientation on the fingertip surface, thereby replicating the nuanced somatosensory input that human fingers naturally provide during everyday manipulation tasks.

## BACKGROUND

The restoration of sensory function in upper limb prostheses remains one of the most persistent and consequential challenges in the field of neuroprosthetics. While significant advances have been made in the mechanical dexterity, actuation speed, and force output of modern prosthetic hands—now capable of generating closing forces exceeding 100 newtons and achieving actuation times under one second—the sensory feedback mechanisms that accompany these systems remain rudimentary at best. Existing commercial and research-grade prosthetic hands typically rely on discrete force-sensitive resistors or strain gauges that provide only a single scalar measurement of normal force applied at or near the center of the fingertip. These sensors are incapable of detecting the spatial distribution of contact forces, the angular orientation of applied loads, or the subtle pre-contact proximity cues that precede physical interaction. As a result, users of such prostheses operate in a state of sensory deprivation, lacking the intrinsic tactile and proprioceptive awareness necessary for fine motor control, object manipulation, and safe interaction with fragile or irregularly shaped items. This absence of sensory feedback has been shown to lead to increased cognitive load, reduced manipulation efficiency, heightened risk of object slippage, and diminished sense of embodiment—the psychological perception that the prosthesis is an extension of the user’s own body.

Prior attempts to address this deficiency have focused primarily on the development of neural interfaces capable of delivering electrical stimulation to residual peripheral nerves in order to evoke somatosensory percepts. Techniques such as the Utah Slant Electrode Array and the Flat Interface Nerve Electrode (FINE) have demonstrated the feasibility of eliciting distinct, localized tactile sensations in amputees by stimulating specific fascicles within the median or ulnar nerves. These systems have successfully enabled users to discriminate between different levels of force intensity and even recognize the spatial pattern of stimulation across multiple electrodes. However, the utility of these neural interfaces is fundamentally constrained by the quality and richness of the sensory signals they receive. When driven by simplistic, single-axis force sensors, the resulting percepts are impoverished, lacking the spatial and directional granularity that characterizes natural touch. For instance, when a prosthetic finger contacts an object off-center or at an oblique angle—a common occurrence during grasping tasks involving cylindrical, spherical, or irregularly shaped items—the sensory feedback delivered to the user remains unchanged from that of a centered, normal-force contact. This discrepancy between the physical interaction and the perceived sensation leads to misinterpretation of object properties, inappropriate grip adjustments, and ultimately, reduced functional utility.

Moreover, existing tactile sensors used in prosthetic applications suffer from a host of technical limitations that preclude their reliable integration into self-contained, wearable systems. Many proximity sensors, such as those based on capacitive or ultrasonic principles, are highly sensitive to environmental variables including ambient temperature, humidity, and the reflectivity or color of the contacted object, leading to inconsistent calibration and unreliable performance across diverse real-world conditions. Force sensors, while more stable, are typically limited in their dynamic range, unable to detect the minute forces associated with initial contact or the subtle pressure gradients that occur during delicate manipulation. Furthermore, the wiring, multiplexing, and signal conditioning required to integrate multiple sensors into a compact prosthetic digit introduce significant complexity, bulk, and points of mechanical failure. Most critically, no prior sensor system has simultaneously achieved the ability to detect proximity (before contact), distinguish zero-force contact (the moment of first touch), measure linear force over a clinically relevant range (0–50 N), and classify the spatial location and angular orientation of the applied force—all within a single, miniaturized, durable, and biocompatible fingertip module that can be overmolded with a soft elastomer to mimic the mechanical properties of human skin.

The need for a sensor capable of capturing this multi-dimensional sensory information is further underscored by recent advances in machine learning and sensor fusion methodologies. While prior research has explored the use of Gaussian process regression and convolutional neural networks for signal interpretation, these approaches have been applied in isolation to single-modality data or in laboratory settings with limited ecological validity. No system has yet been developed that integrates these advanced analytical techniques with a physically robust, clinically viable sensor platform capable of operating reliably under the dynamic, variable, and often unpredictable conditions of daily life. The absence of such a system has left a critical void in the translational pipeline between neural interface development and functional sensory restoration. Without a sensor that can faithfully encode the full spectrum of tactile interactions—including off-center contacts, oblique forces, and pre-contact proximity—the potential of even the most sophisticated neural stimulation paradigms remains unrealized. The present invention directly addresses this gap by introducing a novel, integrated fingertip sensor system that combines infrared proximity detection, barometric pressure sensing, and elastomeric deformation into a unified, multi-modal sensing architecture, enabling the first truly comprehensive tactile feedback system for upper limb prostheses.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a multi-modal prosthetic fingertip sensor system designed to provide comprehensive tactile and proprioceptive feedback to users of upper limb prostheses. The sensor system comprises a compact, integrated assembly that includes an infrared proximity sensor, a microelectromechanical system (MEMS) barometric pressure sensor, and a custom-printed circuit board, all embedded within a molded elastomeric fingertip cap that mimics the mechanical compliance and surface properties of human skin. The infrared sensor detects the presence of nearby objects prior to physical contact by measuring the intensity of reflected infrared light, thereby providing a proximity signal that precedes tactile interaction. The barometric pressure sensor measures the internal pressure changes within the elastomeric structure caused by external forces, generating a linear, calibrated force signal across a range of 0 to 50 newtons. The combination of these two distinct sensing modalities enables the system to differentiate between proximity events, initial contact, and sustained force application with high temporal and amplitude resolution. The sensor assembly is designed to be overmolded using a low-viscosity, medical-grade silicone polymer that ensures mechanical durability, environmental resistance, and consistent force transmission across the entire surface of the fingertip.

The sensor system further includes an embedded signal conditioning circuit that performs real-time temperature compensation, analog-to-digital conversion, and signal normalization, all within a single integrated package. Communication between the sensor and the prosthetic hand’s central controller is established via a standardized I²C bus protocol, enabling reliable, low-latency, and noise-resistant data transmission across multiple fingers without the need for complex multiplexing or additional wiring. The sensor outputs raw, calibrated data streams for both proximity and pressure, which are then processed by a sensor fusion algorithm that employs Gaussian process regression to generate a unified, high-fidelity estimate of the applied force, independent of the spatial location or angular orientation of the contact. This fused force signal is then used as the foundation for two independent classification modules: one that determines the spatial location of the contact point on the fingertip surface, and another that identifies the angular orientation of the applied force relative to the normal plane of the sensor. These classification tasks are performed using supervised machine learning models, including support vector machines and convolutional neural networks, trained on extensive datasets derived from controlled mechanical loading experiments that simulate real-world grasping conditions.

The invention further encompasses a method for calibrating and classifying tactile interactions by collecting multi-modal sensor data under a variety of spatial and angular loading conditions, including five distinct spatial positions (center, distal, proximal, medial, lateral) and three angular orientations (0°, +20°, –20°) relative to the sensor plane. The system is capable of classifying these conditions with high accuracy—96% for spatial location and 89% for angular orientation—using support vector machine classification, and with comparable performance using convolutional neural networks. The sensor system is designed to be modular, scalable, and compatible with existing prosthetic platforms, allowing for seamless integration into multi-digit prosthetic hands without requiring structural modifications to the underlying mechanical architecture. The resulting sensory output provides a rich, multi-dimensional representation of object interaction that far exceeds the capabilities of any prior tactile sensor system, enabling advanced control paradigms such as shared autonomy, predictive grasp adjustment, and neuroprosthetic sensory mapping. By providing not only the magnitude of force but also its precise location and direction on the fingertip, the invention enables amputees to perceive tactile interactions in a manner that closely approximates natural human touch, thereby significantly enhancing functional performance, reducing cognitive load, and improving the psychological embodiment of the prosthetic limb.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention describes a novel multi-modal prosthetic fingertip sensor system that integrates infrared proximity detection, barometric pressure sensing, and elastomeric force transduction into a single, compact, and anatomically conforming sensor module. This system is designed to overcome the fundamental limitations of existing prosthetic tactile sensors, which are typically restricted to measuring only the magnitude of normal force applied at a single point on the fingertip surface. In contrast, the invention provides a comprehensive sensory representation that captures not only force magnitude but also the spatial location of contact, the angular orientation of the applied load, and the pre-contact proximity of objects—all within a single, robust, and clinically viable package. The sensor system is composed of three primary components: the sensing elements, the signal conditioning and communication circuitry, and the overmolded elastomeric interface. Each component is engineered to function synergistically, ensuring high fidelity, repeatability, and long-term reliability under the dynamic and variable conditions of daily use.

The core sensing elements consist of a commercial infrared proximity sensor, specifically the VCNL4010, and a MEMS-based barometric pressure sensor, the MS5637-02BA03. These sensors are mounted on a custom-designed, rigid printed circuit board (PCB) that is precisely aligned along the midline of the fingertip geometry. The infrared sensor emits pulses of infrared light and detects the intensity of the reflected signal, which varies non-linearly with the distance to an approaching object. This provides a proximity signal that is active even before physical contact occurs, enabling the system to detect the presence of objects at distances of up to several centimeters. The barometric pressure sensor, in contrast, measures the internal air pressure within a sealed cavity formed between the PCB and the overmolded elastomer. As an external force is applied to the fingertip surface, the elastomer deforms, compressing the air within the cavity and causing a measurable increase in internal pressure. This pressure change is directly proportional to the magnitude of the applied force over the operational range of 0 to 50 newtons, yielding a linear, calibrated force signal that is largely independent of the material properties or surface reflectivity of the contacted object.

The integration of these two sensors is not merely additive but synergistic. The infrared sensor excels at detecting the onset of interaction and is highly sensitive to very low forces, including those associated with the initial contact of lightweight, compliant materials such as cotton or paper. However, its output is highly susceptible to variations in surface reflectivity, ambient lighting, and object color, making it unreliable as a standalone force measurement tool. The barometric sensor, while insensitive to surface properties, has a minimum detection threshold and cannot detect forces below approximately 0.1 newtons. By fusing the two signals, the system leverages the strengths of each modality: the infrared sensor provides high-resolution detection of proximity and initial contact, while the barometric sensor provides a stable, calibrated measurement of sustained force. This fusion is accomplished through a Gaussian process regression model that learns the complex, non-linear relationship between the raw infrared and barometric inputs and the true applied force, as measured by an external load cell during calibration. The resulting fused force estimate is highly accurate, with an R² value exceeding 0.99, and remains consistent across all spatial locations and angular orientations of contact, effectively decoupling the force measurement from the position and angle of impact.

To enable this fusion, the sensor system incorporates a dedicated microcontroller that performs real-time signal conditioning, including temperature compensation for the barometric sensor, baseline drift correction for the infrared sensor, and digital filtering to remove high-frequency noise. The microcontroller also implements a standardized I²C communication protocol, allowing each fingertip sensor to transmit its raw and processed data to the central prosthetic controller via a two-wire bus. This eliminates the need for individual analog wiring or complex multiplexing schemes, significantly reducing system complexity and increasing reliability. The I²C protocol ensures that data from up to ten fingers (five per hand) can be transmitted simultaneously without signal interference, making the system fully scalable for integration into multi-digit prosthetic hands. The entire sensor assembly, including the PCB, microcontroller, and connectors, is encapsulated within a custom-molded elastomer composed of Dragon Skin 10, a medical-grade silicone polymer known for its low viscosity during casting, high tensile strength after curing, and excellent resistance to mechanical fatigue. The elastomer is poured into a precision 3D-printed mold that contains the sensor assembly and is subjected to a vacuum prior to curing to eliminate air bubbles, ensuring uniform mechanical properties and consistent force transmission across the entire surface of the fingertip.

The mechanical design of the sensor is optimized to replicate the compliance and deformation characteristics of human fingertip skin. The elastomer is engineered to have a Shore A hardness of approximately 10, which closely matches the softness of human skin while maintaining sufficient structural integrity to withstand repeated loading cycles. The thickness of the elastomer is carefully calibrated to ensure that the barometric cavity is neither too shallow (which would reduce sensitivity) nor too deep (which would introduce non-linear compliance). The curvature of the elastomer surface is modeled after anatomical measurements of human fingertips, ensuring that the sensor responds to forces in a manner that is biomechanically plausible. The sensor is designed to be mounted flush with the distal end of the prosthetic digit, such that the elastomeric surface forms a seamless, continuous interface with the external environment, eliminating any protruding components that could interfere with object manipulation or cause discomfort during use.

The sensor system is characterized by its ability to detect and classify five distinct spatial locations of contact: the center of the fingertip, and four peripheral locations at 2.5 millimeters distal, proximal, medial, and lateral from the center. These locations were selected to span the entire functional area of the fingertip, covering the range of contact points encountered during common grasping tasks, such as holding a cup, turning a doorknob, or picking up a coin. In addition, the system is capable of classifying three angular orientations of force incidence: 0 degrees (normal to the surface), +20 degrees, and –20 degrees. These angles were chosen to represent the typical range of oblique contacts that occur during manipulation of irregularly shaped objects, such as when a finger brushes against the side of a bottle or when a thumb applies force at an angle during a pinch grip. During calibration, each of these 15 spatial-angular combinations was tested under four different maximum force levels (1 N, 5 N, 30 N, and 50 N), with ten dynamic loading and unloading cycles performed at each condition, resulting in a comprehensive dataset of 600 unique contact events. This dataset was used to train two independent classification models: one for spatial location and one for angular orientation.

The spatial classification model is trained using a support vector machine (SVM) with a radial basis function kernel and a penalty parameter of C = 8, which was empirically determined to yield optimal generalization performance. The input features for this model consist of the concatenated, normalized time-series data from both the infrared and barometric sensors during each loading cycle. The SVM learns the subtle, non-linear patterns in the sensor responses that correspond to each of the five spatial locations, enabling it to classify the contact point with an accuracy of 96% under sixfold cross-validation. A convolutional neural network (CNN) was also trained on the same dataset, using two convolutional layers followed by a fully connected output layer with five softmax neurons. While the CNN achieved a slightly lower accuracy of 92%, it demonstrated superior robustness to noise and greater potential for real-time implementation due to its ability to learn hierarchical features directly from the raw sensor data without manual feature engineering.

Similarly, the angular orientation classification model employs an SVM with a polynomial kernel and a penalty parameter of C = 1, using a feature vector that includes the ratio of the infrared to barometric sensor signals, the peak and trough amplitudes of each signal, and the slope of the force rise during initial contact. This feature set was selected based on its ability to capture the distinct deformation patterns that occur when force is applied at an oblique angle versus normal to the surface. The SVM achieved an accuracy of 89% under sixfold cross-validation. The CNN model for angular classification used the same architecture as the spatial model but with three output neurons, achieving an accuracy of 83%. Both models were validated using independent test datasets collected on separate sensor units, confirming that the classification performance is consistent across manufacturing batches and does not rely on overfitting to a single training set.

The sensor system is further distinguished by its ability to detect and differentiate between three distinct phases of interaction: proximity (before contact), zero-force contact (the moment of initial touch), and sustained force application. These phases are identified through a combination of thresholding and signal morphology analysis. The infrared signal exhibits a sharp, non-linear increase as an object approaches the fingertip, allowing the system to trigger a proximity alert well before physical contact occurs. At the moment of contact, the infrared signal stabilizes while the barometric signal begins to rise, indicating the transition from proximity to contact. Once the barometric signal reaches a sustained plateau, the system recognizes the onset of force application. This temporal segmentation enables the prosthetic controller to initiate anticipatory actions, such as adjusting grip force or reorienting the hand, before full contact is established—a capability that is impossible with conventional force sensors that only respond after contact has occurred.

The sensor system is designed for seamless integration into existing prosthetic platforms, including commercially available systems such as the Bebionic v2 hand. The sensor module is sized to fit within the distal phalanx of the prosthetic digit, requiring no structural modifications to the underlying mechanical framework. The I²C communication protocol ensures compatibility with any controller that supports standard digital bus interfaces, and the modular design allows for easy replacement or upgrading of individual sensor units without requiring recalibration of the entire system. The elastomeric surface is replaceable and can be swapped out in the event of wear or damage, extending the operational lifespan of the sensor system.

The sensor’s performance has been validated under a wide range of environmental conditions, including variations in ambient temperature, humidity, and lighting. The barometric sensor’s built-in temperature compensation algorithm ensures that pressure measurements remain accurate across a range of 10°C to 40°C, which encompasses all clinically relevant operating environments. The infrared sensor’s sensitivity to surface reflectivity is mitigated by the fusion algorithm, which uses the stable barometric signal as a reference to normalize the infrared response, effectively decoupling the force estimate from the object’s optical properties. This allows the system to perform reliably when interacting with objects of varying colors, textures, and materials—from glossy metal to matte fabric to translucent plastic.

The sensor system also demonstrates exceptional repeatability over time. Sensor units were tested over a period of thirty days, with daily calibration checks and repeated loading cycles under identical conditions. The fused force estimate showed a standard deviation of less than 1.2% across all conditions, and the classification accuracies for spatial location and angular orientation remained stable within 2% of their initial values. This long-term stability is critical for clinical adoption, as it ensures that users do not need to undergo frequent recalibration procedures or experience degradation in sensory fidelity over time.

In operation, the sensor system functions as a closed-loop feedback module within a prosthetic limb. During a grasping task, the infrared sensor first detects the proximity of an object, triggering a pre-grasp adjustment in the prosthetic hand’s posture. Upon contact, the system identifies the moment of initial touch and begins to generate a tactile percept through a neural interface, if one is present. As force is applied, the barometric sensor provides a calibrated force signal, which is fused with the infrared data to produce a high-fidelity estimate of the total load. Simultaneously, the classification models determine the spatial location and angular orientation of the contact, enabling the neural interface to deliver a spatially and directionally appropriate sensory percept. For example, if the contact occurs on the lateral side of the fingertip at a +20-degree angle, the neural stimulation pattern is adjusted to activate the corresponding somatotopic region of the residual nerve, creating a percept that the user interprets as a touch on the lateral side of the finger, angled inward. This level of sensory detail is unprecedented in current prosthetic systems and enables users to perform tasks that were previously impossible, such as picking up a small pill without crushing it, turning a key in a lock, or handling a fragile glass without slipping.

The sensor system is further enhanced by its compatibility with advanced control paradigms, including shared autonomy and predictive grasp planning. In shared autonomy, the user issues a high-level command—such as “grasp the cup”—and the prosthetic hand autonomously adjusts its grip based on real-time sensory feedback. The proximity signal allows the hand to anticipate the object’s location and pre-position the fingers, while the spatial and angular classification enables the hand to adapt its grip geometry to the object’s shape. In predictive grasp planning, the system uses the fused force and classification data to estimate the object’s mass, compliance, and surface friction, allowing the hand to select the optimal grip strategy before contact is even made. This level of intelligence transforms the prosthetic hand from a passive tool into an active, sensory-aware extension of the user’s own body.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in prosthetic sensory feedback. Rather than attempting to replicate the exact physiological mechanisms of human touch—which are complex, poorly understood, and difficult to replicate mechanically—the system embraces a functional approach: it provides the most relevant, discriminative, and actionable sensory information possible, regardless of whether it maps directly to natural percepts. The proximity signal, for instance, does not exist in human skin, yet it provides critical information for grasp planning. The spatial and angular classification provides information that is not explicitly encoded in the peripheral nervous system but can be learned and interpreted by the brain through neuroplastic adaptation. By combining these novel modalities with robust engineering, advanced signal processing, and machine learning, the invention delivers a sensory experience that is not merely adequate but transformative—enabling amputees to interact with the world in ways that were previously unimaginable.

## CONCLUSION

The invention described herein represents a foundational advancement in the field of upper limb prosthetics, introducing a multi-modal fingertip sensor system that fundamentally redefines the capabilities of tactile feedback in prosthetic hands. By integrating infrared proximity detection, barometric pressure sensing, and elastomeric force transduction into a single, compact, and clinically viable module, the system achieves a level of sensory richness and spatial discrimination that has never before been possible in prosthetic technology. Unlike prior systems that rely on single-axis force measurements or binary contact detection, this invention captures the full spectrum of tactile interaction—including proximity, zero-force contact, force magnitude, spatial location, and angular orientation—thereby enabling a level of dexterity, embodiment, and functional performance that closely approximates natural human touch. The sensor’s ability to classify contact location with 96% accuracy and angular orientation with 89% accuracy, even under variable environmental conditions and across diverse object materials, demonstrates a level of robustness and reliability that is essential for real-world clinical adoption.

The fusion of infrared and barometric signals through Gaussian process regression provides a calibrated, noise-resistant force estimate that is independent of the object’s surface properties, a critical breakthrough that overcomes the longstanding limitations of optical and capacitive sensors. This fused signal serves as the foundation for a new generation of neuroprosthetic interfaces, where the spatial and directional information derived from the sensor can be mapped to targeted neural stimulation patterns, creating percepts that are not only discriminable but also intuitively interpretable by the user. The system’s compatibility with standard I²C communication ensures seamless integration into existing prosthetic platforms, while its modular, overmolded design allows for easy replacement, scalability, and long-term durability under the demanding conditions of daily use. The validation of the system across 28 distinct loading conditions, with consistent performance over thirty days of continuous testing, confirms its suitability for long-term implantation and routine clinical application.

Beyond its technical achievements, the invention introduces a new paradigm for sensory restoration: one that does not seek to replicate the biological mechanisms of touch but instead provides the most functionally relevant sensory information possible, leveraging neuroplasticity to enable the brain to interpret novel, non-physiological signals as meaningful tactile percepts. The inclusion of proximity detection, for instance, does not mirror human physiology but enables anticipatory control that significantly enhances manipulation efficiency. The spatial and angular classification does not correspond to any known somatotopic map but provides the brain with the contextual information necessary to distinguish between a gentle brush and a firm grasp, between a centered hold and an off-axis pinch. This functional, rather than anatomical, approach to sensory feedback represents a paradigm shift in neuroprosthetics—one that prioritizes usability, adaptability, and real-world performance over rigid biological fidelity.

The implications of this invention extend far beyond the prosthetic limb itself. By providing rich, multi-dimensional sensory data, the system enables the development of advanced control architectures, including shared autonomy, predictive grasp planning, and machine learning-driven adaptive interfaces that learn from the user’s behavior over time. It opens the door to entirely new sensory paradigms, such as the mapping of proximity to phantom limb sensations, or the use of angular classification to convey object texture or compliance. The system’s compatibility with real-time classification algorithms ensures that it can be deployed in closed-loop systems that respond instantaneously to changes in the environment, transforming the prosthetic hand from a static tool into a dynamic, responsive extension of the user’s motor intent.

In summary, this invention delivers a comprehensive, robust, and clinically practical solution to the most persistent challenge in upper limb prosthetics: the restoration of meaningful sensory feedback. It is not merely an incremental improvement but a transformative leap forward, enabling amputees to interact with the world with a level of precision, confidence, and embodiment that was previously unattainable. The integration of multi-modal sensing, intelligent signal fusion, and machine learning into a single, manufacturable module marks the dawn of a new era in neuroprosthetics—one in which the boundary between human and machine is no longer defined by what is lost, but by what is regained.
