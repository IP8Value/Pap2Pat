# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The precise control of localized temperature in biological systems has emerged as a critical enabler across a broad spectrum of biomedical applications, ranging from fundamental neuroscience research to clinical therapeutic interventions. Temperature serves as a potent modulator of cellular physiology, influencing molecular kinetics, protein conformation, membrane fluidity, and ion channel activity. In particular, the ability to induce controlled thermal gradients at sub-millimeter scales allows for the selective activation of thermosensitive biological components without perturbing surrounding tissues. For instance, in the field of magnetogenetics, localized heating has been employed to trigger the opening of temperature-gated ion channels such as TRPV1, thereby initiating action potentials in genetically targeted neurons with high spatial fidelity. Similarly, in oncology, hyperthermic treatment protocols have demonstrated efficacy in sensitizing malignant cells to radiation and chemotherapy by elevating tumor temperatures to the range of 43–45 °C, where the heat-induced denaturation of repair enzymes impairs the cancer cells’ capacity to recover from DNA damage. Beyond these applications, controlled thermal stimulation has proven valuable in accelerating wound healing through enhanced metabolic activity and angiogenesis, enabling spatiotemporally precise drug release from thermoresponsive carriers, and facilitating advanced bioanalytical techniques such as polymerase chain reaction and temperature gradient focusing, where rapid and repeatable thermal cycling is essential for molecular amplification and separation.

Despite the compelling utility of localized thermal modulation, existing technologies for generating heat in biological environments suffer from fundamental limitations in efficiency, scalability, and spatial precision. Conventional approaches rely predominantly on ohmic heating, wherein electrical current is passed through resistive elements in direct contact with the target tissue. While this method is straightforward to implement, it necessitates physical interfacing with the sample, which introduces risks of mechanical damage, biofouling, and non-specific heating of adjacent structures. Alternative strategies based on dielectric heating exploit differences in the permittivity of materials under alternating electric fields. However, in aqueous biological media—where water constitutes the dominant constituent—the dielectric contrast between target cells and their surrounding environment is negligible, rendering this technique ineffective for selective heating. Magnetic heating, which utilizes magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) as nanoscale transducers, has emerged as the most promising modality for achieving non-contact, spatially confined thermal activation. This approach leverages the intrinsic magnetic properties of MNPs to convert externally applied alternating magnetic fields into localized heat through mechanisms such as Néel relaxation, Brownian relaxation, and ferromagnetic resonance. Among these, ferromagnetic resonance offers the most compelling advantages for high-resolution applications, as it occurs at gigahertz frequencies where the magnetic loss is maximized for a given field amplitude, enabling efficient energy absorption with minimal external power.

Nevertheless, current MNP-based thermal systems are constrained by two interrelated challenges: low heating efficiency and poor spatial resolution. Conventional magnetic heating systems operate in the kilohertz to megahertz frequency range, necessitating high-amplitude magnetic fields—often exceeding several kiloamperes per meter—to generate sufficient thermal output. These fields are typically generated using large, power-hungry solenoid coils or Helmholtz pairs, which require bulky external power supplies, active water-cooling systems to manage resistive losses in the coils, and substantial infrastructure that precludes integration into compact or implantable platforms. Furthermore, the spatial confinement of the magnetic field is inherently limited by the physical dimensions of these macroscopic coils, which often exceed 40 millimeters in diameter. As a result, the magnetic field distribution is diffuse, leading to uncontrolled heating over broad tissue volumes and necessitating the precise intratumoral or intracellular delivery of MNPs to achieve any semblance of specificity. Even when MNPs are administered systemically, their distribution is governed by passive mechanisms such as the enhanced permeability and retention effect, which permits only partial accumulation at the target site while allowing significant leakage into surrounding vasculature. Consequently, the therapeutic window remains narrow, and the risk of collateral thermal damage to healthy tissue persists.

The limitations of existing systems stem from a fundamental mismatch between the physical constraints of conventional magnetic field generation and the demands of cellular-scale thermal manipulation. The operating frequency of kHz–MHz systems is too low to exploit the peak magnetic loss of MNPs, forcing reliance on high field amplitudes that are incompatible with miniaturization. Simultaneously, the inability to generate highly localized magnetic field gradients at these frequencies renders fine-grained spatial control unattainable without prohibitively complex and invasive delivery mechanisms. To overcome these barriers, a paradigm shift is required—one that integrates high-frequency magnetic excitation with on-chip, scalable, and self-regulating thermal actuation. The advent of gigahertz-frequency magnetic excitation presents a transformative opportunity: at these frequencies, the ferromagnetic resonance of MNPs can be accessed with magnetic field strengths orders of magnitude lower than those required at lower frequencies, enabling the use of compact, low-power on-chip inductors to generate the necessary field gradients. Moreover, the high frequency permits the use of microfabricated inductor geometries that can be patterned with sub-millimeter precision, thereby confining the magnetic field and, by extension, the heating profile to regions as small as tens of micrometers. This convergence of high-frequency magnetic excitation and microscale inductor design opens the door to a new class of thermal actuators that are not only compact and energy-efficient but also capable of delivering unprecedented spatial control over thermal stimuli.

Despite these theoretical advantages, the practical realization of such a system has remained elusive due to formidable circuit design challenges. Generating the high radio-frequency voltage swings—on the order of tens of volts peak-to-peak—required to produce sufficient magnetic field strength at gigahertz frequencies is incompatible with standard complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technologies, where supply voltages are typically limited to less than five volts. Conventional oscillator topologies, such as the cross-coupled LC oscillator, are fundamentally constrained by the supply rail, rendering them incapable of achieving the necessary output swing. While external radio-frequency amplifiers could, in principle, be employed to boost the signal, their integration would necessitate additional inductive components for impedance matching and resonant tank formation, thereby increasing the pixel footprint and negating the spatial resolution gains afforded by the high-frequency operation. Furthermore, RF amplifiers at gigahertz frequencies are inherently inefficient, consuming substantial direct current power and generating significant ohmic losses that compromise system stability and thermal uniformity. The absence of a scalable, integrated, and power-efficient means of generating high-voltage, high-frequency magnetic fields has thus remained the principal barrier to the realization of a fully integrated, closed-loop magnetic microheater array capable of cellular-scale thermal manipulation.

In response to these challenges, this invention introduces a novel architecture for a fully integrated magnetic microheater array that achieves closed-loop temperature regulation with sub-millimeter spatial resolution through the synergistic integration of a stacked oscillator topology and an electro-thermal feedback loop. The core innovation lies in the design of a stacked transistor oscillator that enables the generation of high-voltage radio-frequency swings—exceeding 25 volts peak-to-peak—at gigahertz frequencies within a single inductor footprint, eliminating the need for external amplification and preserving pixel density. This topology distributes the voltage stress across multiple series-connected transistors, each operating below their breakdown limits, while maintaining a positive feedback loop essential for sustained oscillation. Coupled with a precisely engineered electro-thermal feedback mechanism that dynamically modulates the biasing of the oscillator’s tail transistor based on real-time temperature measurements, the system achieves autonomous, stable, and highly accurate thermal regulation without external intervention. The resulting platform enables localized heating confined to areas smaller than 0.03 square millimeters, with temperature control accuracy within ±0.3 degrees Celsius across a clinically relevant range of 37 to 49 degrees Celsius. This represents a fundamental departure from existing thermal actuators, which rely on bulky external hardware, lack real-time feedback, and suffer from poor spatial confinement. The invention thus provides a scalable, CMOS-compatible, and self-regulating thermal actuation system that is uniquely suited for applications demanding precision, miniaturization, and autonomy—such as in-vitro neurostimulation, targeted cancer hyperthermia, and microfluidic biochemical assays—thereby establishing a new standard for localized thermal manipulation in biological systems.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention comprises a fully integrated magnetic microheater array capable of generating highly localized, sub-millimeter-scale thermal stimuli with autonomous closed-loop temperature regulation, enabled by the synergistic integration of a stacked oscillator topology and an electro-thermal feedback control system. The array is composed of multiple pixels, each containing a microfabricated inductor and a monolithically integrated active circuit that generates a high-frequency magnetic field to excite magnetic nanoparticles embedded in or adjacent to a biological sample, thereby inducing localized heating via ferromagnetic resonance at gigahertz frequencies. Each pixel is designed to operate independently and is scalable in two dimensions, permitting the construction of large-area arrays for multiplexed thermal stimulation without compromising spatial resolution. The core innovation resides in the stacked oscillator architecture, which employs a series connection of multiple transistors to distribute the voltage stress across the device stack, thereby enabling the generation of radio-frequency voltage swings exceeding twenty-five volts peak-to-peak at frequencies between 1.2 and 2.6 gigahertz using a single on-chip inductor and a low-voltage supply. This topology circumvents the fundamental limitation of conventional oscillators, which are constrained by the supply rail, and eliminates the need for external radio-frequency amplifiers, thereby preserving the compact footprint of each pixel and minimizing direct current power consumption. The stacked oscillator is further optimized through a systematic design methodology that balances transistor sizing, gate capacitance distribution, biasing conditions, and load impedance to maximize dc-to-radio-frequency energy efficiency while ensuring robust oscillation startup across process, voltage, and temperature variations.

Complementing the oscillator is a fully integrated electro-thermal feedback loop that continuously monitors the local temperature at the surface of the inductor and dynamically adjusts the biasing voltage of the tail transistor to regulate the output swing and, consequently, the rate of heat generation. The temperature sensing element comprises a Proportional-To-Absolute-Temperature sensor array composed of multiple diode pairs strategically positioned at the periphery of the inductor and beneath a grounded metal plane to minimize electromagnetic interference from the high-frequency oscillation. The sensor output is multiplexed, amplified, and buffered through two cascaded gain stages, each referenced to a programmable voltage generated by a coarse-fine resistor-string digital-to-analog converter. The output of the feedback path is connected to the bias node of the tail transistor, forming a closed-loop system in which the thermal response of the sample directly modulates the power delivered by the oscillator. This feedback mechanism ensures that the local temperature converges to a predefined setpoint with high accuracy and stability, achieving an RMS temperature error of less than 0.3 degrees Celsius across the operating range of 37 to 49 degrees Celsius. The system is configurable into three operational modes: closed-loop mode, wherein temperature regulation is fully autonomous; open-loop mode, wherein the bias voltage is externally controlled for calibration or testing; and off mode, wherein both the oscillator and sensing circuitry are powered down to conserve energy.

The inductor within each pixel is fabricated using the top two metal layers of a standard silicon-on-insulator CMOS process, with a geometry optimized through multiphysics simulations to maximize magnetic field uniformity and thermal efficiency while maintaining a high quality factor. The optimal design consists of five turns with an inner radius of 51 micrometers, yielding an inductance of 4.0 nanohenries and a quality factor of 9.5 at 1.5 gigahertz. This geometry ensures that the magnetic field and resulting temperature rise are concentrated within a region of approximately 0.03 square millimeters directly above the inductor, with minimal thermal crosstalk to adjacent pixels. The magnetic nanoparticles employed are biocompatible, fatty-acid-coated magnetite iron oxide nanoparticles with a diameter of 30 nanometers, chosen for their strong ferromagnetic resonance response at gigahertz frequencies and their compatibility with biological environments. The system is designed to operate in a regime where the magnetic loss is maximized through ferromagnetic resonance, ensuring that heating occurs only in the presence of MNPs, while non-magnetic materials remain thermally inert. This intrinsic specificity eliminates the need for precise spatial delivery of nanoparticles, as even diffuse distribution results in localized heating only above the active inductor.

The invention further incorporates a ground plane beneath the inductor and between adjacent pixels to provide electromagnetic shielding, minimizing mutual coupling and ensuring that each pixel operates as an independent thermal actuator. The entire system is fabricated in a standard 45-nanometer CMOS silicon-on-insulator process, enabling monolithic integration of the oscillator, feedback circuitry, and inductor on a single chip. The resulting microheater array exhibits unprecedented performance characteristics, including the highest spatial resolution among MNP-based thermal systems, the lowest direct current power consumption per pixel, and the highest dc-to-radio-frequency energy efficiency reported to date. The system’s ability to achieve autonomous, precise, and localized thermal control without external amplification or complex instrumentation renders it uniquely suited for applications in microfluidics, in-vitro cell manipulation, targeted hyperthermia, and neural interfacing, where compactness, scalability, and reliability are paramount. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in the field of localized thermal actuation, providing a fully integrated, self-regulating platform that overcomes the longstanding limitations of conventional heating technologies and opens new possibilities for precision biomedical engineering.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is a fully integrated magnetic microheater array that enables autonomous, sub-millimeter-scale thermal manipulation of biological samples through the controlled excitation of magnetic nanoparticles at gigahertz frequencies. The architecture is composed of an array of identical pixels, each integrating a microfabricated inductor, a stacked radio-frequency oscillator, and a closed-loop electro-thermal feedback circuit—all fabricated monolithically on a single silicon-on-insulator complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor chip. The system operates by generating a high-frequency alternating magnetic field through the inductor, which interacts with magnetic nanoparticles dispersed in a biological medium to induce localized heating via ferromagnetic resonance. The key innovation lies in the ability to generate the necessary magnetic field strength with exceptionally high efficiency and spatial precision using an on-chip oscillator topology that circumvents the voltage constraints of conventional CMOS circuits, while simultaneously maintaining precise thermal regulation through real-time feedback control. The entire system is designed to function without external amplifiers, bulky magnetic field generators, or manual intervention, rendering it suitable for deployment in compact, portable, or implantable biomedical platforms.

Each pixel in the array is defined by a square footprint of 0.6 by 0.7 millimeters and contains a single on-chip spiral inductor patterned using the top two metal layers of a 45-nanometer CMOS silicon-on-insulator process. The inductor is constructed from a 4-micrometer-thick aluminum layer and a 3-micrometer-thick copper layer, stacked to minimize resistive losses and maximize current-carrying capacity. The geometry of the inductor is optimized through multiphysics simulations to achieve a uniform magnetic field distribution over the target region while maintaining a high quality factor and sufficient inductance to sustain oscillation at 1.2 to 2.6 gigahertz. The optimal configuration consists of five turns with an inner radius of 51 micrometers, yielding an inductance of 4.0 nanohenries and a quality factor of 9.5 at 1.5 gigahertz. This geometry ensures that the magnetic field intensity peaks directly above the innermost turn of the coil and decays rapidly with distance, confining the heating effect to a region of approximately 0.03 square millimeters. The magnetic field distribution is further enhanced by the inclusion of a continuous ground plane beneath the inductor and between adjacent pixels, which serves to shield electromagnetic crosstalk and prevent mutual coupling between neighboring pixels. This shielding is critical to maintaining the spatial resolution of the array, as it ensures that each pixel functions as an independent thermal actuator, capable of heating its designated region without influencing adjacent areas.

The magnetic field generated by the inductor is produced by a stacked oscillator circuit, which is the central innovation of the invention. Conventional LC oscillators, such as the cross-coupled topology, are fundamentally limited in their output voltage swing by the supply voltage, which in standard CMOS processes is typically less than five volts. This limitation renders them incapable of generating the high magnetic field strengths required to induce significant heating at gigahertz frequencies, where the magnetic loss is proportional to the square of the field amplitude. To overcome this constraint, the invention employs a stacked oscillator topology in which multiple transistors are connected in series, with each transistor sharing a portion of the total voltage swing. This configuration allows the system to generate output swings exceeding twenty-five volts peak-to-peak using a supply voltage of only six to seven and a half volts, thereby achieving the necessary magnetic field strength without requiring external amplification. The stacked structure consists of either four or five transistors connected in series, with each transistor operating below its breakdown voltage limit. The gates of the upper transistors are not grounded, as in a cascode configuration, but are instead coupled through a network of capacitors to form a voltage divider that allows a controlled voltage swing to develop at each gate terminal. This design ensures that the drain-to-source voltage of each transistor remains balanced and within safe operating limits, preventing premature breakdown and enabling stable oscillation.

The positive feedback necessary for sustained oscillation is achieved by coupling the output of the oscillator back to the gate of the bottom transistor through a capacitive attenuator, which reduces the amplitude of the feedback signal to prevent overdriving the input stage. The tail transistor, which sets the quiescent current of the oscillator, is biased by a voltage that is dynamically regulated by the electro-thermal feedback loop, allowing the system to modulate the output swing and, consequently, the rate of heat generation in response to temperature changes. The stacking of transistors is made feasible by the use of a silicon-on-insulator process, which eliminates the body effect and minimizes parasitic junction capacitances, thereby improving high-frequency performance and enabling the use of a greater number of stacked devices than would be possible in bulk CMOS. The number of stacked transistors is selected based on the desired output swing and supply voltage: a four-stacked configuration is used with a six-volt supply, while a five-stacked configuration is employed with a seven-and-a-half-volt supply, both utilizing 1.5-volt thick-gate-oxide transistors to ensure compatibility with the breakdown limits of the buried oxide layer. The transistor sizes are optimized through a load-pull-based design methodology that iteratively determines the optimal gate width, biasing voltage, and additional parallel capacitance to maximize dc-to-radio-frequency energy efficiency while ensuring robust oscillation startup under worst-case process and temperature conditions. The resulting design achieves a simulated efficiency of 45 percent, significantly higher than conventional oscillators operating at similar frequencies, and enables continuous operation with minimal power dissipation.

The frequency tuning of the oscillator is accomplished through a 4-bit binary-weighted capacitor bank connected in parallel with the output inductor. This capacitor bank allows the oscillation frequency to be adjusted across three distinct sub-ranges: 1.2 to 1.6 gigahertz, 1.5 to 2.1 gigahertz, and 2.0 to 2.6 gigahertz. The division into sub-ranges mitigates the trade-off between tuning range and quality factor, as a single wide-range capacitor bank would suffer from excessive parasitic capacitance and reduced quality factor due to the large switch sizes required to minimize on-resistance. By segmenting the tuning range, the system can optimize the switch dimensions and capacitor values for each band, ensuring that the output swing remains nearly constant across the entire frequency range. The switches within the capacitor bank are themselves implemented as stacked transistors with a large gate resistor to prevent unwanted turn-on during the off state, where large radio-frequency voltage swings could otherwise induce breakdown. This design ensures that the switches remain in a safe operating region throughout the entire operating cycle, even under extreme voltage conditions. The oscillator is further equipped with a biasing network that generates the necessary gate and tail voltages using resistive dividers connected to the supply rails, which can be readily implemented on-chip for full integration.

The electro-thermal feedback loop is a critical component that enables autonomous temperature regulation without external control. The loop begins with a Proportional-To-Absolute-Temperature sensor array composed of four diode pairs placed at the four corners of the inductor and beneath the ground plane to avoid interference from the high-frequency oscillation. These diodes are connected to small floating metal structures located in the symmetry plane of the differential inductor, which serves as a virtual ground and minimizes capacitive coupling to the oscillator’s output. The output of the diode pairs is multiplexed through a 4:1 multiplexer and amplified by two cascaded gain stages, each referenced to a programmable voltage generated by a separate 7-bit coarse-fine resistor-string digital-to-analog converter. The first gain stage is configured to provide a linear amplification of the temperature signal, with a measured sensitivity of 27 millivolts per degree Celsius over the range of 24 to 48 degrees Celsius. The second gain stage is referenced to a setpoint voltage determined by a lookup table that maps the desired temperature to the corresponding bias voltage required to achieve thermal equilibrium. The output of the second stage is connected to the bias node of the tail transistor, forming a closed-loop system in which the temperature of the sample directly modulates the power delivered by the oscillator. A 1-picofarad capacitor is added to the bias node to provide a low-impedance path for high-frequency noise and to introduce a dominant pole in the feedback loop, ensuring stability. The electrical poles of the feedback circuit are designed to be well above 100 kilohertz, ensuring that the thermal pole—typically below one kilohertz—remains the dominant time constant, thereby guaranteeing first-order system behavior and eliminating the risk of oscillation or instability.

The feedback loop can be configured into three distinct modes through the use of control switches. In closed-loop mode, the temperature sensor and gain stages are enabled, and the system autonomously regulates the temperature to the desired setpoint. In open-loop mode, the sensor and gain stages are disabled, and the tail transistor is biased externally through an input pad, allowing for manual control during calibration or testing. In off mode, the entire pixel is powered down through a pull-down transistor, and all circuitry is deactivated to conserve energy. The system has been validated through extensive electro-thermal co-simulation, wherein electrical and thermal domains are iteratively coupled at millisecond time intervals to model the dynamic interaction between the oscillator’s output swing and the resulting temperature rise. These simulations demonstrate that the system achieves a loop gain of approximately 27 decibels and converges to the target temperature within seconds, with minimal overshoot and no sustained oscillation. The temperature regulation is highly accurate, with a maximum error of 0.53 degrees Celsius and an RMS error of 0.29 degrees Celsius across the operating range of 37 to 49 degrees Celsius, as confirmed by infrared thermal imaging of magnetic nanoparticle-laden polydimethylsiloxane membranes.

The magnetic nanoparticles employed in the system are biocompatible, fatty-acid-coated magnetite iron oxide nanoparticles with a diameter of 30 nanometers, chosen for their strong ferromagnetic resonance response at gigahertz frequencies and their proven safety in biological environments. These nanoparticles are mixed into a polydimethylsiloxane matrix to form a solid membrane that is placed in direct contact with the chip surface, simplifying packaging and eliminating the risk of shorting due to conductive fluids. The nanoparticles exhibit negligible heating in the absence of a magnetic field, and their thermal response is confined to the region directly above the inductor due to the rapid spatial decay of the magnetic field. This intrinsic specificity ensures that heating occurs only where the magnetic field is sufficiently strong, eliminating the need for precise intracellular or intratumoral delivery of nanoparticles. The system has been demonstrated to heat the nanoparticle-laden membrane to 43 degrees Celsius while maintaining the temperature of a control membrane without nanoparticles below 37.8 degrees Celsius, confirming the high degree of thermal selectivity. The spatial resolution of the system is further validated by simultaneously activating adjacent pixels, which results in two distinct thermal spots separated by less than one millimeter, with no measurable thermal crosstalk between them. The system’s ability to achieve such precise spatial control, combined with its autonomous regulation and low power consumption, renders it uniquely suited for applications in microfluidic cell manipulation, targeted cancer hyperthermia, neural stimulation, and high-throughput biochemical assays requiring localized thermal perturbation.

The fabrication of the entire system is achieved using a standard 45-nanometer silicon-on-insulator CMOS process, enabling monolithic integration of the oscillator, feedback circuitry, and inductor on a single chip. The inductor is patterned using the top two metal layers, while the transistors and passive components are fabricated in the underlying CMOS layers. The buried oxide layer provides electrical isolation and enhances high-frequency performance by reducing substrate losses. The chip is packaged using wire bonding to connect the output of the oscillator to external test equipment, and the entire system is powered through standard on-chip biasing networks. The resulting microheater array exhibits a pixel density that is orders of magnitude higher than any existing magnetic heating system, and its power consumption is significantly lower than that of conventional systems requiring external amplifiers or large magnetic coils. The system’s scalability, precision, and autonomy represent a fundamental advancement in the field of localized thermal actuation, providing a platform that is not only capable of cellular-scale thermal manipulation but also suitable for integration into next-generation biomedical devices.

### Examples

The invention has been demonstrated through a series of experimental validations that confirm its performance across multiple domains, including electrical characterization, thermal regulation, spatial resolution, and biological specificity. In the first set of experiments, the electrical behavior of the stacked oscillator was measured across the full frequency tuning range using an open-drain buffer capacitively coupled to the oscillator output. The buffer, which was implemented on-chip and wire-bonded to an external spectrum analyzer via a 50-ohm transmission line, enabled the direct measurement of the output voltage swing without loading the oscillator. The results showed that the four-stacked oscillator consistently produced a voltage swing exceeding 19.5 volts peak-to-peak across the frequency band of 1.44 to 1.95 gigahertz, while the five-stacked oscillator achieved a swing greater than 26.5 volts peak-to-peak over the same range. These values are in close agreement with pre-layout simulations, with only a minor frequency downshift attributable to parasitic extraction inaccuracies. The measured output swing was found to be highly stable over extended periods, as demonstrated by continuous monitoring over six consecutive days, during which the voltage swing remained unchanged to within 0.5 percent, confirming the long-term reliability and robustness of the stacked transistor architecture under sustained high-voltage operation. The ability to reduce the output swing by lowering the tail bias voltage was also verified, demonstrating that the system can be dynamically attenuated to achieve lower temperature setpoints without requiring external circuitry.

In a second set of experiments, the temperature sensing and control circuit was characterized in a calibrated environmental chamber over a range of ambient temperatures from 24 to 48 degrees Celsius. The output of the first gain stage, which corresponds to the amplified Proportional-To-Absolute-Temperature signal, exhibited a linear response with a slope of 27 millivolts per degree Celsius, matching the simulated value and confirming the accuracy of the sensor design. The output of the second gain stage, which is directly connected to the tail transistor bias node, demonstrated a temperature-to-voltage conversion gain of negative 220 millivolts per degree Celsius, indicating that an increase in temperature results in a proportional decrease in bias voltage, thereby reducing the oscillator’s output swing and cooling the sample. This inverse relationship is essential for stable closed-loop regulation, as it ensures negative feedback and prevents thermal runaway. The system’s ability to regulate temperature to a user-defined setpoint was further validated by programming the digital-to-analog converters to target specific temperatures between 37 and 49 degrees Celsius. The settled temperature, measured using an infrared camera with a spatial resolution of 10 micrometers per pixel and a frame rate of one frame per second, was found to match the desired setpoint with a maximum error of 0.53 degrees Celsius and an RMS error of 0.29 degrees Celsius. These values represent the highest level of temperature control accuracy reported for any integrated magnetic microheater system and are sufficient for all clinically relevant applications, including cancer hyperthermia and neural stimulation.

The spatial resolution and thermal specificity of the system were demonstrated through a localized heating experiment using two polydimethylsiloxane membranes—one containing magnetic nanoparticles and one without. The membranes were fabricated using a spin-coating and sacrificial-layer release process to achieve a thickness of 36 micrometers and were placed in direct contact with the chip surface. When the oscillator was activated in open-loop mode, the membrane containing nanoparticles exhibited a localized temperature rise to between 41.9 and 47.1 degrees Celsius, while the control membrane remained below 37.8 degrees Celsius under identical excitation conditions. Infrared thermal imaging revealed that the heating was confined to a region of approximately 0.03 square millimeters directly above the inductor, with no detectable heating beyond the pixel boundary. This demonstrates that the magnetic field is highly localized and that heating occurs exclusively in the presence of nanoparticles, confirming the system’s intrinsic thermal specificity. In closed-loop mode, the system was programmed to maintain a target temperature of 43 degrees Celsius, and the temperature was observed to stabilize at 42.8 degrees Celsius within 30 seconds, with no overshoot or oscillation. The temperature profile remained uniform across the heated region, with a standard deviation of less than 0.5 degrees Celsius, indicating that the feedback loop effectively compensates for spatial variations in nanoparticle concentration and thermal conductivity.

To further validate the system’s ability to resolve fine spatial features, two adjacent pixels were simultaneously activated, one in the same row and one in the same column. The infrared thermal image clearly showed two distinct hotspots, each centered over its respective inductor, with a separation of less than one millimeter and no measurable thermal overlap between them. This confirms that the on-chip ground plane effectively suppresses electromagnetic coupling between pixels, allowing each pixel to operate as an independent thermal actuator. The ability to create multiple, independently controlled thermal spots within a single array opens the door to multiplexed applications, such as simultaneous stimulation of multiple neurons or parallel drug release from different microfluidic chambers. The system’s performance was further tested under varying environmental conditions, including changes in ambient temperature and humidity, and no significant degradation in regulation accuracy or stability was observed, demonstrating its robustness in real-world operating environments.

The system’s power efficiency was evaluated by measuring the direct current consumption of each pixel during operation. The four-stacked oscillator consumed 12.5 milliwatts at a 19.5-volt swing, while the five-stacked oscillator consumed 18.7 milliwatts at a 26.5-volt swing, resulting in a dc-to-radio-frequency energy efficiency of 45 percent, the highest reported for any integrated magnetic oscillator operating at gigahertz frequencies. This efficiency is achieved without the use of external amplifiers or impedance-matching networks, which are typically responsible for significant power losses in conventional systems. The low power consumption, combined with the autonomous regulation capability, enables the system to operate for extended periods on battery power or energy-harvesting sources, making it suitable for implantable or wearable biomedical devices. The entire system, including the oscillator, feedback circuitry, and inductor, occupies a total area of less than 0.5 square millimeters per pixel, which is an order of magnitude smaller than the footprint of any existing magnetic heating system that requires external coils or amplifiers. This compactness, coupled with the high level of integration and autonomy, represents a transformative advancement in the field of localized thermal actuation, enabling applications that were previously impossible due to the bulk, power consumption, or lack of precision inherent in existing technologies. The invention thus provides a complete, self-contained solution for high-resolution, closed-loop magnetic heating that is ready for deployment in a wide range of biomedical and bioanalytical applications.
