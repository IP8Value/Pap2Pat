# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of differentiable rendering in computer graphics, computer vision, and machine learning, particularly to systems and methods for computing accurate, smooth, and efficient derivatives of rendered images with respect to underlying three-dimensional scene parameters. The invention enables the differentiation of image outputs with respect to geometric and material properties of both explicit surface representations—such as triangle meshes and parametric surfaces—and implicit surface representations—such as isosurfaces derived from volumetric density fields—without requiring custom gradient implementations or explicit handling of occlusion boundaries. The method is particularly suited for inverse rendering tasks, including pose estimation, shape optimization, texture recovery, and neural radiance field refinement, where high-fidelity gradients are essential for stable and rapid convergence. By decoupling non-differentiable surface sampling from differentiable image-space operations, the invention provides a unified, scalable, and architecture-agnostic framework that leverages automatic differentiation libraries to compute gradients for any shading model, including complex neural networks such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), without modification to the underlying rendering pipeline. The resulting system supports both forward and reverse-mode differentiation, operates on standard graphics hardware, and is compatible with existing deep learning frameworks, enabling seamless integration into end-to-end learning pipelines for 3D scene understanding and generative modeling.

## BACKGROUND

Traditional approaches to differentiable rendering have been constrained by the inherent non-differentiability of geometric operations such as rasterization, occlusion resolution, and surface sampling. In computer graphics, triangle meshes remain the dominant representation for 3D shapes due to their efficiency and widespread support in hardware-accelerated rendering pipelines. However, when attempting to compute gradients of rendered images with respect to mesh vertex positions, normals, or textures, the discontinuities introduced by occlusion boundaries—where one surface element hides another—render standard automatic differentiation inapplicable. These discontinuities cause gradients to be undefined or zero at critical regions, leading to unstable optimization, poor convergence, and inaccurate parameter recovery. Previous methods have attempted to circumvent this issue through heuristic gradient replacements, such as image-space filtering or per-triangle derivative approximations, but these approaches often introduce mismatches between the forward rendering process and its gradient, resulting in optimization artifacts, convergence failure, or an inability to support complex shading models and textures.

Alternative strategies have focused on explicitly sampling occluding edges of the mesh, which allows gradients to be computed along silhouette boundaries. While effective in certain contexts, these methods require costly preprocessing to identify and parameterize edges, scale poorly with mesh complexity, and are tightly coupled to specific hardware implementations, limiting their adaptability to new rendering architectures or shading paradigms. Other approaches have reparameterized the rendering integral in the context of Monte Carlo path tracing, but these are restricted to global illumination settings and are not applicable to the simpler, local shading models used in most vision and machine learning applications. A separate class of methods modifies the forward rendering itself to produce smooth, differentiable outputs by softening visibility or using probabilistic rasterization. While these techniques produce continuous gradients, they do so at the cost of introducing blur, altering the appearance of the rendered image, and requiring expensive closest-point queries or spatial binning that hinder scalability to high-resolution or high-complexity scenes.

For implicit surface representations, such as signed-distance fields or volumetric density functions, the challenge is twofold: first, the surface must be extracted from the volume, typically via algorithms like Marching Cubes, which are inherently non-differentiable due to topological ambiguities and singularities when grid values are nearly equal; second, even after extraction, the resulting mesh must be rendered differentiably, reintroducing the same occlusion boundary problems faced by explicit meshes. Volume rendering, which integrates along rays to produce smooth gradients, avoids these issues but is computationally prohibitive, requiring hundreds of evaluations per pixel to approximate the surface, making it impractical for real-time or optimization-intensive applications. Recent work has attempted to combine non-differentiable surface extraction with differentiable visibility, but these methods rely on custom gradient formulations tied to specific surface representations—such as true signed-distance fields—and fail to generalize to arbitrary isosurfaces or grid-based parameterizations. Moreover, they do not support deferred shading or neural network-based appearance models without significant architectural modifications.

The need for a general, efficient, and robust differentiable rendering method that unifies explicit and implicit surface representations, supports arbitrary shading functions, and produces accurate derivatives at occlusion boundaries without custom gradient code or scene-specific preprocessing has remained unmet. Existing systems are either too specialized, too slow, too inaccurate, or too tightly coupled to proprietary hardware or rendering pipelines to serve as a foundational tool for emerging applications in neural rendering, 3D reconstruction, inverse rendering, and differentiable graphics. There is a critical gap in the state of the art for a method that can render complex, high-detail surfaces—whether defined by meshes, splines, or volumetric fields—with the same efficiency as conventional rasterization, while simultaneously providing smooth, physically meaningful gradients that enable stable and accurate optimization of scene parameters through automatic differentiation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention introduces a novel method for differentiable rendering that decouples the non-differentiable process of surface sampling from the differentiable process of image-space shading and accumulation, thereby enabling accurate, smooth, and efficient computation of gradients with respect to arbitrary 3D scene parameters. The method, termed rasterize-then-splat (RtS), operates in three sequential stages: first, a non-differentiable sampling function extracts surface points from the underlying geometry—whether explicit (e.g., triangle mesh, parametric surface) or implicit (e.g., isosurface of a density field)—and produces screen-space parameter buffers containing the necessary geometric information for each pixel; second, a differentiable evaluation function interpolates surface attributes such as position, normal, and texture coordinates from these buffers to construct G-buffers containing shaded surface properties; third, a depth-aware, multi-layer splatting operation convolves these shaded samples with a spatial kernel centered at their projected screen positions, accumulating contributions across multiple depth layers to produce a final differentiable image that correctly resolves occlusions and disocclusions.

Crucially, the sampling step is treated as a black-box oracle that requires no gradient computation, allowing any conventional surface extraction or rasterization technique—such as Z-buffering, Marching Cubes, or B-spline subdivision—to be used without modification. The evaluation and splatting stages, by contrast, are implemented entirely within an automatic differentiation framework, ensuring that gradients flow seamlessly from the output image back through the shading model and into the underlying scene parameters. This design eliminates the need for custom gradient functions, enabling immediate support for both forward and reverse-mode differentiation, as well as higher-order derivatives, without additional implementation effort. The splatting operation employs a depth-aware, multi-layer accumulation strategy that distinguishes between splats in front of, behind, or coincident with the visible surface at each pixel, thereby correctly handling self-occlusions and disocclusions that plague single-layer splatting approaches. The resulting image is not merely a blurred version of the rasterized output, but a mathematically consistent differentiable approximation that preserves the forward rendering appearance while providing non-zero, physically meaningful gradients at occlusion boundaries.

The method is broadly applicable to a wide range of surface representations, including triangle meshes with texture maps, bicubic B-spline surfaces, Catmull-Clark subdivision surfaces, and isosurfaces extracted from volumetric grids or neural networks. When applied to Neural Radiance Fields, the method enables the conversion of a pre-trained volume-rendered NeRF into a surface light field that requires only a single network evaluation per pixel, achieving over a 100-fold speedup in rendering time while preserving image quality within 0.3 PSNR. The system is implemented using standard deep learning frameworks, requires no custom CUDA kernels or specialized hardware, and scales efficiently with scene complexity because the computational cost of the differentiable stages is bounded by the number of pixels, not the number of surface primitives. The invention thus provides a unified, general-purpose differentiable rendering pipeline that bridges the gap between traditional graphics rendering and modern machine learning systems, enabling high-fidelity optimization of 3D geometry, appearance, and lighting in applications ranging from pose estimation and shape reconstruction to neural rendering and generative 3D modeling.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The rasterize-then-splat method is a principled framework for computing gradients of rendered images with respect to arbitrary 3D scene parameters, including geometry, material properties, lighting, and camera pose, without requiring custom gradient implementations or explicit handling of geometric discontinuities. The core insight of the invention is that the non-differentiable nature of surface sampling—such as finding ray-surface intersections, resolving occlusions, or extracting isosurfaces—is not an obstacle to differentiation, but rather an opportunity to simplify the gradient computation by isolating it from the differentiable stages of the pipeline. By separating the process into two distinct phases, the invention ensures that the forward rendering remains faithful to conventional graphics techniques, while the backward pass leverages the power of automatic differentiation to compute accurate, smooth gradients across all scene parameters.

The method begins with a scene description defined by a set of parameters θ, which may include vertex positions, control points of parametric surfaces, grid values of a volumetric density field, camera extrinsics and intrinsics, material reflectance properties, or neural network weights. These parameters are passed through a sampling function U, which is non-differentiable and operates as a black-box oracle. The sampling function U takes θ as input and outputs a set of screen-space parameter buffers U_k, where each buffer corresponds to a layer of surface intersections with the camera rays. For triangle meshes, U_k may contain triangle indices and barycentric coordinates; for parametric surfaces, it may contain patch identifiers and local parameter coordinates; for implicit surfaces, it may contain lattice indices and barycentric weights derived from Marching Cubes triangulation. The key requirement is that U_k contains sufficient information to reconstruct the surface point, normal, and other attributes at each pixel, but it does not need to be differentiable. This allows the use of any existing, highly optimized rasterization or surface extraction algorithm without modification.

The next stage is the evaluation function G, which takes the scene parameters θ and the sampled buffers U_k as inputs and produces G-buffers G_k. These G-buffers contain interpolated surface attributes such as world-space positions, surface normals, texture coordinates, or other shading-relevant quantities. The evaluation function G is implemented entirely using standard, differentiable operations such as linear interpolation, matrix multiplication, and lookup tables, all of which are natively supported by automatic differentiation frameworks. Because the complexity of G is independent of the underlying geometry and bounded by the number of pixels, it can be efficiently computed even for scenes with millions of triangles or high-resolution volumetric grids. Importantly, G does not need to be aware of the topological structure of the surface; it only needs to interpolate attributes from the parameter buffers provided by U. This decoupling allows the method to handle complex, dynamic, or topologically varying surfaces without any change to the differentiable codebase.

Once the G-buffers are computed, a deferred shading function C is applied to generate shaded color buffers C_k. This step is fully differentiable and may employ any shading model that can be expressed as a function of the surface attributes in G_k. For example, a simple Lambertian reflectance model may be used for diffuse surfaces, a Phong model for specular highlights, or a neural radiance field network for photorealistic appearance. The shading function C is evaluated at each pixel using the interpolated attributes from G_k, producing a set of RGBA color buffers that encode the appearance of the surface under the current lighting and viewing conditions. Because the shading is performed in image space on a fixed-resolution grid, its computational cost is independent of the complexity of the underlying geometry, enabling the rendering of highly detailed scenes with minimal overhead.

The final stage is the splatting operation S, which converts the discrete, point-sampled shaded colors C_k into a continuous, differentiable image by distributing each color over a small spatial neighborhood using a weighted kernel. The splatting kernel is defined as a Gaussian with narrow variance, centered at the projected screen position of each surface sample. Because the surface samples are resampled at every frame and may overlap at occlusion boundaries, a simple accumulation of splats would produce incorrect gradients due to the lack of depth ordering. To resolve this, the method employs a multi-layer splatting strategy that maintains three separate accumulation buffers: S_+ for splats that lie in front of the visible surface at a pixel, S_- for splats that lie behind, and S_o for splats that are coincident with the visible surface. The depth relationship between a splat and the visible surface at each pixel is determined by pairing the multi-layer surface intersections at the splat’s location with those at the target pixel, assigning each splat to the appropriate buffer based on its relative depth. After all splats have been assigned, each accumulation buffer is normalized independently using a weighted sum over the 3x3 neighborhood of pixels, ensuring that the gradient of the final image matches the forward rendering process. The three buffers are then composited in order: S_- first, followed by S_o, then S_+, using standard over-compositing to produce the final rendered image S.

The entire pipeline—from sampling to splatting—is implemented using standard automatic differentiation primitives, meaning that no custom gradient functions are required. This allows the method to support both forward and reverse-mode differentiation out of the box, enabling the use of advanced optimization algorithms such as Levenberg-Marquardt, which require full Jacobian matrices, as well as higher-order derivatives for sensitivity analysis or second-order optimization. The method is entirely compatible with existing deep learning frameworks such as TensorFlow and PyTorch, and can be deployed on standard GPUs without specialized hardware or custom kernels. The result is a rendering system that combines the efficiency and fidelity of conventional rasterization with the gradient accuracy of differentiable rendering, enabling stable, high-quality optimization of 3D scenes in applications ranging from inverse rendering to neural rendering and generative modeling.

### Example Devices and Systems

The rasterize-then-splat method is implemented as a software system that operates on general-purpose computing devices equipped with graphics processing units capable of executing parallel floating-point computations. The system is designed to be deployed on standard workstations, servers, or cloud-based computing clusters, and is compatible with widely used graphics and machine learning frameworks such as TensorFlow, PyTorch, and JAX. The core components of the system include a scene description module, a non-differentiable rasterization engine, a differentiable evaluation and shading pipeline, and a multi-layer splatting accumulator, all of which are integrated into a single computational graph that supports automatic differentiation.

The scene description module receives input parameters that define the geometry, appearance, and lighting of the 3D scene. These parameters may be represented as vertex arrays for triangle meshes, control point grids for parametric surfaces, voxel grids for implicit surfaces, or neural network weights for learned appearance models. The module also accepts camera parameters such as intrinsic matrices, extrinsic poses, and field-of-view settings, as well as lighting parameters such as directional light directions, intensities, and spectral distributions. The input parameters are organized into a structured tensor representation that is compatible with the automatic differentiation framework being used. For example, in a PyTorch implementation, the parameters are stored as torch.Tensor objects with requires_grad=True, enabling gradient computation through the entire pipeline.

The non-differentiable rasterization engine is responsible for computing the screen-space parameter buffers U_k. This component is implemented using conventional graphics APIs such as OpenGL or Vulkan, or via high-performance rasterization libraries such as nvdiffrast or CUDA-based Z-buffering routines. The engine casts rays from the camera through each pixel of the output image and determines the closest K surface intersections per pixel, where K is a small constant (typically 2 to 4). For triangle meshes, the engine computes triangle indices and barycentric coordinates using depth peeling techniques to retrieve multiple intersections. For parametric surfaces such as B-splines or subdivision surfaces, the engine performs adaptive subdivision or direct parameter search to locate the surface points intersecting each ray. For implicit surfaces defined by a volumetric density field, the engine applies the Marching Cubes algorithm to extract a triangulated isosurface at a specified threshold, then computes the lattice indices and barycentric weights for each surface vertex. The output of this stage is a set of non-differentiable tensors U_k, each representing a layer of surface intersections, with dimensions corresponding to the output image resolution and the number of layers.

The differentiable evaluation and shading pipeline takes the parameter buffers U_k and the scene parameters θ as inputs and computes the G-buffers G_k and shaded color buffers C_k. This stage is implemented entirely using differentiable operations within the deep learning framework. For example, to compute world-space positions from barycentric coordinates in a triangle mesh, the system performs a linear interpolation of the vertex positions using the barycentric weights, a standard operation supported by all major frameworks. Similarly, surface normals are computed by interpolating vertex normals or by differentiating the implicit surface function using automatic differentiation. The shading function C is implemented as a neural network or a procedural shader, and is evaluated at each pixel using the interpolated attributes from G_k. For instance, when rendering a NeRF-derived surface, the system feeds the world-space position and viewing direction into a pre-trained neural network, which outputs the corresponding radiance and density values. The output of this stage is a set of RGBA buffers C_k, each of the same resolution as the output image, containing the shaded color of each surface sample.

The multi-layer splatting accumulator receives the shaded color buffers C_k and the screen-space position buffers P_k, which are computed by projecting the world-space positions from G_k into screen space using the camera projection matrix. The accumulator then applies a depth-aware splatting operation to each pixel in the output image. For each splat centered at position p with color c, the system determines its depth relationship to the visible surface at each target pixel q by comparing the depth of p with the depths of the surface intersections at q. Based on this comparison, the splat is assigned to one of three accumulation buffers: S_+, S_o, or S_-. The splat’s contribution to each pixel in a 3x3 neighborhood is computed using a Gaussian kernel with standard deviation σ = 0.5 pixels, and the weights are normalized per pixel to ensure consistency between the forward and backward passes. The normalization includes a small adjustment factor to ensure that the sum of weights in the neighborhood always exceeds one, preventing division-by-zero or underflow at image boundaries. The three accumulation buffers are then composited using over-compositing to produce the final rendered image S.

The entire system is executed as a single computational graph, with gradients flowing automatically from the final image S back through the splatting, shading, and evaluation stages, and ultimately into the scene parameters θ. Because no custom gradient functions are required, the system supports both forward and reverse-mode differentiation without modification. For example, in a forward-mode setting, the system can compute the full Jacobian matrix of the image with respect to the pose parameters, enabling efficient optimization using Levenberg-Marquardt. In reverse-mode, the system can compute the gradient of a loss function—such as mean squared error between the rendered image and a target photograph—with respect to all scene parameters, enabling end-to-end training of neural networks or optimization of geometry using gradient descent. The system is designed to be modular, allowing users to swap out the sampling engine, shading model, or splatting kernel without altering the core differentiable pipeline. This modularity enables rapid prototyping and experimentation with new surface representations, shading techniques, or optimization strategies.

### Example Model Arrangements

The rasterize-then-splat method is compatible with a wide variety of surface representations, each requiring a specific arrangement of the sampling and evaluation functions to properly extract and interpolate surface attributes. The method is designed to be representation-agnostic, meaning that the same differentiable pipeline can be applied to triangle meshes, parametric surfaces, and implicit surfaces without modification, provided that the sampling and evaluation functions are appropriately configured for each case. This flexibility is a key advantage of the invention, as it allows researchers and practitioners to apply differentiable rendering to novel surface representations without having to redesign the entire gradient computation pipeline.

For triangle meshes, the sampling function U is implemented using a conventional Z-buffering algorithm with depth peeling to retrieve multiple surface intersections per pixel. The output of U consists of two buffers: T_k, which contains the index of the triangle intersecting the ray at layer k, and B_k, which contains the three barycentric coordinates of the intersection point within that triangle. The evaluation function G then uses these buffers to interpolate the vertex attributes—such as position, normal, and texture coordinates—from the mesh’s vertex arrays. For example, the world-space position of the surface point at pixel q and layer k is computed as a linear combination of the three vertices of triangle T_k, weighted by the corresponding barycentric coordinates in B_k. Similarly, the surface normal is interpolated from the vertex normals, and the texture coordinates are interpolated from the UV map. These interpolated values are stored in the G-buffers G_k, which are then passed to the shading function C. The shading function may be a simple Lambertian model, a Phong model, or a neural network that predicts color based on position and normal. The resulting shaded color C_k is then splatted using the multi-layer splatting operation, with the screen-space position P_k computed by projecting the interpolated world-space position through the camera matrix.

For parametric surfaces such as bicubic B-spline surfaces, the sampling function U is adapted to handle the continuous parameterization of the surface. Instead of triangle indices, U outputs patch identifiers and local parameter coordinates (u, v) within each patch. The surface is defined by a grid of control points, and each patch is a rectangular region of the parameter space spanned by four control points in each direction. The sampling function performs a search over the parameter space to find the patch and local coordinates that correspond to the intersection of each camera ray with the surface. This search may be performed using adaptive subdivision, root-finding algorithms, or direct parameter sampling. Once the patch and parameter coordinates are determined, the evaluation function G computes the surface point and normal using the B-spline basis functions. The position x(u, v) is computed as a weighted sum of the control points, where the weights are determined by the tensor product of cubic B-spline basis functions in u and v. The normal is computed by differentiating the position function with respect to u and v and taking the cross product. These values are stored in the G-buffers, and the shading function C is applied as before. The screen-space position P_k is computed by projecting the world-space position through the camera matrix, and the splatting operation proceeds identically to the mesh case.

For implicit surfaces defined by a volumetric density field f: R³ → R, the sampling function U is implemented using the Marching Cubes algorithm. The density field is discretized on a regular 3D grid, and the algorithm identifies voxels where the density crosses a specified isosurface threshold. For each such voxel, the algorithm computes the intersection points of the isosurface with the voxel edges and triangulates the resulting points to form a local mesh. The output of U is a 9-dimensional vector for each pixel: six lattice indices that identify the corners of the voxel containing the intersection, and three barycentric coordinates that define the location of the surface point within the resulting triangle. The evaluation function G then interpolates the density values at the six lattice points, computes the edge intersection coefficients using linear interpolation, and combines them with the barycentric coordinates to produce the world-space position of the surface point. The normal is computed by numerically differentiating the density field using finite differences or by applying automatic differentiation to the density function if it is differentiable. The shading function C may be a simple Lambertian model or, in the case of a NeRF-derived surface, a neural network that predicts color as a function of position and viewing direction. The screen-space position P_k is computed by projecting the world-space position, and the splatting operation proceeds as before. This arrangement allows the method to handle topological changes—such as the emergence or merging of surfaces—without requiring any modification to the differentiable pipeline, since the Marching Cubes triangulation is handled entirely within the non-differentiable sampling stage.

The method also supports hybrid representations, such as a mesh with an implicit detail layer, or a parametric surface with a neural texture map. In such cases, the sampling function U may produce multiple sets of parameter buffers, each corresponding to a different layer of the surface. The evaluation function G then combines the attributes from each layer—for example, by blending the base mesh normals with the implicit detail normals using a weight determined by the density field. The shading function C may sample from multiple G-buffers, applying different shading models to different layers. The splatting operation then accumulates all layers using the multi-layer strategy, ensuring that occlusions between layers are correctly resolved. This capability enables the rendering of highly detailed surfaces with both coarse geometry and fine-scale detail, all within a single, unified differentiable framework.

### Example Methods

The rasterize-then-splat method is implemented as a sequence of computational steps that are executed in a fixed order during each rendering pass, with gradients computed automatically through the entire pipeline. The method is designed to be executed on a graphics processing unit to leverage parallel computation, and is structured to minimize memory bandwidth and maximize computational efficiency. Each step is implemented using standard operations supported by deep learning frameworks, ensuring compatibility with existing software ecosystems and enabling seamless integration into training pipelines.

The first step is the initialization of the scene parameters θ, which may include vertex positions, control points, grid values, neural network weights, camera pose, and lighting parameters. These parameters are stored as tensors with gradient tracking enabled. The camera parameters are used to construct the view and projection matrices, which are then applied to transform the world-space geometry into screen space. The sampling function U is invoked, which performs a non-differentiable traversal of the scene geometry to determine the set of surface intersections with the camera rays. For each pixel in the output image, the function returns K surface samples, each represented by a set of parameters that uniquely identify the surface point and its local context. These parameters are stored in a set of non-differentiable buffers U_k, where k ranges from 1 to K, and each buffer has dimensions corresponding to the image resolution.

The second step is the evaluation of surface attributes using the function G. For each layer k, the system retrieves the parameter buffer U_k and the scene parameters θ, and computes the interpolated attributes for each pixel. For triangle meshes, this involves looking up the three vertex positions, normals, and texture coordinates corresponding to the triangle index T_k, and computing the weighted average using the barycentric coordinates B_k. For parametric surfaces, the system evaluates the B-spline basis functions at the parameter coordinates (u, v) to compute the position and normal. For implicit surfaces, the system interpolates the density values at the six lattice points, computes the edge intersection coefficients, and combines them with the barycentric coordinates to obtain the surface point. The resulting attributes are stored in the G-buffers G_k, which contain the world-space position, surface normal, texture coordinates, and any other shading-relevant quantities. The screen-space position P_k is computed by applying the camera projection matrix to the world-space position, producing a 2D coordinate for each surface sample.

The third step is the application of the shading function C. The G-buffers are passed to the shading function, which computes the RGBA color for each surface sample. The shading function may be a simple procedural model, such as a Lambertian reflectance model that computes the dot product between the surface normal and the light direction, or a complex neural network such as a NeRF that predicts color as a function of position and viewing direction. In the case of a NeRF, the system feeds the world-space position and the viewing direction into the network, which outputs the radiance and density values. The density is not used in the shading step but may be used for subsequent volume rendering or regularization. The output of the shading function is a set of RGBA buffers C_k, each of the same resolution as the output image, containing the shaded color of each surface sample.

The fourth step is the multi-layer splatting operation S. For each surface sample, the system determines its depth relationship to the visible surface at each target pixel by comparing the depth of the sample with the depths of the surface intersections at the target pixel. Based on this comparison, the sample is assigned to one of three accumulation buffers: S_+, S_o, or S_-. The splat’s contribution to each pixel in a 3x3 neighborhood is computed using a Gaussian kernel with standard deviation σ = 0.5 pixels. The weights are normalized per pixel by dividing by the sum of weights in the neighborhood, with a small adjustment factor added to ensure the sum is always greater than one. The three accumulation buffers are then composited in order: S_- first, followed by S_o, then S_+, using over-compositing to produce the final rendered image S. The normalization ensures that the gradient of the final image matches the forward rendering process, even though the splats are resampled at every frame.

The fifth and final step is the computation of the loss function and the automatic differentiation of the entire pipeline. The rendered image S is compared to a target image using a loss function such as mean squared error, L1 loss, or perceptual loss. The loss is then backpropagated through the splatting, shading, evaluation, and sampling stages using the automatic differentiation engine of the framework. Because all operations are implemented using standard differentiable primitives, the gradients flow automatically through the entire pipeline, producing gradients with respect to all scene parameters θ. These gradients are then used to update the parameters using an optimization algorithm such as Adam, SGD, or Levenberg-Marquardt. The process is repeated for multiple iterations until convergence is achieved.

The method supports both forward and reverse-mode differentiation. In reverse-mode, which is most commonly used in machine learning, the system computes the gradient of the loss with respect to all parameters in a single backward pass. In forward-mode, which is useful for computing full Jacobian matrices, the system can compute the derivative of the image with respect to each parameter individually, enabling the use of optimization algorithms that require second-order information. The method is also compatible with higher-order derivatives, allowing for the computation of Hessian matrices or sensitivity analysis, which are useful for uncertainty quantification and robust optimization.

### Additional Disclosure

The rasterize-then-splat method extends beyond the core rendering pipeline to encompass a broad range of applications and enhancements that further expand its utility in computer graphics, vision, and machine learning. One significant extension is the integration of temporal coherence and motion blur, which enables the rendering of dynamic scenes with smooth, differentiable motion. In this extension, the sampling function U is augmented to track surface points across consecutive frames, and the splatting operation is modified to accumulate contributions over time using a temporal kernel. The resulting system can render motion-blurred images that are differentiable with respect to both spatial and temporal parameters, such as object velocities, deformations, or camera motion. This capability is particularly valuable for applications in video-based 3D reconstruction, dynamic shape estimation, and differentiable animation.

Another extension involves the incorporation of semi-transparent and volumetric effects, such as translucency, subsurface scattering, or atmospheric scattering. Although the current implementation assumes opaque surfaces, the multi-layer splatting strategy can be generalized to handle multiple layers of semi-transparent surfaces by maintaining a stack of accumulation buffers and applying a compositing model that accounts for opacity and light attenuation. Each layer would contribute a weighted color and opacity value, and the final image would be computed by compositing the layers in depth order using a modified over-compositing formula. This extension would enable the rendering of materials such as skin, wax, or frosted glass, which are currently difficult to model with differentiable rendering systems.

The method also supports multi-view consistency and self-supervised learning by enabling the simultaneous rendering of multiple camera viewpoints from a single set of scene parameters. In this configuration, the sampling and evaluation stages are executed once per frame, but the shading and splatting stages are applied independently for each camera. The loss function is then computed as the sum of errors across all views, and gradients are accumulated from all views during backpropagation. This approach enables the optimization of 3D geometry and appearance from unposed or loosely posed image collections, without requiring explicit camera calibration. The method is particularly well-suited for applications in structure-from-motion, multi-view stereo, and neural radiance field refinement from sparse input views.

The system can also be extended to support real-time rendering by optimizing the sampling and splatting stages for low-latency execution. For example, the sampling function can be precomputed for static scenes and cached in GPU memory, while the splatting operation can be implemented using a tiled, asynchronous approach that processes pixels in parallel across multiple GPU cores. The use of sparse splatting—where only pixels near the surface are updated—can further reduce computational overhead, enabling interactive frame rates even for high-resolution scenes. This makes the method viable for applications in virtual reality, augmented reality, and real-time 3D content creation.

The method is also compatible with hardware-accelerated ray tracing pipelines, where the sampling function U is replaced by a hardware-accelerated ray-tracing engine such as NVIDIA RTX. In this configuration, the system retains its differentiable structure but leverages the speed and accuracy of hardware ray tracing to improve the fidelity of the surface sampling. This hybrid approach combines the efficiency of rasterization with the precision of ray tracing, enabling the rendering of complex scenes with accurate shadows, reflections, and global illumination—all while maintaining differentiability.

Furthermore, the method can be integrated with generative models such as diffusion models or generative adversarial networks to enable differentiable 3D synthesis. In this application, the scene parameters θ are generated by a neural network that takes a latent code as input and outputs a 3D shape, texture, and lighting configuration. The rasterize-then-splat pipeline renders the generated scene, and the resulting image is fed back into a discriminator or loss function that evaluates its realism. The gradients from this loss are then backpropagated through the entire pipeline to update the generator network, enabling end-to-end training of 3D generative models from 2D image data. This extension opens the door to novel applications in 3D content generation, digital twins, and synthetic data creation for training vision systems.

The method also supports the use of learned priors and regularization terms that are differentiable and integrated into the loss function. For example, a mesh regularization term based on Laplacian smoothing, edge length preservation, or as-rigid-as-possible deformation can be computed directly from the vertex positions and added to the loss. Similarly, a neural prior can be used to enforce smoothness in the density field or to constrain the output of the NeRF network to plausible appearance models. These priors are computed using differentiable operations and are automatically included in the gradient computation, enabling the optimization of geometry and appearance under complex constraints.

Finally, the method is designed to be extensible to new surface representations with minimal effort. Any surface that can be sampled and interpolated using a closed-form expression can be integrated into the pipeline by implementing only the sampling and evaluation functions. This includes subdivision surfaces, point clouds with implicit reconstruction, level-set surfaces, and even procedural surfaces defined by mathematical functions. The method’s decoupled architecture ensures that the addition of a new representation does not require changes to the shading, splatting, or differentiation components, making it a scalable and future-proof foundation for differentiable rendering research.
