# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The manipulation of magnetic textures at the nanoscale has emerged as a cornerstone of modern spintronic and magneto-ionics technologies, where the control of spin orientation, domain wall dynamics, and topological spin structures such as skyrmions dictates device functionality and energy efficiency. Central to this control is the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI), an antisymmetric exchange interaction that arises in systems lacking inversion symmetry and imparts a preferred chirality to noncollinear magnetic spin arrangements. First theorized by Dzyaloshinskii in the context of weak ferromagnetism in antiferromagnetic crystals and later formalized by Moriya through a microscopic treatment of spin-orbit coupling in noncentrosymmetric lattices, the DMI has since become a fundamental mechanism underpinning the stabilization of chiral spin spirals, magnetic skyrmions, and domain walls with defined handedness. The energy contribution of the DMI, expressed as –D_ij · (S_i × S_j), where D_ij is the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya vector and S_i and S_j represent neighboring spin moments, directly determines the rotational sense—right-handed or left-handed—of the magnetic texture, while its magnitude governs the spatial period and stability of such structures against thermal fluctuations and external perturbations.

Historically, the most prominent experimental realizations of DMI have been confined to interfaces between ferromagnetic layers and heavy transition metals such as platinum, iridium, tungsten, tantalum, and palladium, where strong spin-orbit coupling and significant orbital hybridization between the 5d states of the heavy metal and the 3d states of the ferromagnet generate substantial interfacial DMI. These systems have enabled the observation of room-temperature skyrmions, the engineering of chiral domain walls for racetrack memory applications, and the development of low-power spin-transfer torque devices. The magnitude of DMI in these systems has been systematically correlated with the atomic number and electronic structure of the adjacent heavy metal, with DMI values ranging from approximately 0.05 to over 1.4 meV per atom, depending on the specific material combination and interfacial geometry. Notably, the DMI at ferromagnet/heavy metal interfaces is often attributed to the Rashba-Edelstein effect, wherein the broken inversion symmetry at the interface induces a momentum-dependent spin splitting that couples to the magnetic order via spin-orbit coupling, thereby generating an effective DMI vector perpendicular to the interface plane.

Despite the success of heavy metal-based systems, their practical deployment in scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable devices is hindered by the scarcity, high cost, and complex processing requirements associated with many of these elements. Consequently, significant research efforts have been directed toward identifying alternative materials that can induce comparable DMI magnitudes without relying on rare or expensive elements. One promising avenue involves the use of light elements, such as graphene or hydrogen, which have been shown to induce measurable DMI through mechanisms involving charge transfer, orbital hybridization, or surface reconstruction. For instance, the observation of left-handed DMI at the graphene/cobalt interface was attributed to the Rashba effect induced by the asymmetry in the electronic environment created by the carbon lattice, yielding a DMI of approximately 0.16 meV per atom. Similarly, hydrogenation-induced skyrmion formation in iron films on iridium substrates demonstrated that even atomic-scale modifications to the interfacial electronic structure can significantly alter magnetic chirality. However, these examples remain exceptional in their magnitude and reproducibility, and no prior experimental demonstration has established a light element capable of inducing a DMI comparable in strength to that of heavy transition metals under ambient conditions.

Of particular theoretical interest is the role of oxygen, a highly electronegative element with a Pauling electronegativity of 3.44, in modifying interfacial magnetic interactions. Oxygen chemisorption on ferromagnetic surfaces such as iron, nickel, and cobalt has long been known to alter magnetic anisotropy, coercivity, and Curie temperature, primarily through charge transfer and the formation of localized surface dipoles. Recent theoretical predictions have suggested that the large electronegativity mismatch between oxygen and 3d ferromagnets—whose electronegativities range from approximately 1.8 to 1.9—induces a strong electric dipole moment at the oxygen-metal interface, which in turn couples to the spin-orbit interaction and generates a significant DMI. This mechanism, distinct from the conventional Rashba effect observed in heavy metal interfaces, arises not from band structure asymmetry due to heavy atom orbitals, but from the direct electrostatic polarization induced by electron density redistribution across the interface. The predicted DMI magnitude from such a dipole-driven mechanism was estimated to be on the order of 0.5 to 1.0 meV per atom, comparable to the strongest known interfacial DMI values. Yet, despite the theoretical plausibility and the abundance of oxygen as a naturally occurring element, no experimental study had previously demonstrated the direct, measurable, and tunable induction of DMI by chemisorbed oxygen at room temperature, nor had any system been shown to exploit this effect to actively reconfigure the topology of magnetic skyrmions or domain walls.

Moreover, prior experimental approaches to quantify DMI have relied heavily on layer-thickness-dependent transitions between Néel and Bloch domain wall configurations, or on the comparison of chirality switching upon the addition of a new interface to a system with a known DMI sign. These methods, while robust, require precise control over film growth, epitaxial alignment, and interfacial roughness, and are often limited to systems where the DMI contribution from each interface can be isolated and independently varied. The absence of a generalizable experimental platform capable of unambiguously distinguishing and quantifying the DMI contribution of an unknown interface—especially one involving a light element with weak intrinsic spin-orbit coupling—has hindered progress in identifying and characterizing novel DMI-inducing interfaces. The development of such a platform, particularly one that enables the reversible and controllable switching of magnetic chirality through a non-metallic, voltage-tunable agent like oxygen, would open new pathways for the design of next-generation spintronic devices that are not only scalable and low-cost but also dynamically reconfigurable through chemical or electrochemical means.

This invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel class of magnetic heterostructures in which chemisorbed oxygen serves as a highly effective, room-temperature DMI-inducing agent, capable of generating interfacial DMI magnitudes rivaling those of heavy transition metals. The invention further provides a tunable, epitaxial multilayer platform composed of nickel, cobalt, palladium, and tungsten that enables the precise control of the net DMI sign and magnitude through the variation of a buried spacer layer thickness, thereby allowing the unambiguous quantification of the oxygen-induced DMI contribution. Through direct imaging of domain wall chirality using spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy, the invention demonstrates that oxygen chemisorption on nickel-cobalt bilayers can reverse the handedness of magnetic textures when the underlying DMI is left-handed, and that the magnitude of this effect is sufficient to stabilize and reconfigure skyrmions with defined winding numbers at ambient conditions. The physical origin of this phenomenon is linked to the strong electric dipole formed at the oxygen-metal interface due to the electronegativity contrast, providing the first experimental validation of the theoretical connection between DMI and interfacial dipole moments. This discovery not only establishes oxygen as a viable, earth-abundant alternative to noble metals in DMI-based spintronic devices but also introduces a new paradigm for dynamic magnetic control through surface chemistry, paving the way for magnetically reprogrammable materials that respond to environmental stimuli such as gas exposure, voltage bias, or thermal treatment.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel method and system for inducing, controlling, and quantifying the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction in magnetic heterostructures through the chemisorption of oxygen on ferromagnetic surfaces at room temperature. The invention demonstrates that oxygen, despite its low atomic number and absence of intrinsic spin-orbit coupling, can generate a Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction of substantial magnitude—comparable to that induced by heavy transition metals such as platinum, iridium, and palladium—when adsorbed as a monolayer on the surface of a ferromagnetic layer such as nickel or cobalt. This oxygen-induced DMI is not a secondary or incidental effect but a primary, dominant contribution to the overall magnetic chirality of the system, capable of reversing the handedness of domain walls and reconfiguring the internal structure of magnetic skyrmions. The invention further provides a tunable multilayer platform comprising a ferromagnetic bilayer of nickel and cobalt deposited on a palladium spacer layer grown epitaxially on a tungsten substrate, wherein the thickness of the palladium layer is precisely controlled to tune the net DMI from a right-handed (tungsten-like) to a left-handed (palladium-like) state. This tunability enables the unambiguous determination of the sign and magnitude of the oxygen-induced DMI by observing the critical oxygen coverage at which the domain wall chirality switches direction, thereby establishing a quantitative and reproducible method for measuring the DMI contribution of any interfacial modification.

The invention achieves this through the discovery that chemisorbed oxygen atoms bind preferentially to threefold hollow sites on the nickel surface, inducing a strong electric dipole moment perpendicular to the interface due to the large electronegativity difference between oxygen and nickel (3.44 versus 1.91 on the Pauling scale). This dipole moment couples with the spin-orbit interaction present in the ferromagnetic layer, generating an antisymmetric exchange interaction that favors a right-handed chirality in the magnetic domain walls and skyrmions. The magnitude of this oxygen-induced DMI is measured to be approximately 0.63 ± 0.26 meV per atom for a full monolayer of oxygen coverage, a value that exceeds the DMI generated by graphene on cobalt by a factor of four and is comparable to the DMI generated by platinum on nickel or palladium on cobalt. This magnitude is sufficient to stabilize chiral spin textures in ultrathin ferromagnetic films as thin as three nanometers and to drive complete transitions between left-handed, achiral, and right-handed domain wall configurations under ambient conditions without the need for cryogenic temperatures or external magnetic fields.

The invention further demonstrates that the oxygen-induced DMI is not merely a surface effect but is sufficiently strong to propagate through the ferromagnetic bilayer and influence the magnetic texture of the entire stack, enabling the control of skyrmion topology in isotropic systems such as [Co/Ni]_3/Cu(111), where the absence of uniaxial anisotropy permits the observation of pure topological transitions from hedgehog-type to Bloch-type skyrmions. This transition is driven solely by the oxygen-induced DMI, confirming that the effect is intrinsic to the oxygen-metal interface and not an artifact of strain or anisotropy. The invention further shows that the oxygen coverage can be precisely controlled through dosing in ultrahigh vacuum conditions, allowing for continuous, reversible tuning of the DMI magnitude, and that the resulting magnetic configurations are stable for extended periods under ambient conditions, indicating that the oxygen chemisorption is robust and not easily desorbed at room temperature.

The physical mechanism underlying this phenomenon is fundamentally distinct from conventional DMI generation mechanisms. Unlike heavy metal interfaces, where DMI arises from strong spin-orbit coupling in the 5d orbitals of the adjacent element, the oxygen-induced DMI originates from an electric dipole moment created by charge transfer from the ferromagnetic metal to the oxygen adatom. This dipole moment generates an effective Rashba-like field that couples to the spin-orbit interaction within the ferromagnet, producing an antisymmetric exchange interaction whose direction and magnitude are determined by the orientation and strength of the dipole. This mechanism is supported by experimental observations of a significant increase in the work function of the system upon oxygen adsorption, consistent with the formation of a surface dipole, and by density functional theory calculations that confirm the correlation between the oxygen-induced charge redistribution and the emergence of a non-zero Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya vector. The invention thus provides the first experimental validation of the theoretical prediction that DMI can be directly linked to interfacial electric dipole moments, opening a new design principle for magnetic materials in which chirality is controlled not by atomic mass or orbital complexity, but by chemical potential and electronegativity contrast.

The practical implications of this invention are profound. By replacing rare and expensive heavy metals with abundant, inexpensive oxygen, the invention enables the fabrication of low-cost, scalable spintronic devices such as skyrmion-based memory, racetrack memory elements, and spin-torque oscillators without reliance on critical materials. Furthermore, because oxygen adsorption and desorption can be controlled electrochemically or thermally, the invention enables the development of magnetically reconfigurable devices that can be dynamically tuned in situ through voltage pulses, gas exposure, or thermal cycling. This introduces a new class of magneto-ionics devices in which magnetic topology is controlled by ion migration or surface chemistry, rather than by current-driven spin-transfer torque or external magnetic fields. The invention further enables the design of multi-layered architectures in which the DMI sign and magnitude can be locally patterned through selective oxygen exposure, permitting the creation of complex magnetic landscapes for neuromorphic computing, logic gates, or topological data storage. The method of quantifying DMI through chirality switching in a tunable background system provides a generalizable experimental protocol that can be applied to any interface where a new chemical species is introduced, making it a powerful tool for the discovery and characterization of novel DMI-inducing materials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a magnetic heterostructure and method for inducing, controlling, and quantifying the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction through the chemisorption of oxygen on ferromagnetic surfaces at ambient conditions. The invention comprises a multilayered architecture in which a ferromagnetic bilayer of nickel and cobalt is deposited on a palladium spacer layer, which in turn is epitaxially grown on a tungsten substrate. This architecture is specifically engineered to enable the precise tuning of the net Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction by varying the thickness of the palladium layer, thereby creating a platform in which the intrinsic chirality of the magnetic domain walls can be reversibly switched between left-handed and right-handed states. The introduction of chemisorbed oxygen onto the top surface of the nickel layer then acts as a powerful, tunable perturbation that can override the underlying DMI of the system, inducing a new chirality that is quantitatively measurable and reproducible. This approach allows for the first time the direct determination of the magnitude and sign of the DMI induced by oxygen, which is found to be comparable in strength to the most potent heavy metal interfaces known in the field of spintronics.

The multilayer structure is fabricated under ultrahigh vacuum conditions to ensure atomic-level cleanliness and epitaxial growth. The tungsten substrate, oriented along the [110] crystallographic direction, is chosen for its ability to support high-quality epitaxial growth of face-centered cubic metals such as palladium, nickel, and cobalt, while simultaneously providing a strong uniaxial strain that aligns the magnetic easy axis along the W[001] direction. This alignment is critical for the observation of well-defined, linear domain walls whose chirality can be unambiguously resolved using spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy. The palladium layer is deposited in a layer-by-layer fashion, with its thickness controlled to within a fraction of a monolayer using in situ reflection high-energy electron diffraction and low-energy electron microscopy. The thickness of the palladium layer is varied systematically from approximately 1.5 to 3.5 monolayers, during which the net DMI of the system transitions from a right-handed (tungsten-like) configuration to a left-handed (palladium-like) configuration. This transition is not abrupt but occurs gradually over a narrow range of thicknesses, indicating that the DMI contribution from the tungsten and palladium interfaces is additive and that the palladium layer acts as a tunable mediator between the two opposing DMI sources.

The nickel and cobalt bilayer is deposited on top of the palladium layer, with the nickel layer serving as the primary surface for oxygen chemisorption and the cobalt layer providing strong perpendicular magnetic anisotropy and high magnetic moment density. The thickness of the nickel layer is maintained at approximately one monolayer, while the cobalt layer is maintained at three monolayers, ensuring that the magnetic properties are dominated by the interfacial region where oxygen adsorption occurs. The bilayer is grown epitaxially on the palladium layer, preserving the lattice matching and minimizing interfacial disorder. The entire structure is designed to be magnetically perpendicularly anisotropic, which is essential for the formation of chiral domain walls and skyrmions that are stabilized by the competition between DMI, exchange stiffness, and dipolar interactions. The perpendicular anisotropy is confirmed through hysteresis loop measurements and spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy, which reveal clear contrast between up and down magnetized domains.

The key innovation of the invention lies in the use of the palladium thickness as a continuous tuning parameter for the net DMI. When the palladium layer is thin—approximately 1.5 to 2.0 monolayers—the DMI contribution from the tungsten interface dominates, resulting in a right-handed chirality of the domain walls. As the palladium thickness increases beyond 2.2 monolayers, the DMI contribution from the palladium interface becomes dominant, and the chirality of the domain walls switches to left-handed. This switching is not due to a change in magnetic anisotropy or exchange stiffness, as confirmed by the absence of significant changes in coercivity or saturation magnetization across the thickness range, but is solely attributable to the reversal of the net DMI vector. The transition is observed with high precision using spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy, which provides real-space imaging of the domain wall magnetization vector with sub-nanometer resolution. The domain wall chirality is quantified by analyzing the angle α between the domain wall normal and the in-plane magnetization component, with right-handed chirality corresponding to α ≈ 0° and left-handed chirality to α ≈ 180°. The distribution of α values across multiple domain walls in each sample is compiled into histograms, which show a clear bimodal distribution that shifts from one peak to the other as the palladium thickness is varied, confirming the continuous and reversible nature of the DMI tuning.

Once the DMI tuning platform is established, oxygen chemisorption is introduced by controlled exposure to high-purity oxygen gas at pressures ranging from 5 × 10⁻⁹ to 1 × 10⁻⁸ torr, with exposure times calibrated to achieve oxygen coverages between 0.05 and 0.30 monolayers. The oxygen coverage is determined through a combination of work function measurements, low-energy electron diffraction, and kinetic modeling based on previously established adsorption isotherms for oxygen on nickel. The work function of the system increases by approximately 0.7 eV upon oxygen adsorption, consistent with the formation of a surface dipole oriented perpendicular to the interface, with the negative charge localized on the oxygen adatoms and the positive charge on the underlying nickel atoms. This dipole is confirmed to be stable at room temperature, with no measurable decay over periods exceeding 72 hours under ultrahigh vacuum conditions, indicating that the oxygen is chemisorbed rather than physisorbed and that the interaction is strong enough to resist thermal desorption.

The introduction of oxygen onto the nickel surface has a dramatic and predictable effect on the domain wall chirality. When the underlying DMI of the system is left-handed—achieved with a palladium thickness of 2.6 monolayers or greater—the addition of oxygen induces a right-handed chirality, and at a critical oxygen coverage of approximately 0.24 monolayers, the domain wall chirality completely switches from left-handed to right-handed. This switching is not gradual or partial but occurs abruptly, indicating that the oxygen-induced DMI is strong enough to overcome the intrinsic DMI of the multilayer system. In contrast, when the underlying DMI is right-handed—achieved with a palladium thickness below 2.2 monolayers—the addition of oxygen does not induce a chirality switch, but instead enhances the existing right-handed chirality, leading to a narrowing of the domain wall width and an increase in the domain wall energy. This asymmetry in response confirms that the oxygen-induced DMI has a fixed sign—right-handed—and that its magnitude is sufficient to compete with, but not reverse, a pre-existing right-handed DMI. The fact that the oxygen-induced DMI can only reverse a left-handed DMI, but not a right-handed one, provides unambiguous evidence that the oxygen interaction is not merely an additive perturbation but a directional, symmetry-breaking interaction with a well-defined vector orientation.

The magnitude of the oxygen-induced DMI is quantified by comparing the change in DMI induced by a known variation in palladium thickness to the change induced by oxygen adsorption. A variation in palladium thickness of 1.0 monolayer corresponds to a DMI change of 0.41 ± 0.17 meV per atom, as determined from the energy difference between the Néel and Bloch domain wall configurations, calculated using the dipolar energy constant derived from the magnetic moments of nickel and cobalt and the in-plane lattice parameter. A variation in palladium thickness of 0.37 monolayers, which corresponds to the thickness range over which the chirality switch occurs in the presence of oxygen, induces a DMI change of 0.15 ± 0.06 meV per atom. This same DMI change is induced by the adsorption of 0.24 monolayers of oxygen. By scaling this relationship to a full monolayer of oxygen coverage, the oxygen-induced DMI is calculated to be 0.63 ± 0.26 meV per atom. This value is comparable to the DMI values reported for platinum on nickel (1.05 meV/atom), palladium on cobalt (1.44 meV/atom), and iridium on cobalt (0.36 meV/atom), and is approximately four times larger than the DMI induced by graphene on cobalt (0.16 meV/atom). This demonstrates that oxygen, despite its low atomic number and lack of heavy element spin-orbit coupling, is capable of inducing a DMI that rivals or exceeds those of many heavy transition metals.

The invention further demonstrates that the oxygen-induced DMI is not confined to the Ni/Co/Pd/W system but is a general phenomenon that can be observed in other magnetic architectures. In a control experiment, a [Co/Ni]_3/Cu(111) multilayer, which lacks any heavy metal and therefore has negligible intrinsic DMI, is exposed to 0.21 monolayers of oxygen. In this system, the domain walls transition from a left-handed chiral Néel configuration to an achiral Bloch configuration, indicating that the oxygen-induced DMI is strong enough to dominate the magnetic texture even in the absence of any other DMI source. This observation confirms that the oxygen-metal interface itself is the primary source of the DMI, and that the effect is intrinsic to the oxygen chemisorption process and not an artifact of the underlying substrate or spacer layer. The transition to a Bloch wall in this system is accompanied by an increase in domain wall width, consistent with the suppression of the DMI-driven Néel component and the dominance of dipolar interactions, further validating the role of oxygen in modifying the energy landscape of the magnetic texture.

The physical origin of the oxygen-induced DMI is attributed to the formation of a strong electric dipole at the oxygen-metal interface due to the large electronegativity difference between oxygen and nickel or cobalt. The electronegativity of oxygen is 3.44, while that of nickel and cobalt is approximately 1.91 and 1.88, respectively, resulting in a significant electron density transfer from the metal to the oxygen adatom. This charge transfer creates a surface dipole moment oriented perpendicular to the interface, with the negative end on the oxygen and the positive end on the metal. This dipole generates an effective electric field that couples to the spin-orbit interaction within the ferromagnetic layer, inducing a Rashba-type spin splitting that is asymmetric in momentum space. This spin splitting, in turn, leads to an antisymmetric exchange interaction between neighboring spins, manifesting as a Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction. The direction of the DMI vector is determined by the orientation of the dipole and the crystal symmetry of the interface, resulting in a right-handed chirality that is consistent across all observed systems. This mechanism is distinct from the conventional DMI generation in heavy metal interfaces, which relies on strong spin-orbit coupling in the 5d orbitals of the adjacent element, and instead relies on the electrostatic polarization of the interface. The validity of this mechanism is supported by density functional theory calculations, which show a clear correlation between the oxygen-induced charge redistribution, the magnitude of the surface dipole moment, and the emergence of a non-zero DMI vector in the first-principles simulations. The calculations further confirm that the DMI vector is oriented perpendicular to the interface and that its magnitude scales linearly with the oxygen coverage up to one monolayer, beyond which saturation occurs due to site filling and repulsive interactions between oxygen adatoms.

The invention further demonstrates that the oxygen-induced DMI is sufficiently strong to control the internal structure of magnetic skyrmions. In the Ni(1 ML)/Co(3 ML)/Pd(2.6 ML)/W(110) system, the application of oxygen coverage leads to a complete transition of the domain wall type within a magnetic bubble from left-handed to right-handed, with an intermediate achiral state observed at intermediate coverages. The deformation of the bubble shape observed during this transition is attributed to the oxygen-induced change in perpendicular magnetic anisotropy, which is known to increase with oxygen coverage due to the enhanced spin-orbit coupling and reduced magnetic moment at the surface. However, the chirality transition is independent of this shape change and is solely attributable to the DMI. In an isotropic [Co/Ni]_3/Cu(111) system, where no uniaxial anisotropy is present, the oxygen-induced DMI drives a transition of a left-handed hedgehog-type skyrmion to a Bloch-type skyrmion as the oxygen coverage increases. This transition is characterized by a gradual rotation of the spin texture from a radial configuration to a tangential one, with the winding number preserved but the internal structure altered. This demonstrates that the oxygen-induced DMI can be used not only to control domain wall chirality but also to engineer the topological configuration of skyrmions, which has direct implications for their stability, mobility, and response to spin currents.

The invention further enables the development of magnetically reconfigurable devices through the integration of oxygen adsorption with electrochemical or voltage-controlled oxygen migration. Because oxygen chemisorption is reversible and can be induced or removed through controlled exposure to oxygen gas or through electrochemical oxidation and reduction, the DMI of the system can be dynamically tuned in situ. This opens the door to the design of devices in which the magnetic topology is not fixed during fabrication but can be rewritten through chemical or electrical inputs. For example, a memory cell could be designed in which the presence or absence of oxygen at a specific location determines the chirality of the domain wall, thereby encoding a binary state. The oxygen could be introduced or removed through a solid-state electrolyte layer, enabling non-volatile, low-power, and high-density magnetic memory with write speeds limited only by the kinetics of oxygen diffusion. Similarly, logic gates could be constructed in which the DMI sign is switched by applying a voltage pulse that drives oxygen ions to or from a critical interface, altering the output of a spin-torque oscillator or a spin valve. The invention thus provides not only a new material system for generating DMI but also a new paradigm for magnetic device design in which the magnetic state is controlled by surface chemistry rather than by current or magnetic field.

The scalability and manufacturability of the invention are further enhanced by the use of standard thin-film deposition techniques such as sputtering, molecular beam epitaxy, and atomic layer deposition, all of which are compatible with industrial semiconductor fabrication processes. The oxygen exposure can be integrated into existing vacuum processing lines, and the control of oxygen coverage can be achieved through precise gas dosing systems or electrochemical cells. The system does not require cryogenic temperatures, high magnetic fields, or exotic materials, making it suitable for commercial deployment. The stability of the oxygen-induced DMI at room temperature, its reproducibility across multiple samples, and its robustness against minor variations in film thickness and surface roughness further support its viability for practical applications.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive and novel method for generating, controlling, and quantifying the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction through the chemisorption of oxygen on ferromagnetic surfaces. It establishes oxygen as a powerful and practical alternative to heavy transition metals for inducing strong interfacial DMI, demonstrates a generalizable experimental platform for measuring unknown DMI contributions, and reveals a new physical mechanism linking DMI to interfacial electric dipole moments. The invention enables the dynamic reconfiguration of magnetic textures at room temperature, opens new pathways for magneto-ionics and spintronic device design, and provides a foundation for the development of low-cost, scalable, and environmentally sustainable magnetic technologies.
