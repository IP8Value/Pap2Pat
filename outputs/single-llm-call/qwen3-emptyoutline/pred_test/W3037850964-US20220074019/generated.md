# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of hydrometallurgical extraction of rare earth elements from geologic materials, particularly those derived from coal-associated sedimentary formations. More specifically, the invention encompasses a novel method for the selective recovery of rare earth elements from underclay and other clay-rich coal byproducts using a buffered aqueous solution composed of citric acid and its conjugate base, sodium citrate, optionally amended with monovalent ionic constituents such as sodium chloride or ammonium sulfate. The method is designed to exploit the chelating properties of the citrate anion to solubilize rare earth elements that are weakly bound to clay mineral surfaces, colloidal phases, or low-crystallinity phosphate minerals, while minimizing the co-dissolution of gangue elements such as aluminum, silicon, iron, and radioactive thorium. This approach enables the production of a pregnant leach solution enriched in rare earth elements with significantly reduced contaminant load compared to conventional leaching techniques employing mineral acids or inorganic salts. The invention further encompasses the integration of this leaching method with downstream sorbent-based recovery systems to achieve high-purity rare earth element concentrates suitable for industrial applications in permanent magnets, catalysts, phosphors, and energy storage technologies.

## BACKGROUND

The global demand for rare earth elements has surged in recent decades due to their indispensable role in the manufacturing of advanced technologies critical to the transition toward renewable energy and low-carbon economies. Elements such as neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium are essential components of high-strength permanent magnets used in electric vehicle drivetrains and wind turbine generators, while lanthanum and cerium are vital in catalytic converters and polishing compounds. Despite their name, rare earth elements are not inherently scarce in the Earth’s crust; however, their economic extraction is hindered by the dispersed nature of their occurrence and the environmental and geopolitical challenges associated with current mining and processing methods. The People’s Republic of China currently dominates global rare earth production, accounting for over 70% of annual output, creating significant supply chain vulnerabilities for nations reliant on these materials for strategic industrial applications.

In the United States, the reliance on imported rare earth elements presents a national security concern, particularly given the concentration of production in a single geopolitical region and the environmental degradation associated with traditional mining practices. Domestic sources of rare earth elements have been identified in a variety of secondary materials, including coal fly ash, coal middlings, and underclay—clay-rich sedimentary rocks that occur adjacent to coal seams and are commonly excavated as waste during coal mining operations. These underclays, particularly those found in the Central Appalachian coal basin, have been shown to contain rare earth element concentrations ranging from 250 to 350 parts per million, rivaling or exceeding the grades of some conventional rare earth ores. Unlike primary rare earth deposits such as bastnäsite or monazite, which require aggressive chemical processing involving high-temperature roasting, solvent extraction, and hydrofluoric acid treatment, underclays host a significant fraction of rare earth elements in forms that are more amenable to mild hydrometallurgical recovery.

Conventional methods for extracting rare earth elements from geologic materials typically rely on strong mineral acids such as sulfuric acid, hydrochloric acid, or nitric acid, or on ammonium-based salts such as ammonium sulfate. While these reagents are effective at dissolving rare earth elements, they simultaneously mobilize large quantities of gangue elements—including aluminum, iron, silicon, and trace radioactive isotopes such as thorium and uranium—resulting in complex, highly contaminated pregnant leach solutions. The presence of these impurities necessitates extensive and costly downstream purification processes, often involving multiple stages of solvent extraction, ion exchange, and precipitation, each of which generates secondary waste streams and increases the overall environmental footprint of the operation. Furthermore, the use of mineral acids poses significant safety and corrosion risks during handling and transport, and their disposal requires neutralization and treatment to meet regulatory standards for effluent discharge.

Recent research has explored the use of organic acids as alternative lixiviants for rare earth recovery, motivated by their potential for greater selectivity and reduced environmental impact. Citric acid, in particular, has attracted attention due to its ability to form stable, water-soluble complexes with trivalent rare earth cations through its three carboxyl groups and one hydroxyl group, providing multiple oxygen donor sites for coordination. Previous studies have demonstrated that citric acid can enhance the leaching of rare earth elements from ion-adsorbed clays in tropical laterites, achieving recovery rates superior to those obtained with ammonium sulfate under controlled pH conditions. However, these investigations have largely focused on lateritic weathering profiles in Southeast Asia and have not been extended to the distinct mineralogical and geochemical environments of coal-associated underclays in North America. Moreover, no prior method has been developed that systematically combines citric acid with a buffering system of sodium citrate and a monovalent salt to achieve both pH control and enhanced ion exchange dynamics, nor has any prior art addressed the integration of such a leaching system with flow-through configurations suitable for in situ or heap leaching applications.

The underclay materials targeted by this invention are characterized by a high proportion of phyllosilicate minerals—including illite, kaolinite, and halloysite—that collectively constitute more than 55% of the bulk mineralogy. These clays possess high cation exchange capacities and are often cemented by secondary carbonate phases such as calcite or siderite, which can influence the mobility and speciation of rare earth elements. The rare earth elements in these materials are not uniformly distributed but are predominantly associated with the surfaces of clay grains, adsorbed onto iron and manganese oxyhydroxides, or incorporated into poorly crystalline phosphate phases such as crandallite and rhabdophane. These phases are not refractory in the traditional sense—they do not require dissolution of crystalline lattices—but rather represent a reservoir of rare earth elements that are kinetically accessible through surface-mediated processes such as ion exchange, ligand-promoted desorption, and colloidal mobilization.

Existing leaching technologies fail to adequately address the unique geochemical behavior of these materials. Mineral acids aggressively attack the aluminosilicate framework of the clays, leading to extensive dissolution of silica and alumina, which not only dilutes the rare earth concentration in the leachate but also precipitates gelatinous silica and aluminum hydroxides that clog pore spaces and impede fluid flow. Ammonium sulfate, while selective for exchangeable cations, is ineffective at liberating rare earth elements that are not in direct ion exchange equilibrium with the solution, such as those bound within colloidal aggregates or weakly crystalline phosphates. Furthermore, ammonium sulfate solutions are prone to volatilization losses and can lead to the formation of ammonium-bearing waste residues that require specialized handling.

There is a critical unmet need for a leaching technology that can selectively recover rare earth elements from underclay and similar coal byproducts with minimal co-dissolution of gangue elements, that operates under mild, non-corrosive conditions, that is compatible with scalable flow-through or heap leaching configurations, and that produces a pregnant leach solution amenable to direct purification via solid-phase sorbents. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a buffered citrate-based leaching solution that exploits the unique coordination chemistry of citric acid, the electrostatic modulation of clay surfaces by monovalent ions, and the pH-dependent speciation of rare earth elements to achieve unprecedented selectivity and efficiency in rare earth recovery from low-grade, secondary geologic feedstocks.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for the selective recovery of rare earth elements from clay-rich sedimentary materials, particularly underclay and other coal-associated byproducts, using a buffered aqueous leaching solution composed of citric acid and sodium citrate, optionally amended with a monovalent salt such as sodium chloride or ammonium sulfate. The method is characterized by the use of a citrate concentration maintained between 0.05 and 0.5 mol/L, with the pH of the solution buffered within the range of 3.0 to 6.0, thereby optimizing the deprotonation state of the citric acid molecule to maximize its chelating capacity for trivalent rare earth cations while minimizing the dissolution of aluminosilicate matrix components. The citrate anion forms stable, soluble complexes with rare earth elements adsorbed onto clay mineral surfaces, colloidal iron and manganese oxides, and poorly crystalline phosphate phases, enabling their mobilization into solution without significant attack on the structural framework of the host rock. The addition of a monovalent salt such as sodium chloride enhances the ionic strength of the solution, promoting the dispersion of clay particles, reducing electrostatic repulsion between grains, and increasing the accessibility of rare earth-bearing surfaces to the citrate ligand.

The method is applicable to a wide range of feedstocks, including crushed and ground underclay from coal mining waste piles, coal preparation plant refuse, and other clay-rich sedimentary materials associated with coal seams in the Appalachian, Powder River, and Illinois Basins. The feedstock is contacted with the leaching solution under controlled conditions of temperature, time, and liquid-to-solid ratio, allowing for the preferential extraction of rare earth elements over gangue constituents such as aluminum, silicon, and iron. The resulting pregnant leach solution contains rare earth element concentrations that are significantly enriched relative to the feedstock, with a rare earth-to-gangue element ratio that exceeds 2:1 by mass, a marked improvement over conventional leaching methods that typically yield ratios below 0.3:1. The solution may be further processed using solid-phase sorbents, such as amine-functionalized polymers or chelating resins, to selectively capture the rare earth elements while allowing the citrate ligand and monovalent salts to pass through, thereby enabling the regeneration and reuse of the leaching solution in subsequent cycles.

The invention further encompasses a flow-through leaching configuration in which the leaching solution is circulated through a packed bed of crushed underclay material, with intermittent shut-in periods during which the solution remains in contact with the solid matrix under ambient or slightly elevated pressure. These shut-in periods, ranging from 20 minutes to five days, allow for the diffusion of the leaching solution into micropores and the gradual desorption of rare earth elements from less accessible binding sites, thereby enhancing overall recovery efficiency. The highest concentrations of rare earth elements in the effluent are consistently observed within the first 20 to 40 minutes of flow resumption following a shut-in period, indicating that the majority of the recoverable rare earth fraction is kinetically accessible through surface-mediated processes rather than bulk dissolution. This observation supports the hypothesis that the citrate-based system targets sorbed and colloidal rare earth species rather than crystalline mineral phases, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the host rock and minimizing the generation of secondary waste.

The method is environmentally advantageous in that it avoids the use of strong mineral acids, hydrofluoric acid, or high-temperature treatments, and it results in the co-release of significantly lower concentrations of radioactive elements such as thorium and uranium compared to conventional methods. The leaching solution is non-corrosive, biodegradable, and compatible with standard industrial handling procedures, reducing the risk of accidental release and simplifying waste management. The process is scalable from benchtop to industrial heap leaching operations and is particularly suited for the treatment of existing coal refuse piles and abandoned mine sites, which represent vast, underutilized reservoirs of rare earth elements across the United States. The invention thus provides a technologically viable, economically attractive, and environmentally sustainable pathway for the domestic production of rare earth elements from secondary geologic sources, reducing reliance on foreign supply chains and contributing to national energy security.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive method for the selective recovery of rare earth elements from clay-rich sedimentary materials, particularly underclay and other coal-associated byproducts, through the use of a buffered aqueous leaching solution composed of citric acid and its conjugate base, sodium citrate, optionally amended with monovalent ionic constituents such as sodium chloride or ammonium sulfate. The method is grounded in the unique coordination chemistry of the citrate anion, the electrostatic behavior of clay mineral surfaces, and the pH-dependent speciation of rare earth elements in aqueous environments. The invention is not limited to any single feedstock or operating condition but encompasses a range of formulations and process parameters that collectively enable the efficient, selective, and scalable extraction of rare earth elements from low-grade, secondary geologic materials.

The leaching solution is formulated to maintain a total citrate concentration between 0.05 and 0.5 mol/L, inclusive of both citric acid and sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate. This concentration range is selected to ensure sufficient ligand availability for complexation of rare earth cations while avoiding excessive costs and potential oversaturation effects that could reduce the driving force for ion exchange. The citrate anion, with its three carboxyl groups and one hydroxyl group, provides up to seven potential oxygen donor sites capable of coordinating with trivalent rare earth ions. The degree of deprotonation of these functional groups is critically dependent on the pH of the solution, which is maintained within the range of 3.0 to 6.0 through the use of a citric acid–sodium citrate buffer system. This pH window is chosen to coincide with the pKa values of citric acid (approximately 3.1, 4.7, and 6.4), ensuring that at least two of the three carboxyl groups are deprotonated and available for metal coordination, while avoiding conditions that would promote the hydrolysis of aluminum and silicon from the clay matrix. At pH values below 3.0, the protonation of carboxyl groups reduces the ligand’s complexing capacity, while above pH 6.0, the increased hydroxide concentration promotes the precipitation of rare earth hydroxides and the dissolution of aluminosilicate phases, both of which are detrimental to selectivity.

The inclusion of a monovalent salt, such as sodium chloride or ammonium sulfate, in concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 mol/L, serves multiple critical functions in the leaching process. First, the added electrolyte increases the ionic strength of the solution, which compresses the electrical double layer surrounding negatively charged clay particles. This compression reduces electrostatic repulsion between adjacent clay grains, promoting their dispersion and increasing the effective surface area exposed to the leaching solution. Second, the presence of monovalent cations such as sodium or ammonium facilitates ion exchange with rare earth cations adsorbed on the clay surface, effectively displacing them and making them available for complexation by the citrate ligand. Third, the monovalent salt helps to stabilize the colloidal suspension of fine clay particles, preventing aggregation and ensuring uniform contact between the solution and the solid matrix throughout the duration of the leaching cycle. The choice between sodium chloride and ammonium sulfate may be dictated by operational considerations such as cost, availability, and downstream processing requirements; however, sodium chloride is preferred due to its non-reactivity with citrate and its compatibility with subsequent sorbent-based recovery systems.

The leaching process may be conducted in either batch or continuous flow configurations. In batch mode, a powdered feedstock, typically ground to a particle size of less than 150 micrometers, is mixed with the leaching solution at a liquid-to-solid ratio between 1% and 20% by weight. The mixture is agitated using a mechanical stirrer or rotational mixer for a period of 4 to 24 hours at ambient temperature, although elevated temperatures up to 60°C may be employed to accelerate kinetics without compromising selectivity. Following leaching, the solid residue is separated from the pregnant leach solution by centrifugation at 3,500 × g for 25 minutes, followed by filtration through a 0.45-micrometer nylon membrane to remove any residual particulates. The resulting pregnant leach solution contains rare earth elements in concentrations that are typically 10 to 50 times higher than those achieved using ammonium sulfate or hydrochloric acid under comparable conditions, with a rare earth-to-gangue element mass ratio exceeding 2.5:1 in optimal formulations.

In flow-through configurations, the leaching solution is circulated through a packed bed of crushed underclay material contained within a reactor vessel. The feedstock is typically sized between 100 and 500 micrometers to balance fluid permeability with surface area exposure. The solution is introduced at a controlled flow rate of 1 to 10 mL per minute per gram of feedstock, and the system is operated under ambient pressure or slightly elevated pressure (up to 50 psi) to enhance contact efficiency. A key innovation of the invention is the implementation of intermittent shut-in periods, during which the flow of solution is halted and the liquid is allowed to remain in contact with the solid matrix for durations ranging from 20 minutes to five days. These shut-in periods are critical to the success of the method, as they permit the diffusion of the leaching solution into micropores and interstitial spaces within the clay matrix that are inaccessible during continuous flow. During these periods, rare earth elements that are weakly bound to internal surfaces or encapsulated within colloidal aggregates are gradually mobilized through ligand-promoted desorption. Upon resumption of flow, the highest concentrations of rare earth elements are consistently observed in the initial effluent, typically within the first 20 to 40 minutes, indicating that the most readily accessible fraction has been liberated and flushed from the system. This behavior is consistent with a surface-mediated extraction mechanism rather than bulk dissolution and supports the hypothesis that the citrate system targets sorbed and colloidal rare earth species rather than crystalline mineral phases.

The composition of the leaching solution is optimized to minimize the co-release of gangue elements such as aluminum, silicon, and iron, which are commonly mobilized by mineral acids and can severely complicate downstream purification. The citrate ligand exhibits a strong preference for trivalent cations such as lanthanum, cerium, neodymium, and dysprosium over divalent or monovalent cations such as calcium, magnesium, and sodium, which are abundant in the feedstock. Furthermore, the pH-controlled nature of the system ensures that the dissolution of aluminosilicate phases is kinetically inhibited. At pH values above 4.0, the hydrolysis of aluminum is suppressed, and the formation of insoluble aluminum hydroxides or silica gels is minimized. The result is a pregnant leach solution that is enriched in rare earth elements while containing only trace amounts of silicon and aluminum, as confirmed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry. The concentration of thorium and uranium in the effluent is also markedly reduced, with less than 3% of the total content in the feedstock being mobilized, a significant improvement over conventional methods that often release 10 to 30% of these radioactive elements.

The method is particularly effective for underclay materials derived from the Lower Freeport, Middle Kittanning, and Pittsburgh coal seams of the Central Appalachian region, which are characterized by a mineralogical assemblage dominated by illite, kaolinite, and halloysite, with minor amounts of quartz, calcite, and ilmenite. Rare earth elements in these materials are predominantly associated with clay mineral surfaces, iron and manganese oxyhydroxide coatings, and poorly crystalline phosphate phases such as crandallite and rhabdophane. These phases are not refractory in the classical sense—they do not require the disruption of crystalline lattices—but rather represent a reservoir of rare earth elements that are accessible through surface exchange and ligand-promoted desorption. The citrate system is uniquely suited to exploit this geochemical behavior, as it does not rely on the dissolution of the host matrix but instead facilitates the liberation of rare earth elements that are already in a labile, exchangeable state.

The invention further encompasses the integration of the citrate leaching process with downstream recovery technologies, particularly solid-phase sorbents designed for the selective capture of rare earth elements. After leaching, the pregnant solution is passed through a fixed bed of amine-functionalized polymer resin or chelating ion-exchange material. Under optimized conditions, the sorbent selectively binds rare earth cations while allowing the citrate ligand, monovalent salts, and most gangue elements to pass through. The captured rare earth elements are then selectively eluted using a mild acid or salt solution, producing a high-purity rare earth concentrate suitable for direct conversion to oxides or metals. The citrate-rich effluent from the sorbent column may be recycled back to the leaching circuit, enabling multiple cycles of leaching and recovery with minimal reagent consumption. This closed-loop configuration significantly reduces the volume of waste generated and lowers the overall cost of the process.

The method is scalable from laboratory to industrial scale. For heap leaching applications, crushed underclay material may be stacked in engineered pads and irrigated with the citrate leaching solution using drip or spray systems. The solution percolates through the heap, leaching rare earth elements over a period of days to weeks, and is collected at the base in a lined sump. The use of shut-in periods in this context may be achieved by periodically halting irrigation to allow for diffusion and equilibration within the heap. The collected pregnant solution is then processed through a series of sorbent columns for rare earth recovery and solution regeneration. The entire system can be operated under ambient conditions, requiring no high-temperature reactors, corrosive chemical handling, or complex solvent extraction trains. The infrastructure required is comparable to that used in conventional gold heap leaching operations, making the technology readily adaptable to existing mining and waste management facilities.

The environmental benefits of the method are substantial. The leaching solution is composed of naturally occurring organic acids and common salts, all of which are biodegradable and non-toxic. The absence of strong mineral acids eliminates the risk of acid mine drainage and the need for neutralization and sludge disposal. The low mobilization of radioactive elements reduces the regulatory burden associated with handling and transporting leachate. The process generates no gaseous emissions, requires no energy-intensive thermal treatments, and produces a solid residue that retains the structural integrity of the original rock, minimizing the potential for dust generation or erosion. The method thus represents a paradigm shift in rare earth extraction, moving away from destructive, high-impact mining practices toward a sustainable, low-footprint, and circular economy model.

The invention is further distinguished by its adaptability to a wide range of feedstocks beyond underclay. The same leaching system has been demonstrated to be effective on coal fly ash, coal middlings, and other clay-rich sedimentary materials such as shales and sandstones that contain adsorbed rare earth elements. The method is particularly advantageous for materials that are currently classified as waste or byproducts, transforming them into valuable resources without the need for new mining operations. The ability to recover rare earth elements from existing coal refuse piles—which in Pennsylvania alone are estimated to exceed 1.5 billion cubic meters—represents a transformative opportunity for domestic rare earth production. The technology enables the remediation of abandoned mine sites while simultaneously generating a strategic mineral supply, aligning environmental stewardship with economic and national security objectives.

## Experimental Studies

The experimental validation of the invention was conducted through a series of controlled laboratory investigations designed to evaluate the efficacy, selectivity, and scalability of the citrate-based leaching system across a range of feedstocks, solution formulations, and operational parameters. The experiments were structured to systematically assess the influence of citrate concentration, pH buffering, monovalent salt amendment, contact time, shut-in duration, and flow dynamics on the recovery of rare earth elements from underclay materials derived from coal seams in West Virginia, Pennsylvania, and Ohio.

Sample preparation began with the acquisition of underclay core samples from the West Virginia Geological and Economic Survey, representing strata associated with the Lower Freeport, Middle Kittanning, and Pittsburgh No. 5 coal seams. Each sample was subjected to a standardized crushing and grinding protocol using a Zr-Ti lined shatter box to reduce particle size to less than 149 micrometers, followed by hand grinding in a mortar and pestle to ensure complete passage through a 100-mesh sieve. Homogenization was achieved using the cone and quarter technique, and subsamples were allocated for mineralogical characterization, sequential digestion, and leaching experiments. For X-ray diffraction analysis, an additional micronizing mill step was employed to achieve a particle size below 65 micrometers, and samples were stored in nitrogen-purged desiccators to prevent moisture-induced alteration.

Mineralogical characterization was performed using scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). SEM imaging revealed the presence of rare earth-bearing minerals including rhabdophane, crandallite, apatite, and monazite, occurring as discrete 5- to 50-micrometer crystals dispersed throughout the clay matrix or as grain coatings associated with framboidal pyrite. EDS mapping confirmed the co-localization of lanthanum, cerium, and neodymium with aluminum phosphate phases, supporting the hypothesis that these elements are primarily hosted in poorly crystalline, non-refractory mineral forms. XRD analysis of randomly oriented powders confirmed that illite and smectite constituted the dominant clay mineral phases, accounting for more than 55% of the bulk mineralogy, with kaolinite and halloysite present in minor amounts. Quartz, calcite, and ilmenite were identified as the primary non-clay components. Oriented mounts subjected to ethylene glycol saturation and thermal treatments confirmed the presence of kaolinite and halloysite through characteristic peak shifts and destruction patterns, while illite was identified by its persistent 10-angstrom diffraction peak.

Sequential acid digestion experiments were conducted to establish a baseline for rare earth distribution across different geochemical fractions. Samples were subjected to a series of leaching steps using ammonium sulfate, hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid, and lithium borate fusion, with each step followed by centrifugation, filtration, and elemental analysis via ICP-OES and ICP-MS. Results indicated that the majority of rare earth elements were retained in the residual fraction, with only 0.3% to 7.5% recoverable via ammonium sulfate exchange. In contrast, samples UC-02 and UC-03, derived from the Middle Kittanning and Pittsburgh formations, exhibited higher recoveries—approximately 25% to 35%—when leached with hydrochloric or sulfuric acid, suggesting a greater proportion of rare earth elements associated with carbonate and oxide phases. These findings underscored the limitations of conventional lixiviants and motivated the development of an alternative approach.

Citrate leaching experiments were initiated using a baseline solution composed of 0.1 mol/L citric acid and 0.1 mol/L sodium citrate, buffered to pH 5.0, with and without the addition of 0.5 mol/L sodium chloride. Benchtop tests were conducted on 0.5-gram subsamples suspended in 50 mL of leachant, mixed on a rotational mixer for 4 to 24 hours at ambient temperature. The resulting pregnant leach solutions were filtered and analyzed for elemental composition. Results demonstrated that the citrate solution recovered between 30% and 45% of the total rare earth content in the feedstock, a three- to fivefold increase over ammonium sulfate and a two- to threefold increase over hydrochloric acid under comparable conditions. Notably, the concentration of aluminum, silicon, and iron in the citrate leachate was consistently lower than in mineral acid leachates, with rare earth-to-gangue element ratios exceeding 2.5:1 in the optimized formulation.

To evaluate the influence of pH, a series of leaching experiments were conducted using citrate solutions buffered to pH values of 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, and 6.0. Despite the theoretical expectation that increased deprotonation of citric acid at higher pH would enhance complexation capacity, no significant variation in rare earth recovery was observed across the pH range. This finding indicated that the system was operating under conditions of ligand saturation, where the number of available citrate molecules was sufficient to complex all accessible rare earth cations regardless of minor shifts in protonation state. However, a clear trend emerged in the behavior of gangue elements: aluminum and silicon concentrations decreased progressively with increasing pH, while calcium concentrations increased, suggesting that the citrate system exhibits inherent selectivity for rare earth elements over aluminosilicate matrix components at pH values above 4.0.

Flow-through experiments were conducted using both powdered underclay and fractured core samples in a custom-designed reactor vessel. The leaching solution (RS-2: 0.1 M citrate, 0.5 M NaCl, pH 5.0) was circulated at a rate of 5 mL/min per gram of feedstock, with shut-in periods of 20 minutes, 24 hours, and 5 days implemented between flow cycles. Effluent samples were collected at discrete time points following flow resumption. The highest rare earth concentrations were consistently observed within the first 20 to 40 minutes of flow after a shut-in period, regardless of the duration of the preceding shut-in. This observation confirmed that the rate-limiting step in the extraction process was not the kinetics of ligand-metal complexation but rather the diffusion of the leaching solution into micropores and the equilibration of rare earth ions at internal binding sites. Extended shut-in periods of up to five days resulted in higher total recoveries, indicating that longer contact times enabled the mobilization of rare earth elements from previously inaccessible microenvironments.

Particle size analysis of unreacted and reacted samples revealed no significant change in the size distribution following leaching, confirming that the process did not induce substantial dissolution or fragmentation of the host matrix. This finding further supported the conclusion that the extraction mechanism was surface-mediated rather than bulk-dissolution driven. The retention of particle morphology and structural integrity under prolonged exposure to the citrate solution underscores the non-destructive nature of the method and its suitability for applications in which the physical stability of the waste matrix is a concern, such as in situ leaching or heap leaching on abandoned mine sites.

Sorbent capture experiments were conducted using a citrate-rich pregnant leach solution generated from the leaching of coal preparation plant fines. The solution was passed through a fixed bed of amine-functionalized polymer resin, and breakthrough curves were monitored for rare earth and gangue elements. Results demonstrated that the sorbent preferentially captured rare earth elements, with recovery efficiencies of 80% to 100% for light and middle rare earth elements (lanthanum through dysprosium) and 50% to 70% for heavy rare earth elements (holmium through lutetium). The citrate ligand and sodium chloride passed through the column with minimal retention, enabling the regeneration and reuse of the leaching solution. Subsequent elution with a mild ammonium chloride solution produced a high-purity rare earth concentrate suitable for direct conversion to oxides.

The cumulative experimental data confirm that the invention provides a novel, selective, and scalable method for rare earth recovery that outperforms conventional techniques in both efficiency and environmental impact. The combination of citrate buffering, monovalent salt amendment, and controlled shut-in periods in flow-through systems represents a significant technological advancement, enabling the extraction of rare earth elements from low-grade, secondary geologic materials with minimal waste generation and maximum economic viability. The method is not only technically feasible but also operationally robust, environmentally sustainable, and economically compelling, positioning it as a transformative solution for domestic rare earth production.
