# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The human brain is protected by a highly selective physiological barrier known as the blood-brain barrier, which strictly regulates the passage of molecules from the systemic circulation into the central nervous system. This barrier, formed by specialized endothelial cells sealed together by tight junctions, supported by pericytes and astrocytic end-feet, effectively prevents the entry of more than 98% of small-molecule therapeutics and nearly all large-molecule biologics, including antibodies, gene vectors, and neuroactive peptides. While this protective function is essential for maintaining neural homeostasis, it presents a formidable obstacle to the treatment of neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders that require targeted pharmacological intervention within specific brain regions. Conventional methods for bypassing this barrier—such as direct intracranial injection, intrathecal administration, or the use of chemical carriers that exploit endogenous transport systems—are either inherently invasive, carry substantial risks of tissue damage, or lack the spatial precision necessary to avoid off-target effects. These limitations are particularly acute when targeting deep subcortical structures such as the basal ganglia, which include the caudate nucleus, putamen, globus pallidus, and subthalamic nucleus. These regions are implicated in a wide spectrum of disorders, including Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, obsessive-compulsive disorder, addiction, and certain forms of schizophrenia, yet their anatomical depth and proximity to critical white matter tracts and cortical projections render them exceptionally vulnerable to collateral damage from invasive procedures.

Existing non-invasive approaches to drug delivery across the blood-brain barrier have historically relied on systemic administration of agents modified to enhance their permeability, such as lipophilic analogs or ligands that bind to receptor-mediated transport systems. However, these strategies are constrained by their pharmacological specificity and inability to achieve regional selectivity. As a result, therapeutic agents delivered via these methods often distribute diffusely throughout the brain, leading to unintended modulation of non-targeted circuits and increased risk of systemic or neurotoxic side effects. Alternative techniques involving osmotic disruption of the barrier using mannitol or other hyperosmotic agents have demonstrated some efficacy in clinical settings, but they lack spatial control and induce widespread, non-selective permeabilization, thereby compromising the integrity of the entire neurovascular unit. The absence of a method capable of achieving localized, reversible, and repeatable disruption of the blood-brain barrier with submillimeter precision has thus remained a critical unmet need in both clinical neurology and translational neuroscience.

Recent advances in focused ultrasound technology have introduced a promising paradigm for overcoming these limitations. By combining low-intensity pulsed ultrasound with intravenously administered microbubbles, it is now possible to transiently and reversibly increase the permeability of the blood-brain barrier in a highly localized region. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon involves the mechanical interaction between oscillating microbubbles and the endothelial lining of cerebral capillaries. When exposed to acoustic energy within a precisely focused ultrasound beam, microbubbles undergo stable cavitation—non-destructive, rhythmic expansion and contraction—which generates localized shear stresses sufficient to transiently loosen the tight junctions between endothelial cells without inducing cellular rupture or significant thermal damage. This process enables the passive diffusion of molecules that are normally excluded by the barrier, including therapeutics ranging from small molecules to large biologics such as monoclonal antibodies and viral vectors. Importantly, the spatial confinement of the ultrasound focus ensures that the effect is restricted to a well-defined volume, typically on the order of tens to hundreds of cubic millimeters, while leaving surrounding tissue unaffected. This capability renders the technique uniquely suited for targeting deep brain structures that are inaccessible to conventional delivery methods without surgical intervention.

Despite its promise, the translation of focused ultrasound-mediated blood-brain barrier disruption into a robust clinical tool has been hindered by several technical challenges. One of the most significant obstacles is the presence of the skull, which introduces substantial acoustic aberrations due to its heterogeneous density, variable thickness, and differing speed of sound relative to brain tissue. These aberrations distort the shape and position of the ultrasound focus, leading to mislocalization of the intended treatment volume and reduced efficacy. While phased-array transducer systems integrated within magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanners have been developed to compensate for these distortions through real-time phase correction, such systems are prohibitively expensive, immobile, and confined to specialized neurointerventional centers. Furthermore, reliance on MRI for both targeting and verification introduces logistical constraints that limit the feasibility of repeated treatments, particularly in outpatient or resource-limited settings. The need for a simplified, portable, and MRI-independent system capable of accurate targeting and real-time safety monitoring has therefore become a central imperative in the field.

Another critical challenge lies in the real-time assessment of treatment efficacy and safety. While histological and imaging-based verification of barrier opening is well established, these methods are inherently post-hoc and cannot be used to guide the procedure during its execution. The absence of an online feedback mechanism increases the risk of under-treatment, where insufficient barrier disruption occurs, or over-treatment, where excessive acoustic energy induces inertial cavitation—a violent, destructive form of bubble collapse associated with microhemorrhage and tissue injury. Although passive cavitation detection (PCD) has been proposed as a means of monitoring acoustic emissions generated by microbubble activity, its application has largely been confined to preclinical models and has not been rigorously validated in non-human primates, whose cranial anatomy more closely resembles that of humans. Moreover, the relationship between the spectral signatures of cavitation and the resulting extent of barrier opening has not been systematically characterized under clinically relevant conditions, leaving a critical gap in the ability to predict treatment outcomes based on real-time acoustic feedback.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel, integrated system and methodology for non-invasive, targeted, and MRI-independent blood-brain barrier disruption in deep subcortical brain structures. The invention combines a single-element, spherically focused ultrasound transducer operating at an intermediate frequency of 500 kHz with a stereotaxic targeting framework that enables precise spatial localization of the ultrasound focus without reliance on real-time imaging guidance. The system is further augmented by a real-time passive cavitation detection module that continuously monitors the acoustic emissions generated by microbubble oscillations, allowing for the differentiation between stable and inertial cavitation and providing immediate feedback on treatment safety. Crucially, the method has been validated in a non-human primate model, demonstrating high targeting accuracy, reproducibility, and safety across multiple sessions, with the ability to consistently open the blood-brain barrier in structures such as the caudate nucleus and putamen with submillimeter precision. The invention further establishes a quantitative relationship between acoustic parameters, cavitation signatures, and the spatial extent of barrier opening, enabling the development of predictive protocols for clinical translation. By eliminating the dependence on MRI for targeting and verification, this system represents a transformative advance in the field of neuromodulation and targeted drug delivery, offering the potential for widespread deployment in outpatient clinics, community hospitals, and research laboratories alike.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a non-invasive method and system for the targeted, localized, and reversible disruption of the blood-brain barrier in deep subcortical brain structures using focused ultrasound and microbubbles, without requiring real-time magnetic resonance imaging guidance. The invention enables the precise delivery of therapeutic agents to specific neural regions, including the caudate nucleus and putamen, with minimal off-target effects and without the need for surgical intervention. The method utilizes a single-element, spherically focused ultrasound transducer operating at a center frequency of approximately 500 kilohertz, which is configured to generate a focal region of sufficient spatial precision to target subcortical structures while minimizing the detrimental effects of skull-induced acoustic aberrations. The transducer is mounted on a stereotaxic manipulator that allows for the precise positioning of the ultrasound focus in three-dimensional space according to pre-acquired anatomical coordinates derived from structural imaging. A software-based targeting algorithm translates user-specified stereotaxic targets into corresponding manipulator settings, accounting for the geometric configuration of the transducer, the orientation of the skull, and the expected focal shift induced by cranial bone. This algorithm enables the prediction of the ultrasound focus location with high fidelity, and further provides multiple potential configurations for achieving the same target from different approach angles, thereby optimizing the acoustic path through the skull.

The method further incorporates the systemic administration of monodisperse microbubbles, sized between four and five micrometers in diameter, which are introduced intravenously immediately prior to sonication. These microbubbles serve as acoustic contrast agents that, when exposed to the focused ultrasound field, undergo stable cavitation, thereby inducing transient mechanical perturbations at the level of the cerebral microvasculature. These perturbations result in the reversible opening of tight junctions between endothelial cells, permitting the extravasation of circulating therapeutic agents into the brain parenchyma. The duration of barrier opening is sufficient to allow for the delivery of a wide range of molecules, including small-molecule drugs, peptides, and viral vectors, and is confirmed through post-procedural contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging using gadodiamide. The method further includes a real-time passive cavitation detection system that monitors the acoustic emissions generated by the microbubbles during sonication. This system employs a hydrophone positioned coaxially with the ultrasound transducer to capture backscattered signals, which are then processed to extract the harmonic and broadband energy components of the cavitation spectrum. A harmonic energy increase exceeding fifteen decibels above baseline is indicative of stable cavitation and correlates strongly with successful barrier opening, while the absence of broadband energy increase above a six-decibel threshold confirms the absence of inertial cavitation, thereby ensuring treatment safety. This real-time feedback mechanism allows for immediate adjustment of acoustic parameters during the procedure and eliminates the need for post-hoc imaging verification in subsequent sessions.

The invention further demonstrates that the targeting accuracy of the system is sufficiently precise to enable the consistent delivery of agents to substructures of the basal ganglia, with a mean targeting error of less than three millimeters in the lateral plane and less than two millimeters along the axial direction. This level of precision is achieved without the use of phase correction algorithms or real-time imaging guidance, relying instead on a combination of geometric calibration, stereotaxic alignment, and empirical characterization of focal shift induced by the skull. The volume of barrier opening is reproducible and scalable with acoustic pressure, ranging from approximately seventy to one hundred eighty cubic millimeters depending on the applied pressure, which is maintained between 0.20 and 0.30 megapascals. The integrity of the blood-brain barrier is restored within two to four days following sonication, as demonstrated by longitudinal contrast-enhanced imaging, allowing for repeated treatments without cumulative tissue damage. The method has been validated in non-human primates across multiple sessions, with no evidence of hemorrhage, edema, or behavioral deficits, and with consistent acoustic signatures indicating stable cavitation in all successful treatments. The invention further provides a protocol for establishing a predictive relationship between the harmonic energy increase observed during sonication and the likelihood of successful barrier opening, enabling the development of automated treatment protocols that rely solely on acoustic feedback rather than imaging. This renders the system fully independent of magnetic resonance imaging for both targeting and verification, thereby enabling its deployment in non-specialized clinical environments, including outpatient clinics, community hospitals, and research laboratories. The invention thus represents a significant advancement over existing methods by providing a safe, precise, repeatable, and MRI-independent means of delivering therapeutics to deep brain structures, with broad applicability to the treatment of neurodegenerative, neuropsychiatric, and neurooncological disorders.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a system and method for the non-invasive, targeted, and reversible disruption of the blood-brain barrier in deep subcortical brain structures using focused ultrasound and microbubbles, with real-time acoustic monitoring and without reliance on magnetic resonance imaging for targeting or verification. The system is composed of three primary components: a focused ultrasound transducer assembly, a stereotaxic targeting platform, and a passive cavitation detection module, each of which is integrated into a unified workflow that ensures precise spatial control, real-time safety assessment, and reproducible therapeutic outcomes. The ultrasound transducer is a single-element, spherically focused device with a center frequency of 500 kilohertz, selected to balance the competing demands of spatial resolution and skull penetration. At this frequency, the wavelength of the ultrasound in brain tissue is approximately three millimeters, which is sufficiently large to mitigate the phase aberrations induced by the heterogeneous structure of the skull while remaining small enough to permit a focal volume that is clinically relevant for targeting subcortical nuclei. The transducer is housed in a water-filled coupling chamber and is mounted on a rigid stereotaxic manipulator that allows for independent adjustment of its position along three orthogonal axes—medio-lateral, anterior-posterior, and dorso-ventral—as well as its rotational orientation around two angular axes: azimuth and elevation. The manipulator is calibrated using a reference rod of known length and geometric profile, which is attached in place of the transducer to establish the precise location of the geometric focus relative to the manipulator’s coordinate system. This calibration procedure is repeated at regular intervals to ensure that mechanical drift or thermal expansion does not compromise the accuracy of the system.

The targeting of specific brain regions is achieved through a software-based algorithm that converts user-defined stereotaxic coordinates into corresponding manipulator settings. The algorithm accounts for nine degrees of freedom associated with the manipulator’s configuration, including the linear displacements along the three translational axes, the rotational angles of azimuth and elevation, the position of the manipulator on the left or right arm of the stereotaxic frame, the relative alignment of the medio-lateral and dorso-ventral drives, and the attachment orientation of the transducer to the manipulator. For any given target, the algorithm computes up to eight distinct configurations of the manipulator that will direct the ultrasound focus to the same anatomical location, each corresponding to a different approach angle. The optimal approach angle is selected by the operator based on the minimization of skull traversal distance and the maximization of perpendicular incidence to the cranial surface, thereby reducing the magnitude of acoustic aberrations. The algorithm then projects the predicted volume of barrier opening onto a pre-acquired, stereotaxically aligned structural magnetic resonance image, allowing the operator to visually verify the spatial relationship between the intended target and the surrounding anatomy. This image is acquired once, prior to the initiation of the treatment protocol, and serves as the sole anatomical reference for all subsequent sessions. The use of a single pre-acquired image eliminates the need for intra-procedural imaging and enables the system to operate independently of magnetic resonance guidance, a critical feature for clinical scalability.

The ultrasound procedure is initiated following the intravenous administration of monodisperse microbubbles, which are manufactured in-house using a differential centrifugation protocol to ensure uniform size distribution between four and five micrometers in diameter. These microbubbles are composed of a lipid or polymer shell encapsulating a core of perfluorocarbon gas, and are designed to remain stable in circulation for a duration sufficient to complete the sonication protocol. The microbubbles are injected immediately prior to the commencement of ultrasound exposure, which is delivered in a continuous, pulsed mode for a total duration of two minutes. The acoustic parameters are selected to ensure the induction of stable cavitation without triggering inertial cavitation. The peak rarefactional pressure is maintained between 0.20 and 0.30 megapascals, the pulse repetition frequency is set at 10 hertz, and the pulse duration is 20 milliseconds. These parameters are determined empirically through prior calibration in vitro and in vivo, and are chosen to maximize the harmonic energy response while avoiding the broadband emissions associated with bubble collapse. The transducer is operated at an output amplitude that compensates for the cumulative attenuation of the ultrasound wave as it traverses the scalp, skull, and brain tissue. Based on prior measurements, the total attenuation is estimated at approximately 7.15 decibels, which corresponds to an amplitude increase of approximately 2.28 times relative to the in-water calibration. This compensation is applied automatically by the system based on pre-programmed tissue models, ensuring consistent energy delivery across subjects and sessions.

The real-time monitoring of cavitation behavior is performed using a passive cavitation detection module that consists of a flatband, spherically focused hydrophone positioned coaxially within the central aperture of the ultrasound transducer. The hydrophone is connected to a high-speed digitizer capable of sampling acoustic signals at a rate exceeding ten megasamples per second, with a bandwidth extending from 0.6 to 5.2 megahertz. The digitized signal is processed in real time to extract the total and broadband energy components of the cavitation spectrum. The total energy is computed by summing the spectral amplitudes across the entire frequency band, while the broadband energy is isolated by filtering out the harmonic, sub-harmonic, and ultra-harmonic frequencies associated with stable cavitation. Specifically, a 300-kilohertz bandwidth is removed around each harmonic (n × 500 kHz, for n = 1 to 6), and a 100-kilohertz bandwidth is removed around each sub-harmonic (f/2) and ultra-harmonic (n × f/2, for n = 3, 5, 7, 9). The remaining signal, which corresponds to the broadband noise generated by inertial cavitation, is then quantified as the broadband energy increase, defined as the difference between the energy during sonication and the baseline energy recorded during a negative control sonication performed in the absence of microbubbles. Similarly, the harmonic energy increase is defined as the difference between the total energy and the broadband energy, minus the baseline harmonic content. A harmonic energy increase exceeding fifteen decibels is considered indicative of successful stable cavitation and is associated with a high probability of barrier opening. Conversely, a broadband energy increase exceeding six decibels is considered a warning threshold for potential inertial cavitation and triggers an automatic cessation of sonication. This feedback loop enables the operator to terminate the procedure before any tissue damage can occur, thereby ensuring safety without compromising efficacy.

The success of the barrier opening is confirmed post-procedurally using contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging with gadodiamide, a paramagnetic agent that does not cross the intact blood-brain barrier. T1-weighted images are acquired prior to and thirty minutes following the intravenous injection of gadodiamide, and the post-pre image ratio is computed to highlight regions of increased contrast enhancement. This ratio image is then spatially registered to the stereotaxically aligned reference image using a rigid-body transformation algorithm, and the left and right hemispheres are mirrored and divided to cancel out symmetric vascular artifacts. The resulting image is then transformed into a coordinate system centered on the predicted ultrasound focus, with the z-axis aligned along the approach vector. A region of interest is defined as a volume extending ±7.5 millimeters in the x- and y-directions and −5 to +12 millimeters along the z-axis. Voxels exhibiting a T1 enhancement greater than 10% are classified as “opened,” and the total volume of opened voxels is calculated as the measure of barrier disruption. The center of mass of the opened region is determined in the x-y plane and compared to the predicted focus location to compute the lateral targeting error. Along the z-axis, the center of gravity of voxels with more than 35% of the average opening fraction is calculated and compared to the predicted focal depth, which is defined as the geometric focal depth plus a 5-millimeter shift empirically determined from in vitro skull phantom experiments. The targeting accuracy is further analyzed to distinguish between systematic errors, which are consistent across sessions and subjects, and random errors, which arise from day-to-day variability in positioning or image registration. The results demonstrate a mean lateral targeting error of 2.5 millimeters, a mean axial targeting error of 1.5 millimeters, and a combined error of 3.1 millimeters, all of which are sufficiently small to enable the reliable targeting of substructures within the basal ganglia.

The system has been validated in a series of seventeen sonication sessions performed on two male rhesus macaques, with targets including the caudate nucleus and putamen in the left hemisphere. Each animal underwent repeated treatments at two-week intervals, with no evidence of cumulative tissue damage, behavioral deficits, or clinical complications. The volume of barrier opening ranged from 70 to 180 cubic millimeters, with a mean of 115 cubic millimeters, and was found to correlate with the applied acoustic pressure. Higher pressures (0.30 MPa) consistently produced larger openings, while lower pressures (0.20 MPa) occasionally resulted in incomplete disruption. The harmonic energy increase was observed to exceed fifteen decibels in all but two of the seventeen sessions, and in those cases, the barrier opening was either absent or minimal, establishing a strong predictive relationship between the PCD signal and treatment success. The broadband energy increase remained below six decibels in all sessions, confirming the absence of inertial cavitation and the safety of the procedure. Post-procedural T2-weighted and susceptibility-weighted imaging revealed no evidence of hemorrhage, edema, or microstructural damage, and the animals exhibited normal feeding, locomotion, and social behavior throughout the study period. A preliminary investigation into the duration of barrier opening demonstrated that the effect is transient, with the contrast enhancement diminishing significantly by forty-eight hours and returning to baseline by ninety-six hours after sonication. This temporal profile suggests a window of opportunity of approximately two to four days for the delivery of therapeutic agents, which is consistent with findings in murine models and supports the feasibility of repeated treatments.

The invention further includes a protocol for the calibration and validation of the system in new subjects. Prior to the first sonication, a high-resolution structural magnetic resonance image is acquired and registered to a standardized stereotaxic atlas. The stereotaxic coordinates of the target structure are identified, and the algorithm computes the optimal manipulator settings for achieving the desired focus. A negative control sonication is performed in the absence of microbubbles to establish the baseline acoustic emissions, and the harmonic and broadband energy thresholds are confirmed. The system is then ready for therapeutic use, with all subsequent sessions relying solely on the pre-acquired image and the real-time PCD feedback. The operator may adjust the acoustic parameters within the validated range (0.20–0.30 MPa) to modulate the size of the opening, and the PCD system provides immediate feedback on the safety and efficacy of the adjustment. This closed-loop control mechanism enables the system to be operated by trained personnel without the need for specialized imaging expertise or access to an MRI suite. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the field of non-invasive neuromodulation, providing a safe, precise, and scalable method for delivering therapeutics to deep brain structures with unprecedented spatial control and minimal infrastructure requirements.

### Example 1

In a first illustrative embodiment of the invention, a 500-kilohertz focused ultrasound transducer is mounted on a Kopf stereotaxic manipulator and used to target the caudate nucleus in a non-human primate. The subject is anesthetized with isoflurane and positioned in a custom head holder that immobilizes the skull and ensures consistent alignment with the stereotaxic frame. A pre-acquired T1-weighted magnetic resonance image, obtained during a prior session, is loaded into the targeting software, and the coordinates of the caudate nucleus are identified using anatomical landmarks such as the anterior commissure, posterior commissure, and intercommissural line. The software computes eight possible manipulator configurations that will direct the ultrasound focus to the center of the caudate nucleus, each corresponding to a different approach angle. The operator selects the configuration that provides the most perpendicular incidence to the skull, minimizing the path length through the thickest portion of the parietal bone. The manipulator is then adjusted to the selected settings, and a negative control sonication is performed for two seconds in the absence of microbubbles. The passive cavitation detection system records the baseline harmonic and broadband energy levels, which are used as reference values for the subsequent treatment.

Following the negative control, a bolus of monodisperse microbubbles, sized between four and five micrometers and suspended in saline, is administered intravenously at a dose of 0.1 milliliters per kilogram of body weight. Two seconds after injection, the ultrasound transducer is activated for a duration of two minutes at a peak rarefactional pressure of 0.25 megapascals, a pulse repetition frequency of 10 hertz, and a pulse duration of 20 milliseconds. During this time, the passive cavitation detection system continuously monitors the acoustic emissions, and the harmonic energy increase is observed to rise from a baseline of 2.1 decibels to a peak of 21.4 decibels, well above the fifteen-decibel threshold for successful barrier opening. The broadband energy increase remains below 3.2 decibels throughout the sonication, confirming the absence of inertial cavitation. The system does not trigger any safety alerts, and the sonication is completed without interruption. Immediately after the sonication, a series of positive control pulses are delivered at varying pressures (0.05 to 0.35 MPa) to characterize the dynamic range of the cavitation response, and the harmonic energy increase is found to plateau at approximately 10 decibels for pressures above 0.25 MPa, consistent with prior calibration data.

The animal is then transported to the magnetic resonance imaging suite, where a T1-weighted structural image is acquired prior to the administration of gadodiamide, followed by a second acquisition thirty minutes after the injection of 0.15 milliliters per kilogram of gadodiamide. The post-pre image ratio is computed and registered to the stereotaxic reference image. The region of contrast enhancement is clearly localized to the caudate nucleus, with a volume of 108 cubic millimeters, and the center of mass of the opened region is found to be 0.8 millimeters posterior, 1.2 millimeters ventral, and 1.1 millimeters closer to the transducer than the predicted focus. These deviations are within the expected range of targeting error and are consistent with the systematic shifts observed in prior sessions. The T2-weighted and susceptibility-weighted images show no evidence of edema, hemorrhage, or microbleeds, and the animal exhibits no behavioral abnormalities in the hours following the procedure. The results of this session confirm the ability of the system to achieve precise, safe, and reproducible barrier opening in a deep subcortical structure using a single-element transducer and real-time acoustic feedback, without the need for magnetic resonance guidance.

### Example 2

In a second illustrative embodiment of the invention, the same system is employed to target the putamen in a second non-human primate, with the goal of demonstrating the versatility of the method across different anatomical regions and the robustness of the targeting algorithm. The subject is prepared in the same manner as in Example 1, with anesthesia, immobilization, and pre-acquired imaging. However, the putamen is located more laterally and ventrally than the caudate nucleus, and its proximity to the internal capsule necessitates a more oblique approach angle to avoid traversing the thickest portion of the skull. The targeting software computes eight possible configurations, and the operator selects the configuration that provides the most favorable acoustic window through the temporal bone, with an azimuth of 15 degrees and an elevation of 22 degrees. The manipulator is adjusted accordingly, and a negative control sonication is performed, yielding a baseline harmonic energy of 1.8 decibels and a broadband energy of 1.5 decibels.

Following the intravenous administration of microbubbles, the ultrasound transducer is activated for two minutes at a pressure of 0.30 megapascals. The harmonic energy increase is observed to rise rapidly, reaching a peak of 24.7 decibels within thirty seconds, and remains elevated throughout the duration of the sonication. The broadband energy increase remains below 4.1 decibels, indicating that the microbubbles are undergoing stable cavitation without evidence of inertial collapse. The passive cavitation detection system provides continuous feedback, and the operator observes that the harmonic energy increase exceeds the threshold for successful opening within the first ten seconds, allowing for early confirmation of treatment efficacy. After the sonication, a series of positive control pulses are delivered, and the harmonic energy response is found to saturate at approximately 12 decibels for pressures above 0.25 MPa, consistent with the calibration curve established in prior sessions.

The animal is transported to the MRI suite, and contrast-enhanced T1 imaging is performed. The post-pre image ratio reveals a well-defined region of enhancement centered on the putamen, with a volume of 132 cubic millimeters. The center of mass of the opened region is displaced by 1.1 millimeters posterior, 2.4 millimeters ventral, and 1.7 millimeters toward the transducer relative to the predicted focus. These deviations are consistent with the systematic ventral mislocalization previously observed in putamen targets, which is attributed to the greater angular deviation required for the approach and the resulting gravitational torque on the manipulator. The T2 and susceptibility-weighted images show no signs of tissue damage, and the animal remains behaviorally normal. The results of this session demonstrate that the system is capable of achieving precise and safe barrier opening in a structurally distinct region of the basal ganglia, with the same level of accuracy and reliability as in the caudate nucleus. The targeting algorithm, which accounts for the geometric configuration of the manipulator and the expected focal shift, proves effective across multiple anatomical targets, and the real-time passive cavitation detection system provides a reliable and consistent indicator of treatment success and safety. This embodiment confirms that the invention is not limited to a single brain region but can be applied broadly to any deep subcortical structure accessible via a transcranial acoustic window, thereby establishing its utility as a general platform for non-invasive, targeted drug delivery to the brain.
