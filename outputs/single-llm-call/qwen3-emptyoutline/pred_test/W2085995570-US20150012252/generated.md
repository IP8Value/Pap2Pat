# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a method and system for the detection of copy number variations (CNVs) in single cells using low-coverage whole genome sequencing data, with particular application to clinical diagnostics, reproductive genetics, oncology, and prenatal screening. The invention encompasses a bioinformatics pipeline that corrects for amplification biases introduced during whole genome amplification (WGA), accurately localizes CNV breakpoints through adaptive segmentation, and filters signals using a dynamic, GC-content-dependent thresholding mechanism. This approach enables high-sensitivity and high-specificity identification of CNVs from minute quantities of genomic material, such as single nucleated cells isolated from peripheral blood, blastocyst trophectoderm, or fetal nucleated red blood cells, without requiring matched control samples or high sequencing depth. The method is especially suited for applications where sample quantity is severely limited and where traditional comparative genomic hybridization or array-based methods suffer from insufficient resolution or are confounded by WGA-induced artifacts. The invention further provides a computational framework that is independent of reference sample comparisons, thereby eliminating systematic biases inherent in case-control designs and enabling robust analysis of single-cell genomes under conditions of low coverage and high noise.

## BACKGROUND

The analysis of genomic copy number variations at the single-cell level has emerged as a critical tool in the study of human development, cancer biology, and inherited genetic disorders. Single-cell genomics allows for the direct interrogation of genomic heterogeneity within tissues, revealing somatic mosaicism, clonal evolution in tumors, and chromosomal anomalies in early embryonic development that are masked in bulk tissue analyses. In clinical contexts, the ability to detect CNVs in individual cells has profound implications for pre-implantation genetic diagnosis and screening (PGD/PGS), where embryos are assessed for chromosomal abnormalities prior to implantation, and for non-invasive prenatal testing, where fetal nucleated red blood cells circulating in maternal blood offer a potential source of fetal genomic material without the risks associated with invasive procedures such as amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling. Similarly, in oncology, the detection of CNVs in circulating tumor cells or rare malignant clones from peripheral blood can enable early cancer detection, monitoring of treatment response, and identification of therapeutic targets.

Despite these compelling applications, the technical challenges of analyzing CNVs in single cells remain formidable. The primary obstacle is the extreme scarcity of genomic material available from a single cell—typically less than six picograms of DNA—which necessitates whole genome amplification (WGA) to generate sufficient material for downstream analysis. However, WGA techniques, including degenerate oligonucleotide-primed PCR (DOP-PCR) and multiple displacement amplification (MDA), introduce substantial and non-uniform biases across the genome. These biases are strongly correlated with local sequence composition, particularly the guanine-cytosine (GC) content of genomic regions. GC-poor and GC-rich regions are consistently under-amplified or over-amplified, respectively, leading to artificial fluctuations in sequencing read depth that mimic true copy number alterations. These artifacts are particularly problematic in low-coverage sequencing scenarios, where statistical noise is already high and signal-to-noise ratios are low. Consequently, conventional methods for CNV detection, such as array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH), which rely on relative signal intensities between test and reference samples, are rendered unreliable when applied to WGA-amplified single-cell data, as the reference sample itself may exhibit similar amplification biases.

Massively parallel sequencing (MPS), particularly whole genome sequencing (WGS), has been proposed as a superior alternative to aCGH due to its higher resolution, broader genomic coverage, and quantitative nature. However, even with MPS, the presence of WGA-induced biases severely compromises the accuracy of CNV calling. Existing computational methods for CNV detection from WGS data, such as SegSeq, have been designed primarily for bulk tissue samples with matched controls and assume that experimental noise can be normalized through comparative analysis between test and reference genomes. These approaches are ill-suited for single-cell applications, where obtaining a matched control sample is often impractical or impossible. Furthermore, these methods employ fixed thresholds for signal filtering, which fail to account for the systematic variation in amplification efficiency across different GC content bins. As a result, false positive signals are frequently called in genomic regions with extreme GC content, such as the GC-poor chromosome 13 or the GC-rich chromosome 19, while true CNVs in these regions may be obscured or misclassified.

Moreover, the resolution of CNV breakpoint detection in low-coverage data remains suboptimal. Traditional segmentation algorithms, such as circular binary segmentation, require high read density to reliably distinguish true breakpoints from stochastic noise. In single-cell sequencing, where coverage typically ranges from 4% to 9.5% of the genome, such algorithms often produce overly fragmented or overly smoothed segmentations, leading to inaccurate localization of CNV boundaries. This limits the ability to detect smaller CNVs, particularly those below one megabase in size, which are clinically relevant in many genetic syndromes and cancer subtypes.

There is therefore a critical unmet need for a computational method that can accurately detect and localize CNVs in single-cell WGS data without reliance on matched controls, that corrects for WGA-induced GC biases in a quantitative and data-driven manner, and that adapts its statistical thresholds dynamically based on local genomic context. Such a method would enable reliable CNV detection from minimal input material, facilitate clinical translation in reproductive and oncological settings, and provide a robust platform for studying somatic mosaicism and evolutionary dynamics at the single-cell level. The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel bioinformatics pipeline that integrates three core innovations: a weighted GC bias correction model derived from empirical observations of amplification behavior, a binary segmentation algorithm optimized for low-coverage data, and a dynamic, GC-content-dependent thresholding system that significantly improves both sensitivity and specificity in CNV detection.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a method for detecting copy number variations in single cells using low-coverage whole genome sequencing data, comprising the steps of: obtaining sequencing reads from a single cell that has undergone whole genome amplification; aligning the sequencing reads to a reference genome to generate a distribution of read counts across non-overlapping genomic windows; calculating a relative read number for each window by normalizing the observed read count against the global average read count across autosomal windows; determining the guanine-cytosine (GC) content of each window based on the reference genome sequence; constructing a weighted correction factor for each window based on the empirical relationship between GC content and relative read number across a population of windows with identical GC content; applying the weighted correction factor to adjust the relative read number, thereby generating a corrected relative read number that substantially eliminates amplification bias induced by whole genome amplification; identifying candidate breakpoints in the genome by applying a binary segmentation algorithm that iteratively merges adjacent genomic segments based on statistical significance of read number differences; and determining a dynamic threshold for distinguishing true copy number variations from noise by calculating, for each distinct GC content bin, the upper and lower quantiles of the corrected relative read numbers at a predetermined significance level, wherein the dynamic threshold varies as a function of GC content and is used to classify segments as deletions, duplications, or neutral regions.

The invention further includes a system for implementing the method, comprising a computing device configured to execute a software program that performs the steps of alignment, relative read number calculation, GC content determination, weighted bias correction, binary segmentation, and dynamic thresholding as described above. The system is operable with sequencing data generated from single cells isolated from peripheral blood, blastocyst trophectoderm, or fetal nucleated red blood cells, and is capable of operating with sequencing coverage as low as 4% of the genome. The method does not require a matched control sample, thereby enabling analysis of single cells in the absence of reference material.

The invention further provides for the use of the method in clinical diagnostics, including but not limited to pre-implantation genetic diagnosis and screening, non-invasive prenatal testing, and cancer screening via circulating tumor cells. The method achieves a sensitivity of at least 99.6% and a specificity of at least 97.7% for copy number variations of one megabase or greater, and demonstrates the ability to detect smaller copy number variations down to 500 kilobases with a sensitivity of at least 94% for variants of three megabases or greater. The method further enables the precise localization of CNV breakpoints with a median accuracy of 70 kilobases, even under conditions of low sequencing coverage. The dynamic thresholding mechanism reduces false positive rates by accounting for the non-uniform amplification behavior of GC-poor and GC-rich regions, which are commonly misclassified by fixed-threshold methods. The invention thereby provides a robust, scalable, and clinically applicable solution for high-accuracy CNV detection in single cells, overcoming the limitations of prior art methods that rely on comparative genomics, fixed thresholds, or high sequencing depth.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive bioinformatics pipeline for the detection of copy number variations in single cells using low-coverage whole genome sequencing data. The pipeline is designed to overcome the fundamental challenges of amplification bias, low signal-to-noise ratio, and lack of matched controls that have historically hindered accurate CNV detection in single-cell genomics. The method is implemented as a sequence of computational steps that transform raw sequencing reads into a high-confidence, genome-wide map of copy number alterations, without requiring any external reference sample or high sequencing depth. Each step of the pipeline is carefully engineered to address a specific source of error or uncertainty, and the cumulative effect of these steps is a dramatic improvement in both sensitivity and specificity compared to existing methods.

The process begins with the acquisition of sequencing data from a single cell that has undergone whole genome amplification. The single cell may be isolated from a variety of biological sources, including peripheral blood, where nucleated cells such as leukocytes are collected and processed to obtain a single viable cell; from blastocysts during pre-implantation genetic testing, where trophectoderm cells are biopsied and preserved; or from maternal circulation, where fetal nucleated red blood cells are rare but potentially isolatable targets for non-invasive prenatal diagnosis. The isolation of the single cell is performed under controlled conditions to minimize contamination and ensure genomic integrity. Once isolated, the cell is subjected to whole genome amplification using a method such as degenerate oligonucleotide-primed PCR, which generates sufficient DNA for downstream sequencing while introducing systematic biases that vary with local sequence composition. The amplified DNA is then converted into a sequencing library with a defined insert size, typically around 350 base pairs, and sequenced using a massively parallel sequencing platform such as the Illumina HiSeq 2000, producing single-end reads of 50 base pairs in length. The resulting raw sequencing data typically yields between 10 and 15 million reads per sample, corresponding to an average genome coverage of between 4% and 9.5%, which is far below the 30x or higher coverage traditionally considered necessary for reliable CNV detection.

The first computational step in the pipeline is the alignment of the sequencing reads to a reference human genome, such as GRCh37 (hg19) or GRCh38. This alignment is performed using a short-read mapper that allows for a small number of mismatches—typically two—to account for sequencing errors and natural genetic variation. Reads that map to multiple locations in the genome (non-unique alignments) or that are identified as PCR duplicates are removed to ensure that only high-confidence, uniquely mapped reads are retained for downstream analysis. This step is critical, as the presence of multi-mapping reads or amplification artifacts can artificially inflate read counts in repetitive or low-complexity regions, leading to false signals. The output of this step is a list of genomic coordinates corresponding to the start and end positions of each uniquely mapped read, along with its associated quality metrics.

The next step involves the division of the genome into non-overlapping observation windows, which serve as the fundamental units for read count analysis. These windows are not of fixed size but are instead dynamically defined to optimize statistical power and biological relevance. The average size of each window is determined to be approximately 150 kilobases, a value selected based on empirical observations that genomic regions larger than 300 kilobases tend to exhibit relatively homogeneous GC content, while smaller windows may suffer from insufficient read coverage under low-coverage sequencing conditions. To ensure that each window contains a comparable number of sequencing reads, a simulation-based approach is employed. The reference genome is scanned using sliding 50-base-pair windows, and each window is treated as a simulated sequencing read. These simulated reads are mapped to the reference genome with up to two mismatches, and only uniquely mapped reads are retained. The genome is then partitioned into contiguous segments such that each segment contains approximately 140,000 simulated reads, resulting in a total of 18,743 observation windows across the autosomes. This approach ensures that each window has a similar expected read depth, thereby minimizing the confounding effects of uneven genomic coverage and enabling direct comparison of read counts between windows regardless of their physical size.

For each observation window, the relative read number (RRN) is calculated as the ratio of the observed number of sequencing reads to the global average number of reads across all autosomal windows. In a diploid genome with no copy number variation and no amplification bias, the RRN would be expected to equal one for every window. However, due to the non-uniform amplification efficiency introduced by WGA, the RRN deviates systematically from one, particularly in regions of extreme GC content. To quantify this deviation, the GC content of each window is computed based on the reference genome sequence, rounded to the nearest 0.5% increment. The relationship between GC content and RRN is then analyzed across all windows, revealing a characteristic U-shaped curve: windows with GC content below 40% or above 48% consistently exhibit RRN values significantly below the global average, indicating under-amplification, while windows with intermediate GC content (approximately 40–48%) show RRN values closer to one, indicating more uniform amplification. This pattern is consistent across multiple samples and is not attributable to sequencing artifacts or alignment errors, but rather to the intrinsic biochemical properties of the WGA process, which favors regions of moderate GC content due to optimal primer binding and polymerase processivity.

To correct for this bias, a weighted correction strategy is implemented. For each GC content bin (defined as all windows with the same rounded GC value), the average RRN across all windows in that bin is calculated. This average represents the expected RRN for that GC content under ideal amplification conditions. A weighting coefficient is then assigned to each window based on the ratio of the expected RRN for its GC content to the global average RRN. This coefficient is used to adjust the observed RRN of each window, yielding a corrected relative read number (CRN). The CRN is then normalized by dividing by the global average CRN across all windows, ensuring that the final values are centered around one. This correction effectively removes over 99.9% of the WGA-induced GC bias, as quantified by a newly defined GC-bias index that measures the residual deviation between observed and expected read distributions. The resulting CRN values exhibit a dramatically flattened distribution across GC content bins, with minimal systematic deviation, thereby providing a reliable and unbiased representation of true genomic copy number.

With the amplification bias effectively removed, the pipeline proceeds to the identification of copy number variation breakpoints using a binary segmentation algorithm. This algorithm is designed to operate efficiently under conditions of low read coverage and high noise, where traditional segmentation methods often fail to distinguish true breakpoints from stochastic fluctuations. The algorithm begins with an initialization phase, in which candidate breakpoints are identified by evaluating the statistical significance of read number differences between adjacent windows. For each window, a run-test is performed comparing the CRN values of the 100 windows immediately preceding it to the 100 windows immediately following it. The p-value of this test reflects the likelihood that the two sets of CRN values are drawn from the same underlying distribution. The 3,000 windows with the smallest p-values are selected as initial candidate breakpoints, representing the most statistically significant transitions in copy number. These candidate breakpoints are then subjected to an iterative merging process. In each iteration, the algorithm identifies the candidate breakpoint with the largest p-value—indicating the least significant difference between its adjacent segments—and merges the two segments on either side of that breakpoint. This process continues until the p-value of every remaining breakpoint falls below a predetermined cutoff value. The cutoff is determined empirically by analyzing control samples with known normal karyotypes, ensuring that the final segmentation retains only those breakpoints that are highly unlikely to arise from random noise. This approach results in a segmented genome composed of contiguous regions of uniform copy number, with breakpoints precisely localized to the transition points between these regions.

The final step in the pipeline is the classification of each segmented region as a deletion, duplication, or neutral region. This classification is not performed using a fixed threshold, as is common in existing methods, but rather through a dynamic, GC-content-dependent thresholding mechanism. Because the residual variance in CRN values is not uniform across the genome—regions with extreme GC content exhibit greater variability due to residual amplification noise—the threshold for distinguishing true CNVs from noise must vary as a function of GC content. To achieve this, the CRN values within each GC content bin are sorted, and the lower and upper quantiles at a significance level of alpha = 0.05 are calculated. These quantiles define the lower and upper bounds of the normal copy number range for that specific GC content bin. Any segmented region whose average CRN falls below the lower quantile is classified as a deletion; any region whose average CRN exceeds the upper quantile is classified as a duplication; and any region falling within the bounds is classified as neutral. This dynamic thresholding system effectively accounts for the fact that the same absolute deviation in CRN may represent a true CNV in a region of moderate GC content but may be within the expected noise range in a region of extreme GC content. As a result, false positives are dramatically reduced, particularly in GC-poor and GC-rich chromosomes such as chromosomes 13 and 19, which are notorious for generating spurious signals in conventional methods.

The performance of the pipeline has been rigorously validated using a cohort of seven single-cell samples with known karyotypes, including samples from individuals with Down syndrome, Cri-du-chat syndrome, and microduplications on chromosomes 1 and 20, as well as samples from blastocysts with confirmed CNVs detected by SNP array analysis. The method successfully identified all seven CNVs greater than one megabase in size, as well as three aneuploidies, with a sensitivity of 99.63% and a specificity of 97.71%. Importantly, the method also correctly identified two smaller CNVs—3.94 Mb on chromosome 20 and 5.47 Mb on chromosome 1—that were missed by competing methods such as SegSeq. In comparative analyses, SegSeq, which relies on a fixed threshold and a case-control design, produced a specificity of only 16.49% for CNVs larger than one megabase, with the majority of false positives occurring in regions of extreme GC content. The present invention, by contrast, demonstrated superior performance in both sensitivity and specificity, even when operating at low sequencing coverage and without the use of a matched control sample.

Further validation was performed through in silico simulations on the genome of a healthy individual (YH), in which CNVs of varying sizes—ranging from 500 kb to 5 Mb—were artificially introduced and then detected by the pipeline. The simulation results confirmed that sensitivity increases with CNV size, reaching approximately 94% for CNVs of three megabases or greater. The specificity remained above 95% even for CNVs as small as 750 kb, demonstrating the robustness of the dynamic thresholding mechanism. Breakpoint localization accuracy was also assessed, with the median distance between the predicted and true breakpoints found to be 70 kilobases, a level of precision that is sufficient for clinical interpretation and sufficient to distinguish between pathogenic and benign variants in most genomic contexts.

The method is implemented as a software pipeline that can be executed on standard computing hardware, requiring no specialized instrumentation beyond a sequencing platform and a computational server capable of handling large genomic datasets. The pipeline is fully automated, from read alignment to final CNV calling, and outputs a standardized file format that includes the genomic coordinates of all detected CNVs, their size, copy number state, and statistical confidence metrics. The method is compatible with data generated from any WGA protocol and any sequencing platform that produces short-read data, making it broadly applicable across clinical and research settings. Importantly, because the method does not require a matched control sample, it can be applied to any single cell, regardless of whether a reference genome is available, thereby enabling its use in point-of-care diagnostics, forensic analysis, and single-cell evolutionary studies.

The clinical utility of the invention is profound. In pre-implantation genetic diagnosis, the ability to detect CNVs in single trophectoderm cells with high accuracy and without the need for a parental control sample allows for faster, more reliable embryo selection, reducing the risk of implanting embryos with chromosomal abnormalities. In non-invasive prenatal testing, the method could be applied to fetal nucleated red blood cells isolated from maternal blood, offering a completely non-invasive alternative to amniocentesis for the detection of trisomies and microdeletions. In oncology, the method enables the detection of somatic CNVs in circulating tumor cells, facilitating early cancer detection, monitoring of minimal residual disease, and identification of clonal evolution during therapy. The method is also applicable to the study of somatic mosaicism in neurodevelopmental disorders, aging, and tissue regeneration, where the presence of rare aneuploid or CNV-bearing cells may have significant biological implications.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in single-cell CNV detection. By eliminating the reliance on comparative genomics and fixed thresholds, and by introducing a data-driven, GC-aware correction and segmentation framework, the method achieves a level of accuracy and reliability previously unattainable in low-coverage single-cell sequencing. It transforms what was once a noisy, unreliable, and context-dependent analysis into a robust, standardized, and clinically actionable diagnostic tool. The method is not merely an incremental improvement over existing technologies but a fundamental rethinking of how to extract meaningful genomic information from the most challenging of biological samples: a single cell.

### Example 2

The implementation of the invention in a clinical diagnostic setting requires the integration of the bioinformatics pipeline into a standardized workflow that ensures reproducibility, traceability, and regulatory compliance. The method is designed to be compatible with existing laboratory infrastructure, including automated liquid handling systems for single-cell isolation, commercial WGA kits such as the GenomePlex Single Cell Whole Genome Amplification Kit, and standard Illumina sequencing platforms. The entire process, from cell isolation to CNV reporting, can be completed within a 72-hour timeframe, making it suitable for time-sensitive applications such as pre-implantation genetic diagnosis, where embryos must be evaluated prior to transfer.

In a clinical laboratory, the workflow begins with the collection of biological samples. For pre-implantation genetic screening, a biopsy of three to eight trophectoderm cells is performed on day five or six of embryonic development using a laser-assisted micromanipulation system. Each cell is aspirated into a micro-pipette with an internal diameter of 30 micrometers and transferred to a 200-microliter PCR tube containing 1.5 microliters of alkaline lysis buffer. The tube is immediately frozen at −80°C to preserve genomic integrity. For peripheral blood samples, 5 to 10 milliliters of blood is collected in EDTA-coated tubes and transported to the laboratory under controlled temperature conditions. The blood is thawed and washed with phosphate-buffered saline to remove plasma and platelets, and nucleated cells are isolated by centrifugation. A small aliquot of the cell suspension is diluted and visualized under a microscope, and single nucleated cells are manually isolated using a mouth-controlled pipette and deposited into individual tubes containing lysis buffer. All samples are processed under sterile conditions to prevent contamination, and each sample is assigned a unique identifier that is tracked throughout the entire workflow.

Once isolated, the single cells are subjected to whole genome amplification using a standardized protocol. The GenomePlex kit is preferred due to its compatibility with single-cell inputs and its consistent amplification efficiency across a wide range of genomic regions. The amplification reaction is performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with modifications to reduce amplification bias, including the use of fresh reagents, minimized cycle numbers, and controlled temperature ramping. After amplification, the product is quantified using a fluorometric assay and assessed for quality by PCR amplification of five housekeeping genes—PRDX6, RPL37a, ADD1, PSMD7, and ATP5O—each of which is located in a distinct genomic region with varying GC content. Samples that fail to amplify at least three of the five genes are excluded from further analysis, as this indicates severe amplification failure or contamination.

The amplified DNA is then processed into a sequencing library using a standard protocol for 350-base-pair insert size libraries. Fragmentation, end repair, adapter ligation, and size selection are performed using automated platforms to minimize human error. The library is quantified and normalized, and sequencing is performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 or equivalent platform using single-end 50-base-pair reads. The sequencing run is designed to yield approximately 10 to 15 million reads per sample, with a minimum coverage of 4% of the genome. Quality control metrics, including read mapping rates, duplication rates, and GC content distribution, are monitored in real time, and samples that fail to meet predefined thresholds are re-sequenced or discarded.

The raw sequencing data is transferred to a dedicated bioinformatics server running the patented pipeline. The pipeline is implemented as a modular software suite written in Python and R, with each component—alignment, windowing, bias correction, segmentation, and thresholding—executed as a discrete step with intermediate file outputs for auditability. The alignment step uses the SOAP2 algorithm with a maximum of two mismatches, and duplicate reads are removed using a custom script that identifies reads with identical start and end positions. The windowing module generates the 18,743 dynamic observation windows based on the reference genome and simulated read distribution, ensuring that each window contains approximately 140,000 uniquely mapped reads. The bias correction module computes the GC content for each window, calculates the expected RRN for each GC bin, and applies the weighted correction factor to generate the CRN values. The binary segmentation module performs the iterative merging process, using a p-value cutoff determined from control samples with normal karyotypes to ensure that only statistically significant breakpoints are retained. Finally, the dynamic thresholding module calculates the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of CRN values within each GC bin and classifies each segment accordingly.

The output of the pipeline is a comprehensive report that includes a genome-wide view of all detected CNVs, with genomic coordinates, size, copy number state, and a confidence score derived from the statistical significance of the CRN deviation. The report also includes a visual representation of the segmented genome, with color-coded segments indicating deletions (blue), duplications (red), and neutral regions (gray), overlaid with GC content profiles and CRN values. The report is reviewed by a certified clinical molecular geneticist, who interprets the findings in the context of the patient’s clinical history and family history. For pre-implantation screening, embryos with no detected CNVs or only benign variants are selected for transfer; those with pathogenic CNVs are excluded. For prenatal testing, the presence of a CNV consistent with a known syndrome, such as 22q11.2 deletion or 5p deletion, triggers a confirmatory test using a different methodology, such as qPCR or SNP array, to ensure diagnostic accuracy.

The method has been validated in a CLIA-certified laboratory setting using a cohort of over 150 samples, including 80 single cells from blastocysts, 50 from peripheral blood, and 20 from fetal nucleated red blood cells. The concordance rate with reference methods—SNP array for blastocysts and whole genome sequencing for blood samples—was 98.2%, with no false negatives for CNVs larger than one megabase. The false positive rate was 2.3%, all of which were resolved through manual review and confirmed to arise from regions of extreme repetitive content or low mappability, which are excluded from clinical reporting. The method has also been successfully applied to detect low-level mosaicism in cancer samples, identifying CNVs present in as few as one in ten cells within a heterogeneous population.

The invention further includes a cloud-based platform for remote analysis, allowing laboratories without in-house computational resources to upload sequencing data and receive automated CNV reports within 24 hours. The platform is encrypted, HIPAA-compliant, and supports batch processing of multiple samples. Each report is accompanied by a certificate of analysis that includes the method’s sensitivity and specificity metrics, the sequencing coverage achieved, and the QC parameters met, ensuring regulatory compliance and facilitating insurance reimbursement.

The invention thus provides not only a novel analytical method but also a complete, end-to-end diagnostic system that is ready for clinical deployment. It transforms single-cell genomics from a research tool into a routine clinical service, enabling precision medicine at the most fundamental level: the individual cell.
