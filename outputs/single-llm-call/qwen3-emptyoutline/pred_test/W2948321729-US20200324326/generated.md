# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel method and system for the remediation of heavy metal-contaminated soils, particularly those impacted by anthropogenic sources such as industrial discharge, mining operations, and agricultural chemical runoff. More specifically, the invention encompasses an asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical (AACE) filtration system integrated with a recirculating soil washing apparatus, designed to selectively extract, reduce, and immobilize heavy metal cations—such as copper, lead, and cadmium—into their zero-valent metallic states while preserving essential soil nutrients. The invention further includes the use of amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon electrodes that enhance the efficiency of metal capture, electrochemical reduction, and regeneration of the chelating agent used in the washing process. The system is particularly suited for in situ or ex situ treatment of contaminated agricultural and urban soils, as well as for the recovery of valuable heavy metals from industrial effluents, wastewater streams, and leachates. The method operates under low-voltage, energy-efficient conditions, avoids the generation of secondary pollutants, and enables long-term reuse of the washing solution without significant degradation in performance. The invention represents a paradigm shift from conventional soil remediation techniques that rely on irreversible extraction, immobilization via precipitation, or slow biological processes, by introducing a dynamic, electrochemically driven mechanism that transforms mobile, bioavailable metal ions into stable, non-leachable, and recoverable metallic deposits.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Soil contamination by heavy metals has emerged as one of the most persistent and pervasive environmental challenges of the modern era, driven by decades of unchecked industrial expansion, intensive mining activities, and the widespread use of metal-containing agrochemicals. Heavy metal pollutants such as copper, lead, cadmium, mercury, and arsenic accumulate in the upper layers of the earth’s crust due to their non-biodegradable nature and high affinity for soil colloids, organic matter, and clay minerals. Once introduced into the environment, these elements enter the food chain through plant uptake, groundwater infiltration, and atmospheric deposition, posing severe risks to human health, ecosystem integrity, and agricultural productivity. Regulatory agencies worldwide have established stringent thresholds for permissible concentrations of heavy metals in soils intended for residential, agricultural, or ecological use, yet remediation technologies have struggled to meet these standards in a sustainable, scalable, and economically viable manner.

Traditional remediation strategies fall into three broad categories: physical removal, chemical extraction, and biological treatment. Physical methods such as soil excavation and disposal are costly, disruptive, and merely relocate the problem rather than resolve it. Chemical approaches, particularly soil washing using strong chelating agents like ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), have gained attention for their ability to mobilize tightly bound metal ions by forming stable, water-soluble complexes. However, these methods suffer from critical limitations: the high consumption of chelating agents, the lack of effective downstream treatment for the resulting metal-laden effluent, and the unintended leaching of essential plant nutrients such as calcium, magnesium, and potassium. The disposal of spent chelator solutions often leads to secondary contamination of surface and groundwater, negating the environmental benefits of the initial remediation effort. Moreover, the repeated application of fresh chelator solutions is economically unsustainable and environmentally indefensible.

Alternative strategies involving the use of high-surface-area sorbents—such as activated carbon, biochar, or clay minerals—have been explored to immobilize heavy metals through physicochemical adsorption. While these materials can reduce metal mobility and bioavailability, their capacity is inherently limited by equilibrium constraints, and their binding is often reversible under changing environmental conditions such as pH fluctuations or competing ion concentrations. Furthermore, the immobilized metals remain in a potentially bioavailable form, necessitating long-term monitoring and posing a risk of re-mobilization during future land use changes or climatic events.

Phytoremediation, a biologically based approach that employs metal-accumulating plants to extract contaminants from soil, offers a low-energy alternative. However, its applicability is severely restricted by the extremely slow kinetics of metal uptake, the long treatment durations (often spanning multiple growing seasons), and the risk of secondary contamination through the disposal or incineration of metal-laden biomass. In many cases, the harvested plants themselves become hazardous waste, requiring specialized handling and disposal protocols that undermine the sustainability of the entire process.

Electrochemical methods have long been considered promising for the direct reduction of metal ions to their elemental forms. Conventional electrokinetic remediation systems apply a direct current (DC) electric field across contaminated soil to induce electromigration of metal cations toward a cathode, where they are deposited as metallic deposits. However, these systems require prohibitively high voltages—on the order of 100 volts per meter—to overcome the resistive nature of soil and drive sufficient ion mobility. The resulting energy consumption is substantial, and the process is plagued by parasitic reactions such as water electrolysis, which generates hydrogen and oxygen gases at the electrodes, elevates local pH, and promotes the precipitation of metal hydroxides rather than true metal reduction. These precipitates are non-conductive, poorly adherent, and prone to detachment, leading to inconsistent removal efficiency and rapid electrode passivation. Furthermore, under DC conditions, the negatively charged chelator-metal complexes—such as EDTA-bound copper or cadmium—experience strong electrostatic repulsion from the cathode, severely limiting their transport to the electrode surface and thus reducing the overall efficiency of metal recovery.

Recent efforts to improve electrochemical remediation have focused on modifying electrode surfaces with functional groups capable of enhancing metal affinity. Amidoxime groups, known for their strong chelating properties toward transition metals, have shown promise in aqueous metal capture applications, particularly in uranium extraction from seawater. However, their integration into soil remediation systems has not been previously explored, and their synergistic function within a dynamic, alternating current electrochemical environment has remained unaddressed. Crucially, no prior system has successfully combined the recirculation of chelating agents with an electrochemical filtration unit that not only captures metal-chelate complexes but also selectively reduces them to zero-valent metals while simultaneously rejecting nutrient ions based on their electrochemical reduction potentials.

There exists, therefore, a critical and unmet need for a remediation technology that simultaneously achieves: (1) efficient mobilization of heavy metals from the soil matrix using a reusable chelating agent; (2) selective and complete recovery of those metals from the washing effluent through a mechanism that overcomes the limitations of electrostatic repulsion and water splitting; (3) reduction of captured metal ions to their zero-valent, non-bioavailable, and recoverable metallic state; (4) retention of essential soil nutrients within the recirculating solution to prevent long-term soil degradation; and (5) operational sustainability through electrode regeneration, low energy consumption, and minimal chemical consumption. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical filtration system that integrates amidoxime-functionalized electrodes with a closed-loop washing circuit, enabling the first truly sustainable, efficient, and scalable solution to heavy metal soil contamination.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method and system for the remediation of heavy metal-contaminated soils through the synergistic integration of a recirculating chelating agent washing system and an asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical (AACE) filtration unit. The system is designed to extract heavy metal cations from contaminated soil using a chelating agent such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), mobilize them into anionic metal-chelate complexes, and then selectively capture and reduce these complexes to zero-valent metallic states on the surface of amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon electrodes. The key innovation lies in the application of an asymmetrical alternating voltage waveform to the electrodes, which alternates between a mild positive bias and a strong negative bias with precisely controlled durations, thereby overcoming the electrostatic repulsion of anionic metal-chelate complexes, promoting their adsorption onto the electrode surface, and enabling their efficient electrochemical reduction without triggering significant water splitting or nutrient ion extraction.

The method comprises the following essential steps: first, contaminated soil is placed in a treatment column and washed with a solution of chelating agent, which mobilizes heavy metal cations by forming stable, negatively charged metal-chelate complexes such as MEDTA²⁻. The washing effluent, now containing these complexes, is directed through an electrochemical filtration device comprising two parallel electrodes coated with amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon. The electrodes are subjected to an asymmetrical alternating current waveform, wherein the working electrode alternates between a first bias of approximately 5 volts for a duration of 0.5 milliseconds and a second bias of approximately −10 volts for a duration of 2 milliseconds, while the counter electrode remains grounded. During the positive bias phase, the amidoxime functional groups on the electrode surface competitively bind heavy metal cations from the chelate complexes, stabilizing them on the electrode and overcoming the Coulombic repulsion that would otherwise prevent their approach to the negatively charged electrode under direct current conditions. During the subsequent negative bias phase, the bound metal cations are reduced to their zero-valent metallic state, forming discrete, polycrystalline particles adherent to the electrode surface, while the EDTA anions are repelled due to their low affinity for the now charge-neutral metal deposits. Crucially, nutrient cations such as calcium, magnesium, sodium, and potassium, which may also be mobilized by the chelating agent, form analogous chelate complexes but are not reduced under the applied potential window due to their significantly lower standard reduction potentials. These nutrient ions remain in the washing solution and are returned to the soil during subsequent washing cycles, thereby preserving soil fertility and preventing long-term degradation.

The system enables continuous recirculation of the chelating agent solution, reducing chemical consumption by over 95% compared to conventional single-pass washing methods. The filtration efficiency of the AACE unit exceeds 90% for heavy metals such as copper, lead, and cadmium at optimized flow rates, and the system demonstrates consistent performance over multiple treatment cycles. The electrodes, after prolonged operation, may be regenerated through acid elution under a mild reverse bias, restoring their original capture and reduction capacity without structural degradation. Plant growth assays conducted on soil treated by the method confirm the absence of nutrient depletion, the near-complete elimination of bioavailable heavy metals, and the preservation of normal plant development, in stark contrast to soils treated with fresh chelating agent, which exhibit stunted growth and elevated metal uptake due to nutrient leaching. The metallic deposits recovered on the electrodes are in a pure, elemental form, suitable for direct recovery and recycling, transforming the remediation process into a resource recovery operation.

The invention further encompasses the synthesis of the amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon electrodes, which are fabricated by dip-coating a carbon felt substrate with a slurry of polyacrylonitrile and activated carbon, followed by a hydrothermal reaction with hydroxylamine hydrochloride to convert nitrile groups into amidoxime moieties. The resulting electrode exhibits a high specific surface area, excellent electrical conductivity, and superior hydrophilicity, enabling uniform wetting and maximal exposure of chelating sites. The combination of the asymmetric voltage waveform, the tailored electrode chemistry, and the closed-loop washing system constitutes a fundamentally new approach to soil remediation that achieves unprecedented levels of efficiency, sustainability, and selectivity.

The invention is distinguished from all prior art by its unique combination of features: the use of an asymmetrical alternating current waveform to overcome electrostatic repulsion of anionic metal-chelate complexes; the employment of amidoxime-functionalized electrodes to enhance metal capture and enable selective reduction; the preservation of soil nutrients through differential electrochemical reduction potentials; the recirculation of chelating agent with minimal loss; the production of zero-valent metal deposits suitable for recovery; and the demonstrable absence of soil degradation following treatment. The method is scalable, energy-efficient, and applicable to a wide range of contaminated soils, from urban brownfields to agricultural lands, and further extends to the treatment of industrial wastewater streams containing heavy metals. It represents a transformative advancement in environmental remediation technology, offering a practical, economically viable, and ecologically responsible solution to one of the most pressing environmental challenges of our time.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive and integrated system for the remediation of heavy metal-contaminated soils through a novel electrochemical mechanism that fundamentally redefines the relationship between chelation, electrochemical reduction, and nutrient retention. The system is composed of two primary functional units: a recirculating soil washing apparatus and an asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical (AACE) filtration device, each engineered to perform a specific and interdependent role in the overall remediation process. The invention is not merely an incremental improvement upon existing technologies but represents a paradigm shift in how heavy metal contamination is addressed, by transforming the remediation process from one of extraction and disposal into one of selective capture, transformation, and resource recovery.

The recirculating soil washing apparatus is designed to mobilize heavy metal cations from the soil matrix without causing irreversible damage to soil structure or nutrient content. Contaminated soil, excavated from the site of interest, is placed within a cylindrical treatment column constructed from inert, non-reactive materials such as high-density polyethylene or polypropylene. The soil is packed to a controlled bulk density of approximately 1.27 grams per cubic centimeter, ensuring consistent hydraulic conductivity and minimizing channeling during fluid flow. The column is flanked on either end by reservoirs containing a solution of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), typically at a concentration of 30 millimolar, adjusted to a neutral pH to optimize metal complexation while minimizing dissolution of soil minerals. A peristaltic pump circulates the EDTA solution through the soil column at a controlled flow rate, which is selected based on the level of contamination and the desired balance between treatment speed and filtration efficiency. As the solution percolates through the soil, it encounters heavy metal cations bound to clay particles, organic matter, and iron oxides. The EDTA molecules, through their six coordination sites, form stable, water-soluble, and negatively charged complexes with these metals—such as Cu(II)-EDTA²⁻, Pb(II)-EDTA²⁻, and Cd(II)-EDTA²⁻—thereby liberating them from the soil matrix and transporting them into the effluent stream. The flow rate is carefully regulated: too high a rate reduces the contact time between the chelator and the metal ions, resulting in incomplete extraction, while too low a rate increases the duration of treatment and the risk of side reactions such as microbial activity or chelator degradation. The optimal flow rate, determined empirically for each contamination profile, is that which achieves a filtration efficiency of at least 90% in the downstream AACE unit, as demonstrated in experimental trials with soils spiked with copper, lead, and cadmium at concentrations ranging from 100 to 10,000 parts per million.

Following the washing step, the effluent, now enriched with metal-EDTA complexes and potentially containing nutrient cations such as Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺, and K⁺, is directed into the AACE filtration device. This device consists of two parallel, planar electrodes separated by a porous, non-conductive separator—typically a non-woven tissue paper or a microporous polymer membrane—that prevents direct electrical contact while allowing free passage of ionic species. Each electrode is fabricated from a carbon felt substrate, chosen for its high porosity, mechanical resilience, and electrical conductivity. The surface of the carbon felt is modified through a multi-step coating and functionalization process to render it capable of selectively interacting with metal-chelate complexes. First, a slurry is prepared by mixing polyacrylonitrile (PAN), a synthetic polymer with a molecular weight of approximately 150,000, with activated carbon particles of approximately 40 nanometers in diameter and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) as a solvent, in a mass ratio of 1:1:30. This slurry is thoroughly stirred overnight to ensure homogeneity and then applied to the carbon felt via dip-coating. The coated felt is air-dried on a hotplate at 70 degrees Celsius to evaporate the solvent and form a uniform, adherent layer. Subsequently, the coated material is immersed in a hydrothermal reactor containing a solution of hydroxylamine hydrochloride at a concentration of 80 milligrams per milliliter and sodium carbonate at 60 milligrams per milliliter. The reactor is maintained at 70 degrees Celsius for 90 minutes, during which time the nitrile groups (–C≡N) of the PAN polymer undergo nucleophilic addition and cyclization reactions to form amidoxime functional groups (–C(=NOH)NH₂). The resulting electrode, referred to as amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon (Ami-PC), exhibits a hydrophilic surface that enhances wettability and ensures full utilization of the electrode’s internal surface area, while the amidoxime groups serve as high-affinity chelation sites for transition metal cations.

The Ami-PC electrodes are then connected to a programmable power supply capable of generating an asymmetrical alternating current waveform. The waveform is defined by two distinct voltage phases: a short-duration positive bias of +5 volts applied for 0.5 milliseconds, followed by a longer-duration negative bias of −10 volts applied for 2 milliseconds. The counter electrode is held at ground potential throughout the cycle. This specific waveform is not arbitrary but is the result of extensive optimization to achieve maximum metal recovery while minimizing energy consumption and parasitic reactions. During the +5 volt phase, the electrode surface becomes slightly positively charged, inducing the migration of anions—including the metal-EDTA complexes—toward the electrode. The amidoxime groups, with their oxygen and nitrogen donor atoms, compete with EDTA for binding to the metal cations within the complex. This ligand exchange reaction partially dissociates the EDTA-metal bond, releasing the metal cation into a more accessible coordination environment on the electrode surface. The EDTA anion, now freed from the metal center, remains in solution, while the metal ion is stabilized through coordination with the amidoxime ligands. This step is critical: under conventional DC conditions, the negatively charged MEDTA²⁻ complex would be electrostatically repelled by the cathode, preventing its approach and rendering electrochemical reduction impossible. The positive bias phase overcomes this fundamental limitation by temporarily reversing the surface charge, allowing the complex to adsorb and facilitating the subsequent reduction step.

During the −10 volt phase, the electrode potential is rapidly reversed, creating a strongly reducing environment. The metal cations, now loosely bound to the amidoxime sites, undergo a two-electron reduction to their zero-valent metallic state. For copper, this results in the formation of metallic Cu(0); for lead, Pb(0); and for cadmium, Cd(0). The reduction is sufficiently vigorous to overcome the kinetic barriers associated with metal deposition in complex matrices, and the resulting metal atoms nucleate and grow into discrete, polycrystalline particles ranging from hundreds of nanometers to several micrometers in size. These particles are firmly adherent to the electrode surface due to the high surface energy of the freshly formed metal and the physical entrapment within the porous carbon matrix. Simultaneously, the EDTA anions, which have no affinity for the now charge-neutral metal particles, are repelled by the negative electrode potential and return to the bulk solution. This selective repulsion is key to the system’s efficiency: it ensures that the EDTA is not consumed in the reduction process and remains available for reuse. The entire cycle—+5 V for 0.5 ms, −10 V for 2 ms—is repeated continuously, with a frequency of approximately 200 hertz, maintaining a steady-state condition in which metal capture and reduction occur in tandem with minimal energy input.

The system’s ability to preserve soil nutrients is a direct consequence of the electrochemical selectivity inherent in the applied potential window. Nutrient cations such as calcium, magnesium, and potassium are also mobilized by EDTA and form analogous chelate complexes—CaEDTA²⁻, MgEDTA²⁻, KEDTA⁻—which are transported to the AACE filter. However, the standard reduction potentials for these ions are far more negative than those of the target heavy metals: Ca²⁺/Ca is −2.87 volts, Mg²⁺/Mg is −2.37 volts, and K⁺/K is −2.93 volts, compared to Cu²⁺/Cu at +0.34 volts, Pb²⁺/Pb at −0.13 volts, and Cd²⁺/Cd at −0.40 volts. Under the applied −10 volt potential, the reduction of heavy metals proceeds readily, while the reduction of nutrient ions is thermodynamically and kinetically inhibited. As a result, these nutrient ions remain in solution and are returned to the soil during subsequent washing cycles. This dynamic equilibrium ensures that the soil’s native nutrient profile is preserved, preventing the long-term fertility loss that plagues conventional soil washing methods. Experimental data confirm that after multiple treatment cycles, the concentration of magnesium in the soil remains statistically unchanged, whereas soils treated with fresh EDTA exhibit a 40 to 60 percent reduction in available magnesium and calcium, leading to stunted plant growth and reduced crop yields.

The efficiency of the AACE system is further enhanced by the morphology and composition of the Ami-PC electrodes. The use of nano-sized activated carbon within the polymer matrix significantly improves the electrical conductivity of the electrode, reducing internal resistance and enabling uniform current distribution across the entire surface. The fiber diameter of the carbon felt, approximately 20 micrometers, combined with the pore size distribution ranging from tens to hundreds of micrometers, provides a high surface-area-to-volume ratio that maximizes the number of accessible amidoxime binding sites. Scanning electron microscopy confirms the homogeneous coating of amidoxime groups on the carbon fibers, with no observable delamination or degradation after repeated use. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy reveals that the metallic deposits formed on the electrode surface are composed almost exclusively of the target heavy metals, with negligible detection of calcium or other nutrient elements, in stark contrast to DC-based systems where calcium hydroxide precipitates dominate the electrode surface. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy further confirms the chemical state of the deposited metals: the Cu 2p₃/₂ peak at 932.8 electron volts, with a weak shake-up satellite structure, is characteristic of metallic copper, whereas the same peak under DC conditions appears at higher binding energy with pronounced satellite features indicative of Cu(II). Similar analyses for lead and cadmium reveal that the majority of the deposited species are in the zero-valent state, with only minor fractions remaining as divalent hydroxides or oxides.

The long-term performance of the system has been rigorously evaluated through 25 consecutive treatment cycles using soil spiked with 10,000 parts per million of copper. The extraction capacity of the EDTA solution, measured as the mass of copper removed per cycle, remained above 80 percent of its initial value after 25 cycles, demonstrating that the chelating agent does not degrade or become saturated with non-target species. The filtration efficiency of the AACE unit, defined as the percentage of metal cations recovered from the effluent, decreased gradually from 100 percent to approximately 90 percent after 14 cycles, primarily due to partial pore blockage and coverage of amidoxime sites by accumulated metal particles. However, this decline was fully reversible. Regeneration of the electrodes was achieved by flushing the system with 0.1 molar hydrochloric acid under a mild reverse bias of +1 volt for 30 minutes. This process dissolved the metallic deposits without damaging the underlying carbon or polymer matrix, as confirmed by post-regeneration scanning electron microscopy and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy. After regeneration, the filtration efficiency returned to 100 percent, and the electrode retained its structural integrity over multiple regeneration cycles, indicating that the system is not only sustainable but also maintainable over extended operational lifetimes.

The ecological safety of the method was validated through a plant assay using Pisum sativum, commonly known as garden pea. Treated soils, both those remediated by the AACE method and those treated with fresh EDTA, were mixed with coir at a 1:9 mass ratio to improve drainage and planted with pea seeds. After 15 days of growth, the plants were harvested and analyzed for heavy metal content in their roots, stems, and leaves. In soils treated with the AACE method, the cadmium concentration in all plant tissues was below 0.1 parts per million, meeting the safety threshold established by the Codex Alimentarius for edible vegetables. In contrast, plants grown in soils treated with fresh EDTA exhibited cadmium concentrations in the roots exceeding 200 parts per million and in the leaves exceeding 10 parts per million, indicating significant bioaccumulation. Moreover, the growth parameters of the plants—shoot height, leaf area, and dry biomass—were statistically indistinguishable between the AACE-treated soil and the uncontaminated control, whereas plants grown in fresh EDTA-treated soil showed marked stunting and chlorosis, consistent with nutrient deficiency. These results conclusively demonstrate that the AACE method does not degrade soil fertility and is suitable for agricultural land remediation, a critical advantage over all prior technologies.

The system’s applicability extends beyond soil remediation to the treatment of industrial effluents, mine tailings leachates, and wastewater streams containing heavy metals. The same AACE filtration unit, when coupled with a liquid circulation system, can treat aqueous streams without the need for soil matrix, making it adaptable to point-source pollution control. The recovered metallic deposits, which are pure, dense, and easily detachable, can be collected and processed for metal recovery, transforming the remediation process into a circular economy model. The low energy consumption—approximately 0.5 kilowatt-hours per cubic meter of treated effluent—combined with the near-zero chemical consumption, makes the system economically viable even in remote or resource-limited settings. The modular design allows for scalability from small, field-deployable units to large-scale industrial installations, and the absence of hazardous byproducts eliminates the need for costly waste disposal protocols.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, self-sustaining, and ecologically sound solution to the global problem of heavy metal soil contamination. By integrating a recirculating chelating agent system with an asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical filtration unit employing amidoxime-functionalized electrodes, the invention achieves the simultaneous goals of efficient metal removal, nutrient preservation, electrode regeneration, and resource recovery—all under low-voltage, low-energy conditions. The method is robust, repeatable, scalable, and environmentally benign, representing a transformative leap forward in environmental remediation technology. It is not merely an improvement upon existing methods but a fundamentally new paradigm that redefines what is possible in the treatment of contaminated land.
