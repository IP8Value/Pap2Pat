## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The acquisition, analysis, and interpretation of electrocardiographic data have long been foundational to the diagnosis and management of cardiac arrhythmias, ischemic events, and structural heart disease. Modern clinical electrocardiography relies almost universally on digital acquisition systems that convert the minute analog electrical signals generated by the heart into discrete numerical representations through analog-to-digital converters embedded within ECG machines. These digital representations are then processed, stored, displayed, and transmitted using proprietary formats that are typically closed, manufacturer-specific, and incompatible across platforms. While this paradigm has enabled efficient data handling and automated diagnostic algorithms, it has also created a fragmented ecosystem in which clinicians are effectively locked into the interpretive capabilities of a single vendor’s system. This limitation becomes particularly acute when dealing with complex or ambiguous ECG findings, where automated interpretations from one manufacturer may conflict with those from another, or where the diagnostic accuracy of a given algorithm is known to be suboptimal for certain pathologies. Despite decades of clinical experience and technological advancement, no standardized, universally accepted digital interchange format for ECG data has been adopted across the industry, and attempts to establish such interoperability have been consistently hindered by commercial interests, proprietary encryption, and lack of regulatory incentive.

Historically, efforts to overcome this fragmentation have taken two primary forms: digital protocol standardization and analog signal reconstruction. Early initiatives, such as those proposed by Bailey et al. in the 1970s and later refined by Willems et al. in the 1990s, sought to create open digital formats that would allow ECG data to be transmitted between devices regardless of manufacturer. While these efforts laid important conceptual groundwork, they ultimately failed to achieve widespread adoption due to resistance from manufacturers who viewed interoperability as a threat to their proprietary diagnostic software and market exclusivity. In parallel, alternative approaches emerged that circumvented digital incompatibility by returning to analog signal generation. One notable example is the method described by Miyahara et al. in 1984, which involved the painstaking manual regeneration of analog ECG waveforms from digital data using magnetic tape recorders and custom-built signal generators. Although this technique allowed for the refeeding of digitized ECGs into multiple interpretive machines, it was labor-intensive, prone to timing and amplitude errors, required specialized hardware that is now obsolete, and introduced redundant reference points that compromised signal fidelity. Moreover, the method was impractical for clinical use, especially in time-sensitive or resource-limited settings, and could not be scaled for routine application.

More recently, a commercially available system known as LifeSync® has been introduced to address a different clinical need: enhancing patient mobility in hospital settings by eliminating tethered lead wires. This system employs a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) to reconstruct analog ECG signals from proprietary digital inputs, allowing patients to move freely while maintaining continuous monitoring. However, its utility is severely constrained by its design: it accepts digital data exclusively from its own配套 analog-to-digital converter (ADC), and it does not support data transmission to external devices or remote interpretive platforms. Consequently, while LifeSync® serves a valuable niche in inpatient monitoring, it does not enable cross-manufacturer diagnostic interoperability, nor does it permit the use of ECG data collected on one vendor’s system to be analyzed by another vendor’s diagnostic software. Its architecture is fundamentally closed, and its functionality is inseparable from its own hardware ecosystem. As a result, it neither solves the problem of algorithmic bias nor facilitates the comparative analysis of ECG interpretations across platforms.

The limitations of existing approaches have left a critical gap in clinical practice: the inability to obtain independent, second opinions on ambiguous ECG findings without reacquiring the data on a different machine—a process that is often logistically impractical, time-consuming, and clinically infeasible in emergency, remote, or resource-constrained environments. Furthermore, the lack of a universal method to reconstruct analog ECG signals from digital files has hindered the development and validation of automated diagnostic algorithms, as manufacturers are unable to test their systems on a diverse, reproducible, and standardized set of inputs collected from competing devices. This has led to a stagnation in algorithmic improvement, with diagnostic accuracy remaining suboptimal for conditions such as bundle branch blocks, subtle ischemic changes, and arrhythmias with atypical morphology. The absence of a robust, vendor-neutral, and clinically validated DAC system has thus impeded both patient care and technological progress in electrocardiography.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel digital-to-analog conversion system capable of reconstructing high-fidelity analog ECG waveforms from any digital ECG data file, regardless of its original source or format. Unlike prior systems, this invention does not require proprietary hardware, manufacturer-specific data streams, or physical tethering to a single acquisition device. Instead, it operates as a universal interface that accepts digitized ECG data from any commercial or research-grade ECG machine, converts it into an optimized open digital format, and then generates analog signals that are electrically and temporally indistinguishable from those originally recorded from the patient’s body. These reconstructed signals can then be fed into any standard 12-lead ECG machine for re-digitization and automated interpretation, thereby enabling clinicians to obtain multiple diagnostic opinions from different manufacturers’ algorithms without reacquiring the data. The system is designed to function both locally and remotely, supports cloud-based data processing, and is compatible with emerging high-fidelity ADC technologies that employ true simultaneous sampling. Its architecture is modular, scalable, and intentionally vendor-agnostic, making it uniquely suited to bridge the divide between proprietary ECG ecosystems and promote a more collaborative, evidence-based approach to cardiac diagnostics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is a system and method for the faithful reconstruction of analog electrocardiographic signals from digitized 12-lead ECG data, enabling the subsequent re-digitization and automated interpretation of those signals by any brand or model of electrocardiograph machine, irrespective of the original source of the digital data. The system comprises a software module for the conversion of proprietary or non-standard digital ECG formats into a unified, open, and optimized digital representation that preserves the temporal, amplitude, and reference characteristics of the original analog signals; a hardware interface that includes multiple high-precision digital-to-analog converters, each independently driven to generate nine distinct analog voltage outputs corresponding to the standard 12-lead ECG configuration; and a signal conditioning circuit that ensures the electrical integrity, impedance matching, and noise suppression necessary for clinical-grade signal fidelity. The system is designed to operate without reliance on any manufacturer-specific hardware, firmware, or communication protocols, and it does not require access to proprietary diagnostic algorithms or internal data structures of any ECG machine.

The core innovation lies in the method by which the system reconstructs the original analog signals from digital data collected using any standard 12-lead ECG acquisition system. Rather than attempting to replicate the internal signal processing of the original device, the system recognizes that the fundamental electrical relationships between the 10 electrodes used in a 12-lead ECG—left arm, right arm, left leg, right leg, and six chest electrodes—can be mathematically and electrically reconstituted through a carefully structured digital-to-analog transformation. The system operates on the principle that, for any given digital ECG dataset, the relative voltages between the electrodes can be preserved and re-expressed in a reference frame that is compatible with the input requirements of any receiving ECG machine. Specifically, the system converts the original digital data into a format in which all chest leads are referenced to the right arm electrode, while simultaneously setting the right arm electrode input of the reconstruction system to a zero-voltage reference. This configuration ensures that when the reconstructed analog signals are fed into a standard 12-lead ECG machine, the machine’s internal circuitry will correctly compute the standard leads I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF, and V1 through V6 without requiring any external adjustment or calibration. Alternative reference configurations, such as referencing all leads to the left arm or left leg electrode while setting the corresponding input to zero, are also supported and may be selected based on the characteristics of the original data or the requirements of the receiving device.

The system further includes a software layer that performs automated format translation, ensuring compatibility with any known digital ECG file structure, including but not limited to those conforming to the ANSI/AAMI EC13, HL7, or DICOM standards, as well as proprietary formats from manufacturers such as GE Healthcare, Philips, Schiller, Mortara, and Cardiax. This translation process involves parsing the original data, identifying the electrode reference scheme, correcting for sampling rate discrepancies, applying amplitude scaling factors, and aligning temporal markers to preserve the precise timing of P-waves, QRS complexes, and T-waves. The output of this software layer is a standardized digital stream that is optimized for high-fidelity analog reconstruction, with each of the eight independent channels mapped to a dedicated digital-to-analog converter channel. The hardware component employs 16-bit or higher resolution DACs with sampling rates of at least 1,000 samples per second per channel, low output impedance, and galvanic isolation to prevent ground loops and ensure patient safety. The analog outputs are routed through precision passive filtering networks to remove quantization noise and high-frequency artifacts, and the resulting signals are delivered via standard ECG lead connectors to any receiving ECG machine.

The system is designed for both local and remote deployment. In a local configuration, the system may be integrated into a desktop workstation or portable device that accepts digital ECG files via USB, network, or wireless transmission, performs the conversion and reconstruction in real time, and outputs the analog signals directly to an adjacent ECG machine. In a remote configuration, the system may be hosted on a cloud-based server that receives digital ECG data via encrypted internet transmission, performs the conversion and reconstruction on demand, and streams the analog signals over a secure, low-latency connection to a remote DAC unit located near the receiving ECG machine. This enables clinicians in underserved or remote locations—such as military field units, offshore oil platforms, polar research stations, or space habitats—to transmit ECG data collected on low-cost or compact acquisition devices to a central diagnostic hub, where high-fidelity reconstructions are generated and analyzed using advanced diagnostic software unavailable at the point of collection.

The system has been validated through quantitative and qualitative studies demonstrating that the reconstructed analog signals are indistinguishable from the original signals in terms of waveform morphology, amplitude, timing, and diagnostic interpretation. When the same ECG machine is used for both original acquisition and re-digitization, the root mean square difference between the original and reconstructed waveforms is less than 21 microvolts across all channels. When re-digitization is performed using a different manufacturer’s machine, the difference remains below 29 microvolts, a level well within the acceptable clinical tolerance for diagnostic accuracy. Automated diagnostic algorithms from multiple vendors, including proprietary systems and the well-validated Leuven algorithm, produce identical diagnostic statements for the original and reconstructed data in all but one case out of ten, and even in that case, the discrepancy was minor and unlikely to affect clinical decision-making. The system has also been shown to perform reliably with challenging ECG patterns, including bundle branch blocks, ischemic changes, and arrhythmias, and its fidelity improves significantly when used in conjunction with modern ECG machines that employ true simultaneous sampling ADCs.

The invention provides a transformative capability for clinical electrocardiology by enabling universal interoperability between disparate ECG systems, facilitating second opinions from multiple diagnostic algorithms, improving the accuracy and consistency of automated interpretations, reducing the cost and complexity of ECG acquisition in resource-limited settings, and promoting the development of more robust diagnostic software through access to diverse, reproducible, and standardized input data. It eliminates the need for duplicate data collection, removes vendor lock-in, and establishes a new paradigm in which ECG interpretation is no longer constrained by the limitations of a single manufacturer’s hardware or software. The system is compliant with all relevant medical device regulations, incorporates patient safety features such as galvanic isolation and current limiting, and is designed for integration into existing clinical workflows with minimal disruption. It represents a significant advancement in the field of cardiac diagnostics and has the potential to improve patient outcomes, reduce diagnostic errors, and accelerate innovation across the global electrocardiography community.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The preferred embodiment of the invention is a comprehensive, integrated system for the digital-to-analog reconstruction of 12-lead electrocardiographic signals, designed to operate with complete independence from any specific manufacturer’s hardware, software, or data format. The system is composed of three primary functional components: a software-based digital data translator, a hardware-based analog signal generator, and a signal conditioning and delivery subsystem. These components are interconnected through a standardized communication protocol and operate in a coordinated sequence to ensure the faithful reproduction of the original analog ECG waveforms with clinical-grade fidelity. The system is designed to be deployed in both stationary and mobile environments, and its modular architecture allows for scalability, remote operation, and integration with existing clinical information systems.

The software component of the system is responsible for the ingestion, parsing, normalization, and transformation of digital ECG data from any source. It accepts input files in a wide variety of formats, including but not limited to those defined by the American National Standards Institute and the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation (ANSI/AAMI EC13), the Health Level Seven (HL7) standard for medical data exchange, the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standard for medical imaging, and numerous proprietary formats used by commercial ECG manufacturers such as GE Healthcare, Philips, Schiller, Mortara, CorScience, and Cardiax. The software module is implemented as a cross-platform application that runs on general-purpose computing hardware, including desktop computers, embedded systems, and cloud-based virtual machines. Upon receiving a digital ECG file, the software first performs a header analysis to determine the sampling rate, bit depth, electrode configuration, reference scheme, and timestamping method used by the original acquisition device. It then identifies the nature of the digital data channels—whether they represent leads I, II, V1–V6 referenced to Wilson’s central terminal, or whether they are expressed in an alternative reference frame such as right arm, left arm, or left leg. This identification is critical, as the subsequent reconstruction process depends on the correct interpretation of the reference node.

Once the reference scheme is determined, the software applies a mathematical transformation to convert the data into a standardized format in which all chest leads (V1–V6) are referenced to the right arm electrode, and the right arm electrode itself is assigned a fixed voltage of zero volts. This transformation is based on the known electrical relationships between the 10 electrodes in a standard 12-lead ECG configuration. For example, if the original data are referenced to Wilson’s central terminal, the software computes the equivalent voltages relative to the right arm by subtracting the Wilson’s central terminal voltage from each of the limb and chest leads, then reassigning the right arm voltage to zero. Similarly, if the original data are referenced to the left arm, the software performs an analogous transformation, shifting all voltages relative to the left arm and then setting the left arm voltage to zero. The system supports multiple reference configurations, and the user may select the most appropriate one based on the characteristics of the original data or the requirements of the receiving ECG machine. The software also corrects for differences in sampling rate between the original data and the reconstruction system, applying linear interpolation or spline-based resampling as necessary to ensure temporal alignment without introducing phase distortion or waveform artifacts.

The output of the software module is a standardized digital stream consisting of eight independent channels, each corresponding to one of the standard ECG leads: lead I, lead II, and leads V1 through V6. Each channel is represented as a sequence of 16-bit signed integer values, sampled at a rate of at least 1,000 samples per second, with a full-scale range of ±5 millivolts. This format is optimized for high-fidelity analog reconstruction and is designed to be compatible with commercially available digital-to-analog converter chips that support multi-channel synchronous output. The software also embeds metadata into the output stream, including timestamps, patient identifiers (anonymized for privacy), sampling rate, and reference scheme, which are used by the hardware component to synchronize the reconstruction process and ensure temporal accuracy. The software is capable of batch processing multiple files simultaneously, and it includes error-checking routines to detect corrupted data, missing channels, or inconsistent timing. It also provides a graphical interface for visual inspection of the original and transformed waveforms, allowing the user to verify the integrity of the conversion before initiating reconstruction.

The hardware component of the system consists of a multi-channel digital-to-analog converter array, a signal conditioning circuit, and a patient-safe output interface. The DAC array is implemented using high-resolution, low-noise, synchronized DAC chips such as the Texas Instruments DAC8568 or equivalent, which provide 16-bit resolution, a maximum sampling rate of 2,000 samples per second per channel, and built-in output buffering. Each of the eight digital channels from the software module is routed to a dedicated DAC channel, ensuring that no channel is multiplexed or time-shared, which could introduce timing jitter or amplitude distortion. The DAC outputs are connected to a passive analog filtering network composed of low-pass filters with a cutoff frequency of 150 Hz, designed to remove quantization noise and high-frequency artifacts introduced during the digital-to-analog conversion process. These filters are implemented using precision resistors and capacitors with tight tolerances to ensure consistent frequency response across all channels. The filtered analog signals are then passed through a set of unity-gain, high-input-impedance operational amplifiers to provide isolation and drive capability for the output leads.

The output interface is designed to connect directly to the input terminals of any standard 12-lead ECG machine. It provides nine distinct analog output channels: one for each of the eight standard leads (I, II, V1–V6), and one additional channel for the right leg electrode, which serves as the common reference and is connected to the patient’s right leg via a standard ECG lead wire. The right leg electrode output is held at a fixed voltage of zero volts, as required by the reconstruction method, and is electrically isolated from the other channels to prevent ground loops and ensure patient safety. The entire analog output circuit is galvanically isolated from the digital and computing components of the system using optocouplers or isolation transformers, in accordance with the IEC 60601-1 standard for medical electrical equipment. Current-limiting resistors are incorporated into each output path to ensure that, in the event of a fault, the maximum current delivered to the patient does not exceed 10 microamperes, well below the threshold for electrical hazard. The output connectors are standard 4-mm banana plugs or 5-pin DIN connectors, depending on the input requirements of the receiving ECG machine, and are color-coded and labeled in accordance with international ECG lead conventions.

The system is designed to operate in two primary modes: local and remote. In the local mode, the software and hardware components are integrated into a single, compact device that may be placed adjacent to an ECG machine in a clinical setting. The digital ECG file is transferred to the device via USB, SD card, or wired network connection, and the reconstruction process is initiated by the user through a simple graphical interface. Within seconds, the system generates the analog signals and outputs them to the ECG machine, which then digitizes and interprets the signal as if it had been collected directly from the patient. In the remote mode, the software component is hosted on a secure, cloud-based server that receives encrypted digital ECG files via a secure internet connection. The server performs the digital-to-digital conversion and transmits the optimized digital stream to a remote DAC unit located near the receiving ECG machine. This remote DAC unit, which may be deployed in a clinic, ambulance, or remote outpost, receives the digital stream over a low-latency, encrypted connection and performs the analog reconstruction locally. This configuration enables the use of low-cost, compact ECG acquisition devices in resource-limited environments—such as military field units, oceanic oil platforms, or space missions—to transmit high-fidelity ECG data to centralized diagnostic centers where advanced interpretive software is available. The remote system supports real-time streaming, allowing for continuous monitoring and immediate diagnostic feedback, and it includes redundancy features such as data encryption, checksum verification, and automatic retransmission to ensure data integrity.

The system also incorporates a calibration and validation protocol to ensure consistent performance across different hardware units and environmental conditions. Prior to each use, the system performs an automated self-test that includes a zero-voltage offset check, a full-scale amplitude verification, a timing synchronization test, and a noise floor measurement. These tests are conducted using internal precision voltage references and high-stability clock sources, and the results are logged and stored for audit purposes. The system also includes a library of reference ECG waveforms—representing normal sinus rhythm, atrial fibrillation, left bundle branch block, right bundle branch block, ST-segment elevation, and other common pathologies—that are used to validate the fidelity of the reconstruction process. If any parameter falls outside of predefined tolerances, the system will not initiate reconstruction and will alert the user to perform maintenance or recalibration.

The system is designed to be compatible with the latest advancements in ECG acquisition technology, particularly those employing true simultaneous sampling ADCs such as the Texas Instruments ADS1298. When used in conjunction with such devices for re-digitization, the system demonstrates a significant improvement in reconstruction fidelity, with root mean square differences between original and reconstructed waveforms reduced by a factor of two to three compared to systems using conventional time-interleaved ADCs. This improvement is attributed to the elimination of sampling skew and timing jitter inherent in non-simultaneous sampling architectures. The system’s software includes a mode that automatically detects the sampling characteristics of the receiving ECG machine and adjusts its output parameters accordingly, ensuring optimal performance regardless of the re-digitization hardware used.

The system is fully compliant with applicable medical device regulations, including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s guidelines for Class II medical devices, the European Union’s Medical Device Regulation (MDR) 2017/745, and the International Electrotechnical Commission’s IEC 60601-1 standard for electrical safety and electromagnetic compatibility. It includes features such as patient isolation, current limiting, leakage current monitoring, and fail-safe shutdown to ensure that it poses no risk to human subjects. The software is developed using secure coding practices, and all data transmissions are encrypted using AES-256 encryption. The system does not store or transmit any personally identifiable information, and patient data are anonymized prior to processing. The system is designed for use by trained clinical personnel and does not require specialized engineering expertise to operate.

In clinical practice, the system is intended to be used in scenarios where a second opinion on an ambiguous ECG is required, where access to advanced diagnostic software is limited, or where ECG data must be transmitted across disparate systems. For example, a clinician in a rural clinic may collect a 12-lead ECG using a low-cost, portable device and transmit the digital file to a tertiary care center, where the system reconstructs the analog signal and feeds it into a high-end ECG machine equipped with a sophisticated diagnostic algorithm. The resulting interpretation is then returned to the clinician, enabling a more accurate diagnosis without the need for patient transfer. Similarly, in a research setting, a large database of ECG recordings collected on multiple manufacturers’ devices can be converted into a standardized format and analyzed using a single diagnostic algorithm, eliminating variability introduced by hardware differences. The system also enables manufacturers to test and refine their diagnostic algorithms using a diverse, reproducible set of inputs collected from competing devices, thereby accelerating innovation and improving diagnostic accuracy across the industry.

The system’s architecture is intentionally open and extensible, allowing for future enhancements such as integration with artificial intelligence models, real-time telemedicine platforms, and wearable ECG monitoring systems. It does not rely on proprietary protocols or locked-in hardware, and its open digital format ensures long-term compatibility with evolving technologies. The system represents a paradigm shift in electrocardiography, transforming the ECG from a vendor-locked diagnostic tool into a universally accessible, interoperable, and reproducible clinical resource. It empowers clinicians to make more informed decisions, reduces diagnostic uncertainty, and promotes a more equitable and evidence-based approach to cardiac care.
