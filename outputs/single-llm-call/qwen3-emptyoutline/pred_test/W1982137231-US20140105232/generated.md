## BACKGROUND

The pursuit of ultra-stable optical frequency references has long been a cornerstone of precision measurement science, quantum information processing, and high-capacity optical communications. Traditional approaches to achieving high spectral purity in laser systems have relied heavily on passive optical cavities constructed from bulk optical components, such as fused silica disks or Fabry-Perot interferometers, suspended in vacuum and thermally stabilized to sub-millikelvin levels. These systems have demonstrated remarkable frequency stability, with Allan deviations approaching the 10⁻¹⁵ level and linewidths below 1 Hz, but they suffer from inherent limitations in size, weight, power consumption, and susceptibility to environmental perturbations. The need for cryogenic cooling, active vibration isolation, and complex feedback electronics renders them impractical for deployment in mobile, field-based, or space-constrained environments. Moreover, the scalability of such systems to mass production remains elusive due to the labor-intensive assembly processes and stringent material requirements. In recent years, significant progress has been made in the development of integrated photonic resonators fabricated on silicon or silicon-on-insulator platforms, offering the promise of miniaturization, robustness, and compatibility with complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor fabrication techniques. However, these early integrated devices were plagued by high optical propagation losses, limited mode volumes, and pronounced sensitivity to thermorefractive and photo-thermal noise, which fundamentally restricted their achievable quality factors and frequency stability. Even the most advanced chip-scale resonators prior to this invention typically exhibited quality factors below ten million and linewidths on the order of several kilohertz, rendering them unsuitable for applications requiring coherence times exceeding milliseconds. A critical bottleneck in this domain has been the intrinsic trade-off between resonator size and optical loss: longer optical path lengths are necessary to increase mode volume and reduce noise density, but they also increase the probability of scattering and absorption losses along the waveguide, thereby degrading the quality factor. Conventional planar waveguide designs, such as racetrack or ring resonators, are fundamentally constrained by their small cross-sectional dimensions and tight bending radii, which induce mode confinement that amplifies the interaction between the optical field and the thermal fluctuations of the host material. Furthermore, the coupling mechanisms employed in these systems—typically evanescent or grating-based—introduce additional loss channels and modal distortions that further limit performance. The emergence of optical fiber-based reference cavities, where kilometers of low-loss fiber are coiled to form a high-Q resonator, demonstrated that thermorefractive noise could be suppressed by orders of magnitude through geometric scaling, but these systems remain bulky, fragile, and incompatible with integrated photonic circuits. The challenge, therefore, has been to achieve the noise suppression benefits of long optical path lengths without sacrificing the compactness, manufacturability, and robustness of a chip-scale platform. No prior art has successfully resolved this fundamental conflict by simultaneously achieving a mode volume exceeding 10⁻¹² m³, a propagation loss below 0.05 dB/m, a quality factor surpassing 100 million, and a physical footprint smaller than 10 cm²—all within a single, monolithically fabricated device. The present invention overcomes these longstanding limitations through a novel resonator geometry, a precisely engineered waveguide structure, and an optimized coupling architecture that collectively decouple the constraints of size, loss, and noise, thereby enabling a new generation of chip-integrated frequency references with performance previously thought possible only in laboratory-scale optical fiber systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel class of on-chip optical resonators designed for ultra-low-noise laser frequency stabilization, characterized by a spiral waveguide geometry that achieves an exceptionally long optical path length within a compact physical footprint, coupled with ultra-low-loss waveguide fabrication techniques that preserve high optical quality factors while simultaneously minimizing the density of thermorefractive, photo-thermal, and thermo-mechanical noise sources. The resonator comprises two interleaved spiral waveguides arranged in a concentric, counter-winding configuration, connected at the center by a pair of adiabatic S-turn couplers that enable efficient mode transfer with minimal scattering loss and effective spatial filtering of higher-order transverse modes. The waveguide core is formed from a low-loss dielectric material, preferably silicon dioxide deposited on a silicon substrate, with a cross-sectional dimension optimized to suppress both linear and nonlinear absorption mechanisms while maintaining single-mode operation over the entire length of the spiral. The total optical path length of the resonator exceeds one meter, with embodiments ranging from four centimeters to over one hundred centimeters, and the resulting mode volume is sufficiently large to reduce the optical intensity per unit volume to levels that substantially suppress photo-thermal heating and the associated refractive index fluctuations. The quality factor of the resonator exceeds one hundred million, with demonstrated values reaching one hundred and forty million, and is primarily limited by the intrinsic material absorption and the cumulative loss of the adiabatic couplers rather than by scattering or bending losses. The optical coupling to the resonator is achieved via a tapered optical fiber positioned at a corner of the chip, enabling efficient evanescent coupling with minimal back-reflection and alignment sensitivity. The resonator is fabricated using a planar lithographic process that employs a contact aligner for patterning, with subsequent etching and annealing steps that reduce surface roughness and eliminate defect-induced scattering centers. When used to stabilize a laser system via a Pound-Drever-Hall locking scheme, the resonator enables a reduction in the laser linewidth by more than an order of magnitude compared to conventional chip-scale resonators and by a factor of four compared to the best-performing disk resonators of similar footprint. The phase noise of the locked laser system exhibits a 1/f³ spectral dependence at low offset frequencies, consistent with the theoretical signature of thermorefractive noise, yet the amplitude of this noise is reduced by more than 20 decibels relative to state-of-the-art microdisk resonators. The Allan deviation of the stabilized laser frequency reaches a minimum of five point five times ten to the negative thirteenth at a gate time of four hundred microseconds, corresponding to an equivalent frequency stability of seventy-five hertz for a single laser, and remains below the noise floor of conventional crystal oscillators for integration times up to one second. Critically, the resonator demonstrates negligible sensitivity to thermo-mechanical noise, with mechanical resonances below the detection threshold even at frequencies exceeding one megahertz, a consequence of the inverse-square dependence of optomechanical coupling on cavity length. The entire device occupies a footprint smaller than five point four square centimeters, making it compatible with standard semiconductor packaging and enabling integration into portable, battery-operated systems. Unlike prior art, no external thermal stabilization, vibration isolation, or drift correction is required to achieve this level of performance, and the device operates stably under ambient laboratory conditions. The invention thus provides a compact, scalable, and manufacturable solution for generating optical frequency references with coherence properties rivaling those of kilometer-long fiber delays, thereby enabling new applications in quantum sensing, optical atomic clocks, coherent communications, and precision metrology on a chip.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for the design, fabrication, and implementation of spiral optical resonators that achieve unprecedented levels of frequency stability in a chip-scale format. The core innovation lies in the synergistic integration of three interdependent elements: a long-path spiral geometry, an ultra-low-loss waveguide structure, and an adiabatic coupling topology that collectively suppress the dominant noise mechanisms that have historically limited the performance of integrated optical resonators. The spiral resonator is not a mere elongation of a conventional ring resonator but a fundamentally reconfigured optical architecture in which the path length is extended not through increased radius but through controlled, multi-turn coiling that maintains a uniform bending curvature and minimizes mode distortion. The waveguide core is formed from a high-purity silicon dioxide layer deposited via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, with a thickness of approximately 500 nanometers and a refractive index of 1.45 at the operating wavelength of 1550 nanometers. The cladding layer, composed of a lower-index silicon dioxide or polymer material, provides a refractive index contrast sufficient to confine the optical mode while minimizing surface scattering. The waveguide width is precisely controlled to be approximately 700 nanometers, a dimension chosen to ensure single-mode operation across the entire spectral range of interest while maintaining a mode field diameter that is sufficiently large to reduce the power density within the material. The spiral is composed of two concentric, counter-rotating paths that are interleaved to maximize the optical path length within a minimal area. Each spiral arm completes approximately 18 full turns, with a turn-to-turn spacing of 10 micrometers, resulting in a total physical path length of 120 centimeters for the longest embodiment. The inner and outer radii of the spiral are carefully selected to ensure that the bending loss remains below 10⁻⁸ dB per turn, a value that is negligible compared to the intrinsic material loss. At the center of the spiral, the two waveguides are connected via a pair of S-turn couplers, each consisting of a smooth, sinusoidal bend with a radius of curvature exceeding 500 micrometers. These S-turns are not merely transition elements but are engineered to function as spatial filters, selectively suppressing higher-order transverse modes that would otherwise couple into the fundamental mode and degrade the quality factor. The adiabatic nature of these transitions ensures that the optical field evolves smoothly from one waveguide to the other without generating back-scattering or mode conversion, thereby preserving the coherence of the circulating light. The input and output coupling ports are located at the upper-right corner of the chip, where a tapered optical fiber, fabricated by heating and pulling a standard single-mode fiber to a waist diameter of 1.5 micrometers, is brought into close proximity to the spiral’s end facet. The taper is aligned with sub-micron precision using a piezoelectric nanopositioning stage, and the coupling efficiency is optimized by adjusting the gap between the fiber and the waveguide to approximately 200 nanometers. This evanescent coupling mechanism provides a coupling ratio of approximately 20%, which is sufficient to achieve critical coupling without introducing additional loss or nonlinear effects. The entire device is fabricated on a silicon substrate using a combination of photolithography, reactive ion etching, and thermal annealing. The lithographic patterning is performed using a contact aligner with a resolution of 1 micrometer, which, while less precise than projection lithography, is sufficient for this application due to the tolerance of the spiral geometry to minor dimensional variations. After patterning, the waveguide is etched using a fluorocarbon-based plasma to achieve vertical sidewalls with a roughness below 1 nanometer root-mean-square. A subsequent high-temperature anneal at 1100 degrees Celsius in an oxygen ambient is employed to heal surface defects and reduce the density of sub-bandgap absorption centers, resulting in a propagation loss of 0.037 decibels per meter, the lowest ever reported for a chip-scale optical resonator of this geometry. The device is then passivated with a thin layer of silicon nitride to protect against environmental humidity and surface contamination. The resulting resonator exhibits a free spectral range of 173 megahertz, consistent with the theoretical prediction based on the round-trip optical path length and the group refractive index of the waveguide. The measured quality factor of 140 million is determined by fitting the Lorentzian lineshape of the transmission resonance, with a linewidth of 1.4 kilohertz. The absence of spurious resonances in the transmission spectrum, even at high optical powers, confirms the effectiveness of the S-turn couplers in suppressing higher-order modes. The device demonstrates exceptional stability under ambient conditions, with no measurable drift over periods exceeding 24 hours, even without active temperature control. The optical power circulating within the resonator is maintained at approximately 1 milliwatt, corresponding to a power density of less than 100 watts per square centimeter, which is an order of magnitude lower than that in conventional microdisk resonators of similar mode volume. This low power density is a direct consequence of the large mode volume, which reduces the photo-thermal heating rate and thereby minimizes the refractive index fluctuations driven by local temperature gradients. The resulting thermorefractive noise is suppressed by more than 20 decibels compared to a 3-millimeter-diameter disk resonator fabricated from the same material and under identical environmental conditions. The phase noise of the heterodyne signal between two such stabilized lasers exhibits a 1/f³ dependence below 1 kilohertz, indicating that the dominant noise source is indeed thermorefractive in origin, yet its amplitude is reduced to levels comparable to those achieved by kilometer-long fiber delays. The Allan deviation, measured using a zero-dead-time frequency counter, reaches a minimum of 5.5 × 10⁻¹³ at a gate time of 400 microseconds, corresponding to a frequency stability of 75 hertz for a single laser. This value is ten times lower than that of the free-running laser and four times lower than that of the best-performing microdisk resonator. The rise in Allan deviation beyond 10 milliseconds is attributed to environmental fluctuations, such as air currents and electromagnetic interference, and not to any intrinsic limitation of the resonator itself. The device demonstrates no detectable thermo-mechanical noise, even at frequencies up to 1 megahertz, as confirmed by the absence of resonant peaks in the phase noise spectrum and by direct mechanical excitation measurements using the Hänsch-Couillard technique. The optomechanical coupling parameter, which scales inversely with the square of the cavity length, is reduced to such a low value in the 120-centimeter spiral that even high-amplitude mechanical vibrations fail to induce measurable phase modulation. The entire system, including the laser, fiber taper, and resonator chip, occupies a volume smaller than 100 cubic centimeters and requires no external cooling, vibration isolation, or active feedback beyond the standard Pound-Drever-Hall servo loop. This level of integration and robustness represents a paradigm shift in the field of optical frequency references, enabling applications that were previously confined to laboratory environments to be deployed in field settings, aboard satellites, or within portable instrumentation.

### EXAMPLE 1

In a first embodiment of the invention, a spiral optical resonator is fabricated with a total optical path length of 4.5 centimeters, comprising eight complete turns of a silicon dioxide waveguide with a width of 700 nanometers and a height of 500 nanometers. The waveguide is patterned on a silicon substrate using a contact aligner with a resolution of 1 micrometer, followed by reactive ion etching to achieve vertical sidewalls with a surface roughness of less than 1 nanometer. The turn-to-turn spacing is maintained at 10 micrometers, and the inner and outer radii of the spiral are 1.2 millimeters and 3.1 millimeters, respectively. The S-turn couplers at the center of the spiral are formed with a radius of curvature of 600 micrometers and a length of 1.5 millimeters, ensuring adiabatic mode transfer with a transmission loss of less than 0.02 decibels per coupler. The optical coupling to the resonator is achieved using a tapered fiber with a waist diameter of 1.5 micrometers, positioned at a gap of 200 nanometers from the waveguide end facet. The quality factor of the resonator is measured to be 25 million, with a free spectral range of 4.6 gigahertz, consistent with the expected value based on the round-trip path length and the group refractive index of the waveguide. The transmission spectrum exhibits a single dominant resonance with no observable higher-order modes, confirming the effectiveness of the S-turn couplers in spatial filtering. The phase noise of the heterodyne signal between two such resonators, each locked to a separate fiber laser operating at 1550 nanometers, is measured using a phase-noise analyzer with a resolution bandwidth of 10 hertz. At an offset frequency of 1 kilohertz, the phase noise is measured at −115 decibels per hertz, representing a suppression of 22 decibels compared to the free-running laser. The Allan deviation, measured over gate times ranging from 1 microsecond to 1 second, reaches a minimum of 1.8 × 10⁻¹² at a gate time of 200 microseconds. This embodiment demonstrates that even at a reduced path length, the spiral geometry provides superior noise suppression compared to conventional microdisk resonators of similar footprint, confirming the scalability of the design principle. The device operates stably under ambient conditions with no measurable drift over 48 hours, and the optical power required for locking is less than 1 milliwatt, making it suitable for low-power applications such as portable atomic clocks or field-deployable sensors.

### EXAMPLE 2

In a second embodiment, the spiral resonator is extended to a total optical path length of 8.7 centimeters, comprising 15 turns with a turn-to-turn spacing of 10 micrometers and a waveguide width of 700 nanometers. The S-turn couplers are redesigned with a slightly larger radius of curvature of 700 micrometers to accommodate the increased mode size and reduce any residual mode mismatch. The waveguide is fabricated using the same process as in Example 1, with a post-etch anneal at 1100 degrees Celsius to minimize material absorption. The Allan deviation reaches a minimum of 9.2 × 10⁻¹³ at a gate time of 300 microseconds, corresponding to a single-laser stability of 65 hertz. The device is tested under varying environmental conditions, including temperature fluctuations of ±5 degrees Celsius and acoustic noise levels up to 80 decibels, and exhibits no degradation in performance, confirming its robustness. The optical power required for locking remains below 1 milliwatt, and the system achieves lock acquisition within 50 milliseconds, making it suitable for dynamic applications requiring rapid relocking.

The entire system, including the laser, comb generator, spiral resonator, and electronic divider, is fabricated on a single silicon chip using standard CMOS-compatible processes. The output signal exhibits an Allan deviation of 3.1 × 10⁻¹³ at 1 second, making it suitable for use in portable atomic clocks, quantum computing control systems, and secure communication networks.
