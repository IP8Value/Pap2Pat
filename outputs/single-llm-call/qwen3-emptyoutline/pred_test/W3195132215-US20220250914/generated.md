# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of advanced carbon materials and sustainable industrial feedstock utilization, specifically to a novel method for the production of high-quality graphene-family nanomaterials from petroleum coke, a low-value by-product of petroleum refining. The invention provides a scalable, electrochemical exfoliation process that transforms needle coke into structurally defined, few-layer graphene nanosheets with controlled morphology, high crystallinity, and enhanced electrical conductivity. This method enables the repurposing of abundant, low-value petroleum streams—historically combusted or used in high-emission industrial processes—into high-value nanomaterials suitable for next-generation energy storage, flexible electronics, structural composites, and transparent conductive films. The invention further encompasses post-processing thermal treatments that significantly improve the electrical performance of the derived graphene material, rendering it competitive with or superior to conventional graphite-derived graphene in specific application contexts. The process is uniquely adapted to the physical and chemical characteristics of petroleum coke, leveraging its inherent layered aromatic structure while overcoming the limitations of prior attempts to exfoliate coke into graphene, which have yielded only quantum dots, oxidized derivatives, or poorly defined carbonaceous debris. The invention thus bridges the gap between fossil fuel refining infrastructure and the emerging nanomaterials economy, offering a commercially viable pathway to decarbonize petrochemical waste streams while simultaneously generating high-margin products.

## BACKGROUND

The global petrochemical industry generates vast quantities of solid by-products, among which petroleum coke stands out as one of the most problematic in terms of environmental impact and economic underutilization. Petroleum coke is produced during the thermal cracking of heavy residual oils in coker units, where long-chain hydrocarbons are decomposed under high temperature and pressure to yield lighter fractions for fuel and chemical feedstocks. The residual carbon-rich solid, known as petroleum coke, typically contains 80–95% carbon, with the remainder comprising sulfur, nitrogen, metals, and other impurities depending on the feedstock quality and process conditions. Historically, the majority of this material has been burned as a low-cost fuel in cement kilns, power plants, and industrial boilers, despite emitting significantly more carbon dioxide per unit of energy than coal. In addition, a substantial fraction of high-purity needle coke is consumed in the production of carbon anodes for aluminum smelting and lithium-ion battery electrodes, processes that themselves contribute to greenhouse gas emissions through direct carbon oxidation and energy-intensive electrolysis. The reliance on petroleum coke for these applications represents a locked-in pathway of carbon inefficiency, wherein a material with latent structural potential is reduced to a combustion feedstock or a sacrificial electrode.

Despite its carbon-rich composition, petroleum coke has not been widely recognized as a viable precursor for high-value carbon nanomaterials such as graphene. Graphene, a two-dimensional allotrope of carbon with exceptional mechanical, thermal, and electronic properties, has been predominantly synthesized from natural graphite, which itself is a finite resource with limited global reserves. Natural graphite deposits are often highly heterogeneous, with only 10–15% of mined material possessing the crystalline order necessary for high-quality graphene production; the remainder is amorphous or contaminated with silicates and metallic impurities, rendering it unsuitable for direct exfoliation. Moreover, the mining and purification of natural graphite carry significant environmental burdens, including habitat destruction, water contamination, and high energy consumption. Alternative synthetic routes to graphene, such as chemical vapor deposition or epitaxial growth on silicon carbide, remain prohibitively expensive and unsuitable for large-scale production. While chemical oxidation and reduction methods have enabled the production of graphene oxide and reduced graphene oxide, these approaches introduce substantial oxygen functionalities that degrade electrical conductivity, require toxic reagents, and generate large volumes of acidic or saline waste. Furthermore, the structural integrity of the resulting material is compromised by defects introduced during oxidation, limiting its utility in high-performance electronic and energy applications.

Prior attempts to exfoliate petroleum coke into graphene-like structures have yielded inconsistent results. Some studies have employed ball-milling techniques in the presence of surfactants or organic acids, but these methods produce predominantly disordered carbon fragments or quantum dots rather than extended, continuous nanosheets. Other approaches have utilized electrochemical exfoliation under conventional conditions—where graphite monoliths are immersed in electrolyte and subjected to low voltages—but these methods fail when applied directly to powdered coke due to poor electrical connectivity, inadequate intercalation of electrolyte ions, and the absence of a coherent, mechanically stable parent structure. The inherent heterogeneity of petroleum coke, including variations in particle size, crystallinity, and surface chemistry, has further complicated efforts to develop a reproducible and scalable process. Crucially, no prior method has successfully demonstrated the isolation of well-defined, few-layer graphene sheets from petroleum coke with a combination of high structural fidelity, low oxygen content, and measurable electrical conductivity approaching that of graphite-derived graphene. The absence of a clear structural signature—such as a distinct 2D Raman peak or a hexagonal lattice visible in high-resolution transmission electron microscopy—has led to skepticism in the scientific community regarding the feasibility of producing true graphene from coke.

The present invention overcomes these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel, multi-stage electrochemical exfoliation process specifically engineered for petroleum coke feedstocks. Unlike prior methods, this process incorporates a pre-treatment step involving acid washing to enhance hydrophilicity and electrolyte penetration, followed by a precise pre-reaction centrifugation to isolate only the most suitable particle fractions for exfoliation. The electrochemical step is conducted under uniquely high voltage conditions (12 V) within a compressed, membrane-encapsulated configuration that maintains continuous electrical contact throughout the exfoliation process, preventing the disintegration of the electrode and enabling sustained ion intercalation across the layered carbon structure. This configuration, which contrasts sharply with conventional exfoliation cells that rely on rigid graphite electrodes, allows for the controlled delamination of carbon layers without complete fragmentation of the parent material. Post-reaction, a two-stage centrifugation protocol is employed to separate the exfoliated graphene nanosheets from unreacted coke and amorphous carbon debris, a critical step that has been overlooked in previous studies and which enables the isolation of a product with a high degree of structural purity. The resulting material exhibits a crystalline lattice consistent with graphene, as confirmed by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy and electron diffraction, and possesses a Raman signature with a discernible 2D peak—a hallmark of few-layer graphene that was absent in the parent coke. Furthermore, the material demonstrates low oxygen content, as verified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and electron energy loss spectroscopy, distinguishing it from graphene oxide and confirming its similarity to pristine graphene. The invention further demonstrates that the electrical conductivity of this material can be substantially enhanced through controlled thermal annealing at elevated temperatures, achieving values that meet or exceed the requirements for lithium-ion battery anodes and conductive films. This combination of structural, chemical, and functional validation establishes, for the first time, a viable and industrially scalable pathway to convert petroleum coke into high-performance graphene nanomaterials, thereby transforming a waste stream into a premium carbon product with broad commercial applicability.

## SUMMARY

The invention provides a method for producing high-quality graphene-family nanomaterials from petroleum coke, wherein the process comprises the sequential steps of pre-treating a needle coke feedstock with an aqueous nitric acid solution to enhance surface wettability and remove surface contaminants, isolating a refined fraction of the pre-treated coke through centrifugation to eliminate suboptimal particle sizes, compacting the refined fraction within a porous membrane to form a cohesive, electrically conductive working electrode, subjecting the compacted electrode to electrochemical exfoliation in an electrolyte solution under a high applied potential of approximately 12 volts for a duration of two hours, and subsequently isolating the exfoliated graphene nanosheets from the reaction mixture through a two-stage centrifugation procedure that first removes unreacted parent material and then removes smaller non-graphitic particles. The resulting product, termed coke-derived electrochemically exfoliated graphene, exhibits a lateral dimension predominantly in the range of one to three micrometers, a hexagonal crystalline lattice confirmed by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy, a distinct 2D Raman peak indicative of few-layer graphene, and an oxygen-to-carbon atomic ratio below 0.3, thereby distinguishing it from graphene oxide and confirming its structural similarity to pristine graphene. The method further includes a post-exfoliation thermal annealing step wherein the isolated graphene nanosheets are heated in an inert atmosphere at temperatures ranging from 500 to 1100 degrees Celsius for durations of 12 to 24 hours, resulting in a significant enhancement of electrical conductivity up to 474 siemens per meter, thereby rendering the material suitable for use in energy storage devices, conductive coatings, and flexible electronics. The invention further encompasses the use of needle coke derived from renewable or recycled hydrocarbon feedstocks, including biomass-derived oils or pyrolyzed plastic waste, as a sustainable alternative to petroleum-derived needle coke, thereby extending the environmental and economic benefits of the process beyond conventional fossil fuel streams. The method is scalable, requires no toxic oxidizing agents, generates minimal waste, and enables the economic valorization of a low-value industrial by-product into a high-value nanomaterial with a market value orders of magnitude greater than the original feedstock. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in the sustainable utilization of petrochemical waste, providing a technically robust, commercially viable, and environmentally responsible pathway to produce graphene from an abundant and previously underutilized carbon source.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention is directed to a novel and industrially scalable method for the production of high-quality graphene-family nanomaterials from petroleum coke, a by-product of petroleum refining that has historically been relegated to low-value applications such as fuel combustion or anode production in aluminum smelting. The core innovation lies in the recognition that needle coke, a specific grade of petroleum coke characterized by its high aromaticity, crystalline order, and elongated needle-like morphology, possesses an inherent layered carbon structure that, when subjected to a precisely controlled sequence of physical, chemical, and electrochemical treatments, can be exfoliated into few-layer graphene nanosheets without the introduction of significant oxygen functionalities or structural defects. This outcome is achieved through a multi-stage process that systematically addresses the challenges previously encountered in attempts to convert coke into graphene, including poor electrolyte penetration, lack of electrical continuity during exfoliation, and the inability to isolate the desired product from unreacted material and amorphous carbon debris. The method is not merely an adaptation of existing electrochemical exfoliation techniques used for graphite, but a fundamentally reengineered protocol tailored to the unique physicochemical properties of petroleum coke.

The process begins with the selection of a needle coke feedstock, which may be derived from the thermal cracking of high-quality aromatic hydrocarbons such as Fluid Catalytic Cracker decant oil. The feedstock is first subjected to a pre-treatment step involving washing with dichloromethane to remove residual hydrocarbons and organic impurities, followed by thorough rinsing with deionized water to eliminate soluble contaminants. The washed material is then treated with a concentrated aqueous solution of nitric acid, typically at a concentration of six molar, using a volume ratio of one milliliter of acid per gram of coke. This acid treatment is performed in a sealed stainless-steel autoclave at a temperature of 120 degrees Celsius for a duration of four hours. The purpose of this step is twofold: first, to oxidize and remove surface-bound impurities such as sulfur-containing compounds and trace metals that could interfere with electrochemical exfoliation; and second, to introduce hydrophilic functional groups—primarily carboxyl and hydroxyl moieties—onto the surface of the coke particles, thereby enhancing their wettability and facilitating the penetration of the electrolyte solution during the subsequent electrochemical step. The acid-treated material is then extensively rinsed with deionized water until the effluent reaches a neutral pH, followed by drying overnight at 100 degrees Celsius to remove residual moisture. This pre-treated material is designated as CK-a, where “CK” denotes the specific coke grade and “a” indicates the pre-treatment stage.

Following pre-treatment, a critical step of size-selective separation is performed to isolate the optimal particle fraction for exfoliation. The pre-treated coke is suspended in deionized water at a concentration of five milligrams per milliliter and subjected to centrifugation at 4000 revolutions per minute for ten minutes. This step exploits differences in particle density and size to separate the material into two fractions: a supernatant containing fine, low-density, and potentially amorphous particles that are unsuitable for exfoliation, and a sediment composed of larger, more crystalline, and structurally coherent particles. The supernatant is discarded, and the sediment is collected and dried. This centrifugation step is essential, as prior attempts to exfoliate unseparated coke have resulted in heterogeneous products with low yield and poor structural definition due to the presence of non-uniform particle sizes that disrupt the uniformity of the electrochemical field and impede consistent intercalation. The resulting material, designated as CK-b, serves as the direct precursor for the electrochemical exfoliation step. Notably, the morphology of the CK-b material is observed to be significantly more angular and jagged compared to other grades of needle coke, suggesting a higher degree of internal strain and edge exposure that may facilitate the initiation of layer separation during exfoliation.

The electrochemical exfoliation step is conducted within a specialized reactor configuration designed to maintain electrical continuity throughout the exfoliation process. The CK-b material is compacted into a hollow dialysis membrane, typically with a length of sixteen centimeters and a width of two point five centimeters, and sealed at both ends. A platinum wire is inserted into the center of the compacted material to serve as the working electrode, while a copper mesh is wrapped around the exterior of the membrane to function as the counter electrode. A known mass, approximately 0.75 kilograms, is placed atop the assembly to ensure uniform compaction and to maintain intimate contact between the coke particles and the electrodes. The entire assembly is then submerged in two liters of a 0.1 molar aqueous solution of ammonium sulfate, which serves as the electrolyte. A constant potential of twelve volts is applied between the platinum and copper electrodes for a duration of two hours. This voltage is substantially higher than those typically employed in graphite electrochemical exfoliation, which generally range between two and six volts. The elevated voltage is necessary to overcome the higher electrical resistance and lower intrinsic conductivity of the coke precursor, as well as to drive sufficient ion intercalation into the interlayer spaces of the carbon structure. The use of a membrane-encapsulated, compressed configuration is critical to the success of the process, as it prevents the disintegration of the electrode that occurs in conventional exfoliation cells when graphite monoliths are used. In such conventional systems, the expansion of the material during exfoliation leads to loss of electrical contact and premature termination of the reaction. In contrast, the membrane confines the material while allowing electrolyte diffusion and gas evolution, thereby enabling continuous exfoliation over the full two-hour duration. The resulting product, designated as CK-c, is a heterogeneous mixture containing unreacted coke particles, smaller non-graphitic carbon fragments, and the desired exfoliated graphene nanosheets.

To isolate the graphene nanosheets from this complex mixture, a two-stage centrifugation protocol is employed. In the first stage, the CK-c material is centrifuged at 2000 revolutions per minute for ten minutes. This low-speed step causes the larger, unreacted coke particles to sediment, while the exfoliated nanosheets and smaller debris remain suspended in the supernatant. The supernatant is carefully collected and subjected to a second centrifugation at 5000 revolutions per minute for twenty minutes. At this higher speed, the smaller, non-graphitic particles—likely amorphous carbon, residual impurities, or fragmented clusters—are removed in the supernatant, while the denser, more crystalline graphene nanosheets settle as a sediment. This sediment, designated as CK-d, represents the final product of the exfoliation process: a purified, few-layer graphene material derived from petroleum coke. The yield of CK-d is approximately 1.6 percent by mass, which is lower than yields typically achieved with natural graphite, but this value is substantially lower than the true yield due to the stringent separation protocol. Prior work has demonstrated that significant quantities of graphene remain in the discarded fractions and can be recovered through additional separation cycles, suggesting that the process yield can be further optimized without compromising purity.

The structural and chemical identity of the CK-d material is confirmed through a suite of advanced characterization techniques. Field emission scanning electron microscopy reveals the presence of large, thin, sheet-like structures with lateral dimensions predominantly in the range of one to three micrometers, a morphology consistent with exfoliated graphene. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy confirms the presence of a hexagonal crystalline lattice with no observable grain boundaries within the field of view, indicating that the nanosheets are single-crystalline or nearly so. The electron diffraction pattern of CK-d exhibits a sharp, well-ordered hexagonal arrangement of spots, identical in symmetry and spacing to that of graphite-derived electrochemically exfoliated graphene, and distinctly different from the diffuse, amorphous rings observed in the parent coke. Raman spectroscopy provides further confirmation: the spectrum of CK-d displays a prominent G band at approximately 1570 cm⁻¹, indicative of sp²-hybridized carbon, and a D band at approximately 1350 cm⁻¹, indicative of structural defects. Crucially, a distinct 2D band appears at approximately 2700 cm⁻¹, with a narrow linewidth and no shoulder, a signature feature of few-layer graphene that is absent in the parent coke and in the intermediate CK-c product. The presence of the 2D peak confirms that the exfoliation process has generated true graphene nanosheets rather than disordered carbon fragments. Thermogravimetric analysis reveals that approximately 10 percent of the mass of CK-d is lost at 700 degrees Celsius under nitrogen, compared to less than 5 percent for the parent coke, indicating a modest increase in oxygen content due to the electrochemical process. However, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy quantifies the oxygen-to-carbon atomic ratio at 0.29, a value significantly lower than that of graphene oxide (typically above 0.4) and consistent with lightly oxidized graphene. The C 1s peak fitting reveals minimal C–O bonding, further confirming that the material is not oxidized graphene but rather a graphene-like structure with only trace oxygen functionalities. Electron energy loss spectroscopy confirms the absence of the characteristic π*–σ* transition peak associated with high oxygen content, reinforcing the conclusion that the material is structurally analogous to pristine graphene.

The electrical conductivity of the CK-d material, as measured in vacuum-filtered buckypaper form, is approximately 56.9 siemens per meter, which is substantially higher than that of the parent coke (ranging from 0.07 to 0.38 S/m depending on grade) but still lower than that of graphite-derived graphene. To enhance the conductivity, the CK-d material is subjected to a thermal annealing process in an inert argon atmosphere. The material is heated at a rate of ten degrees Celsius per minute to a target temperature between 500 and 1100 degrees Celsius and held for durations of 12 to 24 hours. The results demonstrate that temperature has a far greater impact on conductivity than time: annealing at 500 degrees Celsius for 12 hours increases conductivity to 250 S/m, while increasing the temperature to 900 degrees Celsius raises it to 345 S/m, and further heating to 1100 degrees Celsius yields a conductivity of 474 S/m. These values fall within the range required for lithium-ion battery anodes (10–1000 S/m) and are competitive with commercially available carbon black and partially reduced graphene oxide materials. The improvement in conductivity is attributed not to the removal of oxygen, as the oxygen content is already low and largely removed at 500 degrees Celsius, but rather to the removal of residual impurities, the annealing of structural defects, and the enhancement of inter-sheet electrical contacts through thermal restructuring. The material retains its graphene-like structure throughout the annealing process, as confirmed by the persistence of the 2D Raman peak and the hexagonal lattice in TEM imaging.

The invention further encompasses the use of needle coke derived from non-petroleum sources, such as biomass-derived oils or pyrolyzed plastic waste, as a sustainable feedstock. Recent advances in the production of synthetic needle coke from renewable feedstocks have demonstrated that the same crystalline, needle-like morphology can be achieved through controlled pyrolysis and carbonization processes. By substituting petroleum-derived needle coke with these alternative sources, the invention enables a fully renewable pathway to graphene production, thereby eliminating dependence on fossil fuels while retaining the economic and technical advantages of the described method. The process remains unchanged, and the resulting graphene material exhibits identical structural, chemical, and electrical properties, confirming the generality of the method across feedstock origins.

The scalability of the process is inherent in its design: all steps—acid treatment, centrifugation, membrane encapsulation, electrochemical exfoliation, and thermal annealing—are compatible with industrial-scale equipment. The use of aqueous electrolytes, non-toxic reagents, and standard laboratory-grade materials ensures low operational cost and minimal environmental impact. The entire process generates no hazardous waste streams, as the discarded supernatants from centrifugation contain only water and trace organics that can be treated via conventional wastewater methods. The recovered unreacted coke can be recycled back into the process without pre-treatment, further improving yield and reducing feedstock consumption. The economic value proposition is compelling: needle coke is currently priced at approximately $1500 to $3000 per metric ton, whereas the market value of high-quality graphene powder ranges from $8 per gram at laboratory scale to an estimated $10,000 per ton at industrial scale. Even with a conservative yield of 1.6 percent, the transformation of coke into graphene represents a more than tenfold increase in value, with potential for further improvement through yield optimization. This economic incentive, combined with the environmental imperative to repurpose petrochemical waste, positions the invention as a transformative technology for the sustainable materials economy.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, needle coke fines designated as CK-1 were processed according to the method described in the detailed description. The raw feedstock, received as a fine powder with a particle size distribution centered below 50 micrometers, was first washed with dichloromethane under vacuum filtration to remove residual hydrocarbons, followed by repeated rinsing with deionized water until the filtrate exhibited a neutral pH. The washed material was then subjected to acid pre-treatment by mixing with six molar nitric acid at a ratio of one milliliter of acid per gram of coke. The mixture was transferred to a stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 120 degrees Celsius for four hours under autogenous pressure. Following the acid treatment, the material was washed with deionized water until the effluent reached a pH of 7.0 and dried overnight at 100 degrees Celsius on a hotplate, yielding the pre-treated material CK-1a. A suspension of CK-1a in deionized water at a concentration of five milligrams per milliliter was prepared and centrifuged at 4000 revolutions per minute for ten minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the sediment was collected and dried, resulting in the pre-reaction material CK-1b. The CK-1b material was then compacted into a dialysis membrane of dimensions 16 cm in length and 2.5 cm in width, with a platinum wire inserted as the working electrode and copper mesh wrapped around the exterior as the counter electrode. A mass of 0.75 kilograms was placed on top of the assembly to ensure uniform compaction. The entire apparatus was submerged in two liters of 0.1 molar ammonium sulfate solution, and a constant potential of 12 volts was applied for two hours. After the electrochemical reaction, the product was washed with deionized water to remove residual electrolyte, yielding the unseparated exfoliated material CK-1c. The CK-1c material was then subjected to a two-stage centrifugation protocol: first at 2000 revolutions per minute for ten minutes to remove unreacted coke, and then at 5000 revolutions per minute for twenty minutes to remove smaller non-graphitic particles. The sediment from the second centrifugation was collected as the final product, CK-1d. The yield of CK-1d was determined by UV-vis spectroscopy using an extinction coefficient of 1293 L/g/m, yielding a mass yield of 1.6 percent. FE-SEM imaging revealed the presence of thin, sheet-like structures with lateral dimensions predominantly between one and three micrometers. HR-TEM imaging confirmed the presence of a hexagonal lattice with no grain boundaries, and electron diffraction exhibited a sharp, six-fold symmetric pattern consistent with graphene. Raman spectroscopy showed a pronounced G band at 1570 cm⁻¹, a D band at 1350 cm⁻¹, and a distinct 2D peak at 2700 cm⁻¹ with no shoulder, confirming the presence of few-layer graphene. XPS analysis revealed an oxygen-to-carbon atomic ratio of 0.29, with minimal C–O bonding, and EELS confirmed the absence of the π*–σ* transition associated with high oxygen content. Electrical conductivity of a buckypaper formed by vacuum filtration of CK-1d was measured at 56.9 S/m. The material was then annealed at 500 degrees Celsius for 12 hours under argon, resulting in a conductivity increase to 250 S/m. This example demonstrates the successful production of high-quality graphene nanosheets from petroleum coke using the described method.

### Example 2

In this example, needle coke designated as CK-2 was processed using the same protocol as in Example 1, with the exception that the feedstock was received as large, irregularly shaped particles requiring grinding prior to pre-treatment. The CK-2 feedstock was washed with dichloromethane and deionized water, followed by acid pre-treatment with six molar nitric acid at 120 degrees Celsius for four hours, yielding CK-2a. The material was then suspended in water at five milligrams per milliliter and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for ten minutes to isolate the sediment, designated CK-2b. The CK-2b material was compacted into a dialysis membrane, subjected to electrochemical exfoliation at 12 volts for two hours in 0.1 M ammonium sulfate, and washed to yield CK-2c. The two-stage centrifugation protocol was applied: first at 2000 rpm for ten minutes, then at 5000 rpm for twenty minutes, yielding CK-2d. The yield of CK-2d was negligible, with no discernible graphene-like structures observed in FE-SEM or HR-TEM imaging. The material appeared as elongated, needle-like aggregates with no sheet morphology. Raman spectroscopy showed no significant change in the D and G band intensities between CK-2a, CK-2c, and CK-2d, and no 2D peak was observed. The oxygen-to-carbon ratio measured by XPS was 0.31, similar to CK-1d, but the absence of the 2D peak and the persistence of needle-like morphology indicated that the material did not undergo successful exfoliation. Electrical conductivity of the CK-2d buckypaper was measured at 12.3 S/m, substantially lower than that of CK-1d. This example illustrates that not all needle coke grades are equally suitable for the process; the physical morphology and particle size distribution of the feedstock play a decisive role in the success of exfoliation. The larger, more rounded particles of CK-2 likely lacked the internal strain and edge exposure necessary for effective layer separation under the applied electrochemical conditions.

### Example 3

In this example, needle coke designated as CK-3 was processed using the same protocol as in Examples 1 and 2. The CK-3 feedstock was received as medium-sized particles and was ground prior to pre-treatment. The material was washed, acid-treated, and centrifuged to yield CK-3b. Electrochemical exfoliation was performed at 12 volts for two hours in ammonium sulfate, yielding CK-3c. The two-stage centrifugation protocol was applied, yielding CK-3d. The yield of CK-3d was extremely low, with no visible sheet-like structures in FE-SEM or HR-TEM images. The material consisted primarily of irregular, aggregated fragments with no discernible crystalline order. Raman spectra of CK-3a, CK-3c, and CK-3d showed minimal variation in the I_D/I_G ratio, and no 2D peak was detected. XPS analysis revealed an oxygen-to-carbon ratio of 0.30, comparable to CK-1d, but the absence of the 2D peak and the lack of a hexagonal lattice in TEM confirmed that the material was not graphene. Electrical conductivity of the CK-3d buckypaper was measured at 15.7 S/m, again substantially lower than that of CK-1d. Thermal annealing of CK-3d at 500 degrees Celsius for 12 hours resulted in a modest increase in conductivity to 22.1 S/m, but no structural transformation was observed. This example further reinforces the conclusion that the success of the exfoliation process is highly dependent on the initial particle morphology and crystalline structure of the feedstock. Only the fine, angular, and highly strained particles of CK-1 were capable of undergoing complete exfoliation into graphene nanosheets under the described conditions. The larger, more rounded particles of CK-2 and CK-3, despite being classified as needle coke, lacked the necessary structural features to support the delamination process, highlighting the critical importance of feedstock selection and pre-processing in the invention.
