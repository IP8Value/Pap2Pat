# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel cytogenomic methodology for the detection, characterization, and quantification of chromosomal inversions at high resolution on a single-cell basis. Specifically, the invention encompasses a directional genomic hybridization technique, referred to as chromatid painting, which employs strand-specific, sequence-directed oligonucleotide probes to visualize the orientation of DNA sequences along individual sister chromatids in metaphase cells. This method enables the unambiguous identification of intra-chromosomal rearrangements, particularly inversions, by exploiting the obligate reversal in strand polarity that occurs when a segment of DNA is inverted and reinserted into its chromosome. Unlike conventional chromosome painting or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), which rely on double-stranded probes and provide no information regarding the directionality of hybridization targets, the disclosed method leverages the unique biochemical properties of single-stranded DNA and the controlled incorporation of brominated nucleotides during DNA replication to render each sister chromatid as a distinct, orientationally defined template. The invention further includes the design, synthesis, and application of probe sets that are bioinformatically selected for uniform 5′ to 3′ directionality, optimal hybridization kinetics, and complete coverage of chromosomal regions of interest, enabling the detection of inversions as small as a few kilobases with high sensitivity and specificity. The method is applicable to human and non-human primate cells, as well as to other species with sequenced genomes, and is particularly suited for the analysis of heterogeneous cell populations such as those found in solid tumors, irradiated tissues, and genetically unstable cell lines. The invention also provides for the simultaneous detection of both intra-chromosomal and inter-chromosomal structural variants, including translocations and dicentric chromosomes, within the same metaphase spread, thereby offering a comprehensive, unbiased, and cell-resolved view of genomic architecture that is not achievable through bulk sequencing or array-based methods.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Chromosomal inversions represent a class of structural genomic rearrangements in which a segment of DNA is excised, rotated 180 degrees, and reinserted into the same chromosome, resulting in a reversal of the linear order of genetic elements within that segment. Although first recognized over a century ago through classical cytogenetic banding techniques, inversions have historically posed significant challenges for detection due to their often cryptic nature—particularly when they do not alter the gross banding pattern of the chromosome or involve regions lacking distinctive morphological features. Traditional methods such as G-banding, R-banding, and even whole-chromosome fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) are fundamentally limited in their ability to resolve inversions smaller than approximately 10 megabases, and even larger inversions may remain undetected if the breakpoints occur within gene-poor, repeat-rich, or heterochromatic regions where banding patterns are indistinct. Moreover, because these methods rely on the visualization of chromosomal morphology or the hybridization of double-stranded probes that bind indiscriminately to both complementary strands, they are incapable of discerning the orientation of DNA sequences relative to the chromosome’s 5′ to 3′ axis, which is the defining characteristic of an inversion.

The advent of molecular techniques such as array comparative genomic hybridization (a-CGH), single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays, and next-generation sequencing (NGS) has significantly improved the ability to detect copy number variations and, to a limited extent, structural rearrangements. Paired-end sequencing, in particular, has demonstrated utility in identifying inversions by detecting discordant read orientations and abnormal insert sizes. However, these approaches suffer from critical limitations that render them unsuitable for many clinical and biological applications. First, NGS methods require the pooling of DNA from thousands to millions of cells, thereby averaging out cellular heterogeneity and obscuring the presence of rare or mosaic rearrangements that may be biologically significant, such as those arising in early carcinogenesis or following genotoxic exposure. Second, the assembly of sequencing data is heavily dependent on the quality and completeness of the reference genome, and inversions that occur in regions of high sequence similarity, segmental duplications, or repetitive elements are frequently misassembled or entirely missed. Third, these methods provide no direct visualization of the rearrangement within the context of intact chromosomes, making it impossible to determine whether a given inversion is present in one or both homologs, whether it co-occurs with other structural variants, or whether it is confined to a subpopulation of cells within a tissue sample.

Furthermore, while some molecular cytogenetic techniques such as chromosome orientation FISH (CO-FISH) have been developed to exploit strand-specific hybridization for the analysis of telomeric and centromeric regions, these methods are restricted to repetitive sequences and short oligonucleotide probes that lack the resolution and genome-wide applicability required for comprehensive inversion detection. CO-FISH, for instance, relies on the differential incorporation of bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) into nascent DNA strands and subsequent UV-induced degradation to expose single-stranded templates, but its utility has been confined to the interrogation of telomeres and centromeres due to the absence of sequence-specific probes capable of hybridizing to unique, non-repetitive genomic regions. As a result, prior art has lacked a method that combines the cell-by-cell resolution of classical cytogenetics with the sequence specificity and directional sensitivity of molecular genomics.

The need for a method capable of detecting inversions with high resolution, without requiring DNA amplification or population averaging, and with the ability to simultaneously assess multiple types of structural variation in individual cells, has remained unmet. This gap is particularly acute in the fields of cancer biology, radiation biology, and developmental genetics, where inversions can lead to the disruption of tumor suppressor genes, the creation of oncogenic fusion transcripts, or the dysregulation of developmental enhancers. For example, inversions involving the CBFA2T3 and GLIS2 genes on chromosome 16 have been shown to define a highly aggressive subtype of pediatric acute leukemia, yet this rearrangement was only identified through transcriptome sequencing and remained invisible to all conventional cytogenetic methods. Similarly, inversions in the RET gene on chromosome 10 have been linked to radiation-induced papillary thyroid carcinoma, but their detection has been limited to labor-intensive, low-throughput PCR-based assays that cannot be scaled for population-level analysis. The inability to reliably detect and quantify inversions across diverse cell types and tissue contexts has hindered the understanding of their true prevalence, their role in disease etiology, and their potential as biomarkers for exposure, prognosis, or therapeutic response.

Existing approaches also fail to address the critical issue of cellular mosaicism, which is inherent in solid tumors, post-irradiation tissues, and aging populations. Bulk sequencing methods cannot distinguish whether an inversion is present in 1% or 99% of the cells in a sample, and single-cell sequencing, while theoretically capable of resolving this issue, remains prohibitively expensive, technically demanding, and limited in throughput. Moreover, single-cell sequencing does not preserve the spatial and structural context of the chromosome, making it impossible to determine whether an inversion is accompanied by other rearrangements, whether it is clonal, or whether it is associated with aneuploidy or chromothripsis. There is therefore a compelling and long-felt need for a method that can interrogate the orientation of DNA sequences within intact chromosomes on a single-cell basis, with high resolution, without reliance on reference genome assembly, and with the capacity to detect both large and small inversions across the entire genome in an unbiased and quantitative manner. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing chromatid painting—a novel, robust, and scalable methodology that transforms the detection of chromosomal inversions from a rare, serendipitous discovery into a routine, high-fidelity diagnostic and research tool.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for the detection and visualization of chromosomal inversions in individual cells through the use of directional genomic hybridization, termed chromatid painting. This method enables the unambiguous identification of inverted DNA segments by exploiting the obligate reversal in strand polarity that occurs when a chromosomal segment is inverted and reinserted into its native locus. The invention comprises the preparation of metaphase chromosomes from cells that have undergone a single round of DNA replication in the presence of brominated nucleotide analogs, followed by selective degradation of the newly synthesized strands to expose single-stranded templates. These templates are then hybridized with a panel of sequence-specific, single-stranded oligonucleotide probes that are designed to bind exclusively to complementary sequences in a defined 5′ to 3′ orientation. Each probe set is bioinformatically selected to tile along a target chromosome or chromosomal region with uniform directionality, consistent length, and similar melting temperature, ensuring that hybridization signals are spatially ordered and orientationally coherent. In the absence of an inversion, all probe signals align along a single sister chromatid, reflecting the uniform 5′ to 3′ orientation of the underlying genomic sequence. In the presence of an inversion, the orientation of the inverted segment is reversed, causing the hybridization signal to switch from one sister chromatid to its counterpart, thereby producing a visually distinct and easily interpretable discontinuity in signal distribution. This signal switch is detectable by fluorescence microscopy and serves as a direct, cytological readout of inversion status, regardless of the size, location, or complexity of the rearrangement.

The invention further encompasses the design and synthesis of probe sets tailored for specific chromosomal regions, including whole-chromosome paints, targeted breakpoint panels, and high-density tiling arrays that enable detection of inversions as small as a few kilobases. The probe sets are composed of oligonucleotides ranging from 30 to 60 nucleotides in length, synthesized with terminal fluorescent labels such as Cy3, fluorescein, or other spectrally distinct dyes, and pooled in defined combinations to ensure complete and contiguous coverage of the target region. The method is compatible with standard cytogenetic preparation protocols and can be applied to any cell type capable of entering mitosis, including primary fibroblasts, lymphocytes, immortalized cell lines, and tumor cells. The invention also provides for the simultaneous detection of other structural variants, including translocations and dicentric chromosomes, as these events are revealed through the abnormal distribution of hybridization signals across multiple chromosomes. The method does not require the use of blocking DNA such as Cot-1, as the probes are inherently sequence-specific and do not bind to repetitive elements, thereby enhancing signal-to-noise ratio and reducing hybridization artifacts. Furthermore, the same probe sets can be employed in standard double-stranded FISH applications, allowing for flexible use across multiple experimental contexts without the need for re-optimization.

The invention further includes a system for automated image acquisition and analysis, wherein fluorescence microscopy images of hybridized metaphase spreads are captured and processed using specialized software to detect, quantify, and map inversion breakpoints with high precision. The method is scalable to genome-wide applications and can be adapted for use in non-human species with sequenced genomes, including primates, canines, felines, and equines, thereby enabling comparative evolutionary studies of chromosomal architecture. The invention also provides for the quantification of inversion frequencies in exposed populations, making it uniquely suited for risk assessment following genotoxic exposure, monitoring of therapeutic response in cancer, and screening for constitutional rearrangements in developmental disorders. Importantly, the method does not rely on DNA amplification, sequencing, or computational assembly, and therefore provides a direct, physical, and visual confirmation of inversion status that is independent of reference genome quality or bioinformatic bias. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the detection of structural genomic variation, transforming chromatid painting from a theoretical concept into a practical, robust, and clinically translatable methodology for high-resolution, cell-specific inversion detection.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Selection of Sequences:

The selection of genomic sequences for the design of directional oligonucleotide probe sets is a foundational component of the chromatid painting methodology and is performed through a rigorous, bioinformatically driven process that ensures the fidelity, specificity, and orientationally consistent hybridization of probes across the entire target chromosome or chromosomal region. The process begins with the retrieval of contiguous genomic sequences corresponding to the chromosome of interest from a high-quality, publicly available reference genome assembly, such as the Human Genome Reference Consortium GRCh37 or GRCh38. These sequences are then subjected to a series of computational filters designed to eliminate regions of low complexity, repetitive elements, segmental duplications, and known polymorphic or structurally variant loci that could interfere with probe specificity or introduce hybridization artifacts. Repetitive sequences are identified and masked using established software tools such as RepeatMasker, the Genetic Information Research Institute (GIRI) masking suite, or proprietary algorithms trained on human genomic databases to recognize transposable elements, satellite repeats, LINEs, SINEs, and other classes of interspersed repeats. Sequences that remain after masking are further evaluated for uniqueness by performing a BLAST search against the entire human genome to confirm that each candidate region has no significant homology to any other genomic locus outside the intended target region, with a threshold of less than 85% identity over a minimum length of 20 nucleotides.

Following the identification of unique, non-repetitive regions, the next step involves the selection of discrete target intervals along the chromosome for probe placement. These intervals are chosen to be approximately 1 megabase apart, although the spacing may be adjusted depending on the desired resolution, the size of the target region, and the anticipated frequency of inversions in the population under study. For high-resolution applications, such as the detection of small inversions in cancer or radiation-exposed cells, intervals may be reduced to 100–500 kilobases, while for whole-chromosome screening, spacing may be increased to 2–5 megabases to reduce probe complexity and hybridization time. Each selected interval is then analyzed for its sequence composition, GC content, and predicted melting temperature (Tm) to ensure uniform hybridization kinetics across all probes. Probes are designed to be approximately 40 to 50 nucleotides in length, with a target Tm of 70.0 ± 5.0 °C, to ensure consistent stringency during hybridization and washing steps. The directionality of each probe is strictly enforced such that all probes are oriented in the same 5′ to 3′ direction relative to the pter to qter axis of the chromosome, meaning that the 5′ end of each probe corresponds to the proximal end of the chromosome and the 3′ end corresponds to the distal end. This orientation is critical, as it ensures that hybridization signals will be distributed in a linear, sequential manner along a single chromatid in the absence of an inversion, and that any deviation from this pattern—specifically, a reversal in signal distribution between sister chromatids—will unambiguously indicate the presence of an inversion.

The design of individual oligonucleotide probes is performed using specialized software such as ARRAY Designer version 4.2 or proprietary algorithms developed for this purpose, which optimize probe sequences for minimal self-complementarity, minimal secondary structure formation, and avoidance of homopolymer stretches or nucleotide biases that could lead to non-specific binding or hybridization failure. Each probe is synthesized with a terminal fluorescent label, typically a cyanine dye such as Cy3 or fluorescein, or a derivative thereof, which is covalently attached via a phosphoramidite chemistry during solid-phase synthesis. The fluorescent label is positioned at the 3′ end of the oligonucleotide to minimize steric interference with hybridization and to ensure that the label does not interfere with the probe’s ability to bind its complementary target sequence. Once synthesized, individual probes are validated for specificity and hybridization efficiency using a panel of control metaphase spreads from normal human cells, and those that exhibit non-specific binding, weak signal intensity, or hybridization to non-target chromosomes are excluded from the final probe set.

Probe sets are then grouped into functional units, each consisting of 45 to 200 individual oligonucleotides, such that each unit covers a contiguous region of approximately 5 to 63 kilobases. These probe sets are hybridized individually to metaphase chromosomes to confirm that each set produces a discrete, orientationally consistent signal along the expected chromosomal region. Only probe sets that demonstrate high signal-to-noise ratios, uniform intensity across all cells, and strict adherence to the predicted chromosomal location are retained for inclusion in the final composite probe set. For whole-chromosome paints, such as those targeting chromosome 3, the final probe set may consist of over 190 individual probe sets, collectively comprising more than 17,000 unique oligonucleotides, which together provide near-complete coverage of the chromosome with minimal gaps. The probe sets are then pooled in defined ratios to ensure balanced hybridization across the entire chromosome, and the final mixture is purified to remove unincorporated dyes, salts, and synthesis byproducts. The resulting probe cocktail is stored in a proprietary hybridization buffer containing formamide, dextran sulfate, and blocking agents such as salmon sperm DNA, which enhances probe penetration and reduces background noise without compromising specificity.

The selection of sequences is further refined through iterative testing and validation. For example, in the case of targeted inversion detection, probe sets are designed to flank known or suspected breakpoint regions with a spacing of no more than 1 megabase on either side, ensuring that any inversion involving the region will be detected by a signal switch between the flanking probes. In cases where the inversion is expected to be small—such as those involving oncogenic fusion genes—the probe sets are expanded to include additional probes within the suspected breakpoint interval, increasing the probability that at least one probe will hybridize within the inverted segment and thereby produce a detectable signal reversal. In some embodiments, probe sets are designed to span known fragile sites, telomeric regions, or centromeric boundaries to assess whether inversions are preferentially associated with these genomic features. The entire process is documented in a digital database that records the genomic coordinates, probe sequence, Tm, label, hybridization efficiency, and validation status for each oligonucleotide, enabling traceability, reproducibility, and scalability across laboratories and applications.

### EXAMPLE 2

In a representative example of the invention, a targeted chromatid paint was developed to detect inversions involving the RET gene locus on chromosome 10, which is known to be rearranged in radiation-induced papillary thyroid carcinoma. The genomic region encompassing the RET gene, spanning from approximately 10q11.2 to 10q21, was selected as the target region based on prior literature documenting recurrent inversion breakpoints in this interval. The GRCh37 assembly of chromosome 10 was retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), and the region between 45,000,000 and 55,000,000 base pairs was analyzed for unique, non-repetitive sequences. Using the bioinformatic pipeline described above, 180 individual oligonucleotide probes were designed to tile across this 10-megabase region at intervals of approximately 50 to 100 kilobases, with each probe measuring 45 nucleotides in length and possessing a Tm of 70.2 ± 4.8 °C. All probes were oriented in the 5′ to 3′ direction corresponding to the pter to qter axis of chromosome 10.

The probes were synthesized with fluorescein labels and pooled into a single probe set designated as Probe Set 10-RET. This probe set was then hybridized to metaphase spreads prepared from HTori-3 immortalized human thyroid cells that had been exposed to gamma irradiation at a dose of 2 Gy. Prior to hybridization, the cells were cultured for one cell cycle in the presence of 5.0 μM 5-bromo-2-deoxyuridine and 1.0 μM 5-bromo-deoxycytidine to ensure uniform incorporation of brominated nucleotides into nascent DNA strands. Following mitotic arrest with colcemid, cells were harvested, dropped onto glass slides, and subjected to the standard single-stranded hybridization pre-treatment protocol, which included staining with Hoechst 33258, exposure to 365 nm ultraviolet light for 35 minutes, and digestion with exonuclease III to selectively degrade the BrdU-containing strands. The slides were then hybridized with Probe Set 10-RET under standard conditions: 75 °C for 5 minutes, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C in a humidified chamber, and subsequent washes in 2× SSC at 42 °C for 15 minutes per wash.

Upon fluorescence microscopy analysis, the majority of metaphase spreads exhibited a continuous, uninterrupted fluorescein signal along one sister chromatid of chromosome 10, consistent with the expected 5′ to 3′ orientation of the genomic sequence. However, in approximately 1.2% of the irradiated cells, a distinct and reproducible signal switch was observed: the fluorescein signal abruptly terminated on one chromatid and reappeared on its sister chromatid at a position approximately 1.2 megabases distal to the expected breakpoint. This signal discontinuity was not observed in unirradiated control cells, nor in cells treated with non-genotoxic agents. The position of the signal switch corresponded precisely to the known location of the RET/PTC1 fusion breakpoint, as previously documented in the literature. To confirm the identity of the inversion, a second probe set, designated Probe Set 10-RET-FLANK, was designed to hybridize to sequences immediately proximal (44.8–45.2 Mb) and distal (56.0–56.5 Mb) to the breakpoint region. When hybridized in combination with Probe Set 10-RET, the signal switch was observed to occur precisely between these two flanking regions, confirming that the inversion involved a segment of approximately 1.2 megabases that had been inverted and reinserted in the opposite orientation.

Importantly, the signal switch was observed exclusively in one homolog of chromosome 10 in each affected cell, indicating that the inversion was heterozygous and not present in both copies. Furthermore, the sister chromatid bearing the signal switch consistently exhibited a region of complete absence of signal—approximately 1.2 megabases in length—where the inverted segment had been reinserted in the reverse orientation, rendering the probes unable to hybridize to their complementary targets. This absence of signal, which is a hallmark of the chromatid painting method, was never observed in control cells and served as a definitive indicator of inversion status. The method was also able to detect a small subset of cells in which the inversion was accompanied by a translocation involving chromosome 2, as evidenced by the presence of fluorescein signal on a non-homologous chromosome. These dual events were visualized simultaneously in the same metaphase spread, demonstrating the capability of the method to detect multiple structural variants in a single assay.

The sensitivity of the method was further validated by testing a series of irradiation doses ranging from 0.1 to 5.0 Gy. A dose-dependent increase in the frequency of signal switches was observed, with a linear correlation (R² = 0.98) between radiation dose and inversion frequency, confirming the method’s suitability for quantitative risk assessment. The specificity of the method was confirmed by hybridizing the same probe set to metaphase spreads from individuals with known constitutional inversions on chromosome 10, as verified by long-read sequencing, and in all cases, the signal switch was accurately detected. No false positives were observed in over 5,000 metaphase spreads analyzed from unexposed, healthy donors. This example demonstrates that the chromatid painting method can be applied to clinically relevant genomic regions with high precision, sensitivity, and reproducibility, and that it can detect inversions that are otherwise invisible to conventional cytogenetic and molecular techniques.

### EXAMPLE 3

In another illustrative embodiment of the invention, a whole-chromosome chromatid paint was developed for human chromosome 3, encompassing the entire length of the chromosome from the pter to the qter terminus. The GRCh37 assembly of chromosome 3 was divided into four major contiguous sequences: NT_005612 (3q21–q26), NT_005613 (3q26–q29), NT_005614 (3p21–pter), and NT_005615 (3p25–p21). Each contig was independently analyzed for unique, non-repetitive sequences using the bioinformatic pipeline described above, and probe sets were designed to tile across each contig at approximately 1-megabase intervals. A total of 190 probe sets were generated, each consisting of 90 individual 40-mer oligonucleotides, for a total of 17,100 unique probes. The probes were labeled with either Cy3 (red) or fluorescein (green) in alternating patterns to facilitate visualization and to distinguish between overlapping probe sets during hybridization. The entire set was pooled into a single, comprehensive chromosome 3 chromatid paint.

This paint was hybridized to metaphase spreads prepared from normal human fibroblasts, lymphocytes, and the Kasumi-4 cell line, which is known to harbor a large inversion on chromosome 3 involving the q21 and q26 breakpoints. In normal cells, the hybridization signal was observed to be confined exclusively to one sister chromatid of each chromosome 3 homolog, with no signal detected on the other sister chromatid. The signal was continuous and uninterrupted along the entire length of the chromosome, with no gaps or discontinuities, confirming the uniform 5′ to 3′ orientation of the underlying genomic sequence. In the Kasumi-4 cell line, however, a clear and dramatic signal switch was observed: the red signal from the proximal region of chromosome 3q (near q21) was present on one chromatid, while the green signal from the distal region (near q26) was present on the opposite chromatid, with a corresponding region of complete signal absence between the two. This pattern was observed in 100% of Kasumi-4 metaphases analyzed (n = 50), whereas it was absent in all normal controls (n = 100). The position of the signal switch corresponded precisely to the known inversion breakpoints, as previously identified by long-range PCR and sequencing.

To further validate the method, a simulated inversion was engineered in silico within a 10-megabase region of chromosome 3q. A 6-kilobase segment was designated as inverted, and oligonucleotides targeting this segment were synthesized with reverse orientation and labeled with Cy3, while the surrounding region was labeled with fluorescein. When this synthetic probe set was hybridized to normal metaphase spreads, a distinct and reproducible signal switch was observed exactly at the location of the simulated inversion, with the red signal appearing on the opposite chromatid from the green signal, and a 6-kilobase region of signal absence between the two. This experiment confirmed that even very small inversions, on the order of a few kilobases, can be detected by the chromatid painting method, provided that at least one probe set is positioned within the inverted segment.

The method was also applied to metaphase spreads from chimpanzee, gorilla, and orangutan fibroblasts to assess its utility in comparative genomics. In chimpanzee and gorilla, the chromosome 3 chromatid paint hybridized uniformly to one sister chromatid of each homolog, with no signal switches observed, indicating the absence of large inversions in these species. In contrast, in orangutan cells, a large inversion was detected on both homologs of chromosome 3, as evidenced by a consistent signal switch near the centromere, with a region of signal absence spanning approximately 15 megabases. This inversion was not previously documented in the literature and was confirmed by comparative genomic hybridization and long-read sequencing. The ability of the method to detect evolutionarily conserved inversions in non-human primates demonstrates its broad applicability beyond human genetics and its potential for use in evolutionary biology, conservation genetics, and comparative cytogenomics.

In addition, the chromatid paint was used to detect complex rearrangements in irradiated cells. In one metaphase spread, a dicentric chromosome was observed, with one arm consisting of chromosome 3 and the other arm consisting of an unidentified chromosome. The chromosome 3 portion of the dicentric exhibited a signal switch, indicating the presence of an inversion, while the other arm exhibited hybridization signals consistent with a different chromosome. Simultaneously, a compound acentric fragment was observed, consisting of the distal portion of chromosome 3 and a segment of the partner chromosome, confirming the misrepair of two broken chromosomes. This example illustrates that the chromatid painting method is not limited to the detection of inversions alone but can simultaneously reveal the full spectrum of structural rearrangements—including translocations, dicentrics, and acentric fragments—within a single metaphase spread, providing a comprehensive view of genomic instability that is unmatched by any other existing method.

### Hybridization:

The hybridization step in the chromatid painting method is a critical and highly optimized process that ensures the specific, efficient, and orientationally faithful binding of single-stranded oligonucleotide probes to their complementary targets on the exposed sister chromatids. Unlike conventional FISH, which employs double-stranded DNA probes that hybridize indiscriminately to both strands of the target DNA, the chromatid painting method relies on the exclusive hybridization of single-stranded probes to single-stranded templates, which are generated through a precise pre-treatment protocol that selectively degrades the newly replicated DNA strands. This pre-treatment, which involves the incorporation of brominated nucleotides during DNA synthesis, followed by UV irradiation and exonuclease digestion, renders each sister chromatid as a single-stranded, orientationally defined template that is uniquely suited for directional hybridization.

The hybridization reaction is performed in a carefully formulated buffer that promotes probe penetration, minimizes non-specific binding, and maintains the structural integrity of the metaphase chromosomes. The hybridization buffer typically consists of 50% formamide, 10% dextran sulfate, 2× SSC, 10% blocking DNA (such as salmon sperm DNA or human Cot-1 DNA), and 1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, with the pH adjusted to 7.2–7.4. The formamide serves to reduce the melting temperature of the DNA duplex, enabling hybridization to occur at a lower temperature (37 °C) that preserves chromosomal morphology, while the dextran sulfate increases the effective concentration of the probes by excluding volume, thereby enhancing hybridization kinetics. The blocking DNA is included to suppress hybridization to repetitive elements, although in this method, its use is optional due to the inherent sequence specificity of the oligonucleotide probes, which are designed to avoid repetitive regions entirely.

Prior to hybridization, the probe cocktail is denatured at 75 °C for 5 minutes to ensure complete separation of any residual secondary structures, and then immediately applied to the pre-treated metaphase spreads, which are mounted on glass slides and sealed with rubber cement to prevent evaporation. The slides are then subjected to a brief heat denaturation step at 73 °C for 3 minutes to further denature the chromosomal DNA and facilitate probe access to the target sequences. Following this, the slides are transferred to a humidified hybridization chamber and incubated at 37 °C for a period of 12 to 24 hours, allowing sufficient time for the probes to diffuse into the chromatin and bind to their complementary targets. The duration of hybridization is optimized to ensure maximal signal intensity without inducing probe degradation or non-specific binding.

After hybridization, the slides are washed rigorously to remove unbound or weakly bound probes. This is accomplished through a series of five washes in 2× SSC at 42 °C, each lasting 15 minutes, followed by a final rinse in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual salts. The wash temperature is carefully controlled to maintain stringency, ensuring that only perfectly matched probe-target hybrids remain bound, while mismatches of even a single nucleotide are efficiently removed. This stringent washing protocol, combined with the high specificity of the oligonucleotide probes, results in exceptionally low background noise and high signal-to-noise ratios, enabling the clear visualization of even faint or discontinuous hybridization signals.

The hybridization efficiency is routinely greater than 90%, as determined by the proportion of metaphase spreads in which the expected signal pattern is observed across the entire target chromosome. In cases where hybridization efficiency is suboptimal, the probe concentration may be increased, the hybridization time extended, or the denaturation temperature adjusted. The use of fluorescently labeled probes allows for real-time monitoring of hybridization progress using fluorescence microscopy, and the method is compatible with automated hybridization systems that ensure consistency across large numbers of samples.

Importantly, the same probe sets used for chromatid painting can also be employed in standard double-stranded FISH applications without modification. In these applications, the pre-treatment step is omitted, and the probes are hybridized directly to double-stranded chromosomal DNA. In such cases, the probes bind to both strands of the target DNA, producing a signal on both sister chromatids, as is typical in conventional FISH. This dual functionality of the probe sets—being usable in both single-stranded and double-stranded hybridization contexts—greatly enhances their utility, as they can be used for both high-resolution inversion detection and conventional chromosome identification within the same laboratory workflow.

The hybridization protocol is robust and reproducible across different cell types, including primary cells, immortalized lines, and tumor cells, and is compatible with a wide range of fixation and mounting techniques. The method is also scalable, allowing for the simultaneous hybridization of multiple probe sets on a single slide, enabling multicolor detection of multiple chromosomes or chromosomal regions in a single assay. The combination of high specificity, low background, and compatibility with standard cytogenetic workflows makes the hybridization step a cornerstone of the chromatid painting method, enabling its reliable application in research, diagnostic, and clinical settings.

### Detection:

The detection of chromosomal inversions using the chromatid painting method is achieved through high-resolution fluorescence microscopy, which enables the direct visualization of hybridization signals on individual metaphase chromosomes with sub-micron precision. The method relies on the spatial distribution of fluorescent signals along sister chromatids to infer the orientation of DNA sequences, with inversions manifesting as abrupt, visually distinct switches in signal location between the two chromatids. This signal switch is not merely a change in intensity or brightness but a complete reversal in the chromosomal position of the hybridization signal, which is unambiguous and easily distinguishable from artifacts such as signal overlap, chromosome folding, or hybridization noise.

The detection system consists of a high-performance inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with a high-sensitivity charge-coupled device (CCD) camera, appropriate excitation and emission filters for the fluorescent dyes used (e.g., Cy3, fluorescein, Texas Red, or Alexa Fluor derivatives), and a motorized stage capable of automated slide scanning. The microscope is outfitted with a 100× oil immersion objective with a numerical aperture of 1.4 or higher to maximize resolution and light collection efficiency. Image capture is performed using specialized software such as MetaVue 7.1, which allows for the acquisition of multi-channel fluorescence images, automated focus stacking, and precise spatial calibration. Each metaphase spread is imaged in multiple channels to distinguish between different probe sets, and the images are then overlaid to generate a composite view of the entire chromosome complement.

The detection of inversions is based on the observation that, in the absence of rearrangement, all hybridization signals from a given probe set are confined to a single sister chromatid, arranged in a linear, sequential manner that mirrors the 5′ to 3′ orientation of the underlying genomic sequence. When an inversion is present, the orientation of the inverted segment is reversed, causing the hybridization signal to appear on the opposite sister chromatid, with a corresponding region of signal absence on the original chromatid. This signal switch is typically abrupt and occurs at a single, well-defined point along the chromosome, corresponding to the inversion breakpoint. In cases of large inversions, the signal switch is accompanied by a visible gap in hybridization on the original chromatid, which spans the length of the inverted segment and is devoid of any signal, as the probes are unable to bind to sequences that are now in the reverse orientation. In smaller inversions, the gap may be less apparent due to the proximity of adjacent probe signals, but the signal switch remains a definitive indicator of inversion status.

The detection process is further enhanced by the use of digital image analysis algorithms that automatically identify and quantify signal switches. These algorithms are trained on a database of known inversion patterns and are capable of distinguishing true signal switches from artifacts such as chromosome overlap, signal bleed-through, or incomplete hybridization. The software assigns a confidence score to each detected event based on signal intensity, spatial continuity, and the presence of a corresponding signal absence, and flags only those events that meet predefined criteria for inversion status. This automated detection system enables the high-throughput analysis of hundreds of metaphase spreads per day, making the method suitable for large-scale screening applications such as radiation biodosimetry, cancer diagnostics, or population-based studies of structural variation.

The method also allows for the simultaneous detection of multiple types of structural rearrangements. For example, translocations are identified by the presence of hybridization signal from one chromosome on a non-homologous chromosome, while dicentric chromosomes are recognized by the presence of two centromeres connected by a single chromosomal arm bearing hybridization signals from two different chromosomes. Acentric fragments are identified by the absence of a centromere and the presence of hybridization signal from a specific chromosome in a free-floating fragment. All of these events can be detected in the same metaphase spread, providing a comprehensive view of genomic instability that is not possible with any other existing method.

The sensitivity of detection is determined by the density and spacing of the probe sets. With probe sets spaced at 1-megabase intervals, inversions of 1 megabase or larger are reliably detected in nearly all cases. For inversions smaller than 1 megabase, detection probability increases with probe density: at 500-kilobase spacing, approximately 50% of inversions are detected; at 100-kilobase spacing, over 90% are detected; and with targeted probe sets covering a region at 10-kilobase intervals, nearly all inversions within that region are detectable. The method is therefore scalable in resolution, allowing users to tailor the probe density to the specific biological question being addressed.

The specificity of detection is exceptionally high, with false-positive rates below 0.1% in control samples from healthy donors. The absence of repetitive element hybridization, the stringent washing protocol, and the orientationally constrained nature of the probe design collectively ensure that signal switches are not artifacts of non-specific binding or probe mislocalization. The method has been validated against gold-standard techniques such as long-read sequencing and optical mapping, and in every case, the inversion breakpoints identified by chromatid painting have been confirmed with nucleotide-level precision.

### Results:

The results obtained using the chromatid painting method demonstrate its exceptional capacity to detect, characterize, and quantify chromosomal inversions with unprecedented resolution, sensitivity, and specificity across a wide range of biological contexts. In normal human fibroblasts and lymphocytes, the frequency of spontaneous inversions was found to be less than 0.3% per cell, indicating that large-scale inversions are exceedingly rare in healthy, non-exposed populations. This low baseline frequency is critical for the method’s utility in detecting induced rearrangements, as it provides a clear and reliable threshold for distinguishing background noise from biologically significant events. Following exposure to ionizing radiation, a dose-dependent increase in inversion frequency was observed, with a linear relationship between radiation dose and the number of signal switches detected per cell. At a dose of 1 Gy, the inversion frequency increased to approximately 1.5%, rising to over 8% at 5 Gy, with no evidence of saturation even at the highest doses tested. This linear response confirms the method’s suitability for use in radiation biodosimetry and environmental risk assessment, where quantitative measurement of DNA damage is essential.

In cancer cell lines, the method revealed a high prevalence of inversions that were previously undetected by conventional cytogenetic or molecular techniques. The Kasumi-4 cell line, derived from a patient with chronic myeloid leukemia, was found to harbor a large inversion on chromosome 3 involving the q21 and q26 breakpoints, which was confirmed to be heterozygous and present in all metaphase cells analyzed. This inversion was not visible by G-banding or whole-chromosome FISH, and its identification by chromatid painting provided the first cytological confirmation of the rearrangement. Similarly, in the HTori-3 thyroid cell line, a subpopulation of cells was found to contain the RET/PTC1 inversion on chromosome 10, which was previously documented only through PCR-based assays. The chromatid painting method not only confirmed the presence of this inversion but also revealed its heterozygous nature and its co-occurrence with other structural variants, including a translocation involving chromosome 2, in a subset of cells.

In comparative studies involving non-human primates, the method detected a previously undocumented large inversion on chromosome 3 in orangutans, which was present in both homologs and spanned approximately 15 megabases. This inversion was not observed in chimpanzees or gorillas, suggesting that it arose after the divergence of the orangutan lineage from the African great apes. The ability to detect such evolutionarily significant rearrangements demonstrates the method’s utility in comparative genomics and phylogenetic studies.

The method also proved highly effective in detecting small inversions. In a simulated inversion experiment, a 6-kilobase segment was inverted in silico and successfully detected by chromatid painting, with a clear signal switch and a corresponding region of signal absence. This result confirmed that inversions as small as a few kilobases can be detected, provided that at least one probe set is positioned within the inverted region. In clinical samples from patients with developmental disorders, the method identified cryptic inversions in regions associated with autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability, which had been missed by SNP arrays and exome sequencing due to their lack of copy number change.

The method’s ability to simultaneously detect multiple types of structural variation was demonstrated in irradiated cells, where dicentric chromosomes, translocations, and inversions were all observed in the same metaphase spread. This multiplex capability provides a comprehensive view of genomic instability that is unmatched by any other method. Furthermore, the method was shown to be compatible with automated image analysis, enabling the rapid screening of thousands of metaphase spreads with high accuracy and reproducibility.

The results also confirmed the method’s robustness across different cell types, fixation protocols, and hybridization conditions. Hybridization efficiency remained consistently above 90% across primary cells, immortalized lines, and tumor cells, and the signal-to-noise ratio was unaffected by variations in slide preparation or probe storage. The method was also successfully applied to archived clinical samples, demonstrating its compatibility with formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues and cryopreserved cells.

In summary, the results demonstrate that chromatid painting is a transformative technology that overcomes the longstanding limitations of conventional cytogenetics and molecular genomics by providing a direct, cell-specific, and orientationally sensitive method for the detection of chromosomal inversions. The method is precise, sensitive, scalable, and broadly applicable, offering new opportunities for research, diagnostics, and risk assessment in human health and disease.
