## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of histological tissue processing has long relied on formaldehyde-based fixation as the gold standard for preserving cellular architecture and molecular integrity in biological specimens. Formaldehyde, typically administered as a 10% neutral buffered formalin solution, functions by cross-linking primary amines on proteins through the formation of methylene bridges, thereby stabilizing macromolecular structures and preventing autolysis and microbial degradation. While effective for morphological preservation, this chemical modification introduces significant challenges for downstream molecular analyses, particularly immunohistochemistry and mass spectrometry-based proteomics. The covalent modifications induced by formaldehyde often mask or alter epitopes recognized by antibodies, resulting in diminished or entirely absent signal detection despite the presence of the target antigen. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as epitope masking, has been a persistent obstacle in diagnostic pathology, neuroscience research, and biomarker discovery, where accurate localization and quantification of specific proteins are critical. Over the decades, numerous strategies have been developed to reverse or mitigate these effects, most notably through the application of antigen retrieval techniques that employ heat, enzymatic digestion, or chemical treatments to unmask hidden epitopes. Conventional antigen retrieval methods have primarily focused on the use of acidic or alkaline buffers—such as citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or EDTA (pH 9.0)—heated under pressure or in microwave ovens to disrupt methylene cross-links and restore antibody accessibility. While these methods have achieved moderate success, they remain inconsistent across tissue types, exhibit variable efficacy depending on fixation duration, and frequently cause structural damage to delicate tissues such as neural parenchyma or vascular basement membranes. Furthermore, enzymatic approaches using pepsin or proteinase K, while effective in some contexts, are highly sensitive to incubation time and temperature, risking over-digestion and loss of tissue integrity. The limitations of existing retrieval protocols have prompted ongoing efforts to identify alternative chemical agents capable of reversing formaldehyde-induced modifications without compromising tissue morphology. Despite these efforts, no prior art has demonstrated that small-molecule formaldehyde scavengers—compounds known to bind and neutralize free formaldehyde in solution—can function as effective antigen retrieval agents when applied post-fixation. The prevailing assumption in the field has been that once formaldehyde has formed stable adducts with proteins, the cross-links are irreversible under physiological conditions and require physical or enzymatic disruption to be overcome. This belief has led to a narrow focus on thermal and proteolytic methods, leaving unexplored the potential of chemical sequestration of residual or reversibly bound formaldehyde as a mechanism for epitope restoration. Recent advances in mass spectrometry have revealed that formaldehyde does not exclusively form irreversible cross-links; a significant fraction of formaldehyde adducts remain in dynamic equilibrium with free formaldehyde, particularly in the early stages after fixation and in regions of high molecular mobility such as extracellular matrices and vascular structures. These findings suggest that formaldehyde may be competitively displaced from protein amines by molecules with higher affinity for the aldehyde group. Compounds such as 2-imidazolidinone, ascorbic acid, and tris buffer have been extensively studied in biochemistry for their ability to trap formaldehyde in solution, forming stable, non-reactive adducts. Their use has been documented in industrial applications for formaldehyde removal in resins and in medical contexts for mitigating formaldehyde toxicity in vivo. However, their application in histology has been limited to pre-fixation treatments or as additives in fixative solutions to reduce cross-linking during tissue preservation. No prior study has investigated their capacity to reverse established formaldehyde modifications after fixation has occurred, nor has any prior art proposed their use as post-fixation retrieval agents in paraffin-embedded tissues. The discovery that these scavengers can actively reverse formaldehyde-induced epitope masking represents a paradigm shift in antigen retrieval methodology. It challenges the long-held assumption that formaldehyde cross-links are permanent and introduces a novel mechanism of action based on competitive displacement rather than thermal denaturation or proteolytic cleavage. This mechanism offers several compelling advantages: it operates at lower temperatures, minimizing tissue distortion; it is compatible with a broader range of tissue types, including those sensitive to heat or enzymatic digestion; and it preserves the native molecular environment of antigens, enhancing the fidelity of detection. Moreover, the chemical nature of these scavengers allows for precise formulation control, enabling optimization of pH, concentration, and exposure time to maximize retrieval efficiency while minimizing background noise or nonspecific staining. The invention disclosed herein is the first to demonstrate that formaldehyde scavengers, when applied as post-fixation retrieval agents, can restore antigenicity in formaldehyde-fixed tissues with efficacy comparable to or exceeding that of conventional methods, while simultaneously preserving tissue morphology and reducing artifacts. This discovery not only resolves a longstanding technical limitation in histopathology but also opens new avenues for multiplexed staining, quantitative proteomics, and longitudinal studies of fixed tissue archives where antigen integrity has been compromised by prolonged formaldehyde exposure. The invention further extends beyond immunohistochemistry to mass spectrometry applications, where the presence of formaldehyde-derived adducts interferes with accurate peptide mass determination and spectral interpretation. By removing residual formaldehyde from tissue sections prior to matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization, these scavengers enable cleaner, more reproducible mass spectra with reduced background noise and enhanced signal-to-noise ratios for low-abundance peptides. This dual functionality—simultaneously enhancing antibody binding and improving mass spectrometric resolution—constitutes a transformative advancement in tissue-based molecular analysis. The invention therefore represents a fundamental innovation in the field of histological processing, offering a chemically precise, broadly applicable, and mechanistically distinct solution to the problem of epitope masking.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for the retrieval of masked antigens in formaldehyde-fixed tissues through the application of formaldehyde scavengers as post-fixation treatment agents. This method enables the restoration of epitope accessibility for immunohistochemical detection and enhances the quality of mass spectrometric analysis in paraffin-embedded tissues without compromising tissue morphology or introducing structural artifacts. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that compounds known to bind and sequester free formaldehyde—specifically 2-imidazolidinone, ascorbic acid, and tris buffer—can effectively reverse established formaldehyde-protein adducts when applied under controlled thermal conditions following tissue fixation. Unlike conventional antigen retrieval techniques that rely on heat-induced denaturation or enzymatic digestion to disrupt cross-links, the method of the invention operates through a chemical displacement mechanism, wherein the scavenger molecules compete with protein-bound formaldehyde for molecular interactions, thereby liberating epitopes that were previously inaccessible to antibodies. This mechanism allows for highly selective and gentle retrieval, preserving the integrity of delicate structures such as vascular basement membranes, neuronal processes, and extracellular matrices that are often damaged by traditional methods. The invention further encompasses the formulation of retrieval solutions containing one or more of these scavengers at optimized concentrations and pH levels, specifically designed for compatibility with standard histological processing workflows. The solutions are applied to deparaffinized tissue sections following rehydration, and heating is conducted at temperatures between 90°C and 98°C for durations ranging from 30 to 60 minutes, under conditions that maintain solution stability and prevent evaporation. The method is applicable to a wide variety of tissue types, including brain, liver, kidney, lung, and vascular tissues, and is particularly effective for antigens localized to endothelial and basement membrane structures, such as type IV collagen and claudin-5, which are notoriously difficult to detect following formaldehyde fixation. The invention also includes a novel application of these scavengers in mass spectrometry-based proteomics, wherein tissue sections are treated with the scavenger solutions prior to matrix deposition to reduce formaldehyde-derived adducts that interfere with peptide ionization and mass accuracy. This application results in cleaner spectra, improved peak resolution, and enhanced detection of low-abundance peptides, thereby increasing the sensitivity and reproducibility of MALDI-TOF analyses. The invention further provides a kit comprising pre-formulated scavenger solutions, standardized heating protocols, and instructional materials for implementation in both research and diagnostic laboratories. The components of the kit are designed for ease of use, requiring no specialized equipment beyond a standard heating chamber or water bath, and are compatible with existing immunohistochemistry and mass spectrometry platforms. The method of the invention is non-toxic, cost-effective, and scalable, offering a practical alternative to high-pressure retrieval systems and enzymatic treatments that require precise calibration and carry inherent risks of over-digestion. The invention further demonstrates that the efficacy of scavenger-based retrieval is superior to conventional citrate-based methods in tissues subjected to prolonged fixation periods, where formaldehyde cross-linking is more extensive and resistant to traditional retrieval. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the field of histology and molecular diagnostics, providing a chemically rational, broadly applicable, and technically superior approach to antigen retrieval that overcomes the limitations of prior art and establishes a new standard for tissue-based molecular analysis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention provides a comprehensive and novel method for restoring antigenicity in formaldehyde-fixed tissues through the application of formaldehyde scavengers as post-fixation retrieval agents. This method is grounded in the unexpected and previously unrecognized observation that certain small-molecule compounds, traditionally employed for their ability to bind and neutralize free formaldehyde in solution, can actively reverse established formaldehyde-protein adducts when applied under controlled thermal conditions to fixed tissue sections. The mechanism of action is distinct from all previously described antigen retrieval techniques, which rely on either thermal denaturation of protein structures or enzymatic cleavage of cross-linked peptides. In contrast, the method of the invention operates through a competitive displacement mechanism, wherein the scavenger molecules, possessing a higher affinity for formaldehyde than the protein residues to which it is bound, intercept and sequester the aldehyde groups, thereby freeing the epitopes from steric and chemical obstruction. This process does not require the disruption of protein tertiary structure or the degradation of peptide bonds, preserving the native conformation of antigens and minimizing tissue distortion. The invention encompasses the identification, formulation, and application of three specific classes of formaldehyde scavengers—2-imidazolidinone, ascorbic acid, and tris buffer—each of which has been demonstrated to effectively restore epitope accessibility in a wide range of tissue types, including neural, vascular, and epithelial tissues. The scavengers are employed in aqueous solutions at concentrations ranging from 1% to 10% weight/volume, with optimal efficacy observed at 5% for all three agents. The pH of the solutions is carefully adjusted to between 3.0 and 4.0, a range that maximizes the reactivity of the scavenger molecules while maintaining the structural integrity of the tissue matrix. This acidic pH environment enhances the protonation state of the scavenger functional groups, increasing their nucleophilicity and thus their capacity to form stable adducts with formaldehyde. The solutions are applied to deparaffinized and rehydrated tissue sections mounted on glass slides, which are then subjected to controlled heating in a sealed chamber maintained at temperatures between 90°C and 98°C for durations of 45 to 60 minutes. The heating process is conducted in a manner that prevents evaporation and ensures uniform temperature distribution across the tissue surface. The use of a Decloaking Chamber or equivalent device is preferred, as it allows for precise control of temperature gradients and consistent heating profiles. Following the heating step, the slides are cooled to ambient temperature, washed in phosphate-buffered saline to remove residual scavenger, and processed for immunohistochemical staining or mass spectrometric analysis. The invention demonstrates that this protocol results in a marked enhancement in signal intensity and specificity for antigens that are typically poorly detected following formaldehyde fixation, including type IV collagen, claudin-5, and other basement membrane and tight junction proteins. The method is particularly effective in tissues subjected to extended fixation periods, where conventional retrieval techniques such as citrate buffer or pepsin digestion fail to restore adequate signal. The invention further provides evidence that the scavenger-based retrieval method is superior in preserving tissue morphology, with minimal shrinkage, fragmentation, or loss of cellular detail compared to heat-based or enzymatic methods. Histological evaluation of tissue sections treated with the scavenger solutions reveals intact nuclear architecture, preserved vascular lumens, and unaltered extracellular matrix organization, all of which are critical for accurate pathological interpretation. The invention further extends to the application of these scavengers in mass spectrometry workflows, where the presence of formaldehyde-derived adducts on peptides introduces mass shifts that complicate spectral interpretation and reduce analytical sensitivity. By treating tissue sections with the scavenger solutions prior to matrix application, the invention eliminates or significantly reduces the abundance of formaldehyde-modified peptides, resulting in cleaner mass spectra with enhanced signal-to-noise ratios. This is particularly advantageous for the detection of low-abundance peptides and for the accurate identification of post-translational modifications. The method is compatible with standard MALDI-TOF protocols and does not require modification of instrumentation or matrix composition. The invention further includes the formulation of standardized retrieval solutions that are stable for extended periods when stored at 4°C, allowing for batch preparation and consistent performance across multiple experiments. The solutions are prepared using reagent-grade chemicals and are free from contaminants that could interfere with downstream analyses. The invention also encompasses the use of these scavengers in combination with other retrieval agents, such as citrate or EDTA, to achieve synergistic effects in particularly challenging tissue types. However, the most effective results are obtained when the scavengers are used as standalone agents, as their mechanism of action is fundamentally distinct and does not require the addition of other chemical agents. The invention further provides a detailed characterization of the chemical interactions between the scavengers and formaldehyde, including the identification of the specific adducts formed, such as the imidazolidinone-formaldehyde hemiacetal, the ascorbic acid-formaldehyde Schiff base, and the tris-formaldehyde methylol derivative. These adducts are stable under the conditions of the retrieval protocol and do not interfere with subsequent staining or ionization processes. The invention further demonstrates that the efficacy of the scavenger-based retrieval method is concentration-dependent, with diminishing returns observed at concentrations above 10% and reduced efficacy below 1%. The duration of heating is similarly critical, with optimal results achieved at 45 to 60 minutes; shorter durations result in incomplete retrieval, while longer durations do not provide additional benefit and may increase the risk of nonspecific background staining. The invention further provides comparative data demonstrating that the scavenger-based method outperforms conventional citrate-based retrieval in terms of signal intensity, specificity, and reproducibility across multiple independent experiments and tissue types. The method is also shown to be effective in tissues fixed with alternative formaldehyde-containing fixatives, including those containing methanol or glutaraldehyde, and is compatible with both paraffin and cryosectioned tissues. The invention further includes the use of these scavengers in multiplexed staining protocols, where multiple antibodies targeting different epitopes are applied simultaneously, and the enhanced antigen accessibility provided by the scavenger treatment allows for clear and distinct labeling of each target without cross-reactivity or signal overlap. The invention further provides evidence that the scavenger-based retrieval method is effective in archival tissue samples, including those stored for years in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded blocks, where antigenicity is typically severely diminished. This capability has significant implications for retrospective studies, biomarker validation, and clinical diagnostics, where access to historical tissue collections is essential. The invention further encompasses the development of a kit for implementing the method, comprising pre-measured aliquots of each scavenger in lyophilized or liquid form, standardized heating protocols, instructional materials, and controls for validation. The kit is designed for use in both research and diagnostic laboratories and requires no specialized training beyond standard histological procedures. The invention further includes the use of these scavengers in automated staining platforms, where the retrieval step can be integrated into existing robotic workflows without modification. The method is scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally benign, as the scavenger solutions are non-toxic, biodegradable, and do not generate hazardous waste. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in the field of histological processing, providing a chemically rational, broadly applicable, and technically superior approach to antigen retrieval that overcomes the limitations of prior art and establishes a new standard for tissue-based molecular analysis.

### Compounds

The invention employs three distinct chemical compounds as formaldehyde scavengers for the purpose of antigen retrieval in formaldehyde-fixed tissues: 2-imidazolidinone, ascorbic acid, and tris buffer. Each of these compounds possesses unique chemical properties that enable them to effectively bind and sequester formaldehyde, thereby reversing its masking effects on epitopes. 2-Imidazolidinone is a cyclic amide with two primary amine groups that are highly nucleophilic under acidic conditions, allowing it to react rapidly with formaldehyde to form stable, non-reactive hemiacetal adducts. Its molecular structure, characterized by a five-membered ring containing two nitrogen atoms, provides a constrained geometry that enhances its affinity for formaldehyde compared to linear amines. The compound is commercially available in high-purity form and exhibits excellent solubility in aqueous solutions, making it ideal for formulation into retrieval buffers. When dissolved in water at concentrations of 5% (w/v) and adjusted to a pH of 3.5, 2-imidazolidinone forms a homogeneous solution that remains stable for extended periods at 4°C. The reaction between 2-imidazolidinone and formaldehyde proceeds through a nucleophilic addition mechanism, wherein one of the amine groups attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon of formaldehyde, forming a carbinolamine intermediate that subsequently cyclizes into a stable five-membered ring structure. This adduct is not only thermodynamically favorable but also kinetically inert under the conditions of the retrieval protocol, ensuring that formaldehyde is permanently sequestered and unable to rebind to protein epitopes. Ascorbic acid, also known as vitamin C, is a naturally occurring enediol compound with a highly reactive enol group that readily forms Schiff bases with aldehydes. Its ability to act as a formaldehyde scavenger stems from its capacity to undergo oxidation to dehydroascorbic acid upon reaction with formaldehyde, a process that irreversibly traps the aldehyde in a stable adduct. Ascorbic acid is particularly advantageous due to its low toxicity, widespread availability, and compatibility with biological tissues. When formulated at a concentration of 5% (w/v) and buffered to a pH of 3.2, ascorbic acid produces a clear, colorless solution that does not induce autofluorescence or interfere with fluorophore-based detection systems. The reaction kinetics of ascorbic acid with formaldehyde are accelerated under elevated temperatures, making it particularly effective during the heating phase of the retrieval protocol. The adduct formed between ascorbic acid and formaldehyde is a six-membered cyclic structure that is resistant to hydrolysis and does not dissociate under physiological conditions, ensuring complete and irreversible neutralization of formaldehyde. Tris buffer, or tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, is a commonly used biological buffer that contains a primary amine group capable of reacting with formaldehyde to form a methylol derivative. Although tris is widely employed in biochemical assays for pH stabilization, its role as a formaldehyde scavenger has been largely overlooked in histological applications. The invention demonstrates that when tris is formulated at a concentration of 5% (w/v) and adjusted to a pH of 3.8, it exhibits remarkable efficacy in reversing formaldehyde-induced epitope masking. The methylol adduct formed between tris and formaldehyde is stable under the retrieval conditions and does not interfere with subsequent antibody binding or mass spectrometric analysis. The advantage of tris lies in its buffering capacity, which helps maintain the pH of the retrieval solution during heating, preventing pH drift that could otherwise compromise tissue integrity or antibody performance. All three compounds are free from heavy metals, endotoxins, and other contaminants that could interfere with immunohistochemical or mass spectrometric analyses. They are each synthesized or procured from reputable chemical suppliers using reagent-grade purity standards, and their identity and purity are verified by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy and high-performance liquid chromatography prior to use. The invention further includes the use of these compounds in combination with each other, although the most effective results are obtained when each is used individually, as their mechanisms of action are sufficiently robust to function independently. The invention further provides detailed characterization of the chemical interactions between each scavenger and formaldehyde, including mass spectrometric identification of the resulting adducts, kinetic studies of adduct formation, and thermodynamic stability assessments under varying pH and temperature conditions. The invention further demonstrates that the scavenger solutions do not degrade tissue components such as lipids, nucleic acids, or glycoproteins, and do not induce nonspecific binding of antibodies or background staining. The chemical stability of the solutions allows for long-term storage and repeated use without loss of efficacy, making the invention suitable for high-throughput laboratory applications. The invention further includes the formulation of these scavengers in alternative solvents, including phosphate-buffered saline, distilled water, and low-concentration ethanol mixtures, to accommodate different tissue types and staining protocols. However, aqueous solutions are preferred due to their compatibility with standard histological workflows and their ability to penetrate tissue sections uniformly during the heating process. The invention further provides evidence that the scavenger solutions do not interfere with the binding of secondary antibodies, fluorophores, or enzymatic detection systems, and that they are compatible with a wide range of detection platforms, including fluorescence microscopy, brightfield microscopy, and digital imaging systems. The invention thus establishes these three compounds as novel and effective agents for antigen retrieval, offering a chemically precise, reproducible, and broadly applicable solution to the longstanding problem of epitope masking in formaldehyde-fixed tissues.

### EXAMPLE 2

In this example, the efficacy of the scavenger-based antigen retrieval method was evaluated using formaldehyde-fixed brain tissue sections from adult C57BL/6J mice. Tissues were fixed via transcardial perfusion with 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline, followed by post-fixation in the same solution for 72 hours at 4°C. Brains were then processed for paraffin embedding and sectioned at 10 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized using xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series to distilled water. The tissue sections were then subjected to antigen retrieval using one of four different solutions: (1) 10 mM sodium citrate buffer, pH 6.0, as a conventional control; (2) 5% (w/v) 2-imidazolidinone in distilled water, pH 3.5; (3) 5% (w/v) ascorbic acid in distilled water, pH 3.2; and (4) 5% (w/v) tris buffer in distilled water, pH 3.8. Each solution was heated in a Decloaking Chamber to 95°C and maintained at that temperature for 45 minutes, followed by a controlled cooling period to ambient temperature. After retrieval, sections were washed in phosphate-buffered saline and incubated with a primary antibody against type IV collagen (1:500 dilution), a well-established marker of the vascular basement membrane. Following incubation, sections were treated with a biotinylated secondary antibody and visualized using an avidin-conjugated fluorophore. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using standardized imaging parameters on a confocal microscope equipped with a 20× objective and identical exposure settings across all samples. The results demonstrated a marked enhancement in signal intensity for all three scavenger-treated groups compared to the citrate control. Specifically, the 2-imidazolidinone-treated sections exhibited a 3.8-fold increase in mean fluorescence intensity, ascorbic acid-treated sections showed a 3.5-fold increase, and tris buffer-treated sections displayed a 3.2-fold increase. In contrast, the citrate-treated sections showed only a 1.4-fold increase over untreated controls, indicating limited efficacy in reversing formaldehyde-induced epitope masking. The specificity of the staining was confirmed by the absence of signal in negative control sections treated with non-immune serum. Furthermore, the spatial distribution of the collagen IV signal in scavenger-treated sections was more continuous and uniform along the vascular walls, with minimal fragmentation or discontinuity, whereas citrate-treated sections exhibited patchy, irregular staining with frequent gaps in labeling. High-magnification imaging revealed that the scavenger-treated sections preserved the fine structural details of capillary networks, including pericyte associations and basement membrane thickness, whereas citrate-treated sections showed signs of tissue distortion, including slight shrinkage and loss of cellular boundaries. The invention further demonstrated that the scavenger-based retrieval method was effective even in tissues subjected to prolonged fixation periods of up to 14 days, where citrate-based retrieval failed to produce detectable signal. In these samples, the 2-imidazolidinone solution restored 92% of the signal intensity observed in fresh-frozen controls, whereas citrate buffer restored only 28%. The invention further confirmed that the retrieval method did not induce nonspecific background staining, autofluorescence, or artifacts in the tissue matrix. The fluorescence signal was localized exclusively to the vascular basement membrane, with no staining observed in parenchymal cells, nuclei, or extracellular spaces. The reproducibility of the results was confirmed across three independent experimental replicates, with coefficients of variation below 8% for all scavenger-treated groups. The invention further demonstrated that the retrieval efficacy was concentration-dependent, with optimal results achieved at 5% concentration for all three scavengers. Lower concentrations (1% and 2.5%) resulted in significantly reduced signal intensity, while higher concentrations (10%) did not provide additional benefit and occasionally led to increased background noise. The duration of heating was also found to be critical, with 45 minutes being the minimum time required for maximal signal restoration; shorter durations (20 or 30 minutes) resulted in incomplete retrieval, while longer durations (60 or 90 minutes) did not improve signal and occasionally led to slight tissue degradation. The invention further confirmed that the scavenger solutions did not interfere with the binding of the secondary antibody or the fluorophore conjugate, as evidenced by identical signal intensity in positive control sections treated with unfixed tissue. The results of this example establish that the scavenger-based retrieval method is not only superior to conventional citrate-based methods in restoring antigenicity but also provides enhanced preservation of tissue morphology and spatial fidelity, making it particularly suitable for high-resolution imaging applications in neuroscience and vascular biology.

### EXAMPLE 3

In this example, the scavenger-based antigen retrieval method was applied to the detection of claudin-5, a tight junction protein critical for maintaining the integrity of the blood-brain barrier, in formaldehyde-fixed murine brain tissue. Tissues were prepared as described in Example 2, with fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 hours and paraffin embedding. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, then subjected to antigen retrieval using the same four solutions as in Example 2: sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0), 5% 2-imidazolidinone (pH 3.5), 5% ascorbic acid (pH 3.2), and 5% tris buffer (pH 3.8). Following retrieval, sections were incubated with a monoclonal primary antibody against claudin-5 (1:200 dilution), followed by a biotinylated secondary antibody and visualization with an avidin-conjugated fluorophore. The claudin-5 signal was imaged using confocal microscopy with identical acquisition parameters across all samples. Quantitative analysis revealed that the scavenger-treated sections exhibited substantially greater signal intensity and continuity compared to the citrate-treated controls. Specifically, 2-imidazolidinone-treated sections showed a 4.1-fold increase in mean fluorescence intensity, ascorbic acid-treated sections showed a 3.9-fold increase, and tris buffer-treated sections showed a 3.7-fold increase. In contrast, citrate buffer-treated sections demonstrated only a 1.6-fold increase over untreated controls. The spatial distribution of claudin-5 staining in the scavenger-treated sections was markedly improved, with continuous, uninterrupted labeling along the entire length of cerebral capillaries, including small arterioles and venules. The staining pattern closely resembled that observed in fresh-frozen tissue sections, indicating faithful preservation of the native protein localization. In contrast, citrate-treated sections displayed fragmented, discontinuous staining, with frequent gaps in labeling and loss of signal in smaller vessels. High-magnification imaging revealed that the scavenger-treated sections preserved the fine intercellular junctions between endothelial cells, with sharp delineation of cell borders and minimal background staining. The invention further demonstrated that the scavenger-based method was effective in detecting claudin-5 in tissues subjected to extended fixation periods of 10 days, where citrate-based retrieval failed to produce any detectable signal. In these samples, the 2-imidazolidinone solution restored 89% of the signal intensity observed in fresh-frozen controls, whereas citrate buffer restored only 18%. The invention further confirmed that the retrieval method did not induce nonspecific binding to non-endothelial cell types, as no signal was observed in neurons, astrocytes, or microglia. The specificity of the staining was further validated by the absence of signal in sections treated with isotype-matched control antibodies. The reproducibility of the results was confirmed across four independent experimental replicates, with coefficients of variation below 7% for all scavenger-treated groups. The invention further demonstrated that the retrieval efficacy was dependent on the pH of the solution, with optimal results achieved at pH 3.2 to 3.8. Solutions adjusted to pH 5.0 or higher showed significantly reduced efficacy, while solutions below pH 3.0 exhibited increased tissue autofluorescence and nonspecific background. The duration of heating was again found to be critical, with 45 minutes being the optimal time for maximal signal restoration. Shorter durations (20 or 30 minutes) resulted in partial retrieval, while longer durations (60 minutes or more) did not enhance signal and occasionally led to slight tissue swelling. The invention further confirmed that the scavenger solutions did not interfere with the binding of the primary antibody or the fluorophore conjugate, as evidenced by identical signal intensity in positive control sections treated with unfixed tissue. The results of this example establish that the scavenger-based retrieval method is uniquely suited for the detection of tight junction proteins, which are notoriously difficult to visualize following formaldehyde fixation due to their dense, cross-linked structure. The invention demonstrates that the chemical displacement mechanism employed by these scavengers is particularly effective for epitopes that are deeply embedded within protein complexes and resistant to thermal or enzymatic disruption. The ability to restore claudin-5 signal with high fidelity and spatial precision represents a significant advancement in the study of the blood-brain barrier and its role in neurological disease.

### EXAMPLE 4

In this example, the scavenger-based antigen retrieval method was evaluated for its ability to enhance the detection of multiple antigens in multiplexed immunofluorescence staining of formaldehyde-fixed murine brain tissue. The spatial relationship between the two antigens was preserved with high fidelity, revealing a continuous, overlapping pattern of collagen IV surrounding the claudin-5-positive endothelial cells, consistent with their known biological relationship in the blood-brain barrier. The invention further demonstrated that the scavenger-based retrieval method did not induce cross-reactivity between the primary or secondary antibodies, as evidenced by the absence of signal in control sections treated with single primary antibodies or isotype controls. The background fluorescence was minimal, with no autofluorescence observed in the tissue matrix or in the absence of primary antibodies. The reproducibility of the multiplexed staining was confirmed across five independent replicates, with coefficients of variation below 6% for both antigens. The invention further demonstrated that the retrieval method was compatible with other fluorophores, including Cy3, Cy5, and FITC, and that the signal intensity remained stable over extended imaging periods. The invention further confirmed that the scavenger solution did not interfere with the binding kinetics of the antibodies, as evidenced by identical staining patterns in sections treated with unfixed tissue. The results of this example establish that the scavenger-based antigen retrieval method is not only effective for single-antigen detection but also for multiplexed applications, where the simultaneous visualization of multiple epitopes is essential for understanding complex biological interactions. The ability to detect both basement membrane and tight junction proteins with high fidelity and spatial precision in the same tissue section represents a significant advancement in the field of neurovascular biology and provides a powerful tool for the study of blood-brain barrier integrity in health and disease.

### EXAMPLE 5

In this example, the scavenger-based antigen retrieval method was applied to formaldehyde-fixed tissues from multiple organ systems, including liver, kidney, lung, and heart, to evaluate its broad applicability across tissue types. Tissues were harvested from adult C57BL/6J mice, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 72 hours, and processed for paraffin embedding. Sections were cut at 10 μm thickness and deparaffinized and rehydrated as described in prior examples. Antigen retrieval was performed using 5% 2-imidazolidinone, pH 3.5, as the retrieval agent, with heating at 95°C for 45 minutes. Following retrieval, sections were stained with antibodies specific to tissue-specific antigens: albumin in liver, podocin in kidney, surfactant protein C in lung, and cardiac troponin I in heart. Each antibody was applied at its established optimal dilution, and detection was performed using standard immunofluorescence protocols with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies. The results demonstrated that the scavenger-based retrieval method significantly enhanced the detection of all four antigens compared to conventional citrate buffer treatment. In liver tissue, albumin staining revealed a marked increase in signal intensity and continuity along the sinusoidal endothelium, with clear delineation of hepatic cords and minimal background staining. In kidney tissue, podocin staining showed enhanced labeling of the glomerular slit diaphragm, with sharp, uninterrupted signal along the foot processes of podocytes, whereas citrate-treated sections exhibited fragmented and weak staining. In lung tissue, surfactant protein C was clearly visualized in type II alveolar epithelial cells, with uniform labeling across the alveolar walls and no nonspecific staining in interstitial cells. In heart tissue, cardiac troponin I was detected with high specificity in cardiomyocytes, with clear striated patterns corresponding to sarcomeric bands.
