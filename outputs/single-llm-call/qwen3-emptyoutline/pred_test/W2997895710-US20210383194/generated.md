## BACKGROUND

The analysis of event stream data has become a critical component in understanding complex dynamic systems across diverse domains including healthcare, social media, financial markets, cybersecurity, and political science. Event streams consist of sequences of discrete occurrences, each associated with a type and a precise timestamp, unfolding along a continuous timeline. These events are inherently asynchronous, irregularly spaced, and often exhibit intricate dependencies between different event types and over extended temporal horizons. Traditional statistical models for such data, including homogeneous and nonhomogeneous Poisson processes, have long served as foundational tools, but they are limited by their assumption of independence or simple parametric forms of temporal dependence. More sophisticated models, such as Hawkes processes and their variants, introduced the concept of self-excitation and mutual excitation, where past events increase the likelihood of future events through decaying influence functions. However, these models remain constrained by their reliance on predefined functional forms—typically exponential decay—that may not reflect the true underlying dynamics of real-world systems.

Recent advances in machine learning have sought to overcome these limitations by leveraging deep neural networks to model the conditional intensity function of event streams in a more flexible manner. Recurrent neural networks, particularly those based on long short-term memory (LSTM) architectures, have been employed to encode the history of past events into a hidden state that evolves with each new observation. These approaches, such as the recurrent marked temporal point process (RMTPP) and the neural Hawkes process (NHP), represent a significant step forward by replacing fixed parametric assumptions with learned representations. Nevertheless, these models still operate under implicit constraints: they typically treat event arrivals as discrete updates to the hidden state, leaving the time between events unmodeled beyond a simple scalar interval. The internal state of the network remains static between event occurrences, effectively ignoring the continuous evolution of influence that occurs during intervals where no event is observed. This limitation is particularly problematic because the absence of an event at a given moment can be just as informative as its presence, especially in systems where inhibition, delayed activation, or time-varying baseline rates are prevalent.

Moreover, real-world event streams often involve multiple interacting types of events, each with distinct temporal scales and influence patterns. For instance, in electronic health records, a diagnosis of hypertension may influence the likelihood of a subsequent cardiac event after a delay of weeks, while a medication change may suppress symptoms within hours. In political event datasets, diplomatic actions may trigger protests only after a lag of days, and the influence of one actor on another may vary depending on historical context. Existing models struggle to capture such multi-scale, heterogeneous dependencies because they either enforce a single temporal resolution across all event types or rely on hand-engineered basis functions to represent temporal influence. Graphical event models attempt to address this by defining parent-child relationships among event types and modeling influence through fixed temporal windows, but they require prior structural assumptions and are not easily scalable to large label sets or complex interaction patterns.

Furthermore, the ability to learn which historical events are most relevant to predicting future occurrences—without pre-specifying the nature of their influence—is a fundamental challenge. Attention mechanisms have been introduced in sequence modeling to dynamically weight the relevance of past inputs, but their application to event streams has been limited to simple, single-channel representations that do not distinguish between event types or account for the full temporal depth of historical context. As a result, current models lack the capacity to simultaneously learn the structure of inter-event dependencies, the temporal decay profiles of influence, and the relative importance of different historical events in a fully data-driven manner.

There remains a critical need for a modeling framework that can capture the continuous-time evolution of event influences without imposing restrictive parametric assumptions, that can distinguish between multiple event types with independent temporal dynamics, and that can automatically identify which past events and time intervals are most relevant to the current rate of occurrence. Such a framework must be capable of leveraging not only the presence of events but also the absence of events as meaningful signals, must adapt to varying time scales across event types, and must provide interpretable insights into the underlying causal or associative structure of the system under study. The invention described herein fulfills this need by introducing a novel architecture that integrates negative evidence from inter-event intervals, multi-channel state representations, and spatiotemporal attention mechanisms into a unified, non-parametric deep learning model for multivariate event stream analysis.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a system and method for modeling multivariate event streams in continuous time using a non-parametric deep learning architecture that explicitly incorporates negative evidence from inter-event intervals, employs a multi-channel internal state representation to capture label-specific dynamics, and utilizes spatiotemporal attention to determine the most influential historical events and time lags. The invention enables the learning of complex, data-driven conditional intensity functions without relying on predefined parametric forms such as exponential decay or piecewise-constant basis functions. The system operates by augmenting the observed event sequence with synthetic events, referred to as fake epochs, uniformly distributed within each inter-event interval. These fake epochs serve as temporal sampling points that allow the internal state of a recurrent neural network to evolve continuously between real event arrivals, thereby encoding the passage of time and the absence of observable events as informative signals. The internal state of the network is partitioned into multiple channels, each corresponding to a distinct event type, enabling independent evolution of influence patterns across different labels. A memory bank stores the most recent hidden states from each channel across a fixed number of prior event epochs, and an attention mechanism computes a weighted combination of these stored states to produce a context-aware representation for each event type at each point in time. This attention mechanism simultaneously captures spatial dependencies—i.e., which other event types influence the current rate—and temporal dependencies—i.e., which historical time points are most relevant—thereby enabling the model to learn complex, delayed, and non-monotonic influence patterns. The conditional intensity for each event type is then computed from this attended representation and the duration since the last event using a feed-forward neural network with non-linear activation functions that ensure positivity. The entire model is trained end-to-end using maximum likelihood estimation, with additional regularization terms to prevent overfitting and to reinforce the distinction between real and synthetic events. The resulting system provides superior performance in modeling the underlying stochastic dynamics of event streams compared to existing approaches, as demonstrated by higher log-likelihood scores on synthetic and real-world datasets. Furthermore, the attention weights learned by the model can be interpreted as a directed graph, revealing the structure of influence between event types, thus providing both predictive accuracy and structural insight into the system under analysis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a novel deep learning architecture designed to model multivariate event streams in continuous time by integrating three core innovations: the explicit incorporation of negative evidence through synthetic event epochs, a multi-channel internal state representation that enables label-specific temporal dynamics, and a spatiotemporal attention mechanism that dynamically identifies the most influential historical events and time lags. The architecture is implemented as a recurrent neural network with long short-term memory units, augmented with a structured memory bank and an attention layer, and trained using a likelihood-based objective function that accounts for both event occurrences and the absence of events over time. The system operates on a sequence of events, each defined by a label indicating the type of occurrence and a timestamp indicating its precise time of arrival. The sequence is processed in temporal order, and at each event arrival, the model updates its internal state to reflect the new observation, while simultaneously accounting for the passage of time since the previous event. The key innovation lies in the introduction of auxiliary events, referred to as fake epochs, which are inserted uniformly within each interval between consecutive real events. These fake epochs are not actual observations but are treated as pseudo-events within the recurrent network’s computational framework. Each fake epoch has a unique label distinct from all real event types and is assigned a timestamp that lies strictly between the timestamps of the preceding and succeeding real events. The number of fake epochs inserted per interval is a configurable hyperparameter, and their placement is deterministic and evenly spaced. By processing these fake epochs through the same recurrent network used for real events, the internal hidden state of the network is allowed to evolve continuously over time, even in the absence of observable events. This evolution encodes the duration between events as a dynamic signal, effectively capturing the notion that the longer an event type has not occurred, the more its underlying influence may have decayed, accumulated, or transformed in a non-linear fashion. This approach eliminates the need to assume a parametric form for the temporal decay of influence, such as exponential or polynomial decay, and instead allows the network to learn the shape of this decay directly from the data.

The internal state of the recurrent network is partitioned into multiple channels, each corresponding to one of the possible event types in the system. For a system with M distinct event types, the hidden state vector is divided into M sub-vectors, each of equal dimensionality, such that each sub-vector evolves independently under the influence of events of its corresponding type. This multi-channel structure ensures that the temporal dynamics of each event type are modeled separately, allowing for differences in baseline rates, influence patterns, and response times across types. For example, in a healthcare setting, the occurrence of a fever may rapidly increase the likelihood of a subsequent antibiotic prescription, while the occurrence of a laboratory test may have a delayed and weaker influence on the likelihood of a diagnosis. The multi-channel representation enables the model to capture such distinctions without requiring manual feature engineering or predefined interaction rules. The hidden state of each channel is updated at every event arrival, whether real or fake, using a shared long short-term memory cell that processes the concatenated input of the event label and the time since the last event. The embedding of the event label is learned during training and maps each label to a dense vector representation, which is combined with the continuous time value to form the input to the recurrent unit. The use of a shared LSTM across channels reduces the number of parameters and promotes generalization, while the partitioning of the hidden state preserves the independence of influence patterns across event types.

To further enhance the model’s ability to capture long-range dependencies and complex interaction patterns, a memory bank is maintained that stores the most recent hidden states from each channel across a fixed number of prior event epochs. The size of this memory bank, denoted as J, determines the maximum temporal depth of influence that the model can consider. For each event arrival, the memory bank contains J × M hidden state sub-vectors, representing the state of each of the M channels at each of the J most recent event times. These stored states are then processed by an attention mechanism that computes a context vector for each event type by weighting the relevance of each historical hidden state in the memory bank. The attention mechanism operates by comparing the current hidden state of a target event type with each of the stored hidden states, using a learned similarity function to produce a set of attention weights. These weights indicate the degree to which each historical event, and its corresponding type and time lag, contributes to the current instantaneous rate of occurrence for the target event type. The attention weights are computed using a feed-forward neural network that takes as input the dot product between the current hidden state and each historical hidden state, followed by a softmax normalization to ensure that the weights sum to one. The resulting weighted sum of historical states forms a net hidden state that encapsulates the most relevant historical context for predicting the next event. This attention mechanism enables the model to learn which past events are most predictive, regardless of their temporal proximity or type, and to adaptively ignore irrelevant or redundant historical information. For example, in a political event dataset, the model may learn that a protest in one country has little influence on the likelihood of a diplomatic statement in another, but a similar protest in a neighboring country, occurring within the past three days, significantly increases the likelihood of a security response. The attention mechanism captures such nuanced relationships without requiring prior knowledge of the underlying causal structure.

The conditional intensity function for each event type is then computed from the net hidden state and the duration since the last event using a two-layer feed-forward neural network. The first layer applies a rectified linear unit activation function to transform the input into a non-linear representation, and the second layer applies a softplus activation function to ensure that the output is strictly positive, as required for a valid intensity function. The softplus function guarantees that the predicted rate is always greater than zero, which is essential for the mathematical validity of the point process likelihood. The intensity function is evaluated at each event arrival, including the fake epochs, although the likelihood computation only includes the real events. The likelihood of the observed event sequence is computed using the standard formula for the log-likelihood of a marked point process, which consists of a sum over the log-intensities at the times of real events minus an integral of the total intensity over the entire observation period. Because the model has introduced fake epochs, the integral term is approximated using numerical quadrature, with the timestamps of the fake epochs serving as sampling points. This approximation is justified by the fact that the intensity function is assumed to be piecewise constant between consecutive events, whether real or fake, and the density of fake epochs can be increased to achieve arbitrary precision. The inclusion of fake epochs thus transforms the continuous-time integral into a discrete summation that is computationally tractable while preserving the fidelity of the underlying model.

To improve generalization and prevent overfitting, the training objective is augmented with two regularization terms. The first term is a classification loss that encourages the model to predict the label of the next real event based on the predicted intensity vector. This is implemented as a cross-entropy loss between the softmax of the intensity vector and the ground truth label of the next event. This term reinforces the adversarial nature of the fake epochs by ensuring that the model learns to distinguish between real and synthetic events, thereby improving the quality of the learned intensity function. The second term is an L2-norm penalty on the weights of the feed-forward layers that compute the intensity function, which discourages overly complex models and promotes smoother, more stable predictions. The overall training objective is therefore a weighted sum of the point process log-likelihood, the classification loss, and the weight regularization term. The weights of these components are treated as hyperparameters and are tuned using a validation set. The model is trained end-to-end using stochastic gradient descent with adaptive learning rate methods such as Adam, and gradients are computed using backpropagation through time, accounting for the full sequence of real and fake events.

The architecture of the invention is implemented as a computational system that can be executed on general-purpose computing hardware, including central processing units and graphics processing units, and is compatible with standard deep learning frameworks such as PyTorch and TensorFlow. The input to the system is a sequence of event records, each comprising a label and a timestamp, and the output is a set of predicted conditional intensities for each event type at each point in time. During inference, the system processes the event sequence in temporal order, inserting fake epochs as needed, computing the internal state updates, applying the attention mechanism, and producing the intensity predictions. The system can be used for a variety of applications, including anomaly detection, predictive modeling, causal inference, and system diagnostics. For example, in a data center environment, the system can model the sequence of server failures, network timeouts, and maintenance events to predict the likelihood of future outages and identify the most influential historical events leading to system instability. In healthcare, the system can analyze patient visit histories to predict the onset of complications based on prior diagnoses, treatments, and lab results. In social media, the system can model the spread of information, sentiment shifts, and user interactions to forecast viral trends and identify key influencers.

One of the key advantages of the invention is its ability to generate interpretable insights into the underlying structure of the event system. After training, the attention weights learned by the model can be aggregated across the entire dataset to produce a directed graph, where each node represents an event type and each edge represents the average influence of one event type on another. The strength of each edge is determined by the mean attention weight between the source and target event types across all time points and all event sequences. This graph provides a visual representation of the dependencies between event types, revealing patterns of excitation, inhibition, delay, and feedback. For example, in a political event dataset, the graph may reveal that diplomatic negotiations between two countries are frequently followed by public protests, which in turn lead to increased police presence, creating a feedback loop that sustains social unrest. In a healthcare dataset, the graph may show that a specific medication is associated with a delayed reduction in symptom severity, indicating a therapeutic lag that was not apparent from the raw data. These graphs are not pre-specified but are learned entirely from the data, making the model a powerful tool for exploratory data analysis and hypothesis generation.

The invention also demonstrates superior performance compared to existing state-of-the-art models on a variety of benchmark datasets. On synthetic datasets generated from proximal graphical event models, the invention achieves higher log-likelihood scores than both the original graphical model learner and neural Hawkes processes, indicating that it can recover the true underlying dynamics even when the generating model is known. On real-world datasets such as political event logs from the ICEWS system and electronic health records from the MIMIC-II database, the invention consistently outperforms competing models, with improvements in log-likelihood ranging from 5% to 35%, depending on the dataset and the number of fake epochs used. Ablation studies confirm that each component of the invention contributes meaningfully to its performance: removing the fake epochs results in a significant drop in accuracy, as does reducing the number of channels or limiting the memory bank size. The use of a single channel, where all event types share the same hidden state, leads to poorer performance, demonstrating the importance of label-specific dynamics. Similarly, removing the attention mechanism and using a fixed, unweighted combination of historical states results in a loss of predictive power, confirming that dynamic weighting of historical events is essential for capturing complex dependencies. The optimal number of fake epochs is found to be small—typically one or two—indicating that a minimal amount of temporal sampling is sufficient to capture the essential dynamics of the system, and that adding too many fake epochs introduces noise and imbalance in the training objective.

The invention is robust to variations in data density, event type distribution, and temporal scale. It performs well on datasets with sparse events and long intervals, as well as on datasets with high event rates and short time lags. It is also scalable to large numbers of event types, as the multi-channel structure and attention mechanism are designed to handle arbitrary label sets without requiring exponential growth in parameters. The model does not require any prior knowledge of the event structure, making it applicable to any domain where event streams are observed, regardless of whether the underlying dynamics are understood. The use of fake epochs eliminates the need for manual specification of basis functions, temporal windows, or decay kernels, making the model truly non-parametric and data-driven. The attention mechanism provides a mechanism for discovering latent relationships without supervision, and the multi-channel representation ensures that the model can capture heterogeneous dynamics across different types of events. Together, these features make the invention a groundbreaking advancement in the field of event stream modeling, offering a unified framework that combines the flexibility of deep learning with the interpretability of graphical models and the rigor of point process theory.

The invention further includes a method for evaluating the quality of the learned model using out-of-sample log-likelihood as the primary metric. During testing, the model is applied to a held-out sequence of events, and the log-likelihood of the observed events under the learned intensity function is computed. This metric provides a principled, probabilistic measure of how well the model captures the underlying stochastic process, and it is used to compare the invention against other models in a fair and consistent manner. The model is not optimized for prediction accuracy in the traditional sense, such as classification or regression error, but rather for the fidelity of its probabilistic representation of the event dynamics. This makes it particularly suited for applications where understanding the uncertainty and structure of the system is as important as making accurate predictions. The invention can also be extended to handle multiple independent event streams, such as multiple patients in a healthcare setting or multiple users in a social network, by training the model on a collection of sequences and using a shared parameter set across all streams. This enables the model to learn generalizable patterns that hold across individuals or entities, while still allowing for individual variation through the stochastic nature of the event generation process.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, end-to-end solution for modeling multivariate event streams in continuous time. It overcomes the limitations of existing models by incorporating negative evidence through synthetic epochs, enabling label-specific dynamics through a multi-channel representation, and discovering complex dependencies through spatiotemporal attention. The model is non-parametric, interpretable, scalable, and empirically superior to all prior approaches. It represents a significant advancement in the field of temporal point process modeling and opens new possibilities for understanding and predicting the behavior of complex dynamic systems across a wide range of scientific and industrial applications.
