# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates to a method and system for determining the time of formation of a blood pool at a forensic scene through the quantitative analysis of its drying dynamics. Specifically, the invention provides a non-invasive, image-based technique that leverages the physical and thermodynamic properties of evaporating blood to estimate the elapsed time since deposition with a precision of ±30 minutes. This method is particularly applicable in criminal investigations where the presence of a body is absent or unconfirmed, and traditional time-of-death indicators such as algor mortis, rigor mortis, or entomological evidence are unavailable. The invention operates under the premise that the evaporation of a blood pool is governed by a reproducible sequence of physical phenomena, including the transition from a liquid colloidal suspension to a gel-like matrix, the propagation of a drying front, and the diffusion of water vapor through a porous biological structure. By integrating measurements of the wet area of the pool, the geometric configuration of the pool, environmental parameters, and a calibrated diffusion coefficient derived from empirical observation, the invention enables the reconstruction of a temporal timeline for bloodshed events without requiring physical sampling, chemical analysis, or invasive instrumentation. The method is designed for deployment in field conditions using standard photographic equipment and computational image processing, making it immediately practical for forensic investigators, crime scene technicians, and law enforcement personnel. The system does not rely on the presence of biological tissue beyond the blood pool itself, thereby expanding the scope of forensic temporal reconstruction to scenarios previously considered analytically intractable.

## TECHNICAL BACKGROUND

Forensic science has long relied on a suite of methods to estimate the time of events occurring at a crime scene, particularly those involving violent trauma and hemorrhage. The most commonly employed techniques include the measurement of core body temperature to determine algor mortis, the assessment of muscle stiffening through rigor mortis, and the analysis of insect colonization patterns via forensic entomology. Each of these methods requires the presence of a human body and is subject to significant variability due to environmental conditions, individual physiology, and postmortem changes. In cases where the victim’s body is removed, concealed, or never recovered, these standard indicators become inapplicable, leaving investigators without any reliable means to establish when the bloodshed occurred. While the presence of bloodstains is often the most persistent physical evidence at such scenes, their temporal interpretation has historically been limited to qualitative observations—such as the color, texture, or pattern of dried blood—without any quantitative framework for determining the time of deposition. 

Prior attempts to correlate bloodstain morphology with time have focused primarily on the classification of drying stages, such as coagulation, gelation, rim desiccation, central desiccation, and final drying. These stages, while visually discernible, have not been linked to measurable physical parameters that can be translated into a temporal scale. The prevailing assumption in forensic literature has been that the drying process of blood is too variable to be modeled reliably due to the influence of surface type, ambient humidity, temperature, blood composition, and pool geometry. Consequently, no standardized, scientifically validated method has existed to convert the visual appearance of a drying blood pool into an objective time estimate. 

Some research has explored the evaporation dynamics of small blood drops, particularly in the context of spatter pattern analysis, but these studies are fundamentally limited to volumes under 10 microliters and are governed by capillary-driven phenomena such as the coffee-ring effect and Marangoni convection. These mechanisms do not scale to larger volumes, where gravitational forces dominate and the blood assumes a flattened, pancake-like geometry. For pools exceeding one milliliter in volume, the evaporation process transitions from a drop-centric regime to a bulk-phase phenomenon involving the interplay of surface tension, porosity development, and vapor diffusion through a gelatinous matrix. This transition has not been systematically quantified in prior forensic literature, nor has it been connected to a predictive model capable of estimating elapsed time. 

Moreover, while the drying of colloidal gels has been extensively studied in materials science, with well-documented stages such as the constant rate period and falling rate periods, these models have never been adapted to biological fluids like blood. The unique composition of blood—as a biphasic suspension of red blood cells in plasma, undergoing rapid coagulation via fibrin polymerization—introduces complexities not present in synthetic gels. The formation of a porous, elastic network during gelation alters the diffusion pathways of water vapor, creating a drying behavior that is neither purely convective nor purely diffusive, but a hybrid of both. The rate at which water escapes from this evolving structure has never been calibrated against time under controlled environmental conditions, nor has it been linked to the observable geometry of the drying front. 

Existing forensic protocols do not account for the influence of pool shape or size on evaporation kinetics. The assumption that all blood pools dry uniformly, regardless of their footprint or aspect ratio, has led to significant inaccuracies in temporal estimations. Furthermore, while environmental factors such as temperature and humidity are known to affect evaporation rates, no method has been developed to incorporate these variables into a unified equation that normalizes the drying process across diverse crime scene conditions. The absence of a quantitative, physics-based model has left the field reliant on subjective interpretation, rendering temporal analysis of blood pools an unreliable and often disregarded component of forensic reconstruction. 

The present invention addresses these longstanding gaps by introducing a method grounded in fluid mechanics, mass transfer theory, and empirical calibration. It is the first to recognize that the drying front of a blood pool—defined as the boundary between the wet, red, gelatinous region and the dry, black, desiccated residue—is not merely a morphological feature but a dynamic interface governed by reproducible physical laws. By measuring the area of this wet front, normalizing it against the pool’s geometric characteristics, and applying a diffusion coefficient derived from controlled experiments, the invention transforms a visual observation into a precise temporal measurement. This represents a paradigm shift in forensic science, moving from qualitative pattern recognition to quantitative physical modeling, and provides the first scientifically validated tool for determining the time of blood pool formation in the absence of a body.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention provides a method for determining the time of formation of a blood pool at a forensic scene by analyzing the evolution of its drying front through photographic imaging and physical modeling. The method is based on the discovery that, under controlled environmental conditions, the rate of water evaporation from a drying blood pool can be approximated as a constant value when normalized by the pool’s geometric configuration and the thermodynamic properties of the surrounding air. This normalized evaporation rate corresponds to an effective diffusion coefficient of approximately 1.1 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s, which remains invariant across pools of varying size, shape, and initial volume, provided that the ambient temperature, humidity, and surface type are held constant. The invention leverages this invariant property to calculate the elapsed time since deposition by measuring the reduction in the wet area of the pool relative to its initial area, and by applying a mathematical relationship derived from the conservation of mass and the principles of Fickian diffusion.

The method comprises the following steps: first, a high-resolution photograph of the blood pool is captured, ensuring that the drying front is clearly visible and that a reference scale is included in the image. From this image, the total area of the pool and the wet area—defined as the region still exhibiting the characteristic red coloration of unevaporated blood—are measured using digital image analysis software. The perimeter of the pool is also determined to calculate a shape factor, which is derived from the ratio of the pool’s area to the product of its height and perimeter. The height of the pool is estimated from empirical data collected under similar surface conditions, as direct measurement is not feasible in situ. Using the shape factor, the ambient temperature, the atmospheric pressure, the saturation vapor pressure of water at the given temperature, and the known molar mass of water, the effective diffusion coefficient is applied to compute the evaporation flux of water from the pool’s surface. The initial mass of the pool is estimated from the total area and the assumed height, using the known density of blood. The current mass of the pool is then determined by correlating the measured wet area with the initial area through a validated empirical function that relates normalized wet area to normalized mass. The difference between the initial and current mass yields the total mass of water lost since deposition. Dividing this mass loss by the computed evaporation flux and the initial area of the pool produces the elapsed time since the pool was formed, with an accuracy of ±30 minutes under controlled environmental conditions.

The invention further comprises a system for implementing this method in the field, including a digital camera capable of capturing high-resolution images with a reference scale, a portable computational device preloaded with image processing software configured to identify the drying front and calculate wet area, perimeter, and total area, and a database of reference parameters including the calibrated diffusion coefficient, typical pool heights for common surfaces, and correction factors for ambient temperature and humidity. The system may be integrated into a mobile forensic application that guides the user through image capture, automatically extracts geometric parameters, retrieves environmental data from a connected sensor or GPS-based weather service, computes the time of formation, and displays the result with an associated confidence interval. The method is non-destructive, requires no chemical reagents or physical sampling, and can be applied to any non-porous surface commonly encountered at crime scenes, including tile, linoleum, varnished wood, or glass. The invention does not require the presence of a body, nor does it depend on biological markers, making it uniquely suited for cases involving blood removal, cleanup, or concealment. The method is robust across a range of hematocrit levels from 36% to 47%, and is unaffected by the sex or age of the donor, as these factors do not significantly alter the evaporation kinetics of the plasma matrix or the structural development of the gel network. The invention thus provides a novel, reliable, and scientifically grounded tool for temporal reconstruction in forensic investigations, filling a critical gap in the current investigative toolkit.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The invention is implemented through a series of interconnected physical, mathematical, and procedural steps that transform a simple photographic record of a drying blood pool into a precise temporal measurement. The core of the invention lies in the recognition that, despite the apparent complexity of blood evaporation—governed by coagulation, gelation, porosity development, and vapor diffusion—the net rate of water loss, when properly normalized, converges to a single, reproducible value that is independent of the pool’s initial volume or geometric configuration. This value, determined through extensive empirical testing under controlled laboratory conditions, serves as the foundational constant upon which the entire method is built. The embodiment described herein is not limited to any single surface, temperature, or humidity condition; rather, it is designed to be adaptable to a wide range of forensic environments through the use of calibrated correction factors and a standardized computational workflow.

The first step in the method involves the acquisition of a high-resolution image of the blood pool. This image must be captured under conditions that ensure the drying front is clearly delineated, meaning that the transition between the wet, red region and the dry, black residue must be visually distinct. The photograph is taken from a perpendicular angle to minimize perspective distortion and must include a reference object of known dimensions, such as a ruler or a coin, to enable accurate scaling. The camera used may be a standard digital single-lens reflex camera, a smartphone with a high-resolution sensor, or a dedicated forensic imaging device, provided that the image resolution exceeds 5 megapixels and the color fidelity is sufficient to distinguish the red-to-black transition. The image is then transferred to a computational device, which may be a tablet, laptop, or field-deployable forensic workstation, where specialized software is used to process the image.

The software performs a series of automated image analysis operations. First, it applies a color segmentation algorithm to isolate the wet region of the pool based on its characteristic red hue, which corresponds to the presence of intact hemoglobin in the plasma matrix. The dry region, which appears black due to the concentration of red blood cells and the absence of water, is excluded from the analysis. The software then calculates the total area of the pool by tracing the outer boundary of the entire stain, regardless of whether it is wet or dry. Simultaneously, it measures the wet area, defined as the contiguous region of red pixels that remain hydrated and unevaporated. This wet area is critical, as it represents the active surface from which water continues to evaporate. The perimeter of the pool is also computed, which is necessary for the calculation of the shape factor. The shape factor, denoted as L*, is derived from the equation L* = A / (h × P), where A is the total area of the pool, h is the estimated height of the pool, and P is the perimeter. The height h is not measured directly, as this would require invasive instrumentation, but is instead estimated from a pre-established database of average heights for blood pools deposited on common surfaces. For example, on a white tile surface, the average height is determined to be 1.44 mm with a standard deviation of ±0.19 mm, based on a sample of thirty pools of varying volumes. This value is stored in the software’s reference library and is automatically selected based on the surface type identified either manually by the user or through an image recognition algorithm trained to classify surface materials.

Once the geometric parameters are established, the method proceeds to compute the evaporation flux of water from the pool. This flux, denoted as J*, is calculated using the equation J* = Dblood × (M × Pw) / (LK × R × T × L*¹/²), where Dblood is the calibrated diffusion coefficient of 1.1 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s, M is the molar mass of water (0.018 kg/mol), Pw is the saturation vapor pressure of water at the ambient temperature, LK is the Knudsen layer thickness, R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K), T is the ambient temperature in Kelvin, and L*¹/² is the square root of the shape factor. The Knudsen layer thickness, LK, is computed using the equation LK = kT / (π × d² × Pa), where k is Boltzmann’s constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K), d is the molecular diameter of water vapor (2.75 × 10⁻¹⁰ m), and Pa is the atmospheric pressure, which may be obtained from a local weather station or a built-in barometric sensor. The saturation vapor pressure Pw is determined from the ambient temperature using the Magnus formula or a standard vapor pressure table, ensuring that the thermodynamic state of the air is accurately accounted for in the calculation.

With the evaporation flux J* established, the method then calculates the initial mass of the blood pool. This is done by multiplying the total area A by the estimated height h and the density of blood, which is assumed to be 1060 kg/m³, a value consistent with the average density of whole blood containing a hematocrit between 36% and 47%. The initial mass mi is thus given by mi = A × h × ρ. The current mass mx of the pool is then determined through a normalized relationship between the wet area and the mass fraction remaining. This relationship, derived from the experimental data collected across multiple pools of varying initial volumes, is expressed by the empirical function mx/mi = 1 − α[1 − (Ax/Ai)]β, where Ax is the current wet area, Ai is the initial total area, and α and β are constants determined to be 0.78 and 0.16, respectively, through nonlinear regression of over two hundred drying experiments. This function effectively maps the visible wet area to the actual mass of water remaining in the pool, accounting for the fact that the mass loss does not occur uniformly across the pool but is concentrated at the drying front.

The difference between the initial and current mass, δm = mi − mx, represents the total mass of water that has evaporated since the pool was formed. This mass loss is then divided by the evaporation flux J* and the initial area Ai to yield the elapsed time tx since deposition, according to the equation tx = δm / (J* × Ai). The result is expressed in seconds and is converted into hours and minutes for forensic reporting. The entire calculation is performed automatically by the software, which also computes the uncertainty in the estimate based on the standard deviations of the input parameters, including the height estimation, the image segmentation error, and the environmental measurement tolerances. The output is displayed as a time estimate with a confidence interval of ±30 minutes, which has been validated through repeated testing under controlled conditions and across multiple surface types.

The method is further enhanced by the inclusion of environmental correction factors that account for variations in temperature and humidity. While the diffusion coefficient Dblood of 1.1 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s was calibrated at 23 ± 1 °C and 20% relative humidity, the invention recognizes that temperature and humidity exert a measurable influence on the evaporation rate. To accommodate this, the system includes a lookup table that adjusts the effective diffusion coefficient based on ambient conditions. For example, at 30 °C, the diffusion coefficient increases by approximately 15% due to the higher vapor pressure and increased molecular mobility, while at 15 °C, it decreases by 12%. Similarly, for every 10% increase in relative humidity above 20%, the diffusion coefficient is reduced by 5%, reflecting the reduced vapor concentration gradient. These adjustments are applied automatically when the software retrieves environmental data from a connected sensor or from a public weather API, ensuring that the time estimate remains accurate even in uncontrolled field conditions.

The system may also be configured to perform iterative time estimation by capturing multiple images of the same pool over time. In such a scenario, the software processes each image sequentially, updating the wet area and recalculating the elapsed time. The resulting sequence of time estimates is then averaged to reduce the impact of measurement noise, image artifacts, or partial occlusion of the drying front. This iterative approach significantly improves reliability and is particularly useful in cases where the initial image is captured after the pool has already begun drying, as the method can be applied retrospectively to determine the time of formation from any point during the drying process, provided that the drying front remains visible.

The invention further includes a calibration protocol for new surfaces or environmental conditions. If a forensic team encounters a surface type not included in the reference database—such as concrete, carpet, or fabric—the system prompts the user to conduct a controlled calibration experiment using a known volume of blood under identical environmental conditions. The user deposits a reference pool of known mass and captures images at regular intervals until complete drying. The software then computes the effective diffusion coefficient for that specific surface and adds it to the database, allowing future analyses on the same surface to be performed with the same level of accuracy. This adaptive learning capability ensures that the method remains applicable across diverse forensic contexts, from urban crime scenes to rural environments.

The method is also designed to be robust against variations in blood composition. While the experiments were conducted using blood from healthy donors with hematocrit levels ranging from 36.2% to 47.1%, the invention has been tested with blood samples from individuals with anemia, polycythemia, and those under the influence of common medications such as aspirin and anticoagulants. In all cases, the evaporation dynamics remained consistent, as the primary driver of the drying process is the plasma matrix, which constitutes over 90% of the volume and whose water content is the dominant factor in mass loss. The red blood cells, while altering the final residue’s texture and color, do not significantly impede vapor diffusion once the gel network has formed. This robustness to biological variability is a critical advantage, as it allows the method to be applied without prior knowledge of the victim’s physiological state.

The invention does not require the presence of a body, nor does it rely on any biological markers, DNA, or chemical assays. It operates solely on the physical properties of the blood pool and its interaction with the environment. This makes it uniquely suited for cases involving blood cleanup, where perpetrators attempt to remove evidence through washing, scrubbing, or chemical treatment. Even in such cases, if residual traces of blood remain and a drying front is still visible, the method can be applied to estimate the time of the original event. Furthermore, the method is non-destructive, meaning that the evidence remains intact for subsequent forensic analysis, including DNA extraction, serological testing, or pattern reconstruction.

The system may be integrated into a mobile application that guides the user through each step of the process with visual prompts, automatic image analysis, and real-time feedback. The application may include a tutorial mode for training forensic personnel, a logging function to timestamp and geolocate each analysis, and a cloud-based database for sharing results with other investigators or expert witnesses. The output may be formatted as a forensic report, complete with the calculated time of formation, the confidence interval, the environmental parameters, the geometric measurements, and the reference images, all of which may be submitted as evidence in court.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, scientifically validated, and field-deployable method for determining the time of formation of a blood pool through the quantitative analysis of its drying dynamics. By combining image analysis, physical modeling, and empirical calibration, it transforms a previously subjective observation into an objective, reproducible measurement. The method is accurate, non-invasive, adaptable, and grounded in the fundamental principles of mass transfer and fluid mechanics. It represents a significant advancement in forensic science, offering investigators a powerful new tool to reconstruct timelines in cases where traditional methods are unavailable, and where the passage of time is the most elusive and critical piece of evidence.
