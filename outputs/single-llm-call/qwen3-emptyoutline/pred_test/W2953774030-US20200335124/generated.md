# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The proliferation of conversational systems—commonly referred to as chatbots—has fundamentally transformed the landscape of customer service, technical support, sales automation, and user engagement across industries. These systems, once limited to simplistic rule-based interactions, have evolved into sophisticated platforms capable of handling complex, multi-turn dialogues that mimic human-like reasoning and response patterns. As organizations increasingly deploy these systems at scale, the need to monitor, evaluate, and improve their performance has become critical. Unlike human agents, whose behavior can be observed, coached, and adapted in real time, bots operate according to predefined logic structures, often encoded as directed graphs that dictate the flow of conversation. This structural rigidity, while enabling scalability and consistency, also introduces unique challenges in performance analysis. Traditional methods of bot evaluation rely heavily on surface-level metrics such as conversation length, response time, or keyword matching, which fail to capture the nuanced behavioral patterns that distinguish effective bots from ineffective ones. Moreover, many deployed bots remain unused or underutilized, languishing in testing environments without ever interacting with real users—a phenomenon that renders conventional usage analytics ineffective. The absence of a unified, learnable representation of bot behavior impedes the development of automated tools capable of identifying high-performing bots, detecting systemic flaws, or recommending targeted improvements across entire bot ecosystems. Existing approaches to conversational analysis have largely focused on individual bots, treating each as an isolated entity and extracting features from textual content alone—such as word frequencies, intent labels, or response coherence—without accounting for the underlying architecture that governs how the bot navigates user input. This narrow perspective neglects the critical insight that a bot’s behavior is not merely a function of what it says, but of how it processes, interprets, and responds to user intent through its internal decision-making structure. Furthermore, while recent advances in representation learning have enabled the embedding of words, documents, and even static graphs, no prior work has successfully adapted these techniques to model bots as dynamic, behaviorally rich entities whose identity is encoded not in static nodes and edges, but in the patterns of traversal that emerge during actual user interactions. The result is a significant gap in the ability to generalize insights across bots, to detect anomalies at scale, or to cluster bots by behavioral similarity rather than by domain or developer. This invention addresses this gap by introducing BOT2VEC, a novel framework for learning dense, low-dimensional vector representations of conversational bots that encode both their structural architecture and their observed usage patterns. By treating each bot as a system whose behavior is revealed through the paths taken during conversations, BOT2VEC transforms the problem of bot analysis from one of manual feature engineering into one of automated, data-driven representation learning. The framework leverages the inherent graph-based nature of bot platforms, mapping each conversation to a sequence of traversals across the bot’s decision tree, and then encoding these traversals into a standardized, fixed-size vector that captures not only which nodes were visited, but how often, in what sequence, and with what outcome. Crucially, this approach does not require access to the underlying code or training data of the bot, nor does it depend on human-labeled intent classifications; instead, it operates entirely on observable interaction logs, making it universally applicable across diverse bot platforms and deployment environments. The resulting embeddings enable a wide range of downstream analytical tasks—including the detection of production-grade bots, the identification of egregious conversational failures, and the clustering of bots by behavioral similarity—with performance significantly exceeding that of traditional feature-based methods. This invention thus represents a paradigm shift in bot analytics: moving from isolated, content-centric evaluations to holistic, structure-aware representations that capture the essence of how a bot behaves when engaged by real users.

## SUMMARY

This invention discloses a novel system and method for generating vector representations of conversational bots that encapsulate both their structural design and their observed behavioral patterns during user interactions. The core innovation lies in the BOT2VEC framework, which learns dense, low-dimensional embeddings for each bot by analyzing the sequences of paths traversed through its decision graph during real-world conversations. Unlike prior approaches that treat bots as black-box text generators or rely on manually engineered features derived from conversation logs, this invention introduces a dual-mode representation learning architecture that operates on two complementary input modalities: a content-based representation derived from the textual utterances exchanged between users and bots, and a structure-based representation derived from the topological traversal patterns within the bot’s graph-based control flow. The structure-based representation is particularly novel in that it maps each node visited during a conversation to a fixed-size bin vector, where each bin corresponds to a specific region of the bot’s decision tree, and each bin maintains four counters tracking the frequency with which nodes in that region are encountered as successful endpoints, failed endpoints, intermediate steps, or uninvolved states. This binning mechanism enables the system to normalize and compare bots with vastly different graph sizes and complexities by projecting their dynamic conversation paths into a common, standardized feature space. The content-based representation, by contrast, encodes the lexical composition of user utterances and bot responses using term frequency vectors derived from a shared vocabulary, with special preprocessing to mask identity-revealing tokens such as bot names, URLs, or internal identifiers. Both representations are fed into a neural network architecture that predicts the identity of the bot responsible for a given conversation, using a softmax output layer over all bots in the training corpus. The weights connecting the final hidden layer to this output layer are extracted as the bot embeddings, effectively encoding each bot as a vector in a continuous space where similarity reflects behavioral similarity. The invention further encompasses a method for applying these learned embeddings to downstream classification tasks, including the detection of production bots—those actively engaged with real users—as well as the identification of egregious conversations—those so severely flawed that they would necessitate human intervention. In both cases, the BOT2VEC embeddings significantly outperform traditional feature-based classifiers, achieving relative improvements of over 16% in F1-score across multiple datasets and evaluation protocols. The system is designed to operate without requiring access to bot source code, training data, or intent labels, relying solely on anonymized conversation logs and the graph structure of the bot’s decision flow. This makes the invention broadly applicable across commercial bot platforms, regardless of underlying implementation, programming language, or domain specificity. The embeddings generated by this system are not merely descriptive but predictive, enabling the discovery of latent behavioral patterns that are invisible to surface-level metrics. For instance, bots from the same domain—such as banking or IT support—tend to cluster together in the embedding space, suggesting that the learned representations capture domain-specific conversational norms and structural tendencies. The invention further enables the automated identification of underperforming bots, the prioritization of improvement efforts by pinpointing the most problematic conversation paths, and the development of recommendation systems that suggest structural modifications to bot designers based on the behavior of high-performing peers. By transforming bot analysis from a reactive, manual process into a proactive, data-driven discipline, this invention provides a foundational tool for the scalable management of conversational AI systems at enterprise level.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention comprises a comprehensive system and method for generating and utilizing bot embeddings through the BOT2VEC framework, which enables the representation of conversational bots as dense, continuous vectors that capture both their structural architecture and their behavioral patterns during real-world interactions. The system operates in three primary phases: data ingestion and preprocessing, representation learning via neural embedding, and application of learned embeddings to downstream classification tasks. Each phase is designed to be modular, scalable, and platform-agnostic, ensuring compatibility with any commercial conversational bot platform that represents bot logic as a directed graph and logs conversation histories in structured form. The foundational assumption underlying the invention is that a bot’s behavior is not determined solely by the words it generates, but by the way it navigates through its internal decision tree in response to user inputs. This insight leads to the creation of a novel representation paradigm that treats each bot as a dynamic system whose identity is encoded in the patterns of traversal across its graph, rather than in the content of its responses or the complexity of its rules.

The first phase of the system involves the ingestion and preprocessing of two distinct data sources: the bot graph structure and the associated conversation logs. Each bot is represented as a directed graph composed of nodes and edges, where each node corresponds to a specific user intent or decision point, and each edge represents a transition triggered by the classification of a user utterance as matching or not matching the node’s associated intent. Nodes may contain optional bot responses, and edges are typically bifurcated into positive and negative branches, corresponding to whether the intent classifier deems the utterance relevant or irrelevant to the node’s intended scope. The graph may also contain special nodes such as sink nodes, which trigger default fallback responses when no intent is recognized, and jump nodes, which allow for non-sequential transitions to other parts of the graph to avoid redundancy. The graph is parsed to extract its complete topology, including the depth of each node (the number of positive edges from the root), the width of each level (the maximum number of nodes connected by negative edges at that depth), and the coordinate mapping of each node within the graph’s hierarchical grid structure. Simultaneously, conversation logs are collected, each consisting of a sequence of alternating user utterances and bot responses, along with timestamps and metadata such as user identifiers and session IDs. These logs are anonymized by removing any tokens that could identify the bot, its developer, or its deployment environment, such as bot names, company-specific URLs, internal identifiers, or proprietary terminology. The vocabulary for the content-based representation is constructed by aggregating all remaining terms across all conversations, filtering out rare words, and retaining only the k most frequent terms, where k is determined empirically based on dataset size and computational constraints. This ensures that the representation focuses on general conversational patterns rather than bot-specific lexical artifacts. The structure-based representation is constructed by defining a common bin vector that standardizes the representation of nodes across all bots, regardless of their individual graph sizes. This bin vector is divided into S sections, each corresponding to a level of depth in the bot graph, and each section is further subdivided into a fixed number of bins, determined by the average width of nodes at that depth across the entire corpus. For example, if the average depth of all bot graphs is seven and the average width at level one is 108, then section one of the bin vector will contain 108 bins, each representing a potential node at that depth. Nodes from different bots are mapped to these bins according to their coordinate position in the graph: a node at depth two, third position from the top within its parent node’s level, would be mapped to bin three of section two. This mapping is performed using a deterministic algorithm that ensures consistency across all bots, enabling direct comparison of traversal patterns even between bots with entirely different underlying structures. Each bin in the vector maintains four counters: one for success nodes (the final node in a path that is not a sink), one for failure nodes (the final node in a path that is a sink), one for regular nodes (intermediate nodes traversed during a path), and one for uninvolved nodes (nodes that were not visited during the conversation). For each user utterance in a conversation, the system traces the path taken through the graph, updates the appropriate counters in the bin vector, and then aggregates these counters across all utterances in the conversation to produce a single, fixed-size vector that encapsulates the bot’s behavioral footprint over the entire interaction. This vector serves as the input to the structure-based model.

The second phase of the system involves the training of a neural network to learn the bot embeddings. The architecture consists of a fully connected feedforward network with multiple hidden layers, optimized using the Adam algorithm with a learning rate of 0.001 and dropout regularization at a rate of 0.5 to prevent overfitting. The network is trained using a supervised learning objective: given a representation of a conversation—either content-based or structure-based—the model is tasked with predicting which bot generated that conversation. The input to the network is therefore a representation of a single conversation, and the output is a probability distribution over all bots in the training set, computed via a softmax layer whose dimensionality equals the total number of bots. The loss function is cross-entropy, which encourages the network to assign high probability to the correct bot and low probability to all others. The key insight driving this approach is that bots which handle similar conversations—whether due to similar domains, similar design philosophies, or similar failure modes—will generate similar conversation patterns, and therefore, their representations will be mapped to similar regions in the embedding space. The embeddings themselves are extracted from the weights of the output layer, specifically the row corresponding to each bot in the weight matrix connecting the last hidden layer to the softmax output. These rows constitute the BOT2VEC embeddings: dense, low-dimensional vectors that encode the behavioral essence of each bot. The content-based model, referred to as BOT2VEC-C, takes as input a concatenation of two term-frequency vectors: one representing the aggregated user utterances and one representing the aggregated bot responses. To capture interaction dynamics, the system also computes the element-wise product and squared difference between these two vectors, and concatenates all three into a single input vector. This allows the model to learn not just what was said, but how the user’s language relates to the bot’s responses—whether they are aligned, contradictory, or mismatched in tone or intent. The structure-based model, referred to as BOT2VEC-S, takes as input the single bin vector described above, which has a fixed size determined by the total number of bins multiplied by the four counters per bin. Both models are trained simultaneously on the same corpus of conversations, with the only difference being the input representation. The training process is unsupervised with respect to bot labels beyond their identity, meaning no external annotations regarding bot quality, success, or domain are required. The model learns to distinguish bots purely based on the statistical patterns of their conversations, resulting in embeddings that are inherently discriminative and generalizable. The use of a neural network with ReLU activations and dropout ensures that the learned representations are nonlinear, robust to noise, and capable of capturing complex interactions between traversal patterns and lexical content. The training is performed on a corpus of over 90 bots and more than 1.3 million conversations, ensuring sufficient diversity and statistical power to learn meaningful representations. The resulting embeddings are not tied to any specific bot platform or implementation; they are purely behavioral, derived from observable interaction data, and thus portable across systems.

The third phase of the system involves the application of these learned embeddings to downstream classification tasks, specifically the detection of production bots and the identification of egregious conversations. For the production bot detection task, the goal is to classify each bot as either active in production—engaged with real users—or inactive—used only for testing, debugging, or development. Traditional approaches to this problem rely on manually engineered features such as the number of unique user IDs, the frequency of repeated responses, the average number of turns per conversation, or the variance in response time. These features are often heuristic and domain-dependent, and they fail to capture the deeper behavioral signatures that distinguish genuine user engagement from simulated testing. In this invention, the BOT2VEC embeddings are used as the sole input to a binary classifier, which is trained to predict whether a bot is in production. The classifier is implemented as a support vector machine (SVM) with radial basis function kernel, chosen for its effectiveness on small datasets and its ability to handle high-dimensional feature spaces. The embeddings are fed into the classifier alongside a baseline feature set, and performance is evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation. The results demonstrate that the structure-based embeddings (BOT2VEC-S) achieve a relative improvement of 18.6% in F1-score over the baseline, while the content-based embeddings (BOT2VEC-C) achieve a modest but consistent improvement of 4.2%. This indicates that the traversal patterns of a bot—how it navigates its decision tree in response to user input—are a far more reliable indicator of real-world usage than lexical features or simple statistical metrics. The model is able to detect subtle behavioral signatures such as bots that consistently traverse deep paths with high success rates, indicating genuine user engagement, versus bots that repeatedly loop back to the root node or trigger fallback responses, suggesting testing or incomplete deployment. For the egregious conversation detection task, the goal is to identify conversations in which the bot’s behavior is so severely flawed that a human agent would be required to intervene. Prior work on this problem has relied on models that analyze conversation text for signs of confusion, repetition, or logical inconsistency. The invention enhances these models by augmenting their feature vectors with the BOT2VEC embedding of the bot responsible for the conversation. This allows the classifier to leverage not just the content of the conversation, but the bot’s overall behavioral profile—its tendency to misclassify intents, its propensity to trigger sink nodes, or its failure to recover from errors. The baseline model used for comparison is the state-of-the-art EGR model, which uses text-based features to detect egregious conversations. When the BOT2VEC-S embedding is concatenated to the EGR feature vector, the resulting model achieves a relative improvement of 16.4% in F1-score over the baseline, demonstrating that the bot’s structural behavior provides complementary information to the conversation’s textual content. The content-based embedding also improves performance, though to a lesser extent, suggesting that while lexical content is informative, it is the bot’s underlying decision logic that most strongly determines whether a conversation becomes egregious. The system further enables unsupervised analysis of bot similarity: by computing the cosine distance between embeddings of bots within the same domain—such as banking, IT support, or human resources—the invention reveals that bots from the same domain tend to cluster together in the embedding space, with an average cosine distance of 0.614, compared to 0.694 for bots from different domains. This suggests that the learned representations capture not only individual bot behavior but also domain-specific conversational norms, such as the typical depth of inquiry, the frequency of fallback triggers, or the preferred sequence of information gathering. This clustering capability enables the development of recommendation systems that suggest structural improvements to underperforming bots by identifying high-performing peers with similar domains and recommending structural modifications based on their traversal patterns. For example, a banking bot that frequently triggers sink nodes after three turns might be advised to adopt the recovery strategy of a similar bot that successfully redirects users to a live agent after two failed attempts. The invention also enables anomaly detection: bots whose embeddings lie far from the centroid of their domain group may be flagged for review, indicating either exceptional performance or critical design flaws. The system is designed to be deployed in a production environment as a continuous learning pipeline: as new conversations are logged, the embeddings can be periodically retrained to adapt to evolving user behaviors, bot updates, or shifting domain requirements. The embeddings themselves can be stored and queried efficiently, enabling real-time classification of new bots or conversations without requiring retraining of the entire system. The entire framework is implemented using standard deep learning libraries and can be scaled to handle tens of thousands of bots across multiple platforms. The invention does not require any changes to existing bot platforms, nor does it necessitate access to source code, training data, or intent classifiers. It operates entirely on the surface-level interaction logs and the graph structure that are already available in commercial bot management systems. This makes the invention immediately deployable, universally applicable, and highly scalable. The resulting embeddings serve as a universal language of bot behavior, enabling the automated analysis, comparison, and optimization of conversational systems at unprecedented scale and precision. By shifting the paradigm from content-centric to structure-aware representation learning, this invention provides the foundational tool for the next generation of bot analytics, transforming bot management from an art into a science.
