# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The development and deployment of predictive models in clinical medicine have accelerated dramatically over the past two decades, driven by advances in computational power, the availability of large-scale electronic health records, and the increasing adoption of machine learning methodologies. These models are now routinely employed to estimate the likelihood of adverse clinical outcomes such as myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, and death, with the goal of guiding diagnostic, therapeutic, and prognostic decision-making. Despite their widespread use and often impressive aggregate performance metrics—such as accuracy, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and calibration scores—there remains a critical gap in the ability of clinicians to determine, at the point of care, whether a specific prediction generated by such a model can be trusted for an individual patient. This limitation is particularly acute in high-stakes domains such as cardiovascular medicine, where a single misclassification can lead to inappropriate intervention, delayed treatment, or even patient mortality.

Traditional performance metrics, while valuable for evaluating model behavior across a population, provide no insight into the reliability of individual predictions. For instance, a risk model may achieve an AUC of 0.85 across an entire cohort, suggesting strong discriminatory capacity, yet this aggregate measure obscures the fact that certain subgroups of patients—perhaps those with rare comorbidities, atypical presentations, or underrepresented demographic characteristics—may consistently receive misleading risk estimates. Similarly, calibration curves that demonstrate overall agreement between predicted and observed event rates may mask systematic over- or under-estimation in specific risk strata. The consequence is a clinical environment in which providers are left to interpret algorithmic outputs without any clear indication of when those outputs are likely to be erroneous, thereby undermining the very principle of evidence-based medicine: that decisions should be grounded in reliable, context-sensitive information.

Existing approaches to quantifying prediction reliability fall into two broad categories: model-dependent and model-independent methods. Model-dependent techniques, such as Bayesian neural networks, Monte Carlo dropout, or Gaussian process regression, attempt to estimate predictive uncertainty by incorporating probabilistic assumptions directly into the architecture of the classifier itself. These methods are mathematically elegant and can provide well-calibrated confidence intervals under ideal conditions. However, they require the model to be explicitly designed with uncertainty quantification in mind, rendering them incompatible with the vast majority of clinical risk scores currently in use—many of which are derived from logistic regression, Cox proportional hazards models, or other classical statistical techniques that do not natively support uncertainty estimation. Furthermore, even when such methods are applicable, they often demand substantial computational resources and specialized software infrastructure, which are not universally available in clinical settings.

Model-independent approaches, by contrast, are designed to be agnostic to the underlying classification algorithm and can, in principle, be applied to any predictive model regardless of its internal structure. Among these, the most prominent is the trust score, which evaluates the plausibility of a prediction by comparing the distance of a test sample to the nearest neighbors in the predicted class versus the nearest neighbors in the alternative class, using the original training dataset as a reference. While this method offers a compelling geometric intuition—predictions are less trustworthy when they lie closer to the boundary between classes—it suffers from a fundamental practical limitation: it requires direct access to the full training dataset used to develop the original model. In the context of clinical medicine, where patient data are subject to stringent privacy regulations, institutional data governance policies, and legal restrictions on data sharing, this requirement renders the trust score largely inapplicable outside the research laboratories that originally developed the models. Even when data access is granted, the computational burden of computing nearest-neighbor distances for large, high-dimensional datasets can be prohibitive, particularly in time-sensitive clinical environments where rapid decision-making is essential.

Moreover, neither model-dependent nor model-independent approaches have been rigorously evaluated in the context of significant class imbalance—a defining feature of most clinical prediction problems. In cardiovascular risk modeling, for example, the incidence of death or major adverse cardiac events typically ranges between 1% and 10% in the general population, meaning that the positive class (those who experience the outcome) constitutes a small minority of the dataset. Under such conditions, standard performance metrics become inherently biased: a model that predicts all patients to be low risk will achieve high accuracy simply by virtue of the rarity of the event, yet such a model would be clinically useless. More insidiously, class imbalance can distort the relationship between predicted risk and actual risk, leading to systematic miscalibration that is not easily detected by conventional validation procedures. Existing reliability metrics have not been designed to account for this phenomenon, and as a result, they often fail to identify the very subgroups where predictive performance is most compromised.

There is therefore an urgent and unmet need for a clinically actionable, model-agnostic, and data-efficient method that can identify, in real time and without access to the original training data, when a given prediction is likely to be unreliable. Such a method must satisfy four essential criteria. First, it must be capable of detecting patient subgroups in which the predictive model demonstrably underperforms—whether due to insufficient training examples, distributional shift, or inherent ambiguity in the feature space. Second, it must operate independently of the underlying classification algorithm, enabling its application to legacy risk scores such as the Framingham Risk Score, the GRACE Score, or the CHA₂DS₂-VASc Score, as well as to newer machine learning models. Third, it must require only summary statistics from the training data—such as the overall prevalence of the outcome, and the distribution of predicted scores in the positive and negative classes—thereby eliminating the need for direct access to individual patient records. Fourth, it must remain effective and interpretable in the presence of severe class imbalance, which is not an exception but the norm in clinical prediction tasks.

The absence of such a method has profound implications for patient safety and clinical decision-making. Clinicians are increasingly being asked to rely on algorithmic outputs to guide high-stakes decisions, yet they are provided with no mechanism to assess the trustworthiness of those outputs on a case-by-case basis. This creates a dangerous asymmetry: the model may be highly accurate on average, but its failures may be concentrated in precisely those patients who are most vulnerable and least understood by the training data. Without a reliable way to flag such cases, the risk of harm increases—not because the model is inherently flawed, but because its limitations are invisible to the user. The development of a metric that can illuminate these blind spots is not merely a technical challenge; it is a moral imperative in the pursuit of equitable, safe, and transparent clinical AI.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel, model-independent method for identifying unreliable clinical predictions by quantifying the degree of disagreement between a given predictive model’s output and an alternate, statistically derived estimate of risk that is computed using only summary statistics from the training data. This method, referred to herein as the prediction unreliability metric, enables clinicians and healthcare systems to determine, for any individual patient, whether the risk estimate generated by a clinical model is likely to be misleading, without requiring access to the original training dataset or any modification of the underlying model. The invention operates by comparing the predicted risk score from the primary clinical model to a recalibrated probability estimate derived from the generative likelihoods of the input features under the assumption of binary classification, conditioned on the overall prevalence of the outcome and the distribution of predicted scores in the positive and negative classes. When these two estimates diverge significantly, the prediction is flagged as unreliable, indicating that the training data are insufficient to produce a robust and consistent risk assessment for that patient.

The unreliability metric is defined mathematically as the absolute difference between the predicted risk score from the clinical model and the posterior probability of the outcome, given the predicted score, as estimated using Bayes’ theorem and a generative model of the feature space. This posterior probability is computed using only the mean and covariance of the predicted scores in the positive and negative classes, along with the global prevalence of the outcome, all of which can be derived from summary statistics and do not require access to individual patient records. The resulting metric is bounded between zero and one, with higher values indicating greater unreliability. Crucially, the method does not depend on the internal structure of the predictive model—it can be applied to logistic regression models, neural networks, decision trees, or any other classifier that outputs a continuous risk score—making it universally applicable across the spectrum of clinical risk assessment tools currently in use.

The invention has been validated in multiple clinical contexts, including the prediction of all-cause mortality following acute coronary syndrome using the GRACE risk score and the prediction of in-hospital stroke in a separate cohort of patients. In both settings, patients with high unreliability scores were consistently found to belong to subgroups in which the predictive model exhibited significantly reduced calibration, increased prediction error, and diminished discriminatory power, as measured by normalized Brier scores and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Notably, these performance deficits were not observed in patients with low unreliability scores, demonstrating that the metric effectively isolates the subset of predictions that are most likely to be erroneous. Furthermore, the method was shown to outperform the trust score—a previously proposed model-independent reliability metric—in identifying clinically meaningful subgroups of unreliable predictions, particularly in the context of severe class imbalance. Unlike the trust score, which requires access to the full training dataset and is sensitive to the geometry of feature space, the unreliability metric relies solely on aggregated, anonymized statistics, making it compatible with real-world clinical deployment under strict data privacy regulations.

The invention further includes a practical implementation framework that allows for seamless integration into existing clinical decision support systems. The required inputs—namely, the predicted risk score, the prevalence of the outcome in the training population, and the empirical distributions of predicted scores for the positive and negative classes—are easily obtainable from model documentation, published nomograms, or institutional model repositories. These parameters can be precomputed and stored in a lightweight, encrypted database that requires minimal storage and computational resources, enabling real-time calculation of the unreliability score at the point of care using standard clinical workstations or mobile devices. The output is presented as a single, interpretable value that can be displayed alongside the predicted risk score, with predefined thresholds (e.g., the 99th percentile of unreliability scores from the validation cohort) used to trigger clinical alerts or recommend supplemental evaluation.

The invention addresses a critical gap in the clinical adoption of predictive models by providing a mechanism to identify when a model’s output should be treated with caution, thereby enhancing patient safety, promoting informed clinical judgment, and supporting the ethical deployment of artificial intelligence in medicine. It enables clinicians to distinguish between predictions that are statistically robust and those that are statistically ambiguous, without requiring them to understand the inner workings of the model or to access sensitive patient data. By transforming the abstract notion of model uncertainty into a concrete, clinically actionable signal, the invention facilitates a paradigm shift in clinical risk assessment—from one of blind trust in algorithmic outputs to one of informed, context-aware interpretation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a method, system, and computer-readable medium for identifying unreliable clinical predictions by quantifying the degree of disagreement between a predicted risk score generated by a clinical model and an alternate, statistically derived estimate of risk that is computed using only summary statistics from the training data. The method is designed to be universally applicable to any predictive model that outputs a continuous risk score for a binary clinical outcome, regardless of the underlying algorithmic architecture, training methodology, or data source. It operates without requiring access to individual patient records, thereby preserving patient privacy and enabling deployment in environments where data sharing is restricted by legal, ethical, or institutional constraints. The invention is particularly suited for use in high-stakes clinical domains such as cardiovascular medicine, oncology, critical care, and neurology, where the consequences of misclassification are severe and the prevalence of the outcome of interest is typically low.

The method begins with the receipt of a predicted risk score, denoted as \( \hat{y} \), corresponding to a specific patient’s estimated probability of experiencing a binary clinical outcome, such as death, myocardial infarction, or stroke, within a defined time window. This score is generated by a clinical risk model that may be based on any classification technique, including but not limited to logistic regression, random forest, support vector machine, neural network, or Cox proportional hazards model. The model may have been developed using any dataset, and its internal parameters, feature weights, or training procedures are not required for the operation of the present invention. The predicted risk score \( \hat{y} \) is a scalar value constrained to the interval [0,1], representing the model’s estimate of the probability that the patient belongs to the positive class (i.e., the class associated with the adverse outcome).

In addition to the predicted risk score, the method requires three summary statistics derived from the training dataset used to develop the original clinical model: (1) the overall prevalence of the outcome in the training population, denoted as \( P(y=1) \); (2) the probability density function of the predicted scores among patients who experienced the outcome, denoted as \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=1) \); and (3) the probability density function of the predicted scores among patients who did not experience the outcome, denoted as \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=0) \). These summary statistics may be obtained from publicly available model documentation, published nomograms, institutional model repositories, or through direct communication with the model developers. Importantly, these statistics do not require access to individual patient records, feature vectors, or raw data; they may be computed from aggregated, anonymized summaries of the training cohort and stored in a compact, encrypted format suitable for clinical deployment.

The core computational step of the invention involves the application of Bayes’ theorem to compute the posterior probability that a patient belongs to the positive class, given the predicted risk score \( \hat{y} \). This posterior probability, denoted as \( P^G(y=1 \mid \hat{y}) \), is derived from the likelihoods \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=1) \) and \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=0) \), the prior probability \( P(y=1) \), and the marginal probability of the predicted score \( P^G(\hat{y}) \), which is computed as the weighted sum of the two likelihoods:  
\[
P^G(\hat{y}) = P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=1) \cdot P(y=1) + P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=0) \cdot (1 - P(y=1)).
\]  
Applying Bayes’ theorem, the posterior probability is expressed as:  
\[
P^G(y=1 \mid \hat{y}) = \frac{P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=1) \cdot P(y=1)}{P^G(\hat{y})}.
\]  
This quantity represents the probability that a patient truly belongs to the positive class, given that the clinical model assigned them a risk score of \( \hat{y} \), under the assumption that the distribution of predicted scores in the training data accurately reflects the underlying generative process. The posterior probability is not a direct estimate of the patient’s risk, but rather a recalibration of the model’s output based on the observed statistical relationship between predicted scores and true outcomes in the training cohort.

The prediction unreliability metric, denoted as \( U(\hat{y}) \), is then defined as the absolute difference between the model’s predicted risk score \( \hat{y} \) and the recalibrated posterior probability \( P^G(y=1 \mid \hat{y}) \):  
\[
U(\hat{y}) = \left| P^G(y=1 \mid \hat{y}) - \hat{y} \right|.
\]  
This metric quantifies the degree of disagreement between the model’s output and the statistically derived expectation of risk, given the observed distribution of scores in the training data. A value of zero indicates perfect agreement between the model’s prediction and the recalibrated estimate, suggesting that the model’s output is consistent with the underlying statistical structure of the training data. Conversely, a value approaching one indicates maximal disagreement, suggesting that the model’s prediction is inconsistent with the expected relationship between risk scores and true outcomes, and therefore likely to be unreliable. The metric is bounded between zero and one, and its value can be interpreted as a continuous measure of prediction unreliability, with higher values indicating greater uncertainty or potential for error.

To facilitate practical implementation, the probability density functions \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=1) \) and \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=0) \) are estimated using a multivariate normal distribution approximation, although other parametric or non-parametric density estimation techniques may be employed without departing from the scope of the invention. The multivariate normal distribution is selected for its computational efficiency, mathematical tractability, and widespread use in statistical modeling. For each class (positive and negative), the mean and covariance of the predicted scores are computed from the training data, and the resulting distributions are used to evaluate the likelihood of any given \( \hat{y} \) value. In cases where the training data are sparse or the distribution of predicted scores is highly skewed, alternative models such as kernel density estimation, mixture models, or histogram-based approximations may be substituted, provided that they yield a smooth, differentiable probability density function over the interval [0,1]. The use of a multivariate normal distribution is not a limitation of the invention but rather a preferred implementation choice that balances accuracy, simplicity, and computational feasibility.

The computation of the unreliability metric is performed in real time at the point of care, using a lightweight algorithm that requires only basic arithmetic operations and a single evaluation of the probability density functions. The algorithm may be implemented as a software module, embedded within a clinical decision support system, electronic health record platform, or mobile application, and may be accessed via an application programming interface (API) or integrated directly into the user interface. Upon receipt of the predicted risk score \( \hat{y} \), the system retrieves the precomputed summary statistics \( P(y=1) \), \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=1) \), and \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y=0) \) from a secure, encrypted database, computes the posterior probability \( P^G(y=1 \mid \hat{y}) \) using Bayes’ theorem, calculates the absolute difference \( U(\hat{y}) \), and returns the result to the clinician. The output may be displayed as a numerical value, a color-coded indicator (e.g., green for low unreliability, yellow for moderate, red for high), or a textual alert (e.g., “This prediction is unreliable; consider additional clinical assessment”). The system may also provide a confidence interval or percentile rank for the unreliability score, derived from the empirical distribution of \( U(\hat{y}) \) values observed in the validation cohort, allowing the clinician to contextualize the magnitude of unreliability relative to historical cases.

The invention further includes a method for determining clinically meaningful thresholds for unreliability. In one embodiment, the 99th percentile of the unreliability metric is computed from a validation dataset and used as a binary threshold: predictions with \( U(\hat{y}) \) exceeding this threshold are flagged as unreliable, while those below are considered reliable. In another embodiment, the threshold is determined dynamically based on the prevalence of the outcome, the size of the training cohort, or the expected level of model calibration, allowing for adaptive deployment across different clinical settings. The threshold may be adjusted periodically as new data become available, ensuring that the metric remains calibrated to the evolving characteristics of the patient population.

The invention has been validated in multiple clinical cohorts, including the Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events (GRACE) and a separate cohort of patients with acute coronary syndrome used to predict in-hospital stroke. In both settings, patients with high unreliability scores were found to belong to subgroups in which the clinical model exhibited significantly reduced calibration, increased prediction error, and diminished discriminatory power. Calibration curves demonstrated that predictions with high unreliability scores consistently underestimated the true risk of adverse outcomes, while those with low unreliability scores were well calibrated. Normalized Brier scores, which account for the prevalence of the outcome, were significantly elevated in the high-unreliability subgroup, indicating greater prediction error relative to a null model that predicts the baseline risk. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was also significantly reduced in the high-unreliability subgroup, confirming that the model’s ability to discriminate between high-risk and low-risk patients was compromised in these cases. These findings were consistent across multiple bootstrapped samples and remained statistically significant after adjustment for confounding variables such as age, sex, comorbidities, and treatment history.

In contrast, the trust score—a previously proposed model-independent reliability metric—failed to consistently identify subgroups with degraded performance. In the GRACE cohort, the most untrustworthy predictions identified by the trust score exhibited similar calibration and discriminatory performance to the remainder of the dataset. In the stroke cohort, the most untrustworthy predictions actually demonstrated improved discriminatory ability, suggesting that the trust score may misidentify reliable predictions as unreliable or vice versa. These results demonstrate that the unreliability metric is uniquely capable of identifying clinically meaningful subgroups of unreliable predictions, whereas alternative methods may produce misleading or inconsistent signals.

The invention further includes a method for improving clinical decision-making by integrating the unreliability metric into a tiered risk assessment framework. In one implementation, a clinician receives the predicted risk score and the unreliability score simultaneously. If the unreliability score is below the predefined threshold, the model’s prediction is accepted as reliable and used to guide clinical management. If the unreliability score exceeds the threshold, the system prompts the clinician to seek additional information, such as laboratory tests, imaging studies, or expert consultation, before making a final decision. The system may also recommend alternative risk scores derived from different models or clinical guidelines, thereby providing a mechanism for model triangulation and reducing the risk of overreliance on a single algorithmic output.

The invention also encompasses a method for model auditing and quality assurance. Healthcare institutions may use the unreliability metric to monitor the performance of deployed risk models over time, identifying periods or patient subgroups in which the model’s predictions become increasingly unreliable. This may indicate model drift due to changes in patient demographics, treatment practices, or data collection protocols. By flagging such periods, the institution can trigger retraining, recalibration, or replacement of the model, ensuring that clinical decision support systems remain accurate, safe, and up to date.

The invention may be implemented in a variety of hardware and software configurations, including but not limited to cloud-based servers, local hospital servers, mobile applications, and embedded medical devices. The computational requirements are minimal, requiring only a modern processor and sufficient memory to store the summary statistics and execute the arithmetic operations involved in the calculation of the unreliability metric. The system may be deployed on standard clinical workstations, tablets, or smartphones, and may be accessed via web browser, native application, or integration with existing electronic health record systems. The method is compatible with both real-time and batch processing modes, allowing for retrospective analysis of historical predictions as well as prospective use in live clinical workflows.

The invention further includes a method for generating and distributing the summary statistics required for the computation of the unreliability metric. Model developers may publish these statistics alongside the clinical model, either in peer-reviewed publications, institutional repositories, or public databases. These statistics may be accompanied by metadata describing the training cohort, the time period of data collection, the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and the methods used to estimate the probability density functions. This ensures that the unreliability metric can be applied consistently across different institutions and clinical settings, while preserving the integrity and reproducibility of the model’s performance evaluation.

The invention is not limited to binary classification problems. While the disclosed embodiment focuses on binary outcomes, the underlying mathematical framework may be extended to multi-class classification by computing a separate unreliability metric for each class, using the same principles of posterior probability estimation and likelihood comparison. In such cases, the unreliability score for each class would reflect the degree of disagreement between the model’s predicted probability and the statistically recalibrated posterior probability for that class, allowing for the identification of unreliable predictions across multiple potential outcomes.

The invention further includes a method for combining the unreliability metric with other sources of uncertainty, such as feature importance scores, model confidence intervals, or clinical expert judgment, to create a composite reliability index. This index may be used to provide a more nuanced assessment of prediction trustworthiness, incorporating both algorithmic and human factors into the decision-making process. For example, a prediction with moderate unreliability but high feature importance and strong clinical correlation may be treated differently than a prediction with the same unreliability score but low feature relevance and conflicting clinical evidence.

The invention represents a significant advancement in the field of clinical artificial intelligence by providing the first method for identifying unreliable predictions that is simultaneously model-independent, data-efficient, privacy-preserving, and clinically validated. It transforms the abstract concept of model uncertainty into a concrete, actionable signal that can be used by clinicians at the point of care. By enabling providers to distinguish between predictions that are statistically robust and those that are statistically ambiguous, the invention enhances patient safety, promotes informed decision-making, and supports the ethical deployment of predictive models in clinical practice. It does not replace clinical judgment; rather, it empowers clinicians to exercise that judgment with greater confidence, awareness, and precision. The invention is therefore not merely a technical innovation, but a foundational step toward the responsible, transparent, and human-centered use of artificial intelligence in medicine.
