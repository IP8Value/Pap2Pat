# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of natural language processing, specifically to systems and methods for abstractive text summarization that ensure factual faithfulness in generated summaries. More particularly, the invention provides a novel framework for controlling the faithfulness of abstractive summaries through the use of entity coverage control codes, which are appended to source documents during both training and inference phases to guide language models toward generating summaries that are strictly grounded in the content of the source text. The invention further extends this framework to zero-shot summarization scenarios by introducing controllable intermediate fine-tuning with domain-specific control codes, enabling a single model to generalize across multiple domains while maintaining high levels of factual accuracy, fluency, and salience. The system is applicable to any sequence-to-sequence transformer-based architecture and is particularly suited for deployment in applications requiring high reliability in factual reporting, such as news aggregation, legal document synthesis, medical record summarization, scientific literature curation, and automated content moderation platforms. The invention addresses a critical limitation in existing abstractive summarization models—namely, their tendency to generate hallucinated entities and factually inconsistent statements—by embedding explicit, quantized signals of entity-level faithfulness directly into the model’s input representation, thereby transforming the summarization task from an unconstrained generation problem into a controllable, faithfulness-guided inference process.

## BACKGROUND

Abstractive summarization has emerged as a cornerstone of modern natural language processing, enabling the generation of concise, fluent, and semantically coherent summaries that paraphrase and condense the essential information from lengthy source documents. Recent advances in pre-trained language models, particularly those based on the transformer architecture, have significantly improved the fluency, coherence, and overall quality of generated summaries. Models such as BART, T5, and PEGASUS have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on benchmark datasets by leveraging massive amounts of unlabeled text to learn rich representations of language structure and semantic relationships. However, despite these gains in surface-level quality, a persistent and pervasive challenge remains: the generation of factually inaccurate or hallucinated content. These hallucinations manifest as entities, events, or relationships that are not supported by, or are entirely absent from, the source document, yet are confidently produced by the model due to its internal biases, over-reliance on pre-training corpora, or insufficient alignment with the specific context of the input.

Hallucinations in summarization can be broadly categorized into two types: intrinsic and extrinsic. Intrinsic hallucinations occur when the model fabricates information that contradicts the source document, such as attributing an action to the wrong person or misrepresenting a temporal or spatial relationship. Extrinsic hallucinations involve the introduction of entities or facts that are unrelated to the source document altogether, often drawn from the model’s pre-training knowledge base rather than the input text. For instance, a model may generate a summary stating that a scientific conference was held in “Los Angeles” when the source document explicitly mentions “London,” or claim that a person named “Gary Anderson” led a team when the document refers to “Steven Anderson.” Such errors are not merely stylistic deviations—they introduce factual inaccuracies that can have serious consequences in domains such as journalism, law, healthcare, and public policy, where trustworthiness and verifiability are paramount.

Prior efforts to mitigate hallucination have largely focused on post-processing techniques, data filtering, or multi-task learning. Post-processing methods attempt to correct hallucinated entities in generated summaries by replacing them with entities from the source document that match the same semantic type. While such approaches can reduce the number of hallucinated entities, they often introduce new errors by substituting entities that alter the intended meaning or context of the summary. For example, replacing “Los Angeles” with “Mexico” in a summary about an event held at E3 may eliminate the hallucination of a specific city, but it creates a new factual error by misrepresenting the location of a globally recognized event. Data filtering methods, on the other hand, remove training examples where the reference summary contains entities not present in the source document, thereby reducing exposure to unfaithful examples during training. However, this approach sacrifices valuable training data, particularly in datasets where reference summaries are inherently less faithful, leading to a degradation in summary quality, fluency, and coverage of salient information. Multi-task learning frameworks attempt to jointly train summarization models with auxiliary tasks such as named entity recognition or fact verification, but these methods often require complex architectures, additional annotations, and substantial computational resources, limiting their scalability and practicality.

Furthermore, existing approaches fail to provide a unified mechanism for controlling the degree of faithfulness during inference. Most models are trained to maximize likelihood with respect to reference summaries, which themselves may contain hallucinations, thereby implicitly learning to reproduce those inaccuracies. There is no established method for dynamically adjusting the faithfulness level of a summary based on user requirements or domain-specific constraints. In zero-shot settings, where a model is expected to summarize documents from domains it has never encountered during training, the problem is exacerbated. Pre-trained models tend to either copy verbatim from the source document, producing summaries that are factually accurate but lack abstraction and fluency, or generate fluent but entirely hallucinated summaries that bear little resemblance to the source. Current state-of-the-art methods for zero-shot summarization, such as WikiTransfer, rely on domain-specific fine-tuning using Wikipedia articles, but require separate models to be trained for each target domain, resulting in a proliferation of models, increased maintenance overhead, and a lack of generalization across heterogeneous domains.

There is therefore a critical unmet need for a summarization system that can generate fluent, salient, and abstract summaries while guaranteeing a high degree of factual faithfulness, without relying on post-hoc corrections, data filtering, or domain-specific training. There is also a need for a scalable, unified framework that enables controllable summarization across multiple domains in a zero-shot setting, using a single model capable of adapting its output style and faithfulness level through simple, interpretable control signals. The present invention fulfills these needs by introducing a novel paradigm in which entity-level faithfulness is encoded as discrete control codes, embedded directly into the input sequence of a sequence-to-sequence model, thereby transforming the summarization process into a guided, controllable, and verifiably faithful generation task.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Systems for Abstractive Summarization

The present invention provides a comprehensive system for abstractive summarization that ensures factual faithfulness through the use of entity coverage control codes. The system operates by modifying the input representation of a sequence-to-sequence language model to include a control code that encodes the degree of entity-level faithfulness observed between the source document and its corresponding reference summary during training. This control code is not a heuristic or a post-processing filter but an integral component of the model’s input conditioning mechanism, learned end-to-end during training and leveraged during inference to steer generation toward desired levels of factual accuracy. The system is composed of three core components: an entity coverage precision calculator, a discrete binning and control code generation module, and a controllable sequence-to-sequence summarization model with an extended vocabulary that includes the control codes as special tokens.

The entity coverage precision calculator is configured to analyze each document-summary pair in the training corpus and compute a quantitative measure of how many named entities present in the summary are also present in the source document. Named entities are identified using a domain-appropriate named entity recognition system, such as Stanza for general-domain text or scispaCy for biomedical documents. The entity coverage precision is defined as the ratio of the number of named entities in the summary that are also found in the source document to the total number of named entities in the summary. This metric provides a direct, objective, and computationally tractable proxy for faithfulness at the entity level, capturing the extent to which the summary is grounded in the source text rather than relying on external knowledge or fabricated content.

Once computed for each training example, the entity coverage precision values are quantized into a predetermined number of discrete bins, typically three, although the system supports any number of bins greater than one. The bin boundaries are selected such that each bin contains approximately the same number of training examples, ensuring balanced representation across the faithfulness spectrum and preventing data imbalance that could bias the model toward high- or low-faithfulness patterns. For example, in one embodiment, the entity coverage precision values are divided into three bins: low faithfulness (e.g., 0.0 to 0.33), medium faithfulness (e.g., 0.34 to 0.66), and high faithfulness (e.g., 0.67 to 1.0). Each bin is then assigned a unique, reserved control code token, such as <FF-low>, <FF-mid>, and <FF-high>, which are added to the vocabulary of the sequence-to-sequence model. These control codes are not derived from the source text but are synthetic tokens introduced solely for the purpose of conditioning the model on faithfulness levels.

During the training phase, each source document is prepended with its corresponding control code token before being fed into the model. For instance, if a document-summary pair has an entity coverage precision falling within the high faithfulness bin, the input to the model becomes: “<FF-high> [source document]”. The model is then trained to predict the reference summary conditioned on both the source document and the control code. This conditioning enables the model to learn distinct generation strategies for each faithfulness level. When conditioned on <FF-high>, the model learns to prioritize the inclusion of entities that appear in the source document, avoid introducing new entities not grounded in the text, and preserve the semantic structure of the original content. When conditioned on <FF-low>, the model learns to generate more abstract, potentially less faithful summaries, which may be useful in scenarios where brevity or stylistic variation is prioritized over strict factual adherence. Importantly, the model does not learn to generate fewer entities overall; rather, it learns to select which entities to include based on their presence in the source document, as confirmed by empirical analysis showing no significant difference in the mean number of entities generated across control codes.

During inference, the system does not require access to a reference summary or any external fact-checking mechanism. Instead, the user or application selects the desired level of faithfulness by prepending the corresponding control code to the source document. For example, in a high-stakes legal or medical context, the user may prepend <FF-high> to ensure that every entity in the summary is verifiably grounded in the source document. In a journalistic or creative context, the user may opt for <FF-mid> to allow for some abstraction while still maintaining a high degree of fidelity. The model then generates the summary by computing the probability distribution over the output sequence conditioned on the source document and the chosen control code. Because the control code has been learned during training to correlate with specific generation patterns, the model produces summaries that are consistently faithful to the specified level, without requiring additional decoding constraints, reranking, or post-processing.

The system further extends this framework to zero-shot summarization scenarios through a technique termed controllable intermediate fine-tuning. In this phase, the model is first pre-trained on a large corpus of general-domain text, such as Wikipedia, and then fine-tuned on a collection of pseudo document-summary pairs synthesized from the same corpus. These pseudo pairs are constructed by selecting Wikipedia articles that match the length, abstractiveness, and summary structure of target downstream datasets. For example, if the target dataset consists of news summaries with an average length of 50 words and high abstractiveness, the system selects Wikipedia articles with similar characteristics and generates pseudo summaries using a heuristic or rule-based method, such as extracting the first sentence or applying a lightweight summarization algorithm. Each pseudo training subset is associated with a unique domain control code, such as <DOMAIN-news>, <DOMAIN-medical>, or <DOMAIN-legal>, which is added to the model’s vocabulary. During fine-tuning, each pseudo document is prepended with its corresponding domain control code, and the model learns to generate summaries that reflect the stylistic and structural norms of that domain. Crucially, the domain control codes are stackable with the entity coverage control codes. Thus, during inference, a user may prepend both <DOMAIN-medical> and <FF-high> to a source document, and the model will generate a summary that is both stylistically appropriate for medical literature and strictly faithful to the entities present in the source. This stacking capability enables a single model to serve multiple domains and faithfulness requirements without the need for separate model instances, dramatically reducing computational overhead and simplifying deployment.

The system is implemented using a transformer-based sequence-to-sequence architecture, such as BART-large, which has been pre-trained on a large corpus of text and fine-tuned on summarization tasks. The model’s vocabulary is expanded to include all entity coverage control codes and domain control codes, allowing the model to treat these codes as regular tokens during both encoding and decoding. The training objective remains the standard maximum likelihood estimation, but the input representation is augmented to include the control codes. The model is trained using standard optimization techniques, such as AdamW with a learning rate of 5×10⁻⁵, weight decay, and gradient clipping, on distributed GPU clusters. The system is designed to be modular and architecture-agnostic, meaning it can be applied to any sequence-to-sequence model that supports vocabulary expansion and input conditioning, including T5, PEGASUS, or future transformer variants.

The system’s effectiveness is demonstrated across multiple benchmark datasets, including XSum, SAMsum, and PubMed, each representing different domains and levels of abstractiveness. In supervised settings, the system achieves significant improvements in entity coverage precision—often exceeding 10 percentage points—without any degradation in ROUGE scores, which measure fluency and content overlap with reference summaries. In zero-shot settings, the system outperforms existing methods such as WikiTransfer by achieving higher ROUGE scores and higher entity coverage precision simultaneously, whereas prior methods exhibit a trade-off between these metrics. Human evaluations conducted by expert annotators confirm that summaries generated by the system are rated significantly higher in faithfulness without any reduction in quality, with a majority of summaries classified as fully faithful and of high quality, even when compared to human-written reference summaries that themselves contain hallucinations.

The system also enables fine-grained control over the summarization process during inference. For example, a user may test multiple control code combinations to explore the trade-offs between faithfulness and abstraction. A summary generated with <FF-low> may be more fluent and stylistically varied but may omit some key entities, while a summary generated with <FF-high> may be more conservative but entirely grounded in the source. This capability allows the system to be deployed in adaptive environments where the required level of faithfulness varies by context, such as in legal discovery, where summaries must be strictly accurate, versus in editorial workflows, where some abstraction is acceptable. The control codes are interpretable, human-readable, and easily integrated into user interfaces, making the system transparent and controllable by non-technical users.

Moreover, the system mitigates the problem of model over-reliance on pre-training knowledge by grounding generation in the source document through explicit control signals. Unlike models that rely on internal knowledge to fill gaps in the input, the system forces the model to prioritize entities present in the source, even when those entities are rare or low-frequency in the pre-training corpus. For example, in a case where the source document refers to “Steven Anderson” as a team captain, but the model’s pre-training data contains more frequent references to “Gary Anderson” or “Paul Anderson,” the system ensures that “Steven Anderson” is retained in the summary because it is present in the source, regardless of its frequency in the pre-training data. This is achieved not by suppressing the model’s knowledge but by biasing its generation toward the input context through the control code, thereby preserving the model’s ability to generalize while ensuring factual integrity.

The system further supports dynamic adaptation to new domains without retraining. When a new domain emerges, such as cryptocurrency or quantum computing, the system can generate pseudo document-summary pairs from relevant sources, assign a new domain control code, and perform lightweight fine-tuning on a small subset of data. The resulting model retains its ability to generate faithful summaries in all previously learned domains while extending its capabilities to the new domain. This incremental adaptability makes the system highly scalable and suitable for real-world deployment in rapidly evolving information environments.

In summary, the system represents a paradigm shift in abstractive summarization by decoupling faithfulness from training data quality and instead embedding it as a controllable, explicit signal within the model’s input representation. It eliminates the need for post-processing corrections, data filtering, or domain-specific models, while providing unprecedented control over the faithfulness-fluency trade-off. The invention enables the generation of summaries that are not only fluent and salient but also verifiably faithful, making it suitable for deployment in any application where factual accuracy is non-negotiable.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Experimental Methods and Results

The experimental validation of the invention was conducted across three benchmark datasets: XSum, SAMsum, and PubMed, each representing distinct domains and summarization challenges. The XSum dataset consists of BBC news articles paired with single-sentence summaries, characterized by high abstractiveness and frequent entity hallucinations in reference summaries. The SAMsum dataset comprises dialogues from online chat platforms, with summaries that capture key conversational turns and speaker intent, presenting challenges in entity resolution and context preservation. The PubMed dataset contains scientific abstracts and their corresponding summaries, requiring precise handling of technical terminology and biomedical entities. All experiments were performed using BART-large as the base model, a pre-trained sequence-to-sequence transformer with 336 million parameters, chosen for its strong performance on summarization tasks and widespread adoption in the research community. The model was fine-tuned on each dataset using eight Tesla A100 GPU pods, with a learning rate of 5×10⁻⁵, weight decay of 0.01, and an Adam optimizer with beta parameters set to 0.9 and 0.999. Training was conducted for 10 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss, and batch sizes were adjusted to maximize GPU utilization while maintaining stable gradients.

For entity coverage precision computation, named entities were identified using Stanza for XSum and SAMsum, and scispaCy for PubMed, due to the domain-specific nature of medical terminology. The entity coverage precision values were quantized into three discrete bins, with boundaries selected to ensure approximately equal distribution of training examples across bins. For XSum, the boundaries were set at 0.33 and 0.66, resulting in low (<FF-low>), medium (<FF-mid>), and high (<FF-high>) faithfulness bins. For PubMed, the boundaries were 0.36 and 0.50, reflecting the higher baseline faithfulness of scientific summaries. For SAMsum, the boundaries were 0.30 and 0.65, accommodating the more conversational and less structured nature of dialogue summaries. These control codes were added to the model’s vocabulary, increasing its size by three tokens, and the model was retrained from the pre-trained checkpoint with the augmented input representation.

In the supervised setting, the system was evaluated against two state-of-the-art baselines: a post-processing correction method that replaces hallucinated entities in generated summaries with entities from the source document, and a data filtering method that removes training examples where the reference summary contains entities not present in the source. The results, presented in Table 2, demonstrate that the proposed system significantly outperforms both baselines in entity coverage precision while maintaining or improving ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, and ROUGE-L scores. On XSum, the baseline BART model achieved an entity coverage precision of 65.2%, while the post-processing method improved this to 70.1% but at the cost of a 1.8-point drop in ROUGE-1. The data filtering method achieved 71.5% entity coverage precision but reduced ROUGE-1 by 3.2 points, indicating a substantial loss in summary quality. In contrast, the proposed system achieved an entity coverage precision of 78.9% with no statistically significant change in ROUGE scores, demonstrating that faithfulness can be improved without sacrificing fluency or content coverage.

In the zero-shot setting, the system was compared against WikiTransfer, a method that trains separate models for each target domain using Wikipedia articles as pseudo training data. The results, shown in Table 4, reveal that the baseline BART model, when applied zero-shot to XSum, achieved a high entity coverage precision of 92.6% but a low ROUGE-1 score of 28.3, indicating that the model defaulted to copying phrases verbatim from the source rather than generating abstract summaries. WikiTransfer improved ROUGE-1 to 34.7 but only increased entity coverage precision to 81.2%, suggesting that while the model learned to generate more abstract summaries, it did not improve faithfulness. The proposed system, using controllable intermediate fine-tuning with domain control codes, achieved an entity coverage precision of 86.4% and a ROUGE-1 score of 37.1, surpassing WikiTransfer in both metrics. This demonstrates that the system not only improves faithfulness but also enhances the quality of abstraction, achieving a true win-win scenario where neither faithfulness nor fluency is compromised.

Human evaluation was conducted on a randomly sampled subset of 50 articles from the XSum dataset, following the protocol established in prior work. Four expert annotators, each with advanced training in linguistics and journalism, evaluated each summary for faithfulness and quality. Faithfulness was categorized into three levels: faithful (no hallucinations), intrinsic hallucination (fabricated information contradicts the source), and extrinsic hallucination (introduced entities not mentioned in the source). Quality was rated on a three-point scale: low (1), medium (2), and high (3). The results, presented in Table 5, show that summaries generated by the proposed system received 74% high-quality ratings and 89% faithfulness ratings, compared to 68% and 71% for the baseline BART model, respectively. The post-processing method received 72% faithfulness ratings but only 65% quality ratings, while the data filtering method received 75% faithfulness but only 60% quality ratings. These results confirm that the system improves both faithfulness and perceived quality, whereas prior methods exhibit a trade-off between the two.

An analysis of entity distribution revealed that the mean number of entities per summary generated by the proposed system was statistically indistinguishable from that of the baseline BART model, with both averaging approximately 4.3 entities per summary. This finding indicates that the system does not achieve higher faithfulness by under-generating entities or avoiding entity mentions altogether, but rather by selecting entities that are grounded in the source document. This is further supported by qualitative examples, such as one in which the source document mentions “a meeting in London” and the baseline model generates “a meeting in Los Angeles,” while the proposed system correctly generates “a meeting in London,” even when “Los Angeles” is a more frequent entity in the pre-training corpus. Another example shows the source document referencing “Rob Kiernan” as a player, a name with low frequency in the pre-training data, and the baseline model replacing it with “Gary Anderson,” a more common name. The proposed system, however, retains “Rob Kiernan,” demonstrating its ability to override pre-training biases in favor of source-grounded content.

The system was also tested for its ability to handle stacked control codes. In one experiment, a document from the PubMed dataset was processed with both <DOMAIN-medical> and <FF-high> appended to the input. The resulting summary was not only stylistically appropriate for medical literature—using precise terminology, passive voice, and structured phrasing—but also contained no hallucinated entities, even when the source document contained rare biomedical terms. In another experiment, a news article was processed with <DOMAIN-news> and <FF-mid>, producing a summary that was fluent, concise, and abstracted appropriately, while still preserving all key entities. These results confirm that the system supports multi-dimensional control, enabling users to tailor summaries to both domain-specific norms and faithfulness requirements simultaneously.

The system was further evaluated for its robustness to variations in control code selection during inference. When the same document was processed with <FF-low>, <FF-mid>, and <FF-high>, the summaries exhibited a clear gradient in faithfulness: the <FF-low> summary contained fewer entities and more paraphrasing, the <FF-mid> summary retained most entities but allowed for minor abstraction, and the <FF-high> summary preserved every entity with minimal rephrasing. Despite these differences, all summaries remained fluent and grammatically correct, demonstrating that the control codes do not impose rigid constraints but rather guide the model’s generation preferences. This flexibility is crucial for real-world deployment, where users may wish to explore different levels of abstraction depending on their needs.

Finally, the system was tested for cross-dataset generalization. A single model, trained on all three datasets using domain and entity control codes, was evaluated on each dataset in a zero-shot setting. The results showed that the model achieved performance comparable to models trained specifically on each dataset, with only minor degradation in ROUGE scores and no loss in faithfulness. This demonstrates that the system’s controllable fine-tuning approach enables true generalization across domains, eliminating the need for separate models and reducing the computational and maintenance burden associated with deploying summarization systems in multi-domain environments.

In conclusion, the experimental results demonstrate that the invention consistently improves faithfulness in abstractive summarization without compromising fluency or salience, outperforms existing methods in both supervised and zero-shot settings, and enables unprecedented control over the summarization process through interpretable, stackable control codes. The system is scalable, adaptable, and deployable in real-world applications where factual accuracy is essential, marking a significant advancement in the field of natural language generation.
