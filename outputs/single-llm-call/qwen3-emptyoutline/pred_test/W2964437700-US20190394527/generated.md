# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of wireless communication systems, and more particularly to methods and apparatus for improving the quality of experience for streaming video users in mobile radio access networks through real-time, experience-centric traffic classification and prioritized scheduling. The invention enables a radio access network to detect, with high accuracy and low computational overhead, the playback state and video resolution quality of users engaged in HTTP Adaptive Streaming (HAS) sessions by analyzing only the unencrypted downlink packet headers, without requiring access to payload data or bidirectional traffic monitoring. Based on this detection, the network dynamically adjusts radio resource allocation to prioritize users experiencing degraded video quality or prolonged playback delays, thereby enhancing perceived video quality while maintaining overall network efficiency and fairness among competing traffic classes. The invention is particularly suited for deployment in Long Term Evolution (LTE), 5G New Radio (NR), and future wireless networks where network operators seek to optimize user experience without requiring modifications to client-side applications, content delivery infrastructure, or end-to-end encryption protocols.

## BACKGROUND

Modern mobile networks are increasingly burdened by the exponential growth of video streaming traffic, which now constitutes the dominant portion of global mobile data consumption. This trend is driven by consumer demand for high-definition, immersive, and on-demand video content delivered through over-the-top (OTT) services such as Netflix, YouTube, and Amazon Prime Video. These services rely on HTTP Adaptive Streaming (HAS) protocols, which segment video content into small chunks encoded at multiple bit rates and deliver them over standard HTTP/TCP connections. While this architecture provides flexibility and compatibility with existing web infrastructure, it introduces significant challenges for mobile network operators. Because HAS traffic originates from external content providers and traverses the public internet before entering the mobile network, it is typically treated as best-effort traffic with no differentiated Quality of Service (QoS) guarantees. As a result, during periods of network congestion, HAS users frequently experience playback interruptions, prolonged buffering delays, and abrupt reductions in video resolution—phenomena collectively known as poor Quality of Experience (QoE).

Traditional approaches to mitigating these issues have been largely reactive or client-centric. Client-side Adaptive Bit Rate (ABR) algorithms attempt to adjust the video quality dynamically based on perceived network conditions, but they operate without knowledge of the underlying radio resource availability, interference levels, or scheduling priorities within the access network. Consequently, these algorithms often request video chunks at bit rates that exceed the available radio capacity, leading to retransmissions, increased latency, and inefficient use of spectrum. Network-centric solutions have attempted to improve QoE by leveraging Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) to identify video flows and apply QoS policies accordingly. However, DPI is rendered ineffective by the widespread adoption of end-to-end encryption, which obscures application-layer metadata and payload content. Furthermore, DPI requires substantial computational resources and introduces latency that is incompatible with real-time scheduling decisions at the base station.

Alternative approaches have proposed using machine learning classifiers to infer traffic characteristics from packet headers, thereby circumventing the limitations of DPI. However, many of these methods rely on features that are impractical for deployment in commercial radio access networks. Some require bidirectional traffic monitoring—analyzing both uplink and downlink packets—which introduces unpredictable delays due to asymmetric channel conditions, variable uplink bandwidth, and differing scheduling policies between directions. Others depend on inter-packet arrival time (IAT) statistics, which demand high-precision timestamping, significant memory buffers, and continuous processing of individual packets, resulting in computational overhead that is prohibitive for resource-constrained base station hardware. Additionally, many existing classifiers require extended observation windows—on the order of several seconds—to achieve acceptable accuracy, rendering them unsuitable for real-time scheduling adjustments that must respond within milliseconds.

Moreover, prior art scheduling algorithms that attempt to improve video QoE often assume ideal knowledge of client-side buffer states, chunk encoding rates, or network throughput, which are not directly observable by the base station. Some solutions rely on external analytics servers to compute user-specific weights, necessitating complex data aggregation across multiple network elements and introducing scalability and latency bottlenecks. Other proposals modify the physical layer or require proprietary signaling between the client and network, which are incompatible with standardized protocols and existing device ecosystems. As a consequence, no prior solution provides a lightweight, scalable, and deployable mechanism that enables a base station to accurately detect the real-time streaming experience of HAS users—specifically, whether they are in a buffering state or receiving suboptimal video resolution—using only standard, unencrypted downlink packet headers, and to act upon that information with minimal computational cost and zero impact on existing network infrastructure.

There remains a critical need for a system and method that can passively, accurately, and efficiently detect the user experience state of streaming video clients in real time, using only the minimal information available in downlink packet headers, and then dynamically prioritize their radio resource allocation to mitigate poor QoE without degrading the service quality of other traffic classes. Such a system must be compatible with existing LTE and 5G architectures, require no modifications to client devices or content servers, operate within the computational and memory constraints of base station hardware, and deliver measurable improvements in key QoE metrics such as initial playback delay and Mean Opinion Score (MOS), while preserving overall network throughput and fairness among diverse traffic types.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel system and method for enhancing the streaming video quality of experience (QoE) in wireless communication networks by detecting the real-time playback state and video resolution quality of users engaged in HTTP Adaptive Streaming (HAS) sessions through the analysis of downlink packet headers alone, and by dynamically adjusting radio resource scheduling based on the detected experience state. The invention comprises three core components: a traffic service classifier, a playback state classifier, and a video resolution classifier, all implemented as Random Forest Classifiers that operate exclusively on standard IP and TCP header fields of downlink packets, without requiring payload inspection, bidirectional traffic monitoring, or inter-packet timing information. The traffic service classifier identifies HAS flows within the first five downlink packets of a newly established session using only three features: the source and destination port numbers, the number of packets with the TCP PUSH flag set, and the median IP packet size. Upon detection of an HAS flow, the playback state classifier determines whether the client is in a Buffering state or a Steady-State by analyzing the cumulative number of downlink packets and total payload size observed over a short, fixed-duration sliding window. If the client is determined to be in Steady-State, the video resolution classifier further infers the current video resolution level by aggregating the same features over a longer sampling interval, leveraging previously collected data to minimize redundant computation. These classifiers operate with high precision and recall, require minimal memory and computational resources, and produce output latencies on the order of hundreds of milliseconds, making them suitable for real-time integration into base station schedulers.

The invention further includes a prioritized scheduling module that integrates the outputs of the classifiers into the radio resource allocation process of a base station. The scheduler assigns user-specific weights to each active user based on their detected experience state: users identified as being in Buffering state or receiving video resolution below a predefined threshold are classified as unhappy users and receive elevated scheduling weights, while all other users are classified as happy users and receive a baseline weight. The scheduler then computes a modified Proportional Fairness metric that incorporates these experience-based weights, ensuring that unhappy users are preferentially scheduled to reduce buffering delays and increase video quality. The system operates without requiring changes to client applications, content delivery networks, or encryption protocols, and is fully compatible with existing LTE and 5G network architectures. The invention achieves substantial improvements in video QoE metrics—including a reduction in initial playback delay by over 50% and an increase in Mean Opinion Score by more than 12%—while maintaining near-identical total traffic volume for non-video traffic classes such as file downloads, due to the efficient transition of HAS users from Buffering to Steady-State, which reduces the frequency of chunk requests and thereby frees up scheduling opportunities for other users. The system demonstrates robustness to classification errors, with QoE gains remaining significant even when a substantial fraction of non-video traffic is misclassified as video, but requiring high recall to detect video flows accurately. The computational and memory overhead of the classifiers is negligible, consuming less than 0.05% of the processing capacity of a standard base station CPU, and requiring only a few megabytes of memory, enabling seamless deployment on existing hardware without upgrades or modifications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is a comprehensive system and method for improving the streaming video quality of experience in mobile wireless networks through real-time, experience-centric traffic classification and prioritized scheduling. The system operates entirely within the radio access network, specifically at the base station, and does not require any modifications to client devices, content servers, or end-to-end encryption protocols. It leverages the inherent structure of HTTP Adaptive Streaming (HAS) traffic to infer the real-time playback state and video resolution quality of users by analyzing only the unencrypted headers of downlink packets, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional methods that rely on payload inspection or bidirectional traffic monitoring. The invention is composed of three interconnected components: a traffic service classifier, a playback state classifier, and a video resolution classifier, each implemented as a Random Forest Classifier trained to operate with minimal feature complexity and low computational overhead. These classifiers are integrated into the base station’s user plane packet processor, where they continuously monitor incoming downlink traffic and generate real-time experience state indicators for each active user. These indicators are then fed into a modified scheduling algorithm that dynamically adjusts radio resource allocation to prioritize users experiencing degraded video quality or prolonged buffering delays, thereby significantly enhancing perceived video quality without compromising the service level of other traffic classes.

The traffic service classifier is the first component of the system and is responsible for identifying whether a newly established traffic flow corresponds to an HTTP Adaptive Streaming session. This classifier operates on the first five downlink packets of any newly detected flow, extracting three key features derived exclusively from standard IP and TCP header fields. The first feature is the combination of the TCP source and destination port numbers, which are used to identify common server-client port pairings associated with known HAS services. The second feature is the count of packets within the first five that have the TCP PUSH flag set, a flag commonly used by HTTP servers to indicate that data should be transmitted immediately rather than buffered, a behavior characteristic of chunked video delivery. The third feature is the median size of the IP packets, calculated from the IP Total Length field, which reflects the typical payload size of video chunks delivered over HAS. These three features are selected because they are universally available in all IP/TCP implementations, require no special timestamping or packet buffering, and are unaffected by encryption or network address translation. The classifier is trained using a supervised learning approach on a large dataset of labeled network traffic, where each flow is classified according to its application type. The Random Forest Classifier, comprising ten decision trees, is chosen for its superior performance in multi-class classification tasks, its inherent resistance to overfitting, and its low inference complexity. The classifier achieves a mean classification accuracy exceeding 98% across seven common traffic categories, including HAS, file transfer, web browsing, and video conferencing, with precision and recall scores above 0.9 for all classes. The entire classification process for a single flow requires fewer than 400 instructions and completes within a few milliseconds, allowing the system to identify HAS flows almost immediately upon their establishment, without introducing perceptible delay to the user.

Once an HAS flow is identified, the system activates two additional classifiers to determine the client’s playback state and video resolution quality. The playback state classifier operates continuously on all packets belonging to the identified HAS flow, using a sliding window approach to extract temporal statistics of downlink traffic. The classifier considers two aggregate features: the total number of downlink packets received during a fixed sampling interval and the cumulative payload size of those packets. The sampling interval is set to 0.1 seconds, a duration chosen to balance detection latency with classification accuracy. Within each interval, the classifier maintains a sliding window of the most recent five sampling periods, storing the packet count and payload size for each period. If fewer than five intervals have been observed, the missing values are filled with the average of the available data. This approach ensures that the classifier can operate from the very first observation and does not require a fixed initialization period. The classifier then inputs these features into a Random Forest model trained to distinguish between two states: Buffering and Steady-State. In the Buffering state, the client rapidly requests new video chunks to fill its playback buffer, resulting in a high frequency of small packet transmissions. In the Steady-State, the client requests chunks at a more regular, lower frequency, resulting in fewer but larger packets corresponding to the delivery of full video chunks. The classifier is trained to recognize these distinct traffic patterns and achieves a detection accuracy of over 95% with a latency of no more than 0.1 seconds. This low latency is critical, as it enables the scheduler to respond to buffering events before the user perceives any delay.

If the playback state classifier determines that the client is in Steady-State, the system activates the video resolution classifier to infer the current encoding quality of the video stream. This classifier uses the same feature set as the playback state classifier but applies a longer sampling interval of 1 second and aggregates data over fifteen consecutive intervals. The rationale for this design is that video resolution changes occur less frequently than playback state transitions, and therefore a longer observation window improves classification accuracy without significantly increasing latency. Crucially, the system reuses the packet count and payload size data collected by the playback state classifier, eliminating the need for redundant data collection and reducing computational overhead. The video resolution classifier is trained to assign one of six discrete resolution levels—144p, 240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, or 1080p—based on the aggregated traffic patterns. Higher resolution streams typically involve larger chunk sizes and more consistent packet delivery, while lower resolution streams exhibit smaller, more sporadic transmissions. The classifier achieves an accuracy of over 90% in distinguishing between these levels, enabling the system to identify when a user is receiving suboptimal video quality. The output of this classifier is used in conjunction with the playback state output to determine whether the user should be classified as “unhappy.” A user is designated as unhappy if either their playback state is Buffering or if their playback state is Steady-State and their detected video resolution is below a predefined threshold, such as 360p. All other users are classified as happy. This binary classification is the key input to the scheduling module.

The prioritized scheduling module receives the experience state classification for each active user and modifies the base station’s radio resource allocation strategy accordingly. The system operates within the framework of a Proportional Fairness (PF) scheduler, a widely deployed scheduling algorithm in LTE and 5G networks that balances throughput and fairness by allocating resources to users based on a metric that considers both their instantaneous channel conditions and their historical throughput. The invention enhances this scheduler by introducing two user-specific, time-varying weights: one for playback state and one for video resolution. For users classified as happy, both weights are set to unity, resulting in no deviation from standard PF behavior. For users classified as unhappy, the weights are elevated to a fixed value greater than one—specifically, a value of four in the preferred embodiment—thereby increasing their scheduling priority. The scheduler computes a modified PF metric for each user by multiplying their baseline PF score by the product of their playback state weight and video resolution weight. The user with the highest modified PF score is selected for scheduling in each subframe. This approach ensures that users experiencing buffering delays or low video quality are preferentially allocated radio resources, enabling them to complete chunk downloads more quickly and transition to higher quality streams. The scheduler operates on a subframe-by-subframe basis, with each subframe lasting one millisecond, ensuring that scheduling decisions are made with sufficient granularity to respond to dynamic network conditions. The weights are applied continuously and are updated in real time as the classifiers produce new state estimates, allowing the system to adapt to changing user behavior and network conditions without requiring manual configuration or external control signals.

The system is designed to operate with minimal computational and memory overhead, making it suitable for deployment on existing base station hardware without requiring upgrades or specialized accelerators. The traffic service classifier requires only 212 bytes of memory per flow to store its feature extraction logic and 21,456 bytes to store the coefficients of its decision trees. For a base station handling 500 new flows per second, the total CPU overhead is approximately 0.1835 million instructions per second (MIPS), representing less than 0.001% of the capacity of a standard Intel i7 processor. The playback state classifier requires 20 bytes of memory per concurrent video session to store its feature values and 120,624 bytes to store its decision tree parameters. For 1,000 concurrent video sessions, the total CPU overhead is 5.71 MIPS, or approximately 0.02% of processor capacity. The video resolution classifier, which reuses the data collected by the playback state classifier, requires 660 bytes of memory per session and 1.43 megabytes of storage for its decision trees. For 1,000 concurrent sessions, the CPU overhead is 6.85 MIPS, or 0.03% of processor capacity. The combined system requires less than 2.26 megabytes of memory and 12.7 MIPS of processing power to classify all traffic in a typical base station, which is negligible compared to the tens of thousands of MIPS typically available in modern base station processors. This low overhead ensures that the invention can be deployed as a software module on existing hardware, without requiring additional memory, specialized processors, or modifications to the base station’s operating system or scheduling architecture.

The system has been evaluated in a realistic simulation environment that replicates the physical layer, user behavior, and traffic patterns of a commercial LTE network. The simulation includes 20 active users, of which five are engaged in streaming video while the remaining fifteen are downloading files. The video traffic is modeled using real-world traces of animated content encoded at nine different resolution levels, with chunk sizes of two-thirds of a second, matching the behavior of actual HAS clients. The file download traffic follows the 3GPP FTP Traffic Model 2, with 0.5 MB files and exponentially distributed reading times. The simulation uses a dynamic system-level LTE simulator with a 10 MHz bandwidth, Time Division Duplex operation, and realistic radio channel conditions including fading, interference, and mobility. The performance of the invention is compared against a baseline Proportional Fairness scheduler that treats all users equally, regardless of their traffic type or experience state. The results demonstrate substantial improvements in video QoE metrics. The median initial playback delay is reduced by over 30%, and the 95th percentile delay is reduced by over 50%. The Mean Opinion Score, a standardized metric for perceived video quality, increases by 0.15 for median users and by 0.44 for the worst-performing 5% of users, representing a 3.5% and 12.1% improvement, respectively. The average video resolution quality also increases significantly, with users spending a greater proportion of their viewing time at higher resolution levels. Crucially, these gains are achieved without a corresponding reduction in total traffic volume for file download users. The total amount of data delivered to file download users remains nearly identical across all scheduling schemes, despite the prioritization of video users. This is because the system’s ability to rapidly resolve buffering states reduces the frequency of chunk requests from HAS clients, thereby freeing up scheduling opportunities for other users. Additionally, the natural idle periods between video clips and the reading delays between file downloads further mitigate any potential service degradation. The system also demonstrates robustness to classification errors. When a single HAS user is misclassified as a file download user (a scenario representing 80% recall), the QoE gains are significantly reduced, highlighting the importance of high recall in detecting video flows. However, when three file download users are misclassified as HAS users (a scenario representing 80% precision), the QoE gains remain nearly identical to those achieved under ideal classification, demonstrating that the system’s performance is largely insensitive to false positives. This indicates that the primary driver of QoE improvement is the accurate identification of unhappy users, not the precise exclusion of non-video traffic.

The invention is modular and extensible, allowing for future enhancements without requiring fundamental changes to its architecture. The classifiers can be retrained to detect additional service types, such as augmented reality, virtual reality, or social media streaming, by incorporating new training data and adapting the feature set accordingly. The scheduling module can be adapted to support more granular prioritization, such as assigning different weights based on the degree of video degradation or the duration of buffering, to further optimize user experience. The system can also be extended to support multi-user scenarios with heterogeneous network slices, where different classes of users are allocated different resource budgets, and the experience-based weights are applied within each slice to ensure fairness and quality within the slice constraints. The invention is fully compatible with existing standards, including LTE Release 13 and 5G NR, and does not require any modifications to the user equipment, the content delivery network, or the end-to-end encryption protocols that secure the traffic. It operates entirely within the base station, making it a deployable solution that can be rolled out incrementally across a network without requiring coordinated upgrades to client devices or content providers. The system’s reliance on standard IP/TCP headers ensures compatibility with any network that uses these protocols, which includes virtually all modern mobile networks. Its low computational footprint ensures that it can be implemented on low-cost hardware, making it accessible to operators of all sizes. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in wireless network management, enabling network operators to deliver a superior streaming video experience to their users without incurring the costs, complexities, or delays associated with infrastructure upgrades or protocol changes.

The system’s operation is entirely passive and non-intrusive. It does not require any signaling between the client and the network, does not modify packet contents, and does not interfere with the operation of the HAS client or server. The classifiers operate solely on the headers of packets that are already being processed by the base station for routing and scheduling purposes, meaning that the system imposes no additional load on the network beyond the normal processing of user traffic. The scheduler operates as a layer above the physical and medium access control layers, modifying only the scheduling decision logic without altering the transmission parameters, modulation schemes, or resource block assignments. This ensures that the system is fully compliant with existing network standards and can be deployed without violating any regulatory or interoperability requirements. The system’s design also ensures privacy and security, as it does not inspect or store any payload data, and the classification decisions are made locally at the base station without transmitting any user-specific experience data to external servers or analytics platforms. This makes the invention suitable for deployment in jurisdictions with strict data privacy regulations, as it does not collect or process any personally identifiable information beyond what is already available in standard packet headers.

The invention has been validated through extensive testing on both public datasets and a custom-built LTE user plane testbed. The public datasets used for training and evaluation include a comprehensive collection of network traffic captured over 11 hours at a research site, containing over 100 million packets from 800,000 flows, and a separate dataset of YouTube streaming traffic captured on smartphones under varying network conditions. The testbed was constructed using a laptop connected to the internet via Wi-Fi, with custom Python scripts generating traffic profiles that mimic real-world HAS and file download behavior. Packet headers were captured using PyShark, a high-performance packet analysis library, and processed in real time using the same Random Forest models deployed in the simulation environment. The results from the testbed confirmed the accuracy and latency performance of the classifiers under real-world conditions, demonstrating that the system performs as expected in a live network environment. The system was also tested under varying levels of network congestion, mobility, and interference, and consistently demonstrated robust classification performance and significant QoE improvements. The system’s performance was found to be stable across different geographic locations, network operators, and device types, indicating that it is not dependent on any specific network configuration or client implementation.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, practical, and highly effective solution to the long-standing problem of poor streaming video quality in mobile networks. By leveraging the inherent structure of HAS traffic and applying machine learning to the minimal set of features available in downlink packet headers, the system enables base stations to detect and respond to user experience degradation in real time, without requiring any changes to the network infrastructure, client devices, or content delivery protocols. The system’s low computational overhead, high accuracy, and robustness to classification errors make it uniquely suited for deployment in commercial networks, where scalability, reliability, and cost-effectiveness are paramount. The invention represents a fundamental shift in the way mobile networks manage video traffic, moving from a reactive, bandwidth-centric approach to a proactive, experience-centric approach that prioritizes the end user’s perception of quality over abstract network metrics. This paradigm shift has the potential to significantly improve customer satisfaction, reduce churn, and increase revenue for network operators, while simultaneously enhancing the overall quality of the mobile internet experience for billions of users worldwide.
