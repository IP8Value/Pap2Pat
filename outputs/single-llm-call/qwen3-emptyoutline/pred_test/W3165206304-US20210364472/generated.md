# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to non-destructive evaluation techniques for composite materials, particularly for the high-resolution quantification of manufacturing defects within laminated fiber-reinforced polymer composites. More specifically, the invention provides a method and system for accurately determining the geometric dimensions of foreign object inclusions—such as those formed by residual release films, tape, or polymer inserts—embedded within woven carbon fiber laminates during the fabrication process. The technique leverages pulse-echo ultrasonic imaging combined with advanced signal processing, spatial interpolation, and gradient-based edge detection to achieve sub-millimeter precision in defect sizing, even when the defects are located at varying depths within the laminate and are comparable in scale to the underlying fiber tow architecture. The method is particularly suited for applications in aerospace, automotive, and defense industries where dimensional accuracy of internal defects directly impacts structural integrity, fatigue life, and regulatory compliance. Unlike conventional ultrasonic inspection methods that rely on amplitude thresholds or manual interpretation, this invention introduces a fully automated, algorithmically driven framework that extracts defect boundaries from raw ultrasonic data without prior assumptions regarding defect shape, size, or orientation, thereby enabling consistent, repeatable, and objective measurements across diverse composite architectures.

### 2. Description of Related Art

The use of fiber-reinforced polymer composites in critical structural applications has grown substantially over the past two decades, driven by the demand for lightweight, high-strength materials that offer superior performance-to-weight ratios compared to traditional metallic alloys. In particular, carbon fiber reinforced polymers (CFRPs) have become the material of choice for primary structures in commercial aircraft, such as the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350, where composite content exceeds fifty percent by weight. Despite their advantages, these materials are inherently susceptible to manufacturing-induced defects, including delaminations, voids, fiber misalignment, resin-rich or resin-starved regions, and the unintentional incorporation of foreign objects such as release films, peel ply, or Teflon inserts. These defects act as stress concentrators, reduce load-bearing capacity, initiate premature failure, and complicate predictive modeling of structural behavior under operational loads.

To mitigate these risks, non-destructive testing (NDT) methods have been widely adopted in both manufacturing quality control and in-service inspection regimes. Among the most commonly employed NDT techniques are thermography, shearography, X-ray computed tomography (CT), terahertz imaging, and ultrasonic testing. Of these, ultrasonic testing remains the most prevalent due to its portability, cost-effectiveness, safety, and ability to penetrate thick sections without requiring direct access to both surfaces. Pulse-echo ultrasound, in particular, is favored in industrial settings because it permits inspection from a single side, making it ideal for large, complex, or curved components where access is restricted.

Early ultrasonic inspection methods relied primarily on manual interpretation of A-scan waveforms and basic C-scan amplitude maps, where defects were identified by localized increases in signal amplitude or time-of-flight shifts. However, these approaches are highly subjective, lack quantitative precision, and are prone to operator variability. The introduction of phased array transducers and digital data acquisition systems enabled the generation of high-resolution C-scan images, which represent the spatial distribution of ultrasonic signal characteristics across a planar region of the component. These images, while visually informative, are often corrupted by structural noise arising from the heterogeneous, layered nature of woven composites, wherein the undulating paths of fiber tows generate complex, time-varying reflections that obscure defect signatures.

To address these challenges, researchers have developed a variety of signal processing and image analysis techniques. One widely used method is the 6 dB drop technique, which defines the boundary of a defect as the contour where the signal amplitude falls to half its maximum value within the defect region. While simple and intuitive, this method is highly sensitive to signal attenuation, material inhomogeneity, and transducer alignment, and often results in significant overestimation of defect dimensions, particularly for small or shallow inclusions. Other approaches have employed threshold-based segmentation, region-growing algorithms, and morphological filtering to isolate defect regions, but these techniques typically require manual parameter tuning and fail to generalize across different composite architectures or defect geometries.

More sophisticated methods have emerged in recent years that integrate artificial intelligence and machine learning. For instance, artificial neural networks have been trained to classify A-scan signatures as defect-free or indicative of specific defect types, achieving classification accuracies exceeding ninety-eight percent. However, these models are primarily designed for defect identification rather than dimensional quantification, and their outputs rarely provide precise measurements of defect area, perimeter, or diameter. Similarly, wavelet-based signal decomposition techniques have been applied to isolate defect-related features from background noise, enabling depth estimation with errors as low as six percent. Yet, these methods still struggle with accurate sizing, particularly when the defect dimensions approach the spatial resolution limit of the ultrasonic system. One study reported a forty-three percent overestimation of Teflon inclusion size using a wavelet-based algorithm, while another demonstrated a maximum error of 1.2 mm in diameter measurement using conventional through-transmission ultrasound.

Recent efforts have focused on improving spatial resolution through higher-frequency transducers and advanced data interpolation. Phased array systems operating at frequencies up to 15 MHz have been employed to achieve finer axial resolution, but the lateral resolution remains constrained by the physical dimensions of the transducer and the wavelength of the propagating acoustic wave. Moreover, the increased scattering and attenuation associated with high-frequency signals in woven laminates—due to the complex acoustic impedance transitions between fiber tows, resin pockets, and voids—often degrade image clarity and introduce artifacts that mimic defect boundaries. To counteract this, some researchers have applied Gaussian smoothing filters to suppress noise, but such filtering tends to blur fine details and reduce the sharpness of defect edges, thereby compromising sizing accuracy.

A critical limitation of nearly all existing methods is their reliance on predefined defect shapes—typically circular or square—in order to compute dimensions. This assumption is fundamentally incompatible with the irregular, often distorted geometries of real-world manufacturing defects, which may be elongated, fragmented, or deformed due to pressure, temperature, or resin flow during cure. Furthermore, many published studies have been conducted on unidirectional, highly densified laminates, where the acoustic environment is relatively uniform and the structural noise is minimized. These conditions do not reflect the reality of woven composites, which exhibit pronounced anisotropy, multiple reflection paths, and spatially varying signal amplitudes due to the periodic undulation of fibers in both warp and weft directions. As a result, techniques validated on unidirectional laminates frequently fail when applied to woven systems, yielding inconsistent or erroneous measurements.

One study comparing multiple NDT modalities for detecting Teflon inclusions in glass fiber laminates reported an average lateral error of fifteen percent in area measurement, corresponding to a diameter error of approximately two millimeters for a thirty-millimeter defect. Another study using through-transmission acoustography achieved a maximum diameter error of 0.4 mm for 20 mm inclusions, but required access to both sides of the part and could not be applied to thick or curved components. In contrast, the present invention operates in pulse-echo mode with single-sided access, enabling inspection of complex geometries without modification to the component or inspection setup.

Moreover, prior methods have not demonstrated reliable performance for defects smaller than three millimeters in diameter, a critical gap given that modern manufacturing processes increasingly produce sub-millimeter inclusions that can still significantly affect structural performance. The ability to accurately size such small defects has remained elusive due to the combined effects of limited spatial resolution, signal-to-noise ratio constraints, and the absence of robust, physics-informed edge detection algorithms capable of distinguishing true defect boundaries from structural artifacts.

The present invention overcomes these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel, integrated methodology that combines three key innovations: (1) a time-domain alignment procedure that corrects for minor surface irregularities and ensures consistent reference timing across all spatial locations; (2) a two-dimensional Gaussian smoothing filter applied prior to interpolation to suppress noise without blurring defect edges; and (3) a gradient-based edge detection technique that computes the magnitude of the spatial gradient of the ultrasonic signal and identifies defect boundaries as the loci of maximum gradient magnitude, independent of defect shape or amplitude profile. This approach does not rely on amplitude thresholds, predefined shapes, or machine learning classification, but instead derives defect geometry directly from the spatial derivatives of the ultrasonic response, making it universally applicable to any defect morphology and any composite architecture. The resulting method achieves an average diameter measurement error of 0.11 millimeters across a range of defect sizes from 1.59 millimeters to 12.59 millimeters, representing a threefold improvement over the best-reported results in the literature and enabling the first reliable quantification of defects at the scale of individual fiber tows.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and system for the high-precision, non-destructive measurement of foreign object defects within laminated composite materials, particularly woven carbon fiber reinforced polymers, using pulse-echo ultrasonic imaging and advanced digital signal processing. The invention enables the automated determination of defect dimensions—including area, perimeter, and effective diameter—with sub-millimeter accuracy, even for defects smaller than two millimeters in size and located at arbitrary depths within the laminate. Unlike prior techniques that rely on amplitude thresholds, manual interpretation, or shape assumptions, the invention employs a sequence of computationally robust, physics-based operations that extract defect boundaries directly from the spatial gradient of the ultrasonic signal, thereby eliminating subjectivity and ensuring consistency across operators and inspection environments.

The method begins with the acquisition of ultrasonic C-scan data from a single side of the composite component using a spherically focused transducer operating at a frequency of at least 7.5 megahertz. The raw ultrasonic data, comprising time-domain waveforms sampled at discrete spatial locations across a defined inspection area, is first processed to correct for minor variations in surface topography and transducer alignment. This is accomplished by identifying the front wall echo at each spatial coordinate, fitting a third-order polynomial surface to the time coordinates of these echoes, and then shifting the entire dataset such that the front wall echo is aligned to a common reference time across all locations. This temporal alignment ensures that subsequent signal analysis is performed on a consistent time basis, eliminating artifacts arising from uneven surfaces or imperfect leveling.

Following alignment, the ultrasonic data is gated to isolate the region of interest corresponding to the interface where the foreign object is expected to reside. The gate is defined as a time window spanning three-quarters of the average peak-to-peak distance between adjacent laminae, as determined from an averaged A-scan across the entire inspection area. Within this gate, the maximum amplitude at each spatial location is extracted to form a two-dimensional amplitude map, known as the maximum gated amplitude (MGA) image. This MGA image represents the intensity of the ultrasonic reflection at the defect interface and serves as the primary input for subsequent processing.

To enhance the clarity of defect boundaries and suppress noise inherent in woven composite structures, the MGA image is convolved with a two-dimensional Gaussian filter whose standard deviation is selected to be five-thirds of the spatial sampling interval. This filtering operation smooths spurious intensity fluctuations while preserving the overall structure of the defect, thereby reducing the likelihood of false edge detection due to structural noise. The filtered image is then subjected to a Fourier-based interpolation process that increases the spatial sampling density by a factor of five in both lateral dimensions. This interpolation is performed using the fast Fourier transform method, which ensures high fidelity in reconstructing the signal between sampled points without introducing aliasing or oscillatory artifacts, thereby effectively enhancing the apparent resolution of the system beyond the physical limitations of the transducer and scanning step size.

The interpolated image is then processed to compute the magnitude of the spatial gradient at every pixel location. This gradient magnitude is calculated using a fourth-order central difference scheme to approximate the partial derivatives in both lateral directions, ensuring high numerical accuracy. The resulting gradient magnitude image highlights regions of rapid intensity change, which correspond precisely to the boundaries between the foreign object and the surrounding composite matrix. Unlike amplitude-based methods, this gradient-based approach is insensitive to absolute signal strength, making it robust to variations in transducer coupling, material density, or defect depth.

The final step in the method involves the automated identification of the defect boundary by tracing the loci of maximum gradient magnitude along the periphery of the defect region. This is accomplished by selecting an initial point within the interior of the defect on the gradient magnitude image and projecting a line in the direction of the maximum gradient vector at that point. The point of maximum gradient magnitude along this projection is recorded as a boundary point. This process is repeated iteratively around the entire perimeter of the defect, with each new point selected at a uniform angular increment relative to the previous point, ensuring complete coverage of the boundary. In regions where multiple local maxima occur due to limited signal resolution, the centroid of the cluster of maxima is used to define the boundary point. The resulting set of boundary points forms a closed contour that accurately represents the true shape of the defect, regardless of whether it is circular, elliptical, irregular, or fragmented.

This contour is then post-processed to compute the defect’s area, perimeter, and effective diameter—the diameter of a circle having the same area as the defect. The effective diameter is calculated using the formula derived from the area of a circle, and serves as the primary metric for comparing defect sizes across different inspection systems and studies. The entire process is fully automated, requiring no manual threshold selection, shape assumption, or operator intervention beyond the initial setup of the scanning parameters and the definition of the inspection region.

The invention further includes a system for implementing the method, comprising an ultrasonic pulse-echo transducer mounted on a precision two-axis translation stage, a data acquisition unit capable of sampling at a rate of at least 160 megahertz, a computer system running custom software for executing the temporal alignment, filtering, interpolation, gradient computation, and boundary tracing algorithms, and a user interface for defining inspection parameters and displaying results. The system is configured to operate in immersion mode, with the composite sample submerged in a water bath to ensure consistent acoustic coupling, and is calibrated using reference standards of known dimensions to validate measurement accuracy.

The invention achieves an average absolute error in defect diameter measurement of 0.11 millimeters across a range of defect sizes from 1.59 millimeters to 12.59 millimeters, with a maximum error of 0.32 millimeters. This performance represents a substantial improvement over all previously published methods, which have demonstrated errors ranging from 0.175 millimeters to over 2 millimeters for comparable defect sizes. Importantly, the method is effective for defects located at any depth within the laminate, with no significant degradation in accuracy observed for defects situated between the sixth and seventh or ninth and tenth laminae. A slight increase in error is observed for defects near the scanning surface, which is attributed to higher signal amplitude and potential saturation effects, but this can be mitigated by adjusting the transducer gain during scan setup.

The invention is particularly valuable for applications in aerospace manufacturing, where the presence of sub-millimeter foreign inclusions can compromise the integrity of primary structures, and in maintenance operations, where accurate sizing of defects informs repair decisions and service life predictions. By providing a fully automated, shape-independent, and highly accurate sizing method, the invention enables the transition from qualitative inspection to quantitative defect characterization, thereby supporting the development of data-driven quality assurance protocols and more reliable structural health monitoring systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The detailed description of the invention encompasses the complete sequence of operations, hardware configurations, algorithmic implementations, and performance validations required to execute the method for high-precision defect sizing in composite laminates. The invention is implemented as an integrated system comprising mechanical, electronic, and computational components that work in concert to acquire, process, and analyze ultrasonic data. The method is designed to be applied to any laminated composite structure where foreign object inclusions are suspected, but is particularly optimized for woven carbon fiber reinforced polymer composites due to the unique challenges posed by their heterogeneous microstructure.

The ultrasonic inspection system is composed of a spherically focused piezoelectric transducer with a nominal center frequency of 7.5 megahertz, a diameter of 9.53 millimeters, and a focal length of 38.1 millimeters. The transducer is mounted on a dual-axis precision translation stage capable of moving in the x₁ and x₂ directions with a step resolution of 0.005 millimeters per step. The translation stages are driven by stepper motors controlled by a custom LabVIEW program that synchronizes movement with data acquisition. The transducer is coupled to the composite sample via an immersion tank filled with deionized water, which serves as the acoustic coupling medium. The sample is submerged to a depth sufficient to ensure full contact between the transducer’s focal zone and the surface of the composite, and the sample is leveled using a three-point adjustable platform to ensure that the transducer remains normal to the surface across the entire scan area. A linear voltage displacement transducer (LVDT) is mounted in the z-direction to monitor the vertical position of the transducer and provide feedback for depth control, ensuring that the focal point remains fixed at the midplane of the laminate throughout the scan.

Data acquisition is performed using a pulser/receiver unit capable of generating a 65-nanosecond negative square wave pulse at 200 volts and sampling the returned echo at a rate of 160 megahertz. The sampling rate is selected to ensure that the axial resolution—the ability to distinguish between two reflectors along the direction of wave propagation—is sufficient to resolve the thickness of the foreign object, which is typically on the order of 0.05 millimeters. Given the speed of sound in the composite material is approximately 2890 meters per second, the wavelength of the 7.5 megahertz signal is approximately 0.385 millimeters, and the axial resolution is determined by half the wavelength, or approximately 0.19 millimeters. The transducer’s focal length is selected to ensure that the beam width at the midplane of the laminate is approximately 0.3 millimeters, which defines the lateral resolution of the system. The spatial sampling interval of 0.2 millimeters is chosen to satisfy the Nyquist criterion for this resolution, ensuring that the spatial frequency content of the signal is adequately sampled.

The inspection area is defined as a rectangular region measuring 38.1 millimeters by 38.1 millimeters, which is sufficient to encompass any defect of interest while minimizing scan time and data volume. The scan begins by locating the bottom-left corner of the sample by moving the transducer beyond the physical edge of the sample in both lateral directions and recording the position at which the front wall echo first appears. This position is used as the origin for the scan grid, and all subsequent data points are referenced to this origin. The sample is scanned in a raster pattern, with the transducer stepping in the x₁ direction across the width of the region and then advancing in the x₂ direction to the next row. At each spatial location, a full A-scan waveform is recorded, consisting of 1024 time samples spaced at 6.25 nanosecond intervals. This results in a total dataset of approximately 36,000 A-scans per inspection, each containing 1024 data points, for a total of over 36 million data points per scan.

The first step in the data processing pipeline is temporal alignment of the ultrasonic waveforms to correct for minor variations in surface height or transducer tilt. This is necessary because even slight deviations in the sample’s flatness or the transducer’s perpendicularity can cause the front wall echo to occur at different times across the scan area, leading to misalignment in the C-scan image. To perform this correction, the system first identifies the earliest time sample at each spatial location (x₁, x₂) that exceeds a predefined detection threshold, typically set to 10 percent of the maximum amplitude observed in the front wall echo. The first peak following this threshold crossing is then identified as the front wall echo time for that location. These front wall echo times are collected into a two-dimensional matrix, and a third-order polynomial surface is fitted to this matrix using a least-squares regression algorithm. The polynomial surface models the expected variation in front wall echo time as a function of lateral position, accounting for any curvature or tilt in the sample surface. Once the surface is fitted, the time value at each spatial location is adjusted by subtracting the predicted front wall echo time from the actual measured time, effectively shifting all waveforms such that the front wall echo occurs at a common reference time t₀ across the entire dataset. The resulting time-shifted waveforms are denoted as F(x₁, x₂, t̃), where t̃ = t − t₀ represents the relative time after alignment.

The next step involves defining the gate for defect detection. The gate is a time window within which the ultrasonic reflection from the defect interface is expected to occur. To determine the appropriate gate boundaries, an averaged A-scan is computed by taking the mean of all 36,000 waveforms across the scan area. This averaged waveform exhibits clear peaks corresponding to the front wall, back wall, and the reflections from each lamina interface. The peak corresponding to the lamina immediately preceding the defect interface is identified, and the gate is defined to extend from this peak to a point three-quarters of the way to the next peak, which corresponds to the lamina following the defect interface. This three-quarter peak-to-peak distance is selected to ensure that the gate captures the full extent of the defect reflection while excluding reflections from adjacent laminae that may interfere with the signal. The duration of the gate is typically between 0.3 and 0.5 microseconds, depending on the laminate thickness and material properties.

Within this gated time window, the maximum amplitude at each spatial location is extracted to form the maximum gated amplitude (MGA) image. This is accomplished by scanning through the time dimension of the time-shifted waveform at each (x₁, x₂) location and selecting the maximum value of the waveform within the gate. Unlike prior methods that use the absolute value of the waveform to compute the MGA, the present invention retains the signed amplitude of the signal, as this preserves the phase information and prevents the introduction of artificial symmetry in the image that can obscure true defect boundaries. The resulting MGA image is a two-dimensional array of amplitude values, where each pixel represents the maximum ultrasonic reflection intensity at that location during the gate period. This image is the primary representation of the defect’s spatial extent, but it is typically corrupted by noise due to the complex scattering environment of the woven composite.

To enhance the quality of the MGA image, a two-dimensional Gaussian filter is applied. The Gaussian filter is defined by the equation:

P(x₁, x₂, μ₁, μ₂, σ₁, σ₂) = (1 / (2πσ₁σ₂)) × exp(−((x₁ − μ₁)² / (2σ₁²))) × exp(−((x₂ − μ₂)² / (2σ₂²)))

where μ₁ and μ₂ are the coordinates of the filter’s center, and σ₁ and σ₂ are the standard deviations in the x₁ and x₂ directions, respectively. For the purposes of this invention, σ₁ and σ₂ are both set to 5/3 of the spatial sampling interval, which is 0.2 millimeters, resulting in σ = 0.333 millimeters. The filter is applied by convolving the MGA image with the Gaussian kernel using a discrete summation over a finite region of 11 × 11 pixels (five steps in each direction), which corresponds to a spatial extent of approximately ±1.1 millimeters around each pixel. This truncation is justified because the Gaussian function decays to less than 0.5 percent of its maximum value beyond five standard deviations, ensuring that the majority of the filter’s energy is retained while minimizing computational cost. The filtered image, denoted as F̃(x₁, x₂, t), is computed numerically using the trapezoidal rule for discrete data, as follows:

F̃(x₁,k, x₂,l, t) ≈ (Δx₁Δx₂ / (2πσ₁σ₂)) × Σₚ₌₋ₘᵐ Σ_q₌₋ₙⁿ exp(−((x₁,k₊ₚ − x₁,k)² / (2σ₁²))) × exp(−((x₂,l₊q − x₂,l)² / (2σ₂²))) × MGA(x₁,k₊ₚ, x₂,l₊q, t)

where Δx₁ and Δx₂ are the spatial sampling intervals in each direction, and m and n are both set to 5. This filtering operation effectively suppresses high-frequency noise while preserving the low-frequency structure of the defect, resulting in a smoother, more visually interpretable image.

Following filtering, the image is interpolated to increase its spatial resolution. Interpolation is performed using the fast Fourier transform (FFT) method, which is known for its accuracy in reconstructing band-limited signals. The MGA image is first interpolated along the x₁ dimension by zero-padding the FFT of the image in the x₁ direction by a factor of five, then applying the inverse FFT to obtain an intermediate image H′(x₁,i, x₂,l), where i now ranges from 1 to 5N₁, and N₁ is the original number of samples in the x₁ direction. The same process is then applied along the x₂ dimension, resulting in a final upsampled image H(x₁,i, x₂,j), where both i and j range from 1 to 5N₂. This fivefold interpolation increases the spatial sampling density from 0.2 millimeters to 0.04 millimeters, effectively reducing the pixel size by a factor of five and enhancing the apparent resolution of the image without requiring a finer physical scan step. This step is critical because it allows the subsequent gradient computation to resolve fine details in the defect boundary that would otherwise be lost due to the coarseness of the original sampling.

The interpolated image H(x₁,i, x₂,j) is then processed to compute the magnitude of the spatial gradient at each pixel. The gradient is a vector quantity that describes the rate and direction of change in signal intensity at each point in the image. Its magnitude, G(x₁,i, x₂,j), is computed as the square root of the sum of the squares of the partial derivatives in the x₁ and x₂ directions:

G(x₁,i, x₂,j) = √[(∂H/∂x₁)² + (∂H/∂x₂)²]

Because the image is discrete, the partial derivatives are approximated using a fourth-order central difference scheme, which provides a high degree of numerical accuracy. The derivative in the x₁ direction is computed as:

∂H/∂x₁ ≈ [−H(x₁,i₊₂, x₂,j) + 8H(x₁,i₊₁, x₂,j) − 8H(x₁,i₋₁, x₂,j) + H(x₁,i₋₂, x₂,j)] / (12h)

and the derivative in the x₂ direction as:

∂H/∂x₂ ≈ [−H(x₁,i, x₂,j₊₂) + 8H(x₁,i, x₂,j₊₁) − 8H(x₁,i, x₂,j₋₁) + H(x₁,i, x₂,j₋₂)] / (12h)

where h is the spatial sampling interval after interpolation, equal to 0.04 millimeters. These equations are evaluated at every pixel in the interpolated image, excluding the boundary pixels where the stencil extends beyond the image domain. The resulting gradient magnitude image highlights regions of rapid intensity change, which correspond to the edges of the defect. Importantly, this image is not dependent on the absolute amplitude of the signal, but rather on its spatial variation, making it robust to variations in material properties, coupling conditions, and defect depth.

The final step in the method is the automated extraction of the defect boundary from the gradient magnitude image. This is accomplished by a boundary tracing algorithm that begins at an arbitrary point within the interior of the defect region. The point is selected by the user by clicking on a region of high gradient magnitude that lies within the suspected defect area, but the algorithm is insensitive to the exact location of this point, as long as it is within the interior of the defect. From this starting point, a radial projection is made in the direction of the maximum gradient vector at that location. The maximum gradient magnitude along this projection line is identified and recorded as a boundary point. The algorithm then rotates the projection direction by a fixed angular increment—typically 5 degrees—and repeats the process, moving around the defect perimeter in a circular fashion until a full 360-degree sweep is completed. This results in a set of boundary points that define the contour of the defect.

In cases where multiple local maxima occur along a projection due to noise or limited resolution, the algorithm identifies the cluster of maxima and computes the centroid of these points as the representative boundary point. This prevents the algorithm from being misled by spurious peaks and ensures that the boundary remains smooth and continuous. The resulting set of boundary points is then connected using linear interpolation to form a closed polygon, and the area enclosed by this polygon is computed using the shoelace formula. The effective diameter of the defect is then calculated as:

d̄ = 2 × √(A / π)

where A is the computed area. This effective diameter serves as the primary metric for comparing defect sizes across different inspection systems and is directly comparable to the diameters reported in prior literature.

The entire process—from data acquisition to boundary extraction—is fully automated and executed in real time on a standard desktop computer equipped with a multi-core processor and sufficient memory to handle the large datasets generated during scanning. The system is controlled by custom software written in MATLAB and LabVIEW, which interfaces with the hardware components and displays the results in real time. The software includes a graphical user interface that allows the operator to define the scan area, set the gate duration, adjust the filter parameters, and view the gradient magnitude image and boundary trace. The output is presented as a table of defect dimensions, including area, perimeter, and effective diameter, along with a visual overlay of the boundary on the original MGA image.

Validation of the method was performed using twelve composite samples, each containing a single foreign object made of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) with known dimensions measured using a high-resolution 3D optical microscope. The true dimensions of the defects were determined using a Keyence VR-3000 microscope, which employs sub-pixel edge detection to measure the diameter of circular features with an uncertainty of less than 0.003 millimeters. The defects ranged in nominal diameter from 1.59 millimeters to 12.7 millimeters, with actual measured diameters ranging from 1.55 ± 0.05 millimeters to 12.59 ± 0.07 millimeters. The ultrasonic inspection system was used to scan each sample, and the effective diameter was computed using the method described above. The absolute error in diameter measurement was calculated as the difference between the measured ultrasonic diameter and the true diameter from the microscope. The results showed an average absolute error of 0.11 millimeters, with a maximum error of 0.32 millimeters. The error was found to be independent of defect depth for defects located between the sixth and seventh and ninth and tenth laminae, with average errors of 0.10 and 0.12 millimeters, respectively. A slightly higher average error of 0.16 millimeters was observed for defects located between the third and fourth laminae, which is attributed to the higher signal amplitude and potential saturation effects near the scanning surface. This effect can be mitigated by reducing the transducer gain during scanning, a feature that is incorporated into the system’s calibration procedure.

The method was also compared against the 6 dB drop technique, which is the industry standard for ultrasonic defect sizing. When applied to the same dataset, the 6 dB drop technique yielded an average error of 0.48 millimeters, more than four times greater than the gradient-based method. Furthermore, the 6 dB technique consistently overestimated the defect size, particularly for smaller defects, due to its reliance on amplitude thresholds that are sensitive to signal attenuation and noise. In contrast, the gradient-based method provided accurate sizing regardless of defect size, shape, or depth, demonstrating its superiority as a quantitative measurement tool.

The invention has been tested on a variety of composite architectures, including unidirectional, braided, and woven laminates, and has been shown to be effective in all cases. The method is not limited to PTFE inclusions and has been successfully applied to other materials, including paper, peel ply, and release tape, with comparable accuracy. The algorithm’s shape-independence makes it particularly suitable for irregularly shaped defects, which are common in manufacturing but have historically been difficult to quantify. Future extensions of the invention include integration with robotic inspection systems, real-time defect classification using machine learning, and automated reporting for compliance with aerospace quality standards such as AS9100 and ISO 9001.

The invention represents a fundamental advancement in the field of non-destructive evaluation of composite materials, providing the first method capable of reliably and accurately sizing sub-millimeter foreign object defects in woven composites without requiring assumptions about defect shape or manual intervention. By combining temporal alignment, Gaussian filtering, Fourier interpolation, and gradient-based edge detection into a single, cohesive framework, the invention transforms ultrasonic inspection from a qualitative, operator-dependent technique into a quantitative, automated measurement system that meets the rigorous demands of modern manufacturing and maintenance environments.
