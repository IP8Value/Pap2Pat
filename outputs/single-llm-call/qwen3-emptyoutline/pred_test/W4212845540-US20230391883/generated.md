# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of immunotherapy and more specifically to monoclonal antibodies targeting the T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), and their use in the treatment of cancer and other immune-mediated diseases. The invention encompasses novel anti-TIGIT antibodies with intact Fc effector functions, including the ability to bind to Fc gamma receptors (FcγRs), and their use in mediating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), trogocytosis, and immune cell activation. The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising such antibodies, methods of their production, and diagnostic and therapeutic applications thereof, particularly in combination with other immune checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-1 antibodies. The antibodies described herein are distinguished by their ability to selectively deplete regulatory T cells (Tregs) within the tumor microenvironment while sparing effector T cells, to activate natural killer (NK) cells and myeloid antigen-presenting cells, and to reduce surface expression of TIGIT on T cells via FcγR-mediated trogocytosis, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immunity in a manner that is critically dependent on an intact, functional Fc domain.

## BACKGROUND

The immune system possesses intrinsic mechanisms to maintain self-tolerance and prevent excessive inflammation, but these same mechanisms can be co-opted by tumors to evade immune surveillance. Among the most prominent of these immune evasion strategies is the upregulation of inhibitory checkpoint receptors on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), which dampen T-cell and NK-cell activation and promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. One such receptor, TIGIT, has emerged as a critical mediator of immune suppression in multiple malignancies. Expressed on activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs), TIGIT binds with high affinity to its ligands PVR (CD155) and PVRL2 (CD112), which are frequently overexpressed on tumor cells and antigen-presenting cells within the tumor microenvironment. Engagement of TIGIT with its ligands delivers direct inhibitory signals to T and NK cells, competes with the activating receptor DNAM-1 for shared ligands, enhances the stability and suppressive function of Tregs, and promotes the development of tolerogenic dendritic cells. The co-expression of TIGIT with other inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 on exhausted T cells further underscores its role as a central node in the network of immune checkpoint pathways that limit anti-tumor immunity.

Preclinical and clinical evidence has demonstrated that high levels of TIGIT expression on TILs correlate with poor prognosis in multiple cancers, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, and colorectal cancer. Notably, TIGIT expression is significantly higher on tumor-infiltrating Tregs than on conventional T cells, suggesting a preferential role in maintaining immunosuppressive niches within tumors. Furthermore, the ratio of TIGIT to DNAM-1 on Tregs has been shown to predict resistance to anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 therapies, indicating that TIGIT may serve as a key mechanism of adaptive resistance to existing checkpoint blockade regimens. These findings have spurred the development of multiple monoclonal antibodies targeting TIGIT, several of which are currently under clinical investigation. However, a critical and unresolved question has persisted across the field: whether the Fc effector function of anti-TIGIT antibodies is necessary for optimal anti-tumor efficacy.

The Fc region of IgG antibodies mediates interactions with Fc gamma receptors expressed on innate immune cells, including NK cells, macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells. These interactions can trigger a range of effector functions, including ADCC, ADCP, complement-dependent cytotoxicity, and FcγR-mediated modulation of cell surface receptor expression through trogocytosis. While some anti-TIGIT antibodies in clinical development have been engineered with Fc-silent or Fc-reduced formats—such as IgG4 isotypes or IgG1 variants with mutations that abrogate FcγR binding—others retain wild-type IgG1 Fc domains designed to preserve full effector functionality. The rationale for Fc silencing has been based on the assumption that TIGIT blockade alone, through competitive inhibition of ligand binding, is sufficient to restore T-cell and NK-cell function, and that Fc-mediated effector functions may pose unnecessary risks of off-target depletion of T cells or systemic immune activation. However, the clinical outcomes of these different Fc formats remain incompletely understood, and preclinical data have been inconsistent.

Prior studies on other immune checkpoint targets, such as CTLA-4, OX40, and GITR, have demonstrated that Fc-dependent depletion of intratumoral regulatory T cells is essential for maximal therapeutic efficacy. In particular, the IgG1 isotype of ipilimumab, an anti-CTLA-4 antibody, has been associated with clinical benefit in melanoma patients, and this benefit has been correlated with FcγR polymorphisms that enhance effector cell function. These observations suggest that the Fc domain may not be a passive scaffold but an active determinant of therapeutic outcome. Despite this, no systematic comparison of Fc-competent versus Fc-silent anti-TIGIT antibodies had been conducted to determine whether effector functions contribute meaningfully to anti-tumor activity, nor had the mechanisms by which Fc engagement might modulate the tumor microenvironment been elucidated. The absence of such data has left the field without a clear rationale for selecting an optimal Fc format for clinical development.

Moreover, the potential for anti-TIGIT antibodies to induce trogocytosis—the FcγR-mediated transfer of target antigens from one cell to another—had not been previously described for any TIGIT-targeting agent. Trogocytosis has been documented for other therapeutic antibodies, such as rituximab, trastuzumab, and daratumumab, and has been shown to reduce target antigen density on tumor cells, alter immune synapse formation, and modulate immune cell activation. Whether such a mechanism could occur with anti-TIGIT antibodies, particularly in the context of TIGIT’s high expression on Tregs and its structural role in disrupting DNAM-1 homodimerization, remained entirely unexplored. Similarly, the ability of anti-TIGIT antibodies to activate NK cells and myeloid cells via FcγR co-engagement had not been rigorously evaluated, despite the known expression of both TIGIT and FcγRIIIA on NK cells and the critical role of myeloid cells in shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

In light of these gaps, there exists a pressing need in the art for a comprehensive understanding of how Fc effector functions contribute to the mechanism of action of anti-TIGIT antibodies. There is a particular need for antibodies that not only block the TIGIT-PVR/PVRL2 interaction but also actively remodel the tumor immune landscape through selective depletion of immunosuppressive Tregs, activation of cytotoxic NK cells and antigen-presenting myeloid cells, and removal of TIGIT from the surface of T cells via trogocytosis. Such antibodies would represent a significant advancement over those that rely solely on ligand blockade, particularly in patients whose tumors exhibit high TIGIT expression on Tregs and abundant FcγR-expressing innate immune infiltrates. The present invention fulfills this need by providing a novel class of anti-TIGIT monoclonal antibodies with intact Fc effector functions that demonstrate superior anti-tumor activity in preclinical models through multiple synergistic, Fc-dependent mechanisms, and by establishing the critical importance of Fc functionality for clinical efficacy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of anti-TIGIT monoclonal antibodies that possess an intact, functional Fc domain capable of binding to Fc gamma receptors (FcγRs), thereby mediating a suite of immune-modulatory effector functions that are essential for optimal anti-tumor activity. These antibodies are distinguished from prior art by their ability to selectively deplete regulatory T cells (Tregs) within the tumor microenvironment through antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), to activate natural killer (NK) cells and myeloid antigen-presenting cells via FcγR co-engagement, and to reduce surface expression of TIGIT on T cells through FcγR-mediated trogocytosis. Unlike prior anti-TIGIT antibodies that utilize Fc-silent or Fc-reduced formats, the antibodies of the present invention require an intact Fc domain to elicit their full spectrum of biological activities, and their therapeutic efficacy is demonstrably superior to that of Fc-silent counterparts in both in vitro and in vivo models of cancer.

The invention encompasses a monoclonal antibody or antigen-binding fragment thereof that specifically binds to human TIGIT, wherein the antibody comprises a heavy chain variable region and a light chain variable region that confer high-affinity binding to TIGIT, and wherein the Fc region of the antibody is capable of binding to at least one FcγR selected from the group consisting of FcγRI, FcγRIIA, FcγRIIB, and FcγRIIIA. In a preferred embodiment, the Fc region is derived from an IgG1 isotype and retains wild-type amino acid residues at positions critical for FcγR binding, including but not limited to positions 233, 234, 235, 236, 237, 297, 327, 329, 330, and 331 of the EU numbering system. In a more preferred embodiment, the Fc region does not comprise mutations that abrogate binding to FcγRs, such as the L234A/L235A (LALA), P329G, or E345R substitutions, and does not comprise an IgG4 isotype or an IgG1 isotype with an S228P mutation.

The invention further provides a pair of anti-TIGIT antibodies that share identical variable regions but differ solely in their Fc domains: one with an Fc region capable of binding FcγRs (Fc-competent), and one with an Fc region engineered to lack binding to all FcγRs (Fc-silent). Comparative studies demonstrate that the Fc-competent antibody induces potent ADCC against TIGIT-expressing Tregs in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells derived from cancer patients, whereas the Fc-silent antibody fails to do so. The Fc-competent antibody also activates NK cells, as evidenced by increased expression of CD107a and perforin, and enhances the expression of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD86 on monocytes and dendritic cells, whereas the Fc-silent antibody exhibits minimal or no activity in these assays. Furthermore, the Fc-competent antibody induces trogocytosis of TIGIT from the surface of T cells onto monocytes and dendritic cells, resulting in a measurable reduction in surface TIGIT expression, whereas the Fc-silent antibody does not induce this phenomenon. In vivo, the Fc-competent antibody significantly inhibits tumor growth in multiple syngeneic mouse models, including CT26.WT, MC38, and Renca, whereas the Fc-silent antibody demonstrates no significant anti-tumor activity. The anti-tumor efficacy of the Fc-competent antibody is associated with a reduction in intratumoral Treg frequency, an increase in DNAM-1 expression on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, and a decrease in surface TIGIT expression on T cells and NK cells, effects that are absent following administration of the Fc-silent antibody.

The invention further provides a method of treating a subject with cancer comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of the Fc-competent anti-TIGIT antibody as described herein. In a preferred embodiment, the cancer is selected from the group consisting of melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, renal cell carcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, ovarian cancer, and triple-negative breast cancer. In another preferred embodiment, the subject has been previously treated with an anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 antibody and has developed resistance or suboptimal response. In yet another preferred embodiment, the antibody is administered in combination with an anti-PD-1 antibody, wherein the combination results in synergistic enhancement of T-cell activation and tumor growth inhibition, as demonstrated by increased interferon-gamma secretion and reduced tumor burden in preclinical models.

The invention further provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising the Fc-competent anti-TIGIT antibody in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, wherein the composition is formulated for intravenous, subcutaneous, or intraperitoneal administration. The composition may further comprise an anti-PD-1 antibody, and may be provided in a kit comprising separate containers for each antibody, along with instructions for co-administration. The invention further provides diagnostic methods for identifying subjects likely to respond to treatment with the Fc-competent anti-TIGIT antibody, comprising detecting elevated expression of TIGIT on regulatory T cells in a tumor biopsy or peripheral blood sample, and/or detecting the presence of FcγR polymorphisms associated with enhanced effector function, such as the FcγRIIIA-V158 allele.

The invention further provides a method of producing the Fc-competent anti-TIGIT antibody, comprising transfecting a host cell with a nucleic acid encoding the heavy and light chain variable regions of the antibody and a nucleic acid encoding an Fc region derived from an IgG1 isotype lacking mutations that impair FcγR binding, culturing the host cell under conditions that permit expression of the antibody, and purifying the antibody from the culture supernatant. The host cell may be a mammalian cell line, including but not limited to CHO, NS0, or HEK293 cells. The invention further provides a method of detecting the presence of TIGIT on immune cells in a biological sample, comprising contacting the sample with the Fc-competent anti-TIGIT antibody and detecting binding via flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, or surface plasmon resonance.

The invention is based on the critical discovery that the therapeutic efficacy of anti-TIGIT antibodies is not solely dependent on ligand blockade but is profoundly enhanced by Fc-mediated effector functions. The Fc-competent antibody achieves superior anti-tumor activity through three interrelated mechanisms: first, by selectively depleting TIGIT-high regulatory T cells via ADCC and ADCP, thereby relieving immunosuppression; second, by activating NK cells and myeloid cells through FcγR co-engagement, enhancing cytotoxicity and antigen presentation; and third, by inducing trogocytosis of TIGIT from T cells, which not only reduces inhibitory signaling but may also restore DNAM-1 function by preventing its disruption by TIGIT. These mechanisms operate synergistically and are absent in Fc-silent versions of the same antibody. The invention thus provides a paradigm-shifting approach to TIGIT-targeted immunotherapy, demonstrating that an intact Fc domain is not merely beneficial but essential for optimal clinical outcome, and that the therapeutic potential of anti-TIGIT antibodies is maximized when they are engineered to engage the innate immune system rather than to avoid it.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “antibody” refers to a glycoprotein comprising at least two heavy (H) chains and two light (L) chains inter-connected by disulfide bonds, or an antigen-binding fragment thereof, which retains the ability to specifically bind to a target antigen. The term encompasses full-length immunoglobulins, including IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE, and IgD isotypes, as well as fragments such as Fab, Fab’, F(ab’)2, Fv, scFv, and diabodies, provided that the fragment retains the ability to bind to TIGIT with high affinity and, where applicable, to mediate Fc-dependent effector functions. In the context of this invention, the term “antibody” primarily refers to an IgG1 molecule with an intact Fc domain capable of binding Fc gamma receptors.

The term “antigen-binding fragment” refers to a portion of an antibody that retains the ability to bind to the target antigen, such as TIGIT. Such fragments include, but are not limited to, Fab, Fab’, F(ab’)2, Fv, single-chain Fv (scFv), and minibodies. In certain embodiments, the antigen-binding fragment is a Fab or F(ab’)2 fragment that retains the ability to bind TIGIT but lacks the Fc region entirely. In other embodiments, the antigen-binding fragment is a bispecific molecule that includes one arm targeting TIGIT and another arm targeting a second antigen, such as PD-1 or CD3. However, for the purposes of the present invention, the term “antibody” specifically refers to a full-length IgG molecule with an intact Fc domain unless otherwise specified.

The term “TIGIT” refers to the T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains, also known as VSIG9, VSTM3, or WUCAM, a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily that functions as an inhibitory receptor on T cells, NK cells, and regulatory T cells. Human TIGIT is a 270-amino acid transmembrane protein comprising an extracellular domain with one IgV-like and one IgC-like domain, a transmembrane region, and an intracellular tail containing an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) and an immunoglobulin tail tyrosine (ITT)-like motif. The extracellular domain of TIGIT binds with high affinity to the ligands PVR (CD155) and PVRL2 (CD112), which are expressed on tumor cells, dendritic cells, and other antigen-presenting cells. The term “TIGIT” encompasses all naturally occurring human isoforms, including splice variants, as well as orthologs in non-human species such as mouse, rat, rabbit, and non-human primate, provided that the antibody cross-reacts with human TIGIT and retains the functional properties described herein.

The term “high affinity” refers to an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) for binding to human TIGIT of less than 1 nM, as determined by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or other quantitative binding assays. In preferred embodiments, the KD is less than 0.5 nM, more preferably less than 0.2 nM, and most preferably less than 0.1 nM. The term “specific binding” refers to the ability of the antibody to bind to TIGIT with substantially greater affinity than to other unrelated proteins, as demonstrated by binding assays in which the antibody shows negligible binding to control antigens such as human serum albumin, IgG, or other immune checkpoint molecules including PD-1, CTLA-4, TIM-3, and LAG-3.

The term “Fc region” refers to the carboxy-terminal portion of the heavy chain of an immunoglobulin molecule, which includes the CH2 and CH3 domains and, in some contexts, the hinge region. The Fc region is responsible for mediating interactions with Fc gamma receptors (FcγRs), complement component C1q, and the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). For the purposes of this invention, the term “Fc region” refers to the portion of the antibody that is capable of binding to at least one FcγR, including FcγRI (CD64), FcγRIIA (CD32a), FcγRIIB (CD32b), and FcγRIIIA (CD16a). The term “intact Fc region” refers to an Fc domain that has not been engineered to abrogate binding to FcγRs and retains the wild-type amino acid sequence at positions critical for FcγR interaction, including but not limited to Leu234, Leu235, Asp265, Asn297, Pro329, Lys330, and Asp331 of the EU numbering system. The term “Fc-silent” refers to an Fc region that has been modified by amino acid substitutions, deletions, or isotype switching to eliminate or substantially reduce binding to all FcγRs, including but not limited to mutations such as L234A/L235A (LALA), P329G, E345R, or the use of an IgG4 isotype with or without the S228P mutation.

The term “FcγR” refers to a family of cell surface receptors that bind the Fc region of IgG antibodies and include FcγRI (CD64), FcγRIIA (CD32a), FcγRIIB (CD32b), FcγRIIIA (CD16a), and FcγRIIIB (CD16b). FcγRI is a high-affinity receptor that binds monomeric IgG, while FcγRIIA, FcγRIIB, and FcγRIIIA are low-affinity receptors that bind immune complexes. FcγRIIA and FcγRIIIA contain immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) and mediate activating signals, whereas FcγRIIB contains an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) and mediates inhibitory signals. In the context of this invention, the term “FcγR” refers to the activating receptors FcγRI, FcγRIIA, and FcγRIIIA, which are expressed on NK cells, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells and are responsible for mediating ADCC, ADCP, and trogocytosis. The term “FcγR binding” refers to the ability of the antibody’s Fc region to bind to one or more of these receptors with sufficient affinity to trigger downstream cellular responses, as measured by binding assays such as SPR, flow cytometry, or cell-based reporter assays.

The term “antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity” (ADCC) refers to a mechanism of cell-mediated immune defense whereby effector cells, such as natural killer (NK) cells, recognize and lyse target cells that have been opsonized with antibodies. ADCC is mediated by the binding of the Fc region of the antibody to FcγRIIIA (CD16a) on NK cells, leading to degranulation, release of perforin and granzymes, and target cell apoptosis. The term “antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis” (ADCP) refers to the process by which phagocytic cells, such as macrophages and monocytes, engulf and destroy antibody-coated target cells via engagement of FcγRI and FcγRIIA. The term “trogocytosis” refers to the FcγR-mediated transfer of membrane fragments, including bound antigen, from one cell to another during cell-cell contact. In the context of this invention, trogocytosis refers to the transfer of TIGIT from the surface of T cells to the surface of FcγR-expressing cells such as monocytes and dendritic cells, resulting in a net reduction of TIGIT expression on T cells.

The term “regulatory T cell” (Treg) refers to a subset of CD4+ T cells that express the transcription factor Foxp3 and function to suppress immune responses and maintain self-tolerance. In the context of cancer, tumor-infiltrating Tregs are a major component of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and are characterized by high expression of TIGIT, CTLA-4, and other inhibitory receptors. The term “effector T cell” refers to CD4+ or CD8+ T cells that have been activated and are capable of producing cytokines such as interferon-gamma and tumor necrosis factor-alpha and of directly killing target cells. The term “natural killer (NK) cell” refers to a type of cytotoxic lymphocyte of the innate immune system that lacks T-cell receptor expression and is capable of killing virus-infected and tumor cells without prior sensitization. The term “myeloid cell” refers to a lineage of immune cells derived from hematopoietic stem cells, including monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and granulocytes, which express FcγRs and play critical roles in antigen presentation and immune regulation.

The term “pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” refers to a non-toxic, inert substance that is suitable for administration to a human or animal subject and that does not interfere with the biological activity of the antibody. Such carriers include, but are not limited to, saline, buffered saline, dextrose, water, glycerol, ethanol, and combinations thereof. The term “pharmaceutical composition” refers to a formulation comprising the antibody and a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, optionally further comprising one or more additional therapeutic agents, such as an anti-PD-1 antibody, a chemotherapeutic agent, or a cytokine.

The term “therapeutically effective amount” refers to the amount of the antibody sufficient to produce a desired therapeutic effect in a subject, such as reduction in tumor burden, prolongation of survival, or enhancement of anti-tumor immune responses, without causing unacceptable toxicity. The term “subject” refers to a human or non-human animal, preferably a human, suffering from or at risk of developing a disease amenable to treatment with the antibody, such as cancer, autoimmune disease, or chronic infection.

The term “synergistic” refers to a combined effect of two or more agents that is greater than the sum of their individual effects. In the context of this invention, synergistic enhancement of T-cell activation or tumor growth inhibition is demonstrated by statistical analysis showing that the combination of the anti-TIGIT antibody and an anti-PD-1 antibody produces a greater biological effect than either agent alone, as measured by interferon-gamma secretion, T-cell proliferation, or tumor volume reduction.

The term “monoclonal antibody” refers to an antibody produced by a single clone of cells, such that all molecules of the antibody are identical in amino acid sequence and binding specificity. The term encompasses antibodies produced by hybridoma technology, recombinant DNA technology, phage display, or other methods that result in homogeneous antibody populations.

The term “humanized antibody” refers to a monoclonal antibody in which the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) are derived from a non-human source, such as a murine antibody, and the framework regions are derived from a human immunoglobulin sequence, thereby reducing immunogenicity in human subjects. The term “fully human antibody” refers to an antibody whose variable regions are derived entirely from human sequences, typically through transgenic mice or phage display libraries.

The term “in vivo” refers to experiments or processes conducted within a living organism, and the term “in vitro” refers to experiments or processes conducted outside a living organism, typically in cell culture or biochemical assays.

The term “surface plasmon resonance” (SPR) refers to a biophysical technique used to measure real-time binding interactions between molecules, such as an antibody and its antigen, using a sensor chip coated with one binding partner and a flowing solution of the other. The term “flow cytometry” refers to a technique for analyzing and sorting cells based on their physical and chemical characteristics using fluorescently labeled antibodies and laser detection. The term “ELISA” refers to enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, a method for detecting and quantifying proteins, such as cytokines, in biological samples.

The term “clinical trial” refers to a research study performed in human subjects to evaluate the safety and efficacy of a medical intervention, such as a monoclonal antibody, and includes phases I, II, and III trials as defined by regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or the European Medicines Agency (EMA).

The term “tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes” (TILs) refers to lymphocytes that have migrated into the tumor tissue and include CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and Tregs.
