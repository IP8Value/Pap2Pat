## FIELD

The present invention resides in the field of synthetic biology, specifically in the area of engineered biological systems for the site-specific incorporation of non-standard amino acids into proteins with enhanced fidelity and selectivity. More particularly, the invention relates to post-translational proofreading mechanisms that exploit the natural N-end rule pathway of protein degradation to discriminate between correctly incorporated non-standard amino acids and misincorporated standard amino acids or structurally similar analogs at the N-terminus of nascent polypeptides. The invention further encompasses the genetic engineering of components of the N-end rule pathway, including the adaptor protein ClpS, to alter its substrate recognition profile such that it stabilizes proteins containing certain non-standard amino acids while promoting the degradation of those containing standard amino acids. This engineered proofreading system is integrated into genomically recoded bacterial hosts that lack endogenous UAG codons and release factor 1, enabling the exclusive use of the UAG codon for non-standard amino acid incorporation. The invention also includes methods for the directed evolution of orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase-tRNA pairs using this proofreading system as a selection pressure, resulting in synthetase variants with dramatically improved specificity for their cognate non-standard amino acids and reduced cross-reactivity with endogenous amino acids or other non-standard analogs. The invention finds utility in the production of high-purity biotherapeutics, the development of synthetic auxotrophy-based biocontainment strategies for industrial bioreactors, the reassignment of codons for expanded genetic codes, and the creation of orthogonal translation systems capable of simultaneously incorporating multiple non-standard amino acids without cross-talk. The field further includes the application of these systems to the study of protein folding, stability, and degradation, as well as to the design of conditional protein switches, biosensors, and programmable cellular circuits that respond to the presence or absence of specific amino acid analogs.

## BACKGROUND

The genetic code, as traditionally understood, consists of 20 canonical amino acids encoded by 61 sense codons and three stop codons, with the UAG codon serving as a termination signal recognized by release factor 1 in most organisms. Over the past two decades, advances in synthetic biology have enabled the expansion of the genetic code through the development of orthogonal translation systems, which consist of engineered aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases and their cognate tRNAs that function independently of the host’s endogenous translation machinery. These systems allow for the site-specific incorporation of non-standard amino acids at UAG codons introduced into target genes, thereby enabling the introduction of novel chemical functionalities into proteins for applications in biochemistry, biophysics, drug discovery, and industrial biotechnology. However, a persistent challenge in this field has been the promiscuity of many orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases, which often misincorporate structurally similar standard amino acids—such as tyrosine, phenylalanine, leucine, or tryptophan—at the UAG codon in the absence of the intended non-standard amino acid. This misincorporation leads to the production of heterogeneous protein populations, complicating downstream purification, reducing functional yield, and undermining the reliability of experiments or industrial processes that depend on precise chemical control. While efforts to improve synthetase specificity have traditionally focused on rational design and directed evolution of the amino acid binding pocket, these approaches are often limited by the difficulty of predicting how mutations will affect substrate discrimination without direct functional readouts. Moreover, many non-standard amino acids bear close structural resemblance to endogenous amino acids, making it exceptionally difficult to evolve synthetases that can distinguish between them based solely on binding affinity. The problem is further exacerbated in industrial settings where non-standard amino acids are synthesized in situ via metabolic pathways, as precursor molecules may be structurally analogous to the desired non-standard amino acid and thus subject to misincorporation. The consequences of such misincorporation are not merely technical; in biocontainment systems that rely on synthetic auxotrophy—where essential proteins are engineered to require a non-standard amino acid for stability—misincorporation of a standard amino acid can lead to the production of non-functional, destabilized proteins that are degraded by the host’s proteolytic machinery, resulting in reduced cell fitness and increased escape frequencies. Escape events, in which cells acquire mutations that allow growth in the absence of the non-standard amino acid, represent a critical safety concern in genetically modified organisms intended for large-scale fermentation or environmental release. Prior attempts to mitigate escape have included the use of toxin-antitoxin systems, metabolic auxotrophies, and engineered nutrient dependencies, but none have achieved the combination of high containment efficacy and robust cellular fitness simultaneously. Furthermore, the natural N-end rule pathway, which targets proteins for degradation based on the identity of their N-terminal residue, has been largely unexploited in the context of orthogonal translation systems, despite the fact that many standard amino acids are known to be N-end destabilizing under certain conditions, while many non-standard amino acids possess bulky, hydrophobic, or sterically hindered side chains that may be recognized as stabilizing. The discovery that the N-end rule could be harnessed to selectively degrade proteins containing misincorporated standard amino acids at the N-terminus, while preserving those containing correctly incorporated non-standard amino acids, represents a paradigm shift in the field. However, the natural N-end rule system, mediated by the adaptor protein ClpS, exhibits limited discrimination between closely related amino acids, particularly among phenylalanine analogs, and cannot be readily adapted to distinguish subtle chemical differences without genetic engineering. Prior attempts to modify ClpS have been limited to single-point mutations in conserved hydrophobic residues, with no systematic exploration of how such mutations alter the recognition profile across a panel of non-standard amino acids. The prior art lacks any disclosure of an engineered ClpS variant that stabilizes a broad range of non-standard amino acids while simultaneously destabilizing all standard amino acids, nor has any prior system combined such a proofreading mechanism with a genomically integrated reporter, a recoded host strain, and a multi-round selection process to evolve orthogonal translation systems with unprecedented specificity. The absence of such a system has prevented the development of biocontainment strategies that are both highly effective and metabolically efficient, and has hindered the scalability of non-standard amino acid-dependent bioproduction processes. The present invention fills this critical gap by providing a complete, integrated platform for post-translational proofreading that enables the selection of orthogonal translation systems with near-perfect fidelity, thereby transforming the reliability, safety, and economic viability of synthetic biology applications.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious system for enhancing the fidelity of non-standard amino acid incorporation in genetically engineered organisms through the use of a post-translational proofreading mechanism that exploits the N-end rule pathway of protein degradation. The system comprises a genetically encoded cassette that includes a ubiquitin cleavase enzyme, which removes an N-terminal ubiquitin domain to expose the residue immediately downstream as the new N-terminus of a reporter protein, and an engineered variant of the ClpS adaptor protein that selectively recognizes and delivers to the ClpAP protease complex proteins bearing standard amino acids at their N-terminus, while sparing those bearing specific non-standard amino acids. This proofreading cassette is operably linked to a reporter construct in which the exposed N-terminal residue is positioned at a site that dictates the stability of a downstream fluorescent protein, such as superfolder green fluorescent protein, such that the fluorescence intensity of the cell correlates directly with the successful incorporation of the intended non-standard amino acid and inversely with misincorporation of standard amino acids. The entire system is implemented in a genomically recoded bacterial host strain that has been engineered to lack all endogenous UAG codons and the release factor 1 gene, thereby ensuring that the UAG codon is exclusively available for suppression by an orthogonal tRNA and that no endogenous termination events interfere with reporter expression. The invention further includes a method for the directed evolution of orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase-tRNA pairs by subjecting libraries of mutant synthetases to iterative rounds of fluorescence-activated cell sorting, wherein cells are first selected for high fluorescence in the presence of the non-standard amino acid, then negatively selected for low fluorescence in its absence, and finally re-selected for high fluorescence under conditions that may include the addition of structurally similar non-standard amino acid analogs to increase stringency. This selection process enriches for synthetase variants that exhibit dramatically reduced misincorporation rates, as confirmed by mass spectrometry and in vitro aminoacylation assays. The invention further encompasses novel engineered variants of the ClpS protein, including but not limited to ClpS V65I, ClpS L32F, ClpS V43I, and ClpS V99A, each of which exhibits altered substrate specificity relative to wild-type ClpS, such that proteins bearing non-standard amino acids with phenyl, indole, or other aromatic side chains are stabilized, while proteins bearing standard amino acids such as tyrosine, phenylalanine, leucine, tryptophan, or isoleucine are targeted for degradation. These engineered ClpS variants are capable of distinguishing between non-standard amino acids differing by a single atom or functional group, such as p-bromophenylalanine and p-iodophenylalanine, or p-azidophenylalanine and p-acetylphenylalanine, based on side-chain volume and polarity. ## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### II. Removable Protecting Groups

The invention encompasses the use of removable protecting groups in the context of non-standard amino acid incorporation to enable conditional protein stability and controlled degradation. In this system, a non-standard amino acid is designed to contain a chemically labile protecting group that masks its N-terminal functionality, rendering it inert to recognition by the N-end rule pathway. Upon exposure to a specific stimulus—such as light, enzymatic cleavage, pH change, or a small molecule inducer—the protecting group is removed, exposing the native side chain of the amino acid and thereby converting it into a substrate for the engineered ClpS adaptor protein. This transformation triggers the targeted degradation of the protein via the ClpAP protease complex, effectively creating a molecular switch that links environmental or chemical cues to protein turnover. The protecting group may be selected from a broad class of chemical moieties known in organic synthesis and bioconjugation chemistry, including but not limited to tert-butyloxycarbonyl, fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl, allyl, trimethylsilyl, benzyl, p-methoxybenzyl, nitroveratryl, o-nitrobenzyl, coumarin, and photocleavable linkers such as those based on o-nitrobenzyl or phenacyl groups. The choice of protecting group is dictated by the desired kinetics of removal, the compatibility with cellular environments, and the specificity of the trigger mechanism. For instance, in applications requiring temporal control, a photocleavable group may be incorporated that is stable under ambient laboratory lighting but rapidly cleaved upon exposure to 365 nm ultraviolet light, allowing for precise spatiotemporal regulation of protein degradation in live cells. In applications requiring chemical induction, a protecting group may be designed that is cleaved by endogenous esterases, phosphatases, or proteases that are either constitutively expressed or induced under specific conditions. In one embodiment, the protecting group is attached via an ester linkage to the α-amino group of the non-standard amino acid, such that intracellular esterase activity removes the group and exposes the free amine, which is then recognized by ClpS as a destabilizing residue if the side chain is tyrosine-like. In another embodiment, the protecting group is attached via a carbamate linkage, which is cleaved by endogenous amidases or under mildly acidic conditions found in endosomes or lysosomes, enabling organelle-specific degradation. In yet another embodiment, the protecting group is a disulfide-linked moiety that is cleaved by the reducing environment of the cytoplasm, allowing for selective degradation only in cells with high glutathione levels. The invention further provides a method for encoding the protected non-standard amino acid at the N-terminus of a target protein via an orthogonal translation system, such that the protein is initially stabilized due to the masked N-terminal residue, but becomes susceptible to degradation upon removal of the protecting group. This strategy enables the construction of synthetic protein circuits that are silent until activated by a specific chemical or physical signal, thereby providing a powerful tool for conditional gene expression, biosensing, and therapeutic control. The invention further provides a method for combining multiple protecting groups on a single protein to create multi-input logic gates, wherein degradation occurs only when two or more stimuli are present simultaneously. For example, a protein may be engineered to contain two non-standard amino acids, each bearing a distinct protecting group that requires a different trigger for removal; degradation only ensues when both triggers are applied, creating an AND gate for protein turnover. The invention further provides a method for using the protecting group strategy in conjunction with the engineered ClpS variants to achieve orthogonal control over multiple proteins within the same cell, wherein each protein is tagged with a different protected non-standard amino acid and each is responsive to a different cleavage condition. This enables the independent regulation of multiple synthetic pathways without cross-talk. The invention further provides a method for the delivery of the protecting group as a small molecule prodrug, wherein the prodrug is administered externally and enters the cell, where it is converted into the active cleaving agent by endogenous enzymes, thereby enabling non-genetic control over protein stability.
