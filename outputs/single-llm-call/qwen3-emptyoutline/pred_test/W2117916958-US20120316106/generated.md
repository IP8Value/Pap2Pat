# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of antimicrobial agents and more specifically to the synergistic inhibition of bacterial protein synthesis through the concurrent binding of two structurally distinct antibiotics to adjacent functional sites on the bacterial ribosome. The invention encompasses the use of lankacidin and lankamycin, or their pharmaceutically acceptable derivatives, as a combination therapy for the treatment of bacterial infections, particularly those caused by Gram-positive pathogens. The invention further extends to the design and synthesis of novel chemical analogs of lankacidin and lankamycin that enhance the synergistic interaction between the two compounds by optimizing their spatial complementarity, binding affinity, and allosteric coupling within the peptidyl transferase center and the nascent peptide exit tunnel of the 50S ribosomal subunit. This discovery represents a novel therapeutic paradigm in antibiotic development, wherein two naturally co-produced compounds, each possessing only modest individual activity, are shown to act in concert to produce a potent, synergistic antibacterial effect through precise, structurally defined interactions with the ribosome. The invention provides a structural and mechanistic foundation for the rational design of next-generation dual-site ribosomal inhibitors that overcome the limitations of single-agent antibiotics, including the rapid emergence of resistance and suboptimal efficacy against multidrug-resistant strains.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The global rise in antimicrobial resistance has rendered many conventional antibiotics ineffective, creating an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that exploit underutilized mechanisms of bacterial inhibition. Historically, antibiotics have been developed and administered as single agents targeting discrete molecular pathways, such as cell wall biosynthesis, DNA replication, or protein synthesis. Among the most clinically significant classes of antibiotics are those that inhibit bacterial protein synthesis by binding to the ribosome, particularly the 50S subunit. These include macrolides, lincosamides, oxazolidinones, phenicols, and pleuromutilins, each of which occupies a distinct binding pocket within the large ribosomal subunit. Despite their widespread use, many of these agents suffer from limited efficacy, poor pharmacokinetics, or the rapid emergence of resistance mutations, particularly in the 23S rRNA or ribosomal proteins that form their binding interfaces.

One notable exception to the paradigm of single-agent ribosomal inhibition is the streptogramin family of antibiotics, which are naturally produced by Streptomyces species as a binary combination of two chemically unrelated compounds: streptogramin A and streptogramin B. Streptogramin A binds to the peptidyl transferase center, directly interfering with peptide bond formation, while streptogramin B binds to the upper portion of the nascent peptide exit tunnel, blocking the progression of the growing polypeptide chain. Although each component exhibits only weak antibacterial activity when used alone, their combined administration results in a synergistic inhibition of protein synthesis that is far greater than the sum of their individual effects. This synergistic interaction has been clinically validated in the formulation Synercid, a semisynthetic mixture of dalfopristin and quinupristin, which is approved for the treatment of complicated skin infections and bacteremia caused by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium. The success of Synercid demonstrates that simultaneous targeting of two adjacent functional sites on the ribosome can yield potent, resistance-evasive antimicrobial activity.

However, despite the proven clinical utility of streptogramins, their structural complexity, poor solubility, and significant side effects have limited their broader adoption. Moreover, no other naturally occurring antibiotic pair exhibiting similar synergistic behavior had been identified or characterized at the molecular level prior to this invention. The existence of such a pair would suggest that nature has evolved multiple independent solutions to the problem of dual-site ribosomal inhibition, and that the underlying structural principles governing this synergy may be generalizable to other antibiotic scaffolds. The identification of such a pair would open new avenues for drug development, particularly in the context of combinatorial antibiotic therapy, where the rational pairing of two low-potency agents can yield a high-efficacy regimen with a reduced likelihood of resistance development.

Lankacidin and lankamycin are two antibiotics produced by the soil bacterium Streptomyces rochei 7434AN4i. Lankacidin is a 17-membered macrocyclic carbocyclic compound featuring a bridged 6-membered lactone ring, while lankamycin is a 14-membered macrolide structurally analogous to erythromycin, bearing two deoxy sugar moieties at its C5 and C3 positions. Both compounds have been known for decades, with lankacidin historically used in veterinary medicine for its antibacterial and antitumor properties, and lankamycin recognized as a weakly active macrolide with limited clinical utility. Their co-production by the same bacterial strain, coupled with evidence of co-regulated biosynthetic pathways, suggested a potential functional relationship, but no biochemical or structural evidence had ever been presented to support the hypothesis that they act cooperatively. Prior studies had focused on each compound individually, and the prevailing assumption was that lankamycin, due to its structural similarity to erythromycin, would bind to the classic macrolide site in the nascent peptide exit tunnel, while lankacidin, based on its competition with chloramphenicol, was presumed to bind near the peptidyl transferase center. However, no investigation had explored whether these two compounds could bind simultaneously, nor had any functional assay been performed to determine whether their combined presence resulted in enhanced antibacterial activity.

Furthermore, attempts to dock erythromycin into the ribosome structure in the presence of lankacidin revealed steric clashes between the desosamine sugar of erythromycin and the macrocyclic core of lankacidin, suggesting that erythromycin and lankacidin were mutually exclusive in their binding. This observation raised a critical paradox: if lankamycin is structurally similar to erythromycin, why would Streptomyces rochei co-produce two compounds that appear to compete for ribosomal binding? The answer to this question lay not in competition, but in cooperation. The present invention resolves this paradox by demonstrating that lankamycin, despite its structural resemblance to erythromycin, binds to a distinct orientation and conformation within the nascent peptide exit tunnel that is fully compatible with lankacidin binding in the peptidyl transferase center. This compatibility is not accidental but the result of evolutionary optimization, wherein the biosynthetic machinery of Streptomyces rochei has fine-tuned the chemical structure of lankamycin to avoid steric interference with lankacidin while maximizing the allosteric coupling between the two binding events. This discovery fundamentally alters the understanding of how natural antibiotic systems function and provides a blueprint for the design of novel synergistic antibiotic pairs that exploit the same structural principles.

The clinical implications of this discovery are profound. Unlike streptogramins, which require complex semisynthetic modifications to achieve clinical utility, lankacidin and lankamycin are naturally occurring compounds with simpler chemical architectures, making them more amenable to structural optimization through semi-synthetic chemistry. Moreover, their synergistic interaction is not merely additive but multiplicative, resulting in a dramatic reduction in the minimal inhibitory concentrations required for bacterial growth inhibition. This synergy is observed both in vitro and in vivo, across multiple bacterial strains, and is mediated by a well-defined structural mechanism that can be precisely manipulated. The invention therefore provides not only a new therapeutic combination but also a new class of drug design principles that can be applied beyond lankacidin and lankamycin to other ribosomal inhibitors, thereby expanding the scope of combinatorial antibiotic therapy to a broad array of bacterial pathogens.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel therapeutic strategy for the inhibition of bacterial protein synthesis through the simultaneous binding of two structurally distinct antibiotics to adjacent functional sites on the bacterial 50S ribosomal subunit. Specifically, the invention discloses that lankacidin and lankamycin, two naturally occurring antibiotics produced by Streptomyces rochei, bind to non-overlapping yet spatially proximal sites on the ribosome—lankacidin at the peptidyl transferase center and lankamycin at the upper segment of the nascent peptide exit tunnel—and that their concurrent binding results in a synergistic inhibition of bacterial growth that is significantly greater than the sum of their individual effects. This synergistic interaction is mediated by a precise structural arrangement in which lankacidin induces conformational changes in the 23S ribosomal RNA that stabilize the binding of lankamycin, while lankamycin, in turn, enhances the occupancy and inhibitory potency of lankacidin through allosteric communication across the ribosomal interface.

The invention further provides a detailed structural characterization of the lankacidin binding site, which is located within the peptidyl transferase center and involves direct interactions with nucleotides U2506, G2505, G2581, C2610, and G2576, as well as indirect contacts with A2059, C2530, U2531, and G2061. The binding of lankacidin induces a unique reorientation of A2602, a nucleotide critical for tRNA translocation, and stabilizes a network of hydrogen bonds and base-stacking interactions that lock the peptidyl transferase center into a catalytically inactive conformation. This binding mode partially overlaps with, but is distinct from, that of streptogramin A antibiotics such as dalfopristin, and is characterized by a shallower penetration into the P-site region, resulting in a different pattern of steric interference with aminoacyl-tRNA positioning.

The invention further provides a detailed structural characterization of the lankamycin binding site, which resides in the upper segment of the nascent peptide exit tunnel and involves interactions with nucleotides U2609, A2058, A2059, and G2505. Unlike erythromycin, which binds in a manner that sterically clashes with lankacidin, lankamycin adopts a distinct orientation in which its C5-linked sugar moiety is positioned away from the macrocyclic core of lankacidin, thereby avoiding steric hindrance and enabling simultaneous binding. The binding of lankamycin induces a conformational shift in the rRNA that enhances the stability of the lankacidin-induced rearrangement, particularly through the stabilization of the G2505-C2610 base pair and the repositioning of A2059 to form a stacking interaction with A2503, thereby reinforcing the overall rigidity of the ribosomal active site.

The invention further demonstrates that lankacidin and lankamycin can bind simultaneously to the ribosome without mutual displacement, as evidenced by RNA probing experiments showing that the protective effects of each compound on specific 23S rRNA residues are additive when both compounds are present. This simultaneous binding is not observed with erythromycin, which competes with lankacidin for ribosomal occupancy, confirming that the structural features of lankamycin have been evolutionarily optimized for synergistic action with lankacidin. The invention further provides biochemical evidence that the combination of lankacidin and lankamycin inhibits bacterial protein synthesis in a synergistic manner, as demonstrated by checkerboard assays in whole-cell cultures and cell-free translation systems, wherein the fractional inhibitory concentration index is consistently below 0.5, indicating strong synergy.

The invention further provides a method for enhancing the synergistic activity of lankacidin and lankamycin through the chemical modification of either or both compounds to increase their binding affinity, improve their pharmacokinetic properties, or introduce additional interactions that strengthen the allosteric coupling between their respective binding sites. Such modifications include the addition of alkyl, aryl, or heterocyclic substituents to the macrocyclic core of lankacidin to enhance van der Waals contacts with G2581 and U2531, the elongation or rigidification of the lankamycin sugar moiety to form hydrogen bonds with U2585 or A2062, and the covalent linkage of lankacidin and lankamycin through a flexible or semi-rigid spacer to create a single molecular entity capable of simultaneously engaging both binding sites with high avidity.

The invention further provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising lankacidin and lankamycin, or their pharmaceutically acceptable salts, esters, or prodrugs, in synergistic ratios, for the treatment of bacterial infections. The invention further provides methods of treating bacterial infections in a subject by administering a therapeutically effective amount of a combination of lankacidin and lankamycin, wherein the combination is formulated for simultaneous delivery in a single dosage form or in separate but co-administered formulations. The invention further provides diagnostic methods for identifying bacterial strains susceptible to lankacidin-lankamycin synergy based on the presence of wild-type 23S rRNA sequences at positions 2506, 2585, 2609, and 2058, and the absence of resistance-conferring mutations in these regions.

The invention further provides synthetic and semi-synthetic methods for the production of lankacidin and lankamycin derivatives with enhanced synergistic activity, including enzymatic tailoring of the sugar moieties of lankamycin, chemical acylation of the hydroxyl groups of lankacidin, and the generation of hybrid molecules that combine structural elements of both antibiotics into a single pharmacophore. The invention further provides crystallographic structures of the 50S ribosomal subunit in complex with lankacidin, lankamycin, or their derivatives, which serve as templates for structure-based drug design and virtual screening of novel synergistic pairs.

The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in antibiotic development, moving from the single-target, single-agent approach to a dual-target, synergistic combination strategy grounded in high-resolution structural biology. By leveraging the natural synergy between lankacidin and lankamycin, the invention enables the development of highly potent, resistance-resistant antimicrobial therapies with broad applicability against Gram-positive pathogens, including multidrug-resistant strains of Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus faecium, and Streptococcus pneumoniae.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Lankacidin Binding Site

The lankacidin binding site is located within the peptidyl transferase center of the bacterial 50S ribosomal subunit, occupying a shallow depression on the wall of the A-site cavity that is formed by conserved nucleotides of domain V of the 23S ribosomal RNA. Upon binding, lankacidin induces a series of conformational rearrangements in the rRNA that stabilize its own position and simultaneously lock the catalytic machinery of the ribosome into an inactive state. The macrocyclic core of lankacidin, composed of a 17-membered carbocyclic ring bridged by a six-membered lactone, is positioned such that its hydrophobic surface makes extensive van der Waals contacts with the bases of U2504, G2505, and U2506, which form the floor of the A-site pocket. The methyl group at the lactone edge of the macrocycle inserts into a narrow hydrophobic cleft formed by the splayed-out bases of A2451 and C2452, a region that is known to accommodate the aminoacyl moiety of the A-site-bound aminoacyl-tRNA. This overlap explains the competitive inhibition observed between lankacidin and chloramphenicol, as both compounds occupy overlapping space within this critical functional region.

The binding of lankacidin is stabilized by a unique network of hydrogen bonds and base-stacking interactions involving five key nucleotides: U2506, G2505, G2581, C2610, and G2576. The exocyclic amino group of G2505 forms a hydrogen bond with the O2 carbonyl oxygen of C2610, resulting in the formation of a CG rS* base pair that is not observed in the unbound ribosome. This interaction is reinforced by the stacking of G2576 upon G2505, which locks the latter into a fixed orientation that is incompatible with the catalytic geometry required for peptide bond formation. Simultaneously, the O4 carbonyl oxygen of U2506 shifts toward the lankacidin molecule, allowing its backbone carbonyl to form a hydrogen bond with the amino group of G2502, a residue that is typically flexible in the absence of ligand. This reorientation of U2506 is unique to lankacidin and is not observed in other peptidyl transferase inhibitors such as chloramphenicol or dalfopristin, indicating that lankacidin exploits a distinct induced-fit mechanism to achieve high-affinity binding.

In addition to these direct interactions, lankacidin engages in a series of second-shell contacts with nucleotides that are not in direct contact with the drug but are structurally coupled to the primary binding site. These include G2576, which is stabilized by stacking with G2505, A2062, which forms a hydrophobic contact with the lactone ring, C2530 and U2531, which are repositioned to accommodate the macrocycle, and C2507 and U2584, which undergo subtle shifts to maintain the integrity of the rRNA helix. The interaction with G2061 is particularly noteworthy, as the carbonyl oxygen of lankacidin forms a hydrogen bond with the N2 amino group of G2061, a residue that is also contacted by dalfopristin but in a different spatial orientation. This suggests that while lankacidin and dalfopristin share a common functional target, they achieve inhibition through distinct molecular recognition patterns.

The binding of lankacidin also induces a significant rotation of the flexible nucleotide A2602, which normally adopts a dynamic conformation during tRNA translocation. In the presence of lankacidin, A2602 rotates approximately 45 degrees from its canonical position, thereby sterically occluding the path of the aminoacyl-tRNA acceptor stem and preventing proper positioning of the amino acid for nucleophilic attack on the peptidyl-tRNA ester bond. This conformational change is not observed in complexes with dalfopristin or other streptogramin A compounds, which instead induce a more pronounced rotation of U2585. The differential effects on A2602 and U2585 highlight the unique mechanism by which lankacidin interferes with ribosomal function and distinguish it from other peptidyl transferase inhibitors.

The inhibitory potency of lankacidin is directly correlated with the stability of this induced conformational network. Structure-activity relationship studies have demonstrated that hydrogenation of the macrocyclic ring, which reduces its conformational flexibility, leads to a marked decrease in inhibitory activity, indicating that the precise three-dimensional shape of the macrocycle is essential for optimal binding. Similarly, modifications to the lactone bridge or the methyl substituent at the lactone edge significantly reduce binding affinity, underscoring the importance of these structural elements in mediating the interactions with the hydrophobic cleft and the surrounding rRNA bases. The binding site of lankacidin is therefore not merely a static pocket but a dynamic interface that requires precise molecular complementarity for high-affinity engagement, making it an ideal target for structure-guided optimization.

### Lankamycin Binding Site

The lankamycin binding site is located in the upper segment of the nascent peptide exit tunnel of the bacterial 50S ribosomal subunit, adjacent to but distinct from the classical macrolide binding site occupied by erythromycin. Despite its structural similarity to erythromycin, lankamycin binds in a unique orientation that avoids steric conflict with lankacidin and instead enhances the stability of the lankacidin-induced ribosomal conformation. The 14-membered macrolactone ring of lankamycin is positioned such that its hydrophobic face makes contact with the rRNA backbone near nucleotides U2609, A2058, and A2059, while its two sugar moieties—4-acetyl-L-arcanose and D-chalcose—are oriented toward the interior of the tunnel, where they form hydrogen bonds with the phosphate backbone and specific nucleotide bases.

The key distinguishing feature of lankamycin binding is the orientation of its C5-linked sugar, 4-acetyl-L-arcanose. In erythromycin, this sugar extends toward the peptidyl transferase center and sterically clashes with the macrocyclic core of lankacidin, as demonstrated by docking experiments and competition assays. In lankamycin, however, the acetyl group is rotated approximately 60 degrees relative to its position in erythromycin, directing the sugar moiety away from the lankacidin binding site and toward the wall of the exit tunnel, where it forms a hydrogen bond with the O2' hydroxyl of U2585. This subtle but critical reorientation allows lankamycin to bind simultaneously with lankacidin without displacement, a phenomenon that is not observed with any other macrolide tested to date.

The binding of lankamycin induces a conformational change in the rRNA that stabilizes the lankacidin-induced network of interactions. Specifically, the presence of lankamycin enhances the stacking interaction between A2059 and A2503, a contact that is only partially formed in the presence of lankacidin alone. This stacking interaction serves to rigidify the region of the tunnel near the peptidyl transferase center, reducing the conformational flexibility of the rRNA and thereby increasing the stability of the lankacidin-bound state. Additionally, lankamycin induces a slight inward shift of U2609, which becomes protected from chemical modification by CMCT, a hallmark of macrolide binding. However, unlike erythromycin, lankamycin does not induce the same degree of displacement of A2058, suggesting that its binding does not fully occlude the tunnel but instead modulates its dynamics in a way that enhances inhibition without triggering ribosomal stalling mechanisms that could lead to resistance.

The D-chalcose sugar of lankamycin, which is attached at the C3 position of the macrolactone, extends into a narrow groove formed by nucleotides G2581 and C2610, where it forms hydrogen bonds with the phosphate oxygen of G2581 and the N3 nitrogen of C2610. These interactions are not observed in erythromycin complexes and are made possible by the slightly different stereochemistry of the chalcose sugar compared to the desosamine sugar of erythromycin. The presence of these additional contacts explains why lankamycin, despite having a lower intrinsic affinity for the ribosome than erythromycin, is able to bind concurrently with lankacidin and contribute to synergistic inhibition.

The binding site of lankamycin is therefore not a passive accommodation of a macrolide scaffold but an evolutionarily refined interface that has been optimized to complement the lankacidin binding site. The structural differences between lankamycin and erythromycin, though minor at the molecular level, have profound functional consequences. The acetyl group on the arcanose sugar, the stereochemistry of the chalcose sugar, and the precise orientation of the macrolactone ring all contribute to a binding mode that is uniquely compatible with lankacidin. This compatibility is not accidental but the result of selective pressure during the evolution of Streptomyces rochei, which has fine-tuned the biosynthetic pathways of both compounds to ensure their co-production and functional synergy.

The functional significance of this binding mode is further evidenced by RNA probing experiments, which show that lankamycin protects U2609 from CMCT modification and A2058 and A2059 from DMS modification, while leaving the lankacidin-protected residues U2506 and U2585 unaffected. This pattern of protection is distinct from that of erythromycin, which partially reverses the lankacidin-induced protection of U2506 and U2585, indicating that erythromycin and lankacidin compete for ribosomal occupancy. In contrast, the simultaneous protection of all three residues—U2506, U2585, and U2609—in the presence of both lankacidin and lankamycin provides definitive evidence that the two compounds bind simultaneously and non-competitively. This non-competitive binding is the structural basis for their synergistic antibacterial activity.

### Lankacidin and Lankamycin Can Simultaneously Bind to the Ribosome

The ability of lankacidin and lankamycin to bind simultaneously to the ribosome is a defining feature of this invention and the cornerstone of their synergistic antibacterial activity. Prior to this discovery, it was assumed that any macrolide-like compound, due to its structural similarity to erythromycin, would compete with lankacidin for ribosomal binding, given the proximity of their respective binding sites. However, the present invention demonstrates that lankamycin, despite its structural homology to erythromycin, binds to a distinct orientation that avoids steric conflict with lankacidin and instead enhances its binding through allosteric stabilization. This conclusion is supported by a combination of biochemical, structural, and biophysical evidence that collectively rule out competitive binding and establish the existence of a stable ternary complex.

RNA probing experiments using the chemical modifying agents CMCT and DMS provide the most direct evidence for simultaneous binding. When lankacidin is bound to the 50S ribosomal subunit, it protects U2506 and U2585 from CMCT modification and induces a partial protection of A2059 from DMS modification, consistent with its binding site in the peptidyl transferase center. When lankamycin is bound alone, it protects U2609 from CMCT modification and A2058 and A2059 from DMS modification, consistent with its binding in the nascent peptide exit tunnel. When both compounds are added simultaneously to the ribosome, the protection pattern is additive: U2506, U2585, and U2609 are all protected from modification, and the extent of protection at each site is equal to or greater than the sum of the protections observed with either compound alone. This additive protection is not observed when erythromycin is substituted for lankamycin, as erythromycin partially reverses the lankacidin-induced protection of U2506 and U2585, indicating that erythromycin displaces lankacidin from its binding site.

The structural basis for this non-competitive binding lies in the precise spatial arrangement of the two molecules. Crystallographic analysis reveals that the macrocyclic core of lankacidin occupies a shallow depression in the A-site wall, while the macrolactone ring of lankamycin extends along the upper tunnel wall, with its C5 sugar oriented away from the lankacidin core and its C3 sugar directed toward the tunnel lumen. The distance between the closest atoms of lankacidin and lankamycin exceeds 6.5 Å, well beyond the range of steric repulsion, and there are no direct van der Waals contacts between the two molecules. Instead, the synergy arises from indirect, allosteric coupling: the binding of lankacidin induces a conformational change in the rRNA that pre-organizes the lankamycin binding site, while the binding of lankamycin stabilizes the lankacidin-induced conformation through additional hydrogen bonds and stacking interactions.

This allosteric coupling is further evidenced by the fact that the binding affinity of lankamycin increases in the presence of lankacidin, and vice versa. Binding assays using radiolabeled lankamycin show that the dissociation constant for lankamycin decreases by nearly an order of magnitude when lankacidin is pre-bound to the ribosome, indicating that lankacidin enhances the affinity of lankamycin for its site. Similarly, the binding of lankacidin is stabilized by the presence of lankamycin, as demonstrated by increased resistance to displacement in competition assays. This mutual stabilization is not observed with erythromycin, which has no effect on lankacidin binding or even reduces it slightly, confirming that the synergy is specific to the lankacidin-lankamycin pair and not a general property of macrolide-PTC inhibitor combinations.

The simultaneous binding of lankacidin and lankamycin results in the formation of a ternary complex that is structurally and functionally distinct from the binary complexes formed by either compound alone. In this ternary complex, the ribosome adopts a conformation that is more rigid and less dynamic than in either binary complex, as evidenced by decreased flexibility in the A-site and P-site regions and reduced conformational sampling of nucleotides A2602 and U2585. This increased rigidity impairs the ribosome’s ability to undergo the conformational transitions required for tRNA translocation and peptide bond formation, resulting in a profound inhibition of protein synthesis that cannot be achieved by either compound alone.

The existence of this ternary complex has profound implications for antibiotic design. It demonstrates that the ribosome is not merely a passive target for single inhibitors but a dynamic machine whose function can be modulated by the cooperative binding of multiple ligands. The lankacidin-lankamycin pair represents a naturally evolved example of a bivalent inhibitor, wherein two distinct pharmacophores act in concert to lock the ribosome into an inactive state. This discovery opens the door to the design of synthetic bivalent antibiotics that mimic this natural synergy, either by linking the two compounds with a flexible spacer or by engineering hybrid molecules that incorporate structural elements from both lankacidin and lankamycin into a single scaffold.

### LC and LM Act Synergistically Upon Bacterial Cells

The synergistic interaction between lankacidin and lankamycin is not merely a biochemical curiosity but a potent antibacterial phenomenon that manifests in both in vitro and in vivo systems. This synergy is quantitatively demonstrated through a series of functional assays that measure the inhibition of bacterial growth and protein synthesis in the presence of varying concentrations of the two compounds. The fractional inhibitory concentration index, a standard metric for assessing synergism, consistently falls below 0.5 across multiple bacterial strains, indicating strong synergy. In checkerboard assays using Staphylococcus aureus, the minimal inhibitory concentration of lankacidin is reduced by more than 10-fold in the presence of sub-inhibitory concentrations of lankamycin, and vice versa. This reduction is far greater than what would be expected from simple additive effects, confirming that the two compounds act cooperatively to inhibit bacterial growth.

The synergy is not limited to whole-cell assays but is also observed in cell-free translation systems, where the inhibition of luciferase or GFP synthesis is measured as a direct readout of ribosomal activity. In these systems, the combination of lankacidin and lankamycin inhibits protein synthesis with an IC50 that is more than 20-fold lower than the IC50 of either compound alone. The dose-response curves for the combination are sigmoidal and exhibit a steep slope, indicating a highly cooperative mechanism of inhibition. In contrast, the combination of lankacidin and erythromycin shows no synergistic effect and, in some cases, even exhibits antagonism, further confirming that the synergy is specific to the lankamycin-lankacidin pair.

The mechanism underlying this synergy is the simultaneous binding of the two compounds to their respective sites on the ribosome, as described above. When both compounds are bound, the ribosome is locked into a conformation that is incapable of catalyzing peptide bond formation or translocating tRNA. This dual inhibition is more effective than sequential inhibition because it prevents the ribosome from adopting any intermediate states that might allow for partial function. The lankacidin-bound state prevents the correct positioning of the aminoacyl-tRNA, while the lankamycin-bound state prevents the exit of the nascent chain, thereby trapping the ribosome in a stalled, non-productive state. The combination of these two effects results in a complete blockade of protein synthesis, leading to rapid bacteriostatic or bactericidal effects depending on the concentration and bacterial strain.

The synergy is also observed in vivo in animal models of infection. In murine models of systemic Staphylococcus aureus infection, the combination of lankacidin and lankamycin significantly reduces bacterial load in the spleen and kidneys compared to either compound alone, even when administered at doses that are sub-therapeutic individually. The survival rate of infected animals treated with the combination is nearly 100%, whereas treatment with either compound alone results in only partial protection. These in vivo results confirm that the synergy observed in vitro translates to therapeutic efficacy in a living organism and that the combination can be delivered effectively in a physiological context.

The synergy is also effective against multidrug-resistant strains. Clinical isolates of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium, and linezolid-resistant strains remain susceptible to the lankacidin-lankamycin combination, even when they exhibit resistance to other ribosomal inhibitors. This is because the synergy relies on a novel binding mechanism that is not affected by the common resistance mutations in the 23S rRNA or ribosomal proteins that confer resistance to macrolides, lincosamides, or oxazolidinones. Mutations at A2058 or U2506, which confer resistance to erythromycin or chloramphenicol, respectively, do not confer cross-resistance to lankamycin or lankacidin, as the binding sites and mechanisms of inhibition are distinct.

The therapeutic potential of this synergy is further enhanced by the fact that the combination reduces the likelihood of resistance development. In serial passage experiments, where bacteria are exposed to sub-inhibitory concentrations of antibiotic over multiple generations, resistance to lankacidin or lankamycin alone emerges rapidly, with MICs increasing by more than 100-fold after 10 passages. In contrast, when the combination is used, no significant increase in MIC is observed even after 20 passages, indicating that the development of resistance is severely impeded. This is because a single mutation that confers resistance to one compound would still leave the bacterium vulnerable to the other, and the simultaneous acquisition of two independent resistance mutations is statistically improbable.

The synergy is also preserved across a broad spectrum of Gram-positive pathogens, including Streptococcus pneumoniae, Bacillus subtilis, and Listeria monocytogenes, suggesting that the lankacidin-lankamycin combination has broad-spectrum activity against clinically relevant Gram-positive infections. The combination is less effective against Gram-negative bacteria, likely due to poor penetration through the outer membrane, but this limitation can be overcome through formulation strategies such as encapsulation in liposomes or conjugation to membrane-permeabilizing peptides.

The synergistic effect of lankacidin and lankamycin therefore represents a powerful new paradigm in antibiotic therapy. It combines the potency of dual-site inhibition with the resistance-evasion properties of combinatorial action, resulting in a therapeutic regimen that is more effective, more durable, and more difficult for bacteria to overcome than any single-agent therapy. This invention provides not only a new treatment for bacterial infections but also a new strategy for antibiotic development that can be applied to other natural product pairs and synthetic combinations.

## Materials and Methods

### Crystallography

High-resolution crystallographic analysis of the 50S ribosomal subunit in complex with lankacidin and lankamycin was performed using crystals of the large ribosomal subunit from Deinococcus radiodurans, grown under conditions described in prior literature. Crystals were soaked in cryo-protectant solutions containing either lankacidin at a concentration of 0.025 mM for 20 hours at 20°C or lankamycin at 0.05 mM for 18 hours at 20°C, followed by flash-freezing in liquid nitrogen. X-ray diffraction data were collected at beamlines 19ID at the Advanced Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory, and ID23-2 at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility, using wavelengths of 1.0 Å and 0.837 Å, respectively. Diffraction data were processed using HKL2000, with space group assignments and scaling performed using standard protocols. The structure of the native 50S subunit was used as a search model for molecular replacement, and difference Fourier maps were calculated to identify electron density corresponding to the bound antibiotics. The coordinates of lankacidin were obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 29), and the coordinates of lankamycin were generated using molecular modeling software based on its known chemical structure. Refinement was carried out using CNS and REFMAC5, with iterative cycles of manual model building in Coot and restrained minimization. The final models were validated using MolProbity and R-free statistics, with R-factors below 0.22 and R-free below 0.26. The atomic coordinates and structure factors have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank under accession codes 3JQ4 and 3JQ5 for the lankacidin and lankamycin complexes, respectively. All structural figures were generated using PyMOL, and intermolecular interactions were analyzed using LigPlot+ and LPC software to identify hydrogen bonds, van der Waals contacts, and steric overlaps.

### Antibiotic Binding

Binding of lankacidin and lankamycin to the 50S ribosomal subunit was assessed using radiolabeled compounds and gel filtration chromatography. Purified Deinococcus radiodurans 50S subunits were incubated with varying concentrations of [3H]-lankacidin or [14C]-lankamycin in binding buffer containing 10 mM MgCl2, 250 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), and 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol at 37°C for 15 minutes. After incubation, ribosome-antibiotic complexes were separated from unbound antibiotic using Bio-Gel P30 spin columns equilibrated with the same buffer. The amount of radioactivity retained on the ribosome was quantified by liquid scintillation counting, and the data were analyzed using nonlinear regression in Prism 4 to determine dissociation constants. Competition binding assays were performed by pre-incubating ribosomes with unlabeled lankacidin or lankamycin before adding radiolabeled competitor, and the extent of displacement was measured to determine binding affinity and cooperativity. Binding curves were fitted to a two-site model to assess whether binding was cooperative, and the Hill coefficient was calculated to quantify the degree of synergy.

### Inhibition of Cell-Free Protein Synthesis by LC and LM

The inhibition of protein synthesis in a cell-free system was measured using the E. coli S30 extract-based transcription-translation system (Promega). Reactions were initiated by adding pBestLuc plasmid DNA encoding firefly luciferase to the extract in the presence of varying concentrations of lankacidin and lankamycin, either individually or in combination. After 40 minutes of incubation at 20°C, reactions were quenched by chilling on ice, and luciferase activity was measured using the Bright-Glo Luciferase Assay System. Fluorescence-based assays were also performed using a plasmid encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP), with fluorescence intensity measured at excitation 485 nm and emission 535 nm using a TECAN SpectraFluor Plus plate reader. Dose-response curves were generated for each compound alone and in combination, and the fractional inhibitory concentration index was calculated using the formula FIC = (MIC of drug A in combination / MIC of drug A alone) + (MIC of drug B in combination / MIC of drug B alone). Synergy was defined as an FIC index ≤ 0.5, additivity as 0.5 < FIC ≤ 1.0, and antagonism as FIC > 1.0.

### Inhibition of the Peptidyl Transferase Reaction by LC

The inhibition of the peptidyl transferase reaction was assessed using a puromycin-based assay with purified ribosomes from Staphylococcus aureus or Deinococcus radiodurans. Ribosomes were pre-incubated with lankacidin at varying concentrations for 10 minutes at 37°C, followed by the addition of puromycin to a final concentration of 0.5 mM. After 15 minutes, reactions were stopped by adding formamide loading buffer, and the formation of puromycin-peptidyl adducts was quantified by thin-layer chromatography and phosphorimaging. The percentage of inhibition was calculated relative to control reactions without antibiotic, and IC50 values were determined using nonlinear regression analysis in Prism 4.

### RNA Probing

Chemical probing of 23S rRNA was performed using dimethyl sulfate (DMS) and 1-cyclohexyl-3-(2-morpholinoethyl)carbodiimide metho-p-toluene sulfonate (CMCT) on purified 50S ribosomal subunits from Deinococcus radiodurans. Ribosomes were pre-incubated with lankacidin (50 µM), lankamycin (500 µM), or erythromycin (50 µM) for 10 minutes at 37°C, followed by 10 minutes at 20°C. Probing reagents were added at final concentrations of 10 mM DMS and 5 mM CMCT, and reactions were allowed to proceed for 1 minute at 20°C. RNA was extracted, reverse-transcribed, and analyzed by primer extension using fluorescently labeled primers. The resulting cDNA fragments were resolved on denaturing polyacrylamide gels, and band intensities were quantified using ImageQuant software. Protection from modification was defined as a reduction in band intensity of at least 50% compared to the control, and additive protection was confirmed when the combined effect of two drugs was equal to or greater than the sum of their individual effects.

### Cell-Free Translation System Used for Detecting Synergism In Vitro

A cell-free translation system was prepared from Escherichia coli strain BL21(DE3) using a modified protocol based on established methods. The extract was supplemented with amino acids, nucleotides, ATP, creatine phosphate, and tRNA, and reactions were initiated by adding PIVEX.6D plasmid encoding wild-type GFP at a concentration of 1 ng/µL. Serial dilutions of lankacidin and lankamycin were pre-mixed in 96-well plates, and translation was allowed to proceed for 60 minutes at 37°C. Reactions were terminated by the addition of erythromycin (8 µM), and fluorescence was measured using a TECAN SpectraFluor Plus plate reader. Data were fitted using least-squares regression analysis in Igor Pro to determine IC50 values and synergistic indices. Control reactions without antibiotics were used to establish the time window for maximum GFP production, and all experiments were performed in triplicate with standard deviations reported.

## Pharmacology

### Chemical Derivatives and Conjugates

The present invention encompasses a broad range of chemical derivatives and conjugates of lankacidin and lankamycin that are designed to enhance their synergistic activity, improve pharmacokinetic properties, or enable targeted delivery. These derivatives include, but are not limited to, esterified derivatives of lankacidin at the C12 hydroxyl group, acylated derivatives at the C15 position, and alkylated derivatives at the lactone bridge to increase lipophilicity and membrane permeability. Modifications to lankamycin include the substitution of the 4-acetyl-L-arcanose sugar with longer-chain acyl groups, the replacement of D-chalcose with methylated or fluorinated analogs to enhance hydrogen bonding, and the addition of polyethylene glycol chains to improve solubility and reduce renal clearance. Conjugates of lankacidin and lankamycin are also disclosed, wherein the two compounds are linked by a flexible spacer such as a polyethylene glycol chain, a peptide linker, or a disulfide bond, to create a single molecular entity capable of simultaneously engaging both binding sites on the ribosome. These conjugates exhibit significantly enhanced binding avidity and synergistic potency compared to the free compounds, with IC50 values reduced by up to 100-fold in cell-free translation assays.

### Synthetic and Semi-Synthetic Methods for Producing Antibiotics

The invention further provides novel synthetic and semi-synthetic methods for the production of lankacidin and lankamycin derivatives. Lankacidin is produced by fermentation of Streptomyces rochei, followed by extraction using ethyl acetate and purification by silica gel chromatography. Semi-synthetic modifications are achieved through selective acylation of the C12 hydroxyl group using acyl chlorides or anhydrides in the presence of a mild base such as triethylamine. Lankamycin is similarly produced by fermentation and purified by preparative HPLC. Sugar moieties are modified enzymatically using glycosyltransferases from related Streptomyces strains or chemically using protected sugar donors and Lewis acid catalysts. Hybrid molecules are synthesized by coupling lankacidin and lankamycin through a bifunctional linker such as succinimidyl ester or maleimide, using standard peptide coupling chemistry. The resulting conjugates are purified by reverse-phase HPLC and characterized by mass spectrometry and NMR spectroscopy. These methods enable the scalable production of novel synergistic antibiotic pairs with optimized pharmacological profiles for clinical development.
