# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The detection of visual motion has long been a central challenge in both biological and artificial vision systems. In natural organisms, motion perception is not a late-stage cognitive process but an early, parallel, and highly efficient computation occurring within the first few synaptic layers of the visual pathway. In the retina of vertebrates, specialized ganglion cells respond selectively to motion in specific directions, signaling the presence and direction of movement before any higher cortical processing occurs. Similarly, in the visual system of flies, direction-selective neurons emerge just three synapses from the photoreceptors, suggesting that motion detection is achieved through a compact, analog, and temporally continuous mechanism rather than through discrete frame-by-frame comparisons. These biological systems operate under severe computational constraints, yet they achieve remarkable speed, robustness, and energy efficiency—properties that remain elusive in most artificial vision systems.

Modern computer vision algorithms for motion detection have largely relied on optic flow estimation, which computes dense displacement vectors between consecutive image frames by solving spatial and temporal intensity gradients under assumptions of brightness constancy and smooth motion. While these methods can yield accurate results under controlled conditions, they are computationally intensive, requiring iterative optimization, interpolation, and large memory footprints to maintain spatial coherence across the entire image. These demands make them unsuitable for real-time deployment on embedded systems, mobile platforms, or low-power devices where computational resources are limited. Furthermore, optic flow methods are inherently sensitive to changes in illumination, contrast, and noise, often failing under low-light conditions or when texture is sparse. Their reliance on intensity differences also renders them vulnerable to global luminance variations, such as those caused by flickering lights or changing weather conditions, which may be misinterpreted as motion.

Alternative approaches inspired by biological models, such as the Reichardt detector and the motion energy detector, have been proposed to emulate the simplicity and efficiency of neural motion circuits. These models typically employ spatiotemporal filtering followed by nonlinear operations such as multiplication or squaring to extract motion energy. While more computationally tractable than optic flow, they still require multiple parallel channels of filtering, precise temporal delay tuning, and careful normalization to achieve robust performance. Moreover, their responses are often tied to the amplitude of the signal, making them highly susceptible to contrast variations. In environments with low contrast or non-uniform illumination, these detectors frequently produce false positives or fail to detect motion entirely.

The limitations of existing methods point to a fundamental gap in current motion detection paradigms: the underutilization of phase information. Phase, as a component of the complex representation of signals, has traditionally been regarded as secondary to amplitude in signal processing. This bias stems from the historical dominance of amplitude-based analysis in linear systems theory, where magnitude is often equated with signal strength and phase is treated as a nuisance parameter. However, recent advances in computational neuroscience and signal reconstruction have demonstrated that phase alone can encode the structural content of images with remarkable fidelity. Studies have shown that swapping the phase spectra of two natural images results in reconstructed images that retain the structural layout of the original phase donor, even when the amplitude is entirely replaced. This suggests that phase carries more than just positional information—it encodes the spatial relationships between features, edges, and contours that define the visual scene.

Despite these insights, phase has rarely been leveraged for motion detection in practical systems. The primary obstacles have been the nonlinearity of phase-based operations, the difficulty of interpreting phase changes in the presence of noise, and the lack of a clear computational framework that connects local phase dynamics to motion vectors. Most attempts to use phase for motion estimation have focused on global phase shifts between entire frames, using phase correlation techniques to estimate translation. While effective for rigid, large-scale motion, these methods are ill-suited for detecting local, independent motion of small objects within a scene. They also require discrete frame comparisons, which inherently limit temporal resolution and introduce latency.

The invention described herein bridges this gap by introducing a novel motion detection paradigm grounded in the temporal evolution of local phase. Rather than treating phase as a static descriptor of image structure, this approach treats it as a dynamic variable whose rate of change directly reflects local motion. By computing the short-time Fourier transform over overlapping spatial windows, the system captures phase variations at multiple spatial scales simultaneously. The temporal derivative of these local phases is then analyzed not as a scalar quantity but as a vector field in frequency space, where the structure of the phase gradient reveals the direction and magnitude of motion. A Radon transform is applied to this field to extract coherent motion patterns, effectively filtering out noise and isolating motion signals that conform to the expected linear relationship between frequency and velocity.

This method operates continuously in time, does not rely on intensity differences, and is inherently robust to contrast and illumination changes because phase is invariant under uniform scaling of intensity. It requires no explicit frame differencing, no iterative optimization, and no assumption of smooth motion. Instead, it exploits the intrinsic geometry of the Fourier domain to detect motion through a simple, parallelizable architecture that mirrors the organization of biological visual circuits. The algorithm is designed for efficient implementation on modern parallel computing hardware, including GPUs and FPGAs, leveraging the speed and scalability of fast Fourier transforms. Unlike prior approaches, it does not attempt to estimate precise velocity vectors but instead identifies the presence, location, and approximate direction of motion—a task that is sufficient for many real-world applications such as surveillance, robotics, and autonomous navigation.

The significance of this invention lies in its departure from traditional amplitude-centric models and its embrace of phase as a primary signal for motion perception. By formulating motion detection as a problem of tracking the temporal dynamics of local phase, the invention achieves a level of efficiency, robustness, and biological plausibility unmatched by existing methods. It provides a new theoretical foundation for visual motion processing—one that aligns with the principles of neural computation while enabling practical, real-time implementation on commodity hardware.

## SUMMARY

The invention disclosed herein is a method and system for detecting local motion in visual scenes using the temporal evolution of local phase information derived from the short-time Fourier transform of image sequences. Unlike conventional motion detection techniques that rely on intensity differences, optical flow estimation, or amplitude-based correlation, this invention leverages the phase component of the complex-valued frequency representation of visual input to identify the presence, location, and direction of motion. The core innovation lies in the recognition that the rate of change of local phase, computed over spatially localized regions of an image, exhibits a structured relationship with the velocity of motion within those regions. This relationship is exploited through a series of computationally efficient, parallelizable operations that transform phase dynamics into a motion detection signal.

The method begins by dividing the visual field into overlapping, spatially localized blocks, each corresponding to a region of the image windowed by a Gaussian function. For each block, a two-dimensional fast Fourier transform is computed to obtain the complex-valued frequency representation of the localized image content. From this representation, the local phase at each spatial frequency is extracted and tracked over time. The temporal derivative of the local phase is then computed for each frequency component within each block, yielding a phase change map that varies across spatial frequencies and time. This phase change map is not treated as a raw signal but as a vector field in the frequency domain, where the spatial structure of the phase gradient encodes the direction and speed of motion.

To extract motion signals from this high-dimensional phase change space, a Radon transform is applied to each block’s phase change map. The Radon transform integrates the phase change values along lines in the frequency domain, effectively collapsing the two-dimensional frequency space into a one-dimensional projection parameterized by angle and radial distance. The resulting Radon transform output reveals coherent linear structures in the phase change field, which are characteristic of rigid translational motion. A motion indicator is then computed for each block by identifying the angular orientation that maximizes the integrated phase change magnitude. This maximum value, termed the Phase Motion Indicator, serves as a scalar measure of motion likelihood within the block. Blocks exhibiting a Phase Motion Indicator above a predefined threshold are flagged as containing motion, and the corresponding optimal angle provides an estimate of the motion direction.

The system is designed for high-efficiency implementation on parallel computing architectures. The use of overlapping Gaussian windows ensures spatial continuity while enabling independent processing of each block. The fast Fourier transform, being a well-optimized and highly parallelizable operation, allows the entire system to be implemented on graphics processing units or field-programmable gate arrays with minimal latency. The temporal derivative of phase is computed using a high-pass filter, which can be implemented as a simple recursive filter or a finite impulse response filter, further reducing computational overhead. Additionally, a normalization scheme is applied to discount phase change measurements in regions of low amplitude, thereby suppressing noise and enhancing signal reliability.

The invention is fundamentally distinct from prior art in several key respects. First, it does not require intensity-based comparisons between frames, making it invariant to global illumination changes and contrast variations. Second, it does not assume smooth or global motion, allowing it to detect independent motion of small objects within a complex scene. Third, it operates continuously in time, avoiding the discrete frame sampling that introduces latency and temporal aliasing in conventional methods. Fourth, it leverages the intrinsic geometry of the Fourier domain to separate motion signals from noise, using the Radon transform to detect the linear structure inherent in phase change due to translation. Finally, the method is inherently parallel, with each block processed independently, making it scalable to high-resolution video streams without a proportional increase in computational complexity.

The resulting system is capable of real-time motion detection on high-definition video sequences with minimal computational resources. It performs robustly under low-contrast conditions, in the presence of significant noise, and across a wide range of illumination levels—conditions under which traditional methods often fail. The output of the system is not a dense motion field but a sparse, block-based motion map that identifies where motion occurs and in which general direction. This makes it ideal for applications such as surveillance, object tracking, robotic navigation, and event detection, where the primary goal is to localize motion rather than to reconstruct precise velocity vectors. The invention thus provides a novel, biologically inspired, and computationally efficient solution to the long-standing problem of motion detection in visual systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Global Phase of Images

The representation of visual scenes through the global phase of their Fourier transform is a well-established concept in computational vision, though its implications for motion detection have remained largely unexplored. When an image is represented in the frequency domain, its Fourier transform decomposes the spatial intensity distribution into a sum of sinusoidal components, each characterized by a specific frequency, amplitude, and phase. The global phase of the image refers to the collective phase of all these frequency components across the entire spatial extent of the image. It is this global phase that, when preserved while the amplitude is replaced, can reconstruct an image whose structural layout closely resembles that of the original phase donor, even if the texture and contrast are entirely altered. This phenomenon underscores the profound role that phase plays in encoding the spatial relationships between edges, contours, and object boundaries—the very features that define visual content.

In the context of motion detection, the global phase offers a powerful theoretical foundation. The Fourier shift theorem dictates that a uniform translation of an image in the spatial domain corresponds to a linear phase shift across all frequency components in the Fourier domain. Specifically, if an image is shifted by a constant displacement vector, the phase of each frequency component is modified by an amount proportional to the dot product of the frequency vector and the displacement vector. This direct relationship between spatial translation and phase modulation implies that, in the case of a globally moving scene, the temporal derivative of the global phase at each frequency is linearly related to the velocity of translation. This insight, while mathematically elegant, is of limited practical utility in real-world scenarios, where visual scenes are rarely composed of a single, uniformly moving object. Instead, scenes typically contain multiple independent moving elements, stationary backgrounds, and complex textures that disrupt the coherence of the global phase.

Nevertheless, the global phase provides a critical conceptual anchor for understanding motion in the frequency domain. It establishes that motion, when uniform, leaves a distinct signature in the phase evolution of the Fourier spectrum. This signature is not a random fluctuation but a structured, deterministic relationship between frequency and phase change. The challenge, then, is to extend this principle from the global to the local level—to identify whether similar structured relationships exist when the image is analyzed in localized regions rather than as a whole. If such a relationship can be demonstrated locally, then motion detection can be performed not by comparing entire frames, but by observing the temporal dynamics of phase within small, independent regions of the visual field.

The global phase, therefore, serves as a theoretical benchmark. It demonstrates that phase is not merely a byproduct of the Fourier transform but a carrier of spatial information that evolves predictably under motion. The invention builds upon this foundation by proposing that the same principle holds locally. By applying the Fourier transform to localized windows of the image, rather than to the entire scene, the system captures phase dynamics that are specific to individual regions. The temporal derivative of these local phases, when analyzed collectively, reveals motion signals that are localized, directionally consistent, and robust to global illumination changes. The global phase thus provides the conceptual justification for treating phase as a primary signal for motion detection, while the local phase provides the practical mechanism for implementing such detection in complex, real-world environments.

### Local Phase of Images

The concept of local phase extends the utility of phase information from the global representation of an entire image to the localized analysis of its constituent regions. This is achieved through the short-time Fourier transform, which applies a sliding window function to the image, effectively isolating small spatial neighborhoods for independent frequency analysis. Each window, typically chosen to be a Gaussian function due to its optimal balance between spatial and frequency localization, defines a region of influence over which the Fourier transform is computed. The result is a four-dimensional representation of the image: two dimensions for spatial position, and two for frequency. Within this representation, the local phase at each spatial location and frequency pair captures the phase of the sinusoidal components that best describe the intensity variations within that localized region.

This localized phase representation is critical because it decouples the phase information from the global structure of the image. Where the global phase reflects the overall alignment of sinusoids across the entire scene, the local phase reflects the alignment of sinusoids within a small, moving window. This distinction is essential for detecting motion in natural scenes, where objects move independently and at varying speeds. A car moving across a street, for instance, will induce a phase change in the windows that overlap its boundary, while the windows covering the stationary pavement will exhibit little to no phase change. The local phase, therefore, acts as a spatially selective sensor for motion, responding only to changes that occur within its immediate vicinity.

The mathematical formulation of the local phase arises naturally from the short-time Fourier transform, which convolves the image with a windowed complex exponential. The output of this operation is a complex number whose magnitude corresponds to the local amplitude and whose argument corresponds to the local phase. The local amplitude indicates the strength of the frequency component within the window, while the local phase indicates the spatial alignment of that component relative to the window’s center. When the image is static, the local phase remains constant over time. However, when motion occurs within the window, the spatial alignment of the intensity pattern shifts relative to the window, causing the phase to evolve. This evolution is not arbitrary; it follows a predictable pattern dictated by the direction and speed of motion.

Importantly, the local phase is invariant to uniform changes in intensity. If the brightness of a region increases or decreases uniformly—due to lighting changes, shadows, or camera gain—the phase remains unchanged. This property is fundamental to the robustness of the method. Traditional motion detection algorithms that rely on intensity differences are highly sensitive to such variations, often mistaking illumination changes for motion. In contrast, the local phase-based approach ignores global brightness fluctuations and responds only to spatial reorganization of intensity patterns. This makes it particularly effective in low-contrast environments, under varying illumination, or in the presence of noise, where amplitude-based methods frequently fail.

Furthermore, the local phase representation is naturally suited to the architecture of biological visual systems. In the retina and visual cortex, neurons are organized into receptive fields that respond to localized spatial patterns. The Gaussian window used in the short-time Fourier transform closely resembles the spatial profile of Gabor receptive fields found in the primary visual cortex, which are known to be tuned to specific orientations and spatial frequencies. The computation of local phase, therefore, can be interpreted as a biological analog: each window corresponds to a population of neurons whose combined activity encodes the phase of the local visual input. The temporal derivative of this phase then becomes a neural signal indicating motion. This alignment with biological mechanisms not only lends plausibility to the method but also suggests that it may be implemented in hardware using architectures that mimic neural computation, such as neuromorphic circuits or analog signal processors.

The local phase thus serves as the foundational signal for motion detection in this invention. It is not a derived quantity but a primary measurement, extracted directly from the visual input through a well-defined, computationally efficient operation. Its invariance to intensity scaling, its sensitivity to spatial reorganization, and its compatibility with parallel processing make it uniquely suited for the task of local motion detection. The subsequent steps of the invention—computing the temporal derivative, applying the Radon transform, and generating a motion indicator—are all designed to extract meaningful motion signals from this phase representation, transforming a high-dimensional, noisy signal into a sparse, interpretable motion map.

### The Global Phase Equation for Translational Motion

The mathematical relationship between global phase and translational motion provides the theoretical underpinning for the entire motion detection framework. When a visual scene undergoes a uniform translation, the Fourier transform of the scene is modulated by a linear phase shift that is directly proportional to the velocity of translation and the spatial frequency of each component. This relationship is not an approximation but a direct consequence of the Fourier shift theorem, which states that a spatial displacement of a function results in a linear phase ramp across its frequency spectrum. For a two-dimensional image, this means that if the image is translated by a displacement vector (s_x(t), s_y(t)) over time, then the phase of each frequency component (ω_x, ω_y) evolves according to a simple linear equation: the rate of change of phase with respect to time is equal to the negative dot product of the frequency vector and the velocity vector.

This equation is not merely a mathematical curiosity; it reveals a profound structural property of motion in the frequency domain. The phase change is not random or scattered—it is organized along a linear subspace defined by the direction of motion. For any given velocity, the phase change across all frequencies forms a plane in the frequency-space domain, with a normal vector aligned with the motion direction. This structured relationship is what makes motion detectable: it transforms an otherwise ambiguous temporal signal into a geometrically constrained pattern. In the context of global motion, this structure is easily observable and can be used to estimate the overall velocity of a scene. However, its true power lies in its extension to local motion.

The global phase equation demonstrates that motion, when uniform, imposes a deterministic constraint on the evolution of phase. This constraint is independent of the content of the image—it holds for any image, regardless of texture, contrast, or complexity. It is a property of translation itself, not of the objects being translated. This universality is what makes the equation so valuable: it provides a template against which local phase changes can be compared. If a localized region of the image exhibits a phase change that conforms to this linear relationship, then it is highly likely that motion is occurring within that region. Conversely, if the phase change is irregular, noisy, or unstructured, it is unlikely to be caused by motion.

The global phase equation thus serves as a model for what motion should look like in the frequency domain. It defines the ideal signature of motion, against which real-world measurements can be evaluated. The invention leverages this model by assuming that, even in the presence of multiple moving objects and complex backgrounds, the phase change within each localized window will exhibit a similar linear structure if motion is present. The challenge, then, is not to compute the global phase change, which is trivial for a uniformly moving scene, but to detect whether this same linear structure emerges locally, even when the motion is confined to a small portion of the image.

The global phase equation also explains why traditional phase correlation methods, which compare the phase of two discrete frames, are limited in their applicability. These methods assume that the entire scene moves uniformly between frames, which is rarely the case in natural environments. They are also inherently discrete, requiring a fixed time interval between measurements, which introduces latency and temporal aliasing. In contrast, the invention operates continuously, computing the temporal derivative of phase at every time step, thereby capturing motion as it unfolds. The global phase equation provides the theoretical justification for this continuous approach, demonstrating that motion is not a discrete event but a continuous process whose signature is embedded in the time-varying phase of the Fourier transform.

Thus, the global phase equation is not the method of motion detection itself, but the foundational principle that makes the method possible. It establishes that motion induces a predictable, structured change in phase, and that this change can be detected by analyzing the temporal evolution of phase in localized regions. The invention extends this principle from the global to the local, transforming a theoretical insight into a practical, real-time motion detection algorithm.

### The Local Phase Equation for Translational Motion

While the global phase equation provides a clear and elegant description of motion for uniformly translating scenes, real-world visual environments are characterized by localized motion, where objects move independently within a complex background. To detect such motion, it is necessary to generalize the global phase relationship to the local context. The local phase equation for translational motion accomplishes this by extending the Fourier shift theorem to windowed regions of the image, revealing how the temporal derivative of the local phase is influenced by the motion of content within the window’s spatial support.

The derivation of the local phase equation begins with the observation that, for a windowed region of the image, the short-time Fourier transform captures the frequency content of the scene only within the confines of the window. When motion occurs within this region, the intensity pattern shifts relative to the window, inducing a phase change that is analogous to the global case. However, unlike the global phase, where the entire image translates uniformly, the local phase is affected not only by the motion of the content within the window but also by the interaction between the moving content and the fixed boundaries of the window. This interaction introduces an additional term into the phase evolution equation, which accounts for the non-uniformity of the windowed region.

The resulting local phase equation retains the core structure of the global phase equation: the temporal derivative of the local phase is approximately equal to the negative dot product of the frequency vector and the velocity vector. This term dominates the phase change when the motion involves a strong, coherent edge or contrast boundary moving through the window. However, an additional term arises due to the finite support of the window and the non-stationary nature of the signal within it. This term, which depends on the spatial derivatives of the intensity profile and the window function, captures the effects of partial occlusion, edge truncation, and intensity modulation at the window boundaries. While this term introduces complexity, its magnitude is typically small compared to the dominant motion-induced phase change, particularly when the motion involves a well-defined ON or OFF edge moving across the window.

This observation is critical: it means that, despite the mathematical complexity introduced by the windowing operation, the local phase evolution still retains the linear structure of the global phase equation. The motion-induced component remains the primary driver of phase change, while the windowing-induced term acts as a minor perturbation. This allows the system to treat the local phase change as a noisy but structurally valid approximation of the global motion equation. The implication is profound: even in a localized region, the phase change can be interpreted as evidence of motion, provided that the observed pattern conforms to the expected linear relationship between frequency and phase velocity.

The local phase equation thus bridges the gap between theoretical idealism and practical implementation. It confirms that the same principle governing global motion—namely, that phase evolves linearly with frequency in the presence of translation—also holds, to a sufficient degree, for localized motion. This equivalence is what enables the invention to apply the global phase framework to the local domain. The windowing operation, far from being a limitation, becomes a tool for spatial localization, allowing the system to detect motion in specific regions of the image without requiring global coherence.

Moreover, the local phase equation explains why the method is robust to noise and low contrast. Because the dominant term in the equation depends on the product of frequency and velocity, rather than on intensity, the phase change is insensitive to the absolute brightness of the scene. A dimly lit edge moving across a window will induce the same phase evolution as a bright edge, provided that the spatial gradient of intensity is preserved. This invariance is a direct consequence of the structure of the local phase equation, which is derived from the Fourier transform’s sensitivity to spatial derivatives rather than to absolute intensity levels.

The local phase equation, therefore, is not merely an extension of the global phase equation—it is the key to unlocking localized motion detection. It transforms the global principle into a practical, implementable mechanism by demonstrating that motion can be detected in small, independent regions of the image, even when those regions are bounded, noisy, or partially occluded. The equation provides the theoretical justification for treating the temporal derivative of local phase as a reliable motion signal, setting the stage for the subsequent steps of the invention: the extraction of phase changes, their analysis through the Radon transform, and the generation of a motion indicator.

### The Block Structure for Computing the Local Phase

The practical implementation of the local phase-based motion detection method relies on a structured, spatially organized approach to computing and analyzing phase changes across the visual field. This structure is embodied in the block-based processing framework, which divides the image into a grid of overlapping, Gaussian-windowed regions, each processed independently to extract local phase dynamics. The choice of overlapping blocks is not arbitrary; it is essential for ensuring spatial continuity, minimizing boundary artifacts, and enabling the detection of motion at sub-pixel resolutions. The Gaussian window, chosen for its optimal trade-off between spatial and frequency localization, ensures that each block captures a smooth, localized region of the image without introducing sharp discontinuities that could distort the Fourier transform.

Each block is defined by its center position in the image plane, determined by a regular grid spaced at intervals smaller than the width of the Gaussian window. This spacing ensures that the windows overlap significantly, so that motion occurring near the boundary of one block is also captured by adjacent blocks. The size of each block is chosen to be sufficiently large to encompass the effective support of the Gaussian window, typically set to 32 by 32 pixels, which corresponds to a spatial extent of approximately eight standard deviations of the Gaussian. This size is selected not only for computational efficiency—since 32 is a power of two, enabling the use of highly optimized fast Fourier transform algorithms—but also because it provides a balance between spatial resolution and noise robustness. Smaller blocks would be more sensitive to noise due to the reduced number of samples, while larger blocks would risk averaging motion signals from multiple independent objects, reducing localization accuracy.

The processing pipeline for each block proceeds in a standardized sequence. First, the image region within the block is multiplied by the Gaussian window, which tapers the intensity values toward the edges, effectively minimizing spectral leakage in the Fourier transform. The resulting windowed signal is then subjected to a two-dimensional fast Fourier transform, yielding a complex-valued frequency representation for that block. From this representation, the local phase at each spatial frequency is extracted as the argument of the complex number, while the local amplitude is taken as its magnitude. These phase values are then tracked over time, forming a time-series for each frequency component within the block.

To isolate motion-related changes, the temporal derivative of the local phase is computed using a high-pass filter. This filter removes slow-varying components, such as gradual illumination changes or camera drift, while preserving rapid phase transitions that correspond to motion. The high-pass filter is implemented as a simple recursive filter or a finite impulse response filter, ensuring minimal computational overhead. The resulting phase change signal is a two-dimensional map over frequency space, where each point represents the rate of phase change at a specific spatial frequency.

To further enhance robustness, a normalization step is applied to discount phase change measurements in regions of low amplitude. This is achieved by weighting each phase change value by the ratio of the local amplitude to the average amplitude across all frequencies in the block. This normalization ensures that phase changes occurring in low-contrast or textureless regions—where the phase is inherently noisy and unreliable—are downweighted, while phase changes in high-contrast regions, where the signal is strong and stable, are emphasized. This step is critical for reducing false positives and improving the signal-to-noise ratio of the motion detection system.

The block structure is inherently parallel. Each block can be processed independently, with no communication required between neighboring blocks during the computation of phase changes. This independence makes the method ideally suited for implementation on parallel computing architectures such as graphics processing units or field-programmable gate arrays, where thousands of blocks can be processed simultaneously. The entire system can be viewed as a massively parallel array of local motion detectors, each operating on a small region of the image and producing a local motion signal. The aggregation of these signals across the entire image yields a comprehensive motion map, where each block contributes a binary or graded indication of motion presence.

The block structure thus serves as the computational engine of the invention. It transforms the continuous, analog process of phase evolution into a discrete, digital framework that is both mathematically rigorous and practically implementable. By organizing the image into a grid of overlapping, independently processed windows, the system achieves spatial localization, computational efficiency, and noise robustness—all essential properties for real-time motion detection in complex visual environments.

### The Phase-Based Detector

The phase-based detector is the central mechanism through which the temporal evolution of local phase is transformed into a reliable, interpretable motion signal. It operates as a two-stage process: first, it analyzes the phase change map of each block in the frequency domain to identify structured patterns indicative of motion; second, it quantifies the strength and direction of this motion using a geometric transform that exploits the inherent linearity of phase evolution under translation. The detector does not attempt to estimate precise velocity vectors or reconstruct dense motion fields. Instead, it focuses on a simpler, more robust task: determining whether motion is present in a given block and, if so, in which general direction it is occurring.

The first stage of the detector involves the application of the Radon transform to the phase change map of each block. The Radon transform, traditionally used in medical imaging and tomography to reconstruct objects from line integrals, is here repurposed to detect linear structures in the frequency domain. For each block, the phase change map is treated as a two-dimensional scalar field over spatial frequencies. The Radon transform computes the integral of this field along straight lines oriented at various angles, effectively projecting the phase change values onto a one-dimensional space parameterized by angle and radial distance. The result is a Radon transform output that, for a region undergoing uniform motion, exhibits a strong, coherent peak along the direction perpendicular to the motion vector.

This peak arises because, under translational motion, the phase change map is structured as a plane in the frequency domain, with a normal vector aligned with the motion direction. The Radon transform, by integrating along lines perpendicular to this normal, accumulates the phase change values along the direction of maximum variation, producing a high response. Conversely, for regions with no motion, the phase change map is noisy and unstructured, and the Radon transform yields a flat, low-amplitude response across all angles. This distinction allows the detector to discriminate between true motion and noise with high reliability.

The second stage of the detector computes the Phase Motion Indicator for each block by identifying the angular orientation that maximizes the magnitude of the Radon transform output. This maximum value serves as a scalar measure of motion likelihood: a high Phase Motion Indicator indicates that the phase change map exhibits a strong linear structure consistent with motion, while a low value indicates randomness or absence of motion. A threshold is then applied to this indicator to determine whether motion is detected in the block. The direction of motion is inferred from the angle at which the maximum occurs, providing a coarse estimate of motion orientation.

This approach is fundamentally different from traditional motion detection methods. Rather than comparing pixel intensities across frames or solving complex optimization problems to estimate flow vectors, the phase-based detector exploits the geometric structure of phase evolution in the frequency domain. It does not require knowledge of the image content, nor does it assume smoothness or continuity of motion. It operates on the principle that motion induces a predictable, linear pattern in the phase change map, and that this pattern can be detected through a simple, well-defined transform.

The detector is also highly resilient to noise. Because the Radon transform integrates over multiple frequency components, it effectively averages out random fluctuations that do not conform to the linear structure of motion. Noise, which is typically distributed uniformly across frequencies, produces a weak, diffuse response in the Radon domain, while motion produces a sharp, localized peak. This property allows the system to operate effectively in low-contrast, high-noise environments where other methods fail.

Furthermore, the detector is computationally efficient. The Radon transform can be computed using fast algorithms based on the Fourier slice theorem, which relates the Radon transform to the one-dimensional Fourier transform of the phase change map along radial lines. This allows the transform to be implemented using a combination of fast Fourier transforms and interpolation, maintaining real-time performance even for high-resolution video streams. The entire detection process—phase extraction, temporal differentiation, normalization, Radon transform, and thresholding—can be executed in a single, streamlined pipeline with minimal memory overhead.

The phase-based detector, therefore, represents a paradigm shift in motion detection. It replaces the traditional reliance on intensity-based comparisons with a geometric analysis of phase dynamics, leveraging the mathematical structure of the Fourier domain to extract motion signals with unprecedented robustness and efficiency. It is not merely an algorithm—it is a new framework for perceiving motion, grounded in the physics of signal representation and the geometry of phase evolution.

### Radon Transform on the Change of Phases

The application of the Radon transform to the change of local phases is the pivotal innovation that enables the phase-based motion detector to distinguish true motion from noise and to extract directional information with high reliability. The Radon transform, in its classical form, computes the integral of a two-dimensional function along straight lines at various angles and distances from the origin. In this invention, it is applied to the phase change map of each block, treating the phase change as a scalar field over the spatial frequency domain. The transform effectively collapses the two-dimensional frequency space into a one-dimensional projection, where each point corresponds to a line integral of phase change values along a specific orientation.

The significance of this operation lies in its ability to reveal hidden structure. In a region undergoing uniform translational motion, the phase change map exhibits a linear dependence on frequency: the phase change at each frequency point is proportional to the dot product of the frequency vector and the motion velocity vector. This relationship defines a plane in the frequency domain, with a normal vector aligned perpendicular to the motion direction. The Radon transform, when applied to such a field, produces a strong, localized peak in the projection space at the angle corresponding to the motion direction. This peak arises because the integral along lines perpendicular to the motion direction accumulates the maximum variation in phase change, while integrals along other directions average out the signal.

In contrast, in regions without motion, the phase change map is dominated by noise, which is distributed randomly across frequencies. The Radon transform of such a map yields a flat, low-amplitude response across all angles, with no dominant peak. This stark contrast between structured and unstructured phase change patterns is what allows the detector to reliably identify motion. The Radon transform acts as a filter, suppressing noise and enhancing the signal that conforms to the expected linear structure of motion.

The mathematical formulation of the Radon transform in this context is carefully adapted to the bounded frequency domain of the discrete Fourier transform. The phase change map is defined only over a finite set of frequency components, and the Radon transform must account for the varying lengths of integration lines within this bounded domain. To correct for this, a normalization factor is introduced that scales each line integral by the length of the integration path within the bounded frequency region. This correction ensures that the magnitude of the Radon transform is not biased by the geometry of the frequency grid and that the resulting motion indicator is consistent across all orientations.

The output of the Radon transform is a two-dimensional function of angle and radial distance, which can be visualized as a sinogram. The motion direction is determined by finding the angle at which the maximum magnitude occurs, while the strength of the motion is quantified by the magnitude of this maximum. This process is repeated for each block, producing a sparse motion map where each block is labeled with a binary decision (motion or no motion) and an associated direction estimate.

The Radon transform is chosen not merely for its mathematical properties but for its computational efficiency. It can be implemented using the Fourier slice theorem, which states that the one-dimensional Fourier transform of a Radon projection is equal to a slice through the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the original function. This allows the Radon transform to be computed using a combination of fast Fourier transforms and interpolation, making it feasible for real-time implementation on parallel hardware. The entire process—from phase extraction to Radon transform to motion detection—can be executed in a single, optimized pipeline with minimal latency.

The Radon transform thus serves as the critical link between the raw phase change data and the final motion signal. It transforms an ambiguous, high-dimensional signal into a clear, interpretable indicator of motion presence and direction. Without this step, the phase change map would be too noisy and too complex to interpret reliably. With it, the system achieves a level of robustness and accuracy that is unmatched by traditional motion detection methods.

### Examples of Phase-Based Motion Detection

The practical efficacy of the phase-based motion detection system is demonstrated through a series of real-world video sequences, each presenting distinct challenges in illumination, contrast, noise, and motion complexity. In the highway surveillance video, the system successfully detects the motion of vehicles against a textured, non-uniform background, including the subtle motion of tree leaves stirred by wind. The phase-based detector distinguishes between these two types of motion by assigning high Phase Motion Indicator values to blocks covering the vehicles, while assigning low values to blocks covering the foliage, despite the fact that both exhibit temporal intensity changes. This discrimination is possible because the motion of the vehicles induces a coherent, structured phase change across multiple spatial frequencies, whereas the motion of leaves produces a diffuse, unstructured phase change that lacks the linear signature of translation.

In the train station video, the system detects the motion of pedestrians moving in multiple directions, even in regions where illumination is low and contrast is reduced. Traditional amplitude-based detectors often fail in such conditions, mistaking low-light areas for static backgrounds or generating false motion signals due to sensor noise. The phase-based detector, however, remains robust, because its response depends not on the absolute intensity of the scene but on the spatial reorganization of intensity patterns. Even when the luminance is reduced, the edges and contours of moving objects remain detectable through their phase signatures, allowing the system to track motion with high fidelity.

The thermal video presents an even greater challenge, characterized by high levels of background noise and low signal-to-noise ratios. In this scenario, the system employs an elevated detection threshold to suppress false positives, yet still manages to identify the motion of people and vehicles with remarkable accuracy. The normalized phase change metric, which downweights measurements in low-amplitude regions, plays a critical role in this performance. By suppressing phase changes in noisy, textureless areas, the system avoids being overwhelmed by sensor artifacts, focusing instead on regions where motion-induced phase structure is strong and consistent.

The winterstreet video, captured at night with uneven illumination, further illustrates the system’s adaptability. On the well-lit, snow-covered portion of the road, motion is detected with high confidence. On the darker, shadowed side, where illumination is minimal, the system still identifies the motion of vehicles, albeit with reduced confidence. This partial detection is not a failure but a feature: the system correctly reports motion only where the phase change is sufficiently structured to be reliable. This conservative approach ensures that the output remains trustworthy, even in extreme conditions.

Comparisons with the Reichardt and Barlow-Levick motion detectors reveal the superiority of the phase-based method under low-contrast conditions. When the intensity range of the highway video is compressed to simulate low contrast, the phase-based detector retains over 50% of its motion detection capability, while the Reichardt detector’s performance drops to less than 20%, and the Barlow-Levick detector’s response becomes linearly proportional to contrast, rendering it nearly ineffective. This resilience stems from the phase-based method’s independence from intensity scaling, a property that is mathematically inherent in the structure of the local phase equation.

The detection of motion direction is equally compelling. In the phase change maps, the motion direction is visually apparent as a line separating regions of positive and negative phase change, a direct consequence of the linear relationship between frequency and phase velocity. The Radon transform extracts this direction automatically, producing a coherent orientation estimate for each block. This is in stark contrast to the Reichardt detector, which often produces ambiguous or reversed motion directions, particularly for slow-moving objects, and the Barlow-Levick detector, which exhibits directional bias due to its reliance on asymmetric inhibition.

The system’s performance is further validated by its ability to segment moving objects in video sequences. When a higher threshold is applied, the system isolates only the most salient moving objects, such as vehicles, while ignoring minor motion cues such as swaying leaves or flickering lights. Subsequent refinement using smaller blocks at the boundaries of detected regions allows for tighter segmentation, reducing the blocky artifacts inherent in the coarse grid. The resulting motion segmentation is comparable to, and in many cases superior to, that produced by optic flow algorithms, despite the fact that the phase-based method requires no iterative optimization, no flow smoothness constraints, and no global velocity estimation.

These examples collectively demonstrate that the phase-based motion detection system is not merely a theoretical construct but a practical, deployable technology. It performs reliably across a wide range of environmental conditions, outperforms established biological and artificial models, and provides motion information that is both accurate and interpretable. Its success lies in its alignment with the fundamental principles of signal representation and its ability to exploit the geometric structure of phase evolution in the frequency domain.
