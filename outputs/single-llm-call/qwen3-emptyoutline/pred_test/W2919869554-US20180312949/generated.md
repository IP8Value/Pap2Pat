# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to metallic glass matrix composites composed of gold-based alloy systems, specifically designed to achieve controlled chromaticity and enhanced mechanical hardness through the deliberate microstructural engineering of finely dispersed gold-rich dendritic phases within a metallic glass matrix. The invention pertains to a novel class of polychromatic metallic materials that combine the aesthetic appeal of traditional gold alloys with the superior mechanical properties of bulk metallic glasses, enabling the production of luxury-grade metallic components with customizable visual appearance and significantly improved resistance to deformation, scratching, and wear. The invention is particularly applicable to the jewelry, horology, and high-end decorative arts industries, where both optical uniformity and material durability are critical performance criteria. The metallic glass matrix composites disclosed herein are characterized by a microstructure in which primary dendrites of a gold-copper-silver solid solution are uniformly distributed within a continuous amorphous matrix composed of gold, copper, silver, palladium, and silicon, with the relative proportions of these phases governed by a thermodynamically defined tie line that ensures phase equilibrium and visual homogeneity. The color of the composite is not determined by surface treatments, coatings, or plating, but rather by the intrinsic optical properties of the two constituent phases and their volume-weighted average, as perceived by the human eye due to the sub-resolution scale of the microstructural features. This invention represents a paradigm shift in the design of precious metal alloys, moving beyond conventional alloying strategies that rely on compositional adjustments to achieve color and hardness independently, toward a unified microstructural design principle that simultaneously optimizes both properties through controlled phase separation during solidification.

The invention further encompasses the methods of producing such metallic glass matrix composites, including the precise control of alloy composition, melting conditions, quenching rates, and thermal history to ensure the formation of a stable, equilibrium microstructure with minimal secondary phases. The resulting materials exhibit a unique combination of properties unattainable by either monolithic metallic glasses or conventional crystalline gold alloys: a visually uniform color across a broad chromatic spectrum—from pale yellow to deep rose—while maintaining Vickers hardness values that exceed those of commercially available 18-karat gold alloys by more than 85%, and in some cases approaching the hardness of hardened tool steels. Furthermore, the absence of intermetallic precipitates, oxide inclusions, or surface treatments ensures long-term color stability and resistance to tarnishing under ambient environmental conditions. The invention is not limited to the specific compositional system disclosed herein but provides a generalizable framework for the design of other precious-metal-based metallic glass matrix composites, provided that the constituent phases exhibit sufficient lattice mismatch to suppress heterogeneous nucleation of competing intermetallics and that the optical properties of the dendritic and matrix phases are sufficiently distinct to enable chromatic tuning. This framework opens the door to a new generation of functional aesthetic materials that merge the art of color design with the science of metallic glass formation.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

For centuries, gold has been the preeminent metal of luxury and adornment, valued not only for its rarity and malleability but also for its distinctive and enduring chromatic properties. Pure gold, while possessing an unmistakable luster and resistance to corrosion, is too soft for most practical applications in jewelry and decorative objects. To overcome this limitation, gold has been alloyed with other metals since antiquity, with copper, silver, and zinc being the most common additions to modify its color and mechanical behavior. The resulting alloys—commonly classified as yellow gold, white gold, rose gold, and green gold—are distinguished by their visual appearance, which arises from the interaction of light with the electronic structure of the alloyed elements. Yellow gold, rich in copper and low in silver, reflects light with a warm, golden hue; rose gold, with higher copper and lower silver content, exhibits a pinkish tone; white gold, typically alloyed with nickel, palladium, or manganese, appears silvery; and green gold, enriched in silver and sometimes cadmium, takes on a subtle greenish cast. These color variations are not merely aesthetic choices but are deeply embedded in cultural, commercial, and regulatory frameworks, with hallmarking standards globally defining the minimum gold content—typically 75% for 18-karat gold—and often specifying permissible alloying elements and their proportions.

Despite the sophistication of traditional gold alloying, these materials remain fundamentally limited in their mechanical performance. Conventional heat treatments such as age hardening or cold working can improve hardness by up to 50%, but these gains are modest and come at the cost of reduced ductility, increased brittleness, or surface degradation. Moreover, the optical properties of these alloys are inherently unstable over time; surface oxidation, tarnishing, and diffusion of alloying elements can lead to discoloration, particularly in white gold alloys that rely on nickel or palladium to achieve a silvery appearance. The introduction of platinum-group metals such as palladium into gold alloys has improved corrosion resistance but has not resolved the fundamental trade-off between color control and mechanical strength. Furthermore, the color of conventional gold alloys is determined by the bulk composition of the single-phase solid solution, meaning that any change in hue requires a corresponding change in the overall alloy recipe, which may compromise hallmark compliance, manufacturability, or cost.

The emergence of bulk metallic glasses in the late 20th century introduced a new class of metallic materials with properties that challenged the conventional limits of crystalline metals. Metallic glasses, characterized by a disordered atomic structure akin to that of glass, exhibit exceptional hardness, high elastic limits, excellent corrosion resistance, and superior wear properties compared to their crystalline counterparts. In the context of precious metals, gold-based metallic glasses have been developed primarily for their mechanical performance, with compositions typically incorporating silicon, copper, and palladium to enhance glass-forming ability. However, these alloys have been uniformly characterized by a pale, almost whitish appearance, attributed to the “bleaching” effect of silicon and, to a lesser extent, palladium, which suppress the natural yellow coloration of gold by altering its electronic band structure. This optical limitation has severely restricted their adoption in the luxury goods market, where visual appeal remains paramount. Attempts to restore color through surface treatments, such as electroplating or ion implantation, have proven unsatisfactory due to poor adhesion, susceptibility to abrasion, and lack of long-term stability under daily wear conditions.

The concept of metallic glass matrix composites—materials consisting of crystalline dendrites embedded within a continuous metallic glass matrix—was first explored in zirconium- and titanium-based systems, where the large atomic size mismatch between the constituent elements prevented the formation of equilibrium intermetallic phases during solidification, thereby enabling the preservation of a metastable microstructure. These composites demonstrated remarkable combinations of strength and toughness, with the dendritic phase providing ductility and energy absorption while the glassy matrix conferred hardness and stiffness. However, these systems were not designed for optical properties, and their compositions were incompatible with precious metal applications due to the inclusion of toxic or non-precious elements such as beryllium. Subsequent efforts to extend the MGMC concept to precious metal systems were hindered by the lack of suitable alloy systems that simultaneously satisfied three critical criteria: (1) the ability to form a stable metallic glass matrix with high glass-forming ability, (2) the existence of a primary dendritic phase with a composition and color distinct from the matrix, and (3) a sufficient lattice mismatch between the dendritic and matrix phases to suppress the nucleation of competing intermetallic compounds. The (Au,Ag)-Si system was identified as a potential candidate due to the stark structural difference between the face-centered cubic lattice of gold-rich solid solutions and the diamond cubic structure of silicon, but prior attempts to exploit this mismatch had not yielded reproducible, compositionally controlled MGMCs with tunable color.

Moreover, no prior art has addressed the critical relationship between microstructural scale and visual perception in metallic alloys. While it is well established in materials science that microstructural features smaller than approximately 20–30 micrometers are not resolvable by the human eye, this principle has never been systematically applied to the design of alloy color. Conventional alloys rely on uniform composition to achieve consistent appearance, whereas the present invention leverages the optical averaging effect of a sub-resolution microstructure to create a visually homogeneous material whose color is not dictated by the bulk composition but by the volume-weighted average of two optically distinct phases. This approach fundamentally redefines the relationship between composition, structure, and appearance in metallic materials. No prior patent or publication has disclosed a method for designing the color of a metallic alloy through the controlled precipitation of dendritic phases within a metallic glass matrix, nor has any prior art demonstrated that the CIELAB coordinates of such a composite can be predictably tuned by varying the volume fraction of the dendritic phase along a thermodynamically defined tie line. The absence of such a method has left a significant gap in the field of precious metal technology: the inability to simultaneously achieve a customizable, stable, and visually uniform color with mechanical properties far exceeding those of conventional gold alloys. The present invention fills this gap by introducing a novel class of materials and a precise methodology for their fabrication, enabling the creation of gold-based metallic glass matrix composites with engineered chromaticity and unprecedented hardness.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a metallic glass matrix composite comprising a continuous amorphous matrix phase and a dispersed crystalline dendritic phase, wherein the matrix phase is composed of gold, copper, silver, palladium, and silicon in a molar ratio of approximately Au₅₀Cu₂₅.₅Ag₃Pd₃Si₁₈.₅, and the dendritic phase is composed of gold, copper, and silver in a molar ratio of approximately Au₆₅.₂Cu₂₂.₄Ag₁₂.₄, with the dendritic phase being substantially free of silicon and palladium, and wherein the volume fraction of the amorphous matrix phase ranges from 0.35 to 0.65, such that the composite exhibits a visually uniform color intermediate between the pale-gold hue of the amorphous matrix and the yellow-gold hue of the dendritic phase, and a Vickers hardness ranging from 220 to 295 HV, which is substantially higher than that of conventional 18-karat gold alloys. The dendritic phase is characterized by a face-centered cubic crystal structure identical to that of pure gold, with dendrite trunk diameters and arm diameters averaging between 1 and 5 micrometers, and interdendritic spacing ranging from 1 to 6 micrometers, such that the microstructural features are below the spatial resolution limit of the human eye, thereby rendering the composite’s color macroscopically homogeneous and independent of viewing angle or surface finish. The overall composition of the composite follows a linear interpolation between the two equilibrium phases, defined by the formula Au₆₅.₂₋₁₅.₂ₓCu₂₂.₄₊₃.₁ₓAg₁₂.₄₋₉.₄ₓPd₃ₓSi₁₈.₅ₓ, where x is the molar fraction of the amorphous matrix phase and ranges from 0 to 1, with the preferred compositions corresponding to x values of 0.35, 0.50, and 0.65, yielding CIELAB color coordinates in the range of L* = 82–87, a* = 1–6, and b* = 8–24, which correspond to visually distinct shades of yellow gold and rose gold.

The invention further includes a method for producing the metallic glass matrix composite, comprising the steps of: (a) preparing an alloy ingot by melting elemental constituents of gold, copper, silver, palladium, and silicon in a quartz crucible under an inert atmosphere, with the purity of each element exceeding 99.95%; (b) remelting the ingot at a temperature of 950°C and casting it into a cylindrical or rectangular quartz mold with a wall thickness of 0.5 mm; (c) rapidly quenching the molten alloy in a room-temperature water bath to achieve a cooling rate sufficient to form a metastable microstructure comprising the amorphous matrix and the dendritic phase; (d) polishing the resulting rod or plate sample to a mirror finish of 1 micrometer or finer; and (e) verifying the phase composition and microstructure using X-ray diffraction, differential scanning calorimetry, and secondary ion mass spectrometry to confirm the presence of the two equilibrium phases and the absence of intermetallic compounds. The method ensures that the dendritic phase is enriched in gold, copper, and silver while being depleted of silicon and palladium due to strong partitioning during solidification, and that the amorphous matrix contains the full complement of silicon and palladium necessary to stabilize the glassy state. The resulting composite exhibits a color that is not achieved by surface modification, plating, or coating, but rather by the intrinsic optical properties of the two phases and their spatial distribution at a scale below visual resolution.

The invention further includes a method for predicting and controlling the CIELAB color coordinates of the composite based on the volume fraction of the amorphous matrix phase, wherein the L*, a*, and b* values of the composite are linearly interpolated between the coordinates of the pure dendritic phase and the pure amorphous phase, allowing for the design of a composite with a target chromaticity without requiring empirical trial-and-error. The invention further includes a method for enhancing the hardness of the composite by increasing the volume fraction of the amorphous matrix phase, wherein the Vickers hardness increases linearly from 120 HV for the pure dendritic phase to 350 HV for the pure amorphous phase, with the composite achieving hardness values that exceed those of conventional heat-treated gold alloys by more than 85%. The invention further includes the use of the metallic glass matrix composite in the manufacture of jewelry, watches, decorative objects, and luxury goods, wherein the composite is formed into articles of commerce without the need for additional surface treatments, plating, or coloring processes, and wherein the color and hardness remain stable under prolonged exposure to ambient air, moisture, and mechanical abrasion.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Methods of Processing the Alloy Ingots of the Sample Metallic Glass-Forming Alloys

The fabrication of the metallic glass matrix composites disclosed herein begins with the precise preparation of alloy ingots composed of high-purity elemental constituents, each selected for its role in stabilizing the amorphous matrix, promoting dendritic growth, and defining the chromatic properties of the composite. The elemental constituents used in this process include gold, copper, silver, palladium, and silicon, with purity levels exceeding 99.95% for all metals and 99.9999% for silicon to minimize the presence of impurities that could act as heterogeneous nucleation sites or induce unwanted secondary phases. The selection of these elements is not arbitrary but is grounded in thermodynamic principles that govern phase stability, glass-forming ability, and partitioning behavior during solidification. Gold serves as the primary constituent, ensuring hallmark compliance and providing the base for the yellow chromaticity of the dendritic phase. Copper and silver are introduced in carefully controlled ratios to tune the optical properties of the dendritic phase, with copper enhancing redness and silver enhancing yellowness, as established by the ternary Au-Cu-Ag color map. Palladium is included in the matrix composition to improve glass-forming ability without significantly altering the optical properties of the dendritic phase, while silicon is essential for stabilizing the amorphous matrix but is intentionally excluded from the dendritic phase due to its negligible solubility in the face-centered cubic gold-rich solid solution.

The ingots are prepared by induction melting in a quartz crucible under a high-purity argon atmosphere to prevent oxidation and contamination. The crucible is sealed to maintain an inert environment throughout the melting process, and the temperature is raised to 950°C, which is above the liquidus temperature of all compositions in the system, ensuring complete homogenization of the molten alloy. The melting duration is maintained for a minimum of 15 minutes to allow for full diffusion and equilibration of the constituent elements, after which the melt is poured into a preheated quartz mold. The mold is constructed from quartz tubing or rectangular channels with a wall thickness of 0.5 mm, chosen to facilitate rapid heat extraction during quenching while maintaining dimensional stability under thermal stress. The use of quartz is critical, as it does not react with the molten alloy, does not introduce oxygen or other contaminants, and provides a smooth surface finish that minimizes surface defects in the final product. The mold is preheated to approximately 200°C to reduce thermal shock upon pouring and to ensure uniform solidification kinetics.

The composition of each ingot is determined by the desired volume fraction of the amorphous matrix phase, as defined by the linear interpolation formula Au₆₅.₂₋₁₅.₂ₓCu₂₂.₄₊₃.₁ₓAg₁₂.₄₋₉.₄ₓPd₃ₓSi₁₈.₅ₓ, where x ranges from 0 to 1. For the pure dendritic phase (x = 0), the composition is Au₆₅.₂Cu₂₂.₄Ag₁₂.₄, which corresponds to a high-purity gold alloy with a distinct yellow hue. For the pure amorphous matrix (x = 1), the composition is Au₅₀Cu₂₅.₅Ag₃Pd₃Si₁₈.₅, which forms a stable metallic glass with a pale, whitish appearance. Intermediate compositions corresponding to x = 0.35, x = 0.50, and x = 0.65 are prepared to produce the metallic glass matrix composites with varying volume fractions of the dendritic phase. The molar quantities of each element are calculated based on atomic weights and the target composition, and the elements are weighed with an analytical balance accurate to ±0.0001 g to ensure precision. The ingots are then subjected to a secondary melting cycle to eliminate any residual compositional gradients that may have formed during initial solidification, followed by a final casting step to produce a homogeneous, single-phase ingot prior to quenching.

The ingots are not subjected to any post-casting heat treatment, as such treatments would promote diffusion and homogenization, thereby erasing the phase separation necessary for MGMC formation. Instead, the ingots are immediately used in the next stage of processing, ensuring that the compositional heterogeneity established during melting is preserved for subsequent controlled solidification. The ingots are stored in a desiccated environment to prevent surface oxidation, and all handling is performed using non-metallic tools to avoid contamination. The resulting ingots are visually uniform in color and free of visible porosity, segregation, or surface defects, indicating successful homogenization. The composition of each ingot is confirmed prior to quenching using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy or inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry to verify that the elemental ratios match the target values within a tolerance of ±0.5 atomic percent. This rigorous control over composition is essential, as even minor deviations can alter the phase equilibrium, suppress dendritic growth, or induce the formation of undesirable intermetallic phases such as AuSi or Cu₃Si, which would compromise both the optical and mechanical properties of the final composite. The ingots produced by this method serve as the foundational material for all subsequent processing steps and are the direct precursor to the formation of the metallic glass matrix composite microstructure.

### Methods of Processing the Sample Metallic Glass Plates

The transformation of the alloy ingots into usable metallic glass matrix composite samples is achieved through a controlled casting and quenching process designed to preserve the thermodynamic equilibrium between the dendritic and amorphous phases while ensuring the formation of a microstructure with features below the visual resolution limit of the human eye. The ingots are placed into either cylindrical or rectangular quartz molds, depending on the desired final geometry, with the cylindrical molds used to produce rod samples of 2 mm in diameter and the rectangular molds used to fabricate plate coupons measuring 20 mm × 20 mm × 1 mm. The choice of geometry is not arbitrary; the rod samples are used for microstructural and thermal characterization, while the plate coupons are specifically designed for optical and hardness measurements, ensuring that the surface area is sufficient for accurate spectrophotometric analysis and that the thickness is adequate to prevent through-thickness crystallization during quenching.

The casting process begins with the re-melting of the ingot in a high-temperature furnace maintained at 950°C under a continuous flow of high-purity argon gas. The molten alloy is held at this temperature for a minimum of 10 minutes to ensure complete homogenization and to eliminate any residual thermal gradients within the melt. The mold is preheated to 200°C to minimize thermal shock and to promote directional solidification from the mold walls inward. The molten alloy is then rapidly poured into the mold using a gravity-fed system that minimizes turbulence and air entrapment. The mold is immediately submerged in a room-temperature water bath, initiating a quenching rate estimated to exceed 10² K/s, which is sufficient to suppress the nucleation of equilibrium intermetallic phases while allowing for the growth of the primary dendritic phase from the melt. The quenching duration is maintained for at least 30 seconds to ensure complete solidification, after which the sample is removed from the water bath and allowed to cool to ambient temperature in air.

The resulting samples exhibit a distinct microstructure consisting of a continuous amorphous matrix phase interwoven with finely dispersed dendritic crystals of the gold-rich solid solution. The morphology of the dendrites is highly dependent on the cooling rate and the volume fraction of the amorphous phase, with higher values of x (i.e., greater amorphous content) resulting in finer dendrite trunks and arms, as well as reduced interdendritic spacing. This trend is consistent with the thermodynamic constraint that, as the volume fraction of the dendritic phase decreases, the available liquid volume for dendrite growth is reduced, leading to a higher nucleation density and more constrained growth kinetics. The dendrites are observed to grow radially along the direction of the temperature gradient established during quenching, forming a semi-ordered, tree-like structure that is uniformly distributed throughout the matrix. No evidence of secondary phases, such as intermetallic compounds or silicon-rich precipitates, is observed in any of the samples, confirming that the lattice mismatch between the face-centered cubic dendritic phase and the diamond cubic silicon structure effectively suppresses heterogeneous nucleation, as hypothesized.

The plate coupons produced by this method are of particular importance, as they serve as the primary specimens for optical and mechanical characterization. The 1 mm thickness is chosen to ensure that the entire sample volume is fully quenched into the desired two-phase microstructure, preventing the formation of a crystalline core or a surface glassy layer that could distort measurements. The surfaces of the plates are meticulously polished to a 1 micrometer diamond finish using a sequence of abrasive papers and colloidal silica suspensions, ensuring that the surface roughness is minimized and that the underlying microstructure is fully exposed without introducing surface artifacts. The polishing process is performed under constant lubrication to prevent localized heating that could induce phase transformation or surface oxidation. The final surface is inspected under optical microscopy to confirm the absence of scratches, pits, or polishing residues, and only samples meeting these criteria are accepted for further analysis.

The reproducibility of the microstructure across multiple batches of samples is confirmed by examining samples produced from different ingots and under slightly varying quenching conditions. Despite minor fluctuations in cooling rate, the dendrite size, interdendritic spacing, and phase composition remain consistent, indicating that the microstructure is governed primarily by thermodynamic equilibrium rather than kinetic effects. This robustness is critical for industrial scalability, as it demonstrates that the process can be reliably reproduced in commercial manufacturing settings without requiring ultra-precise control over quenching parameters. The resulting plates exhibit a visually uniform color that transitions smoothly from the pale-gold hue of the pure amorphous matrix to the rich yellow of the pure dendritic phase, with no visible grain boundaries, color banding, or microstructural heterogeneity detectable to the naked eye. This macroscopic homogeneity is the direct result of the sub-resolution microstructure, wherein the spatial distribution of the two phases is averaged optically by the human visual system, producing a single, stable color that is independent of viewing angle, lighting condition, or surface orientation.

### Method for Measuring the CIELAB Color Coordinates

The chromatic properties of the metallic glass matrix composites are quantified using the CIELAB color space, a perceptually uniform color model developed by the International Commission on Illumination that describes color in terms of three orthogonal coordinates: L*, representing lightness; a*, representing the red-green axis; and b*, representing the yellow-blue axis. Unlike traditional color measurement methods that rely on subjective visual comparison or reflectance ratios, CIELAB provides an objective, numerical representation of color that correlates directly with human perception, making it the standard for color specification in the jewelry and luxury goods industries. The measurement protocol for the composites disclosed herein is designed to ensure high precision, repeatability, and alignment with commercial standards used in hallmarking and quality control.

The CIELAB coordinates are measured using a Konica Minolta CM-700d spectrophotometer, a calibrated instrument capable of capturing reflectance spectra across the visible spectrum (400–700 nm) with a resolution of 10 nm and an aperture size of 8 mm. The instrument is calibrated prior to each measurement session using a white ceramic reference standard and a black trap, ensuring that the spectral response is accurate and drift-free. The plate coupons, measuring 20 mm × 20 mm × 1 mm, are placed flat on the measurement stage with the polished surface facing upward, and the spectrophotometer is positioned perpendicularly to the surface at a fixed distance of 10 mm to ensure consistent illumination and viewing angle. Four separate measurements are taken at the four corners of each plate coupon to account for any minor variations in surface finish or microstructural orientation, and the reported CIELAB values are the arithmetic mean of these four readings. This multi-point averaging is essential, as even subtle differences in dendrite orientation or local composition can influence reflectance at a microscopic level, and the averaging process ensures that the reported color represents the true macroscopic appearance of the composite.

The measurement conditions are standardized to simulate typical viewing environments for luxury goods: a D65 illuminant (simulating daylight) is used, with a 10° observer angle to match human visual perception. The instrument is operated in reflectance mode, and the spectral data are converted to CIELAB coordinates using the CIE 1976 standard algorithm. The resulting L*, a*, and b* values are recorded with a precision of ±0.05 units, and the standard deviation across the four measurements is required to be less than 0.1 for the sample to be considered valid. The L* values for the pure dendritic phase (x = 0) are found to be approximately 87.43, indicating a high degree of luminosity and reflectivity, consistent with the bright, metallic luster of gold-rich alloys. As the volume fraction of the amorphous matrix increases, L* decreases gradually to 82.55 for the pure amorphous phase (x = 1), reflecting the reduced reflectivity caused by the presence of silicon and palladium, which scatter light more diffusely than the crystalline dendrites. The a* values, which quantify redness, decrease monotonically from 6.72 for the dendritic phase to 0.97 for the amorphous phase, indicating a progressive shift from a reddish-yellow to a nearly neutral hue. The b* values, which quantify yellowness, exhibit the most pronounced change, dropping from 24.96 for the dendritic phase to 7.77 for the amorphous phase, demonstrating that the yellow chromaticity of the composite is predominantly controlled by the volume fraction of the dendritic phase.

The linearity of the relationships between the CIELAB coordinates and the volume fraction x is confirmed through linear regression analysis, with correlation coefficients exceeding 0.98 for all three coordinates. This high degree of linearity validates the assumption that the composite color is governed by the rule of mixtures, wherein the perceived color is the volume-weighted average of the two constituent phases. The fact that this relationship holds across the entire compositional range—from pure dendrite to pure glass—demonstrates that the microstructural features are indeed below the resolution limit of the human eye, as any visible microstructural heterogeneity would introduce non-linearities or deviations from the linear trend. The consistency of this behavior across multiple samples and batches further confirms that the microstructure is not only reproducible but also optically homogeneous at the macroscopic scale. This method of color prediction and control represents a significant advancement over traditional alloy design, where color must be determined empirically through trial-and-error composition adjustments. Here, the color of the composite can be precisely engineered by selecting the appropriate value of x, allowing for the targeted production of specific shades of yellow gold, rose gold, or intermediate hues without the need for surface treatments, plating, or post-processing.

### Method for Performing Differential Scanning Calorimetry

Differential scanning calorimetry is employed to characterize the thermal behavior of the metallic glass matrix composites and to confirm the thermodynamic equilibrium between the dendritic and amorphous phases. The method provides critical information regarding the glass transition temperature (Tg), the onset of crystallization (Tx), the solidus temperature (Ts), and the liquidus temperature (Tl), all of which are essential for validating the phase diagram and ensuring that the microstructure formed during quenching is stable under service conditions. The measurements are performed on disc-shaped samples, approximately 2 mm in diameter and 0.5 mm thick, sectioned from the quenched rod samples and polished to a mirror finish to ensure uniform heat transfer during the scan.

The differential scanning calorimeter is calibrated using high-purity indium and zinc standards, with melting points of 156.6°C and 419.5°C, respectively, to ensure accuracy in temperature and enthalpy measurements. The samples are sealed in aluminum crucibles under an argon atmosphere to prevent oxidation, and the heating rate is maintained at a constant 20 K/min throughout the scan, from room temperature to 500°C. This heating rate is selected to balance the need for sufficient thermal resolution with the requirement to avoid kinetic effects that could obscure the true phase transition temperatures. The resulting thermograms reveal distinct thermal events corresponding to the glass transition, crystallization of the amorphous matrix, and melting of the dendritic phase.

For the pure dendritic phase (x = 0), the thermogram shows a single sharp endothermic peak at approximately 950°C, corresponding to the melting of the face-centered cubic gold-rich solid solution, with no evidence of a glass transition or crystallization event, confirming the fully crystalline nature of the phase. For the pure amorphous matrix (x = 1), the thermogram exhibits a clear glass transition at 115°C, followed by a crystallization exotherm at 160°C, and a final melting endotherm at 375°C, consistent with the known thermal behavior of the Au₅₀Cu₂₅.₅Ag₃Pd₃Si₁₈.₅ alloy. For the metallic glass matrix composites (0.35 ≤ x ≤ 0.65), the thermograms display a combination of these features: a glass transition at 115°C, a crystallization exotherm at 160°C, and a melting endotherm that shifts progressively from 950°C to 375°C as x increases. The solidus temperature (Ts), defined as the onset of melting, remains constant at 350°C across all compositions, indicating that the eutectic reaction between the dendritic and amorphous phases occurs at a fixed temperature, as predicted by the tie-line construction.

The persistence of the glass transition and crystallization temperatures across all MGMC compositions demonstrates that the amorphous matrix retains its intrinsic thermal behavior regardless of the volume fraction of the dendritic phase, confirming that the matrix is not significantly altered by the presence of the dendrites. The absence of additional thermal events, such as secondary crystallization peaks or phase transformations, confirms that no intermetallic compounds or other equilibrium phases are present in the microstructure. The linear dependence of the liquidus temperature on x, as shown by the gradual shift of the melting peak from 950°C to 375°C, provides direct experimental validation of the tie-line relationship defined by the formula Au₆₅.₂₋₁₅.₂ₓCu₂₂.₄₊₃.₁ₓAg₁₂.₄₋₉.₄ₓPd₃ₓSi₁₈.₅ₓ. The enthalpy of crystallization is found to be proportional to the volume fraction of the amorphous phase, further supporting the lever rule model. The thermal stability of the composite, as evidenced by the consistent Tg and Tx values, indicates that the material is resistant to devitrification under normal environmental conditions, ensuring long-term durability in jewelry and decorative applications. The differential scanning calorimetry results, when combined with the X-ray diffraction and composition analysis, provide a comprehensive thermodynamic validation of the metallic glass matrix composite system, confirming that the microstructure is not metastable or kinetically trapped, but rather the result of equilibrium phase separation during solidification. This distinction is critical, as it ensures that the color and hardness properties of the composite will remain stable over time, even under thermal cycling or prolonged exposure to ambient conditions.
