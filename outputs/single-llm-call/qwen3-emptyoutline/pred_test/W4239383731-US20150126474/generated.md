## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of microbiology and medicinal chemistry, specifically to small-molecule compounds that modulate bacterial quorum sensing by inhibiting the ATPase activity of LuxO, a central response regulator in the quorum sensing cascade of Vibrio species. More particularly, the invention encompasses novel chemical entities derived from the 5-thio-6-azauracil scaffold that selectively inhibit LuxO-mediated ATP hydrolysis, thereby activating quorum sensing and suppressing virulence factor expression in pathogenic vibrios. The invention further relates to methods of using these compounds to treat or prevent infections caused by Vibrio cholerae, Vibrio parahaemolyticus, Vibrio vulnificus, Vibrio alginolyticus, and other Vibrio species that rely on LuxO as a master regulator of quorum sensing and virulence. The compounds disclosed herein represent the first class of chemical agents capable of uncompetitively inhibiting the ATPase activity of an NtrC-family response regulator, thereby offering a novel mechanism for anti-virulence therapeutic intervention.

## BACKGROUND

Bacterial quorum sensing is a sophisticated cell-to-cell communication system that enables microbial populations to coordinate gene expression in response to changes in population density through the production, detection, and response to diffusible signaling molecules known as autoinducers. This regulatory mechanism governs a wide array of collective behaviors, including bioluminescence, biofilm formation, sporulation, motility, and critically, the production of virulence factors in many pathogenic bacteria. In Gram-negative bacteria, acyl-homoserine lactones (HSLs) serve as the predominant autoinducers, detected by cytoplasmic LuxR-type or membrane-bound LuxN-type receptors. However, several globally significant bacterial pathogens, including members of the genus Vibrio, utilize non-HSL-based quorum sensing systems that rely on alternative signaling molecules such as CAI-1 and AI-2.

Vibrio cholerae, the causative agent of the acute diarrheal disease cholera, employs a complex quorum sensing circuit that integrates signals from two autoinducers—CAI-1 and AI-2—to regulate a transcriptional hierarchy centered on the response regulator LuxO. At low cell density, when autoinducer concentrations are insufficient for detection, the transmembrane receptors CqsS and LuxPQ function as kinases, phosphorylating LuxO. Phosphorylated LuxO (LuxO∼P) activates transcription of four small regulatory RNAs, Qrr1–Qrr4, which, in conjunction with the RNA chaperone Hfq, destabilize the mRNA encoding the master transcriptional regulator HapR while stabilizing the mRNA encoding AphA. Consequently, AphA accumulates and drives expression of genes associated with virulence and biofilm formation, while HapR remains undetected. At high cell density, autoinducers bind to their cognate receptors, switching their enzymatic activity from kinase to phosphatase, resulting in dephosphorylation and inactivation of LuxO. This leads to cessation of Qrr sRNA transcription, derepression of HapR translation, and subsequent repression of virulence genes. Thus, in V. cholerae, quorum sensing functions paradoxically to suppress virulence at high cell density, a strategy that facilitates bacterial dissemination following host exit.

This unique regulatory logic renders V. cholerae and other pathogenic vibrios uniquely susceptible to therapeutic strategies that artificially activate quorum sensing, thereby prematurely inducing the high cell density gene expression program and repressing virulence factor production. While previous efforts have focused on quorum sensing antagonists that block autoinducer-receptor interactions, these approaches are inherently limited by the diversity of autoinducer structures across bacterial species and the potential for resistance through receptor mutation. Moreover, many pathogenic vibrios—including V. parahaemolyticus, V. vulnificus, and V. alginolyticus—share a highly conserved LuxO-dependent quorum sensing architecture, despite differences in their autoinducer systems. This conservation suggests that targeting LuxO, rather than upstream receptors or autoinducer synthases, offers a broad-spectrum opportunity for intervention.

Despite the therapeutic promise of quorum sensing modulation, no small-molecule inhibitors have previously been reported that directly target the ATPase activity of an NtrC-family response regulator. NtrC-family proteins, including LuxO, are characterized by a conserved AAA+ ATPase domain that hydrolyzes ATP to drive open complex formation at σ54-dependent promoters. This ATPase activity is essential for transcriptional activation and is tightly regulated by phosphorylation of the N-terminal receiver domain. While inhibitors of histidine kinases and other two-component signaling components have been explored, targeting the ATPase domain of a response regulator has remained an unmet challenge due to the perceived lack of druggable pockets and the high degree of structural conservation among ATPase domains across bacterial and eukaryotic systems. Furthermore, no chemical probe has been identified that selectively inhibits LuxO without affecting other essential NtrC-family regulators such as E. coli NtrC, nor has any compound been shown to exert anti-virulence effects across multiple Vibrio species through direct LuxO inhibition.

The absence of such agents has hindered both mechanistic studies of quorum sensing signal transduction and the development of novel anti-infective strategies that exploit quorum sensing activation. Traditional antibiotics, which target essential cellular processes such as cell wall synthesis, protein translation, or DNA replication, exert strong selective pressure for resistance development. In contrast, anti-virulence agents that disarm pathogens without killing them are theorized to impose significantly reduced selective pressure for resistance, as survival and proliferation are not directly compromised. The identification of compounds capable of inhibiting LuxO ATPase activity would therefore represent a paradigm-shifting advance in the field of anti-infective therapeutics, offering a novel, resistance-resistant strategy to combat vibrio-associated diseases.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel small-molecule compounds that inhibit the ATPase activity of LuxO, a central response regulator in the quorum sensing pathway of Vibrio species, thereby activating quorum sensing and repressing virulence factor production. These compounds are structurally characterized by a 5-thio-6-azauracil core scaffold substituted with hydrophobic, branched alkyl side chains, with optimal activity conferred by the presence of a tert-butyl carbinol moiety. The compounds function as uncompetitive inhibitors of LuxO ATPase activity, binding preferentially to the pre-formed LuxO-ATP complex and inhibiting ATP hydrolysis without interfering with LuxO’s ability to bind DNA or undergo phosphorylation. This mechanism of action distinguishes the compounds from all previously described inhibitors of bacterial two-component systems and represents the first known chemical class capable of modulating the ATPase activity of an NtrC-family response regulator.

The invention further encompasses a method of treating or preventing infection in a mammalian subject caused by a pathogenic Vibrio species by administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of one or more of the disclosed compounds. The compounds are effective in repressing the production and secretion of key virulence factors, including cholera toxin and toxin co-regulated pilus in Vibrio cholerae, and Type Three Secretion System effector proteins such as VopD in Vibrio parahaemolyticus, thereby reducing bacterial cytotoxicity in vitro and in vivo. The compounds exhibit broad-spectrum activity across multiple Vibrio species, including V. cholerae, V. parahaemolyticus, V. vulnificus, and V. alginolyticus, due to the high sequence conservation of the LuxO ATPase domain among these organisms. The compounds are water-soluble, cell-permeable, and demonstrate low cytotoxicity toward mammalian cells at concentrations that fully inhibit LuxO ATPase activity, rendering them suitable for pharmaceutical development.

The invention further provides a method for identifying compounds that inhibit LuxO ATPase activity, comprising contacting LuxO with a test compound under conditions permitting ATP hydrolysis and measuring the rate of ATP hydrolysis in the presence and absence of the compound. Compounds that reduce ATP hydrolysis by at least 50% at a concentration of 100 µM are identified as candidates. The invention further provides a method for screening compounds for anti-virulence activity in Vibrio species, comprising exposing a Vibrio strain carrying a LuxO-dependent reporter system to a test compound and measuring activation of a high cell density gene expression program, such as bioluminescence or colony opacity, as an indicator of quorum sensing activation.

The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising one or more of the disclosed compounds in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, suitable for oral, intravenous, intramuscular, or topical administration. The invention further provides kits for the detection or treatment of Vibrio infections, comprising one or more of the disclosed compounds, instructions for use, and optionally, control reagents for quorum sensing activation assays.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Materials and Methods

The compounds of the present invention were synthesized and characterized using standard organic synthetic methodologies and analytical techniques. The core scaffold, 5-thio-6-azauracil, was prepared through a multi-step sequence beginning with the condensation of thiourea with ethyl cyanoacetate under basic conditions, followed by cyclization and functionalization to yield the 6-aza-5-thiouracil nucleus. Subsequent alkylation reactions at the N3 position were performed using a variety of alkyl halides bearing branched, sterically hindered side chains, including tert-butyl bromide, isobutyl iodide, and neopentyl chloride, under phase-transfer catalysis conditions to ensure regioselective substitution. The final compounds were purified by preparative high-performance liquid chromatography and characterized by nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy, high-resolution mass spectrometry, and elemental analysis. All compounds demonstrated purity greater than 98% as determined by analytical HPLC.

Bacterial strains used in the present invention included Vibrio cholerae strain C6706str and its derivatives, including the ΔcqsA ΔluxS double mutant (BH1578) and the luxO D47E mutant (BH1651), both carrying the heterologous lux operon from Vibrio harveyi under the control of a constitutive promoter. Additional strains included Vibrio harveyi BB120 and its luxO D47E derivative, as well as Vibrio parahaemolyticus strains LM4476 (luxO*) and LM9688 (ΔluxO), all maintained in Luria-Bertani or Luria-Miller broth supplemented with appropriate antibiotics. Escherichia coli strains DH5α, Top10, and BL21 Gold (DE3) were used for cloning and protein expression. All cultures were grown under standard aerobic conditions at 30°C for Vibrio species and 37°C for E. coli, with shaking at 200 rpm unless otherwise specified. For bioluminescence assays, cultures were diluted to an optical density at 600 nm of 0.05 in fresh medium prior to compound addition, and light production was measured using a microplate reader equipped with a photomultiplier tube, normalized to cell density (OD600).

For protein purification, the luxO D47E gene was amplified from V. cholerae genomic DNA using primers incorporating NdeI and BamHI restriction sites and cloned into the pET28b(+) expression vector to yield an N-terminal hexahistidine-tagged fusion protein. The construct was transformed into E. coli BL21 Gold (DE3), and protein expression was induced with 200 µM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside at mid-log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.8) and allowed to proceed for four hours at 30°C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM imidazole, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride), and lysed using a high-pressure cell disruptor. The soluble lysate was loaded onto a Ni-NTA affinity column equilibrated with lysis buffer, washed with 20 column volumes of wash buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 20 mM imidazole), and eluted with elution buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 250 mM imidazole). Fractions containing LuxO D47E were pooled, concentrated using a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff ultrafiltration device, and buffer-exchanged into storage buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol) using dialysis. Protein concentration was determined by absorbance at 280 nm using a calculated extinction coefficient of 42,340 M−1 cm−1. Purity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE, and protein integrity was verified by mass spectrometry.

ATPase activity was measured using a coupled enzymatic assay adapted from established protocols, wherein the ADP produced by LuxO-mediated ATP hydrolysis is converted to pyruvate by pyruvate kinase, and pyruvate is subsequently reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, consuming NADH in the process. The decrease in absorbance at 340 nm, corresponding to NADH oxidation, was monitored continuously for five minutes using a spectrophotometer. Reactions contained 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM NADH, 1 mM phosphoenolpyruvate, 5–20 units of pyruvate kinase/lactate dehydrogenase mixture, and 10 µM LuxO D47E. ATP was added at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 2.0 mM, and test compounds were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide and added at concentrations between 1 µM and 200 µM. Control reactions contained equivalent concentrations of dimethyl sulfoxide. Initial velocities were calculated from the linear portion of the absorbance-time curve, and kinetic parameters (Km and Vmax) were determined by nonlinear regression fitting to the Michaelis-Menten equation using GraphPad Prism software. Inhibition constants (Ki) and mode of inhibition were determined by Lineweaver-Burk double reciprocal plots and secondary plots of slope and intercept versus inhibitor concentration.

DNA binding assays were conducted using both electrophoretic mobility shift assays and fluorescence anisotropy. For mobility shift assays, a 150-base-pair DNA fragment encompassing the promoter region of qrr4 was amplified by PCR, end-labeled with [γ-32P]ATP using T4 polynucleotide kinase, and purified by gel extraction. LuxO D47E protein was incubated with 0.5 nM labeled DNA in binding buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 5% glycerol, 0.1 mg/mL BSA) for 30 minutes at room temperature in the presence or absence of compound. Protein-DNA complexes were resolved on a 6% native polyacrylamide gel, dried, and visualized by autoradiography. For fluorescence anisotropy, a fluorescein-labeled qrr4 promoter fragment was synthesized and purified by HPLC. Binding reactions were performed in a 96-well plate format with 5 nM fluorescent DNA and increasing concentrations of LuxO D47E (0–500 nM) in the presence or absence of 100 µM compound. Fluorescence anisotropy was measured using a microplate reader with excitation at 485 nm and emission at 535 nm. Data were fitted to a one-site binding model to determine dissociation constants.

A genetic screen for LuxO mutants resistant to compound inhibition was performed by random mutagenesis of the luxO D47E gene using the GeneMorph II Random Mutagenesis Kit. The mutagenized library was cloned into the broad-host-range vector pEVS143 and introduced into a V. cholerae ΔluxO strain by conjugation. Approximately 10,000 colonies were arrayed into 96-well plates containing LB medium supplemented with 100 µM compound 12. After overnight incubation at 30°C, clones exhibiting bioluminescence levels comparable to the background strain (ΔluxO) were selected, re-tested for resistance to compounds 11 and 12, and subjected to DNA sequencing to identify point mutations. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the QuikChange II XL kit to introduce individual mutations into wild-type LuxO and LuxO D47E backgrounds. Mutant proteins were purified and tested for ATPase activity and inhibitor sensitivity as described above.

Western blot analysis was performed to assess virulence factor expression. For V. cholerae, strains were grown in AKI medium for 8 hours at 37°C with or without compound, and whole-cell lysates were prepared by boiling in SDS-PAGE sample buffer. For V. parahaemolyticus, cultures were grown in LM medium with 10 mM MgCl2 and 10 mM sodium oxalate for 4 hours, and both cytoplasmic and secreted fractions were collected by centrifugation and filtration. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and probed with polyclonal antibodies against TcpA (V. cholerae) or VopD (V. parahaemolyticus), followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies and chemiluminescent detection. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to loading controls (e.g., GroEL or RpoA).

Cytotoxicity assays were conducted using HeLa cells seeded at 2×10⁴ cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured for 48 hours in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Prior to infection, cells were treated with 500 µM compound 12 or dimethyl sulfoxide vehicle. V. parahaemolyticus luxO* strains were grown to mid-log phase, washed, and resuspended in fresh medium to achieve a multiplicity of infection of 10. After 2, 3, and 4 hours of co-incubation, lactate dehydrogenase release into the supernatant was measured using a commercial cytotoxicity detection kit, and values were normalized to total lysis controls (1% Triton X-100) and background (uninfected cells).

### Chemical Reactions.

The synthesis of the 5-thio-6-azauracil derivatives employed a modular approach centered on the nucleophilic substitution of 3-bromo-5-thio-6-azauracil with sterically hindered alkylating agents. The precursor 3-bromo-5-thio-6-azauracil was synthesized by bromination of 5-thio-6-azauracil using N-bromosuccinimide in acetonitrile under reflux, yielding the 3-bromo intermediate in 82% yield after recrystallization from ethanol. This intermediate was then reacted with a series of tertiary alcohols, including tert-butanol, isobutanol, and neopentanol, under Mitsunobu conditions using triphenylphosphine and diethyl azodicarboxylate to effect direct ether formation at the N3 position. Alternatively, alkyl halides such as tert-butyl bromide, isobutyl iodide, and 1-bromo-2-methylpropane were employed under phase-transfer catalysis using tetrabutylammonium hydrogen sulfate in aqueous sodium hydroxide and dichloromethane, yielding the corresponding N3-alkylated products in yields ranging from 45% to 78%. The tert-butyl carbinol derivative, designated herein as compound 12, was obtained in 72% yield and characterized by ¹H NMR (DMSO-d6, 400 MHz): δ 8.12 (s, 1H, H-2), 7.95 (s, 1H, H-4), 4.15 (s, 2H, CH2), 1.48 (s, 9H, t-Bu), 1.32 (s, 3H, OH-CH3); ¹³C NMR: δ 162.5 (C-2), 158.2 (C-4), 148.7 (C-5), 130.9 (C-6), 70.3 (CH2), 31.8 (t-Bu), 28.5 (t-Bu), 27.1 (t-Bu); HRMS (ESI): m/z calcd for C10H14N2O2S [M+H]+ 227.0842, found 227.0839. All other derivatives were synthesized analogously, with side chain variations introduced via alkylation of the 3-bromo scaffold using commercially available alkyl halides or alcohols under optimized conditions. The structure-activity relationship was established by systematically varying the branching, length, and steric bulk of the alkyl substituent, with potency directly correlating with the presence of a tertiary carbon center adjacent to the ether oxygen, particularly the tert-butyl group.

## Results

### Investigation of the Targets of the QS Activating Compounds

The three Class 2 compounds identified in the initial high-throughput screen exhibited potent activation of quorum sensing in both the ΔcqsA ΔluxS and luxO D47E strains of Vibrio cholerae, indicating that their mechanism of action lies downstream of the sensor kinases CqsS and LuxPQ. To determine whether these compounds acted on the cytoplasmic components of the quorum sensing cascade, including LuxO, σ54, Hfq, or the Qrr sRNAs, a transcriptional reporter system was constructed in which the promoter of qrr4 was fused to a green fluorescent protein gene. Treatment of the luxO D47E strain with compound 11 resulted in a threefold reduction in GFP fluorescence, suggesting that the compound interferes with transcription of the qrr genes. This observation was consistent with the hypothesis that the compounds activate quorum sensing by preventing the expression of the Qrr sRNAs, thereby derepressing translation of HapR. To determine whether the effect was specific to LuxO or extended to other σ54-dependent promoters, transcription of vpsR, a gene encoding a biofilm regulator under σ54 control, was measured. No significant change in vpsR expression was observed in the presence of compound 11, indicating that the compound does not globally inhibit σ54-dependent transcription and is therefore unlikely to act directly on the RNA polymerase holoenzyme or its co-activator. These findings strongly implicated LuxO as the primary target of the Class 2 compounds, as the only component common to both qrr4 and the quorum sensing pathway whose inhibition would selectively reduce qrr transcription without affecting other σ54-dependent genes.

### Structure-Activity-Relationship of Class 2 Compounds

A focused library of 32 analogs of the 5-thio-6-azauracil scaffold was synthesized to explore the structural determinants of potency. Modifications included linear alkyl chains of varying lengths (C3–C8), branched chains such as isopropyl, isobutyl, and neopentyl, aromatic substituents, and polar functional groups including alcohols, ethers, and esters. The EC50 values for quorum sensing activation in the luxO D47E strain were determined for each analog using bioluminescence as the readout. Compounds bearing linear alkyl chains exhibited EC50 values ranging from 50 to 200 µM, while those with branched side chains demonstrated significantly improved potency, with EC50 values below 10 µM. The most potent compound, designated compound 12, featured a tert-butyl carbinol side chain and exhibited an EC50 of 2.1 µM, representing a threefold improvement over the initial lead compound 11. Introduction of a hydroxyl group on the terminal carbon of the tert-butyl chain enhanced aqueous solubility without compromising potency, suggesting that polarity at the distal end of the side chain is tolerated and may improve pharmacokinetic properties. In contrast, compounds with aromatic substituents, ester linkages, or polar terminal groups such as carboxylic acids or amines showed complete loss of activity, indicating that the hydrophobic nature of the side chain is essential for target engagement. The SAR analysis revealed that steric bulk proximal to the N3 nitrogen is critical, with compounds possessing a quaternary carbon center immediately adjacent to the ether oxygen demonstrating optimal activity. This structural requirement is consistent with the hypothesis that the compounds bind to a deep, hydrophobic pocket adjacent to the ATP-binding site of LuxO.

### Class 2 Compounds Inhibit the LuxO ATPase Activity

To determine which biochemical function of LuxO is inhibited by the Class 2 compounds, a series of functional assays were performed. First, bioluminescence assays confirmed that both compound 11 and compound 12 activated quorum sensing in strains expressing either wild-type LuxO or the constitutively active LuxO D47E mutant, indicating that inhibition does not occur through interference with phosphorylation or dephosphorylation of the receiver domain. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays and quantitative fluorescence anisotropy experiments demonstrated that LuxO D47E retains its ability to bind the qrr4 promoter DNA with identical affinity (Kd ≈ 300 nM) in the presence of up to 200 µM of either compound, indicating that DNA binding is unaffected. These findings ruled out interference with the C-terminal DNA-binding domain as the mechanism of action. Subsequent ATPase assays revealed that both compounds dose-dependently inhibit LuxO ATP hydrolysis, with compound 12 reducing ATPase activity by 92% at 100 µM. Kinetic analysis using Lineweaver-Burk plots demonstrated that both compounds decreased both the apparent Km and Vmax of the ATPase reaction, and the lines for control and inhibitor-containing reactions were parallel, indicating an uncompetitive mode of inhibition. This mode of inhibition is consistent with binding of the compound to the LuxO-ATP complex, stabilizing a non-productive conformation that prevents ATP hydrolysis. The potency of the compounds in quorum sensing activation (EC50) was strongly correlated with their ability to inhibit ATPase activity (IC50), with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.94, confirming that ATPase inhibition is the primary mechanism underlying quorum sensing activation.

### A Genetic Screen to Identify LuxO Mutants Resistant to Compound 12

To identify the binding site of compound 12 on LuxO, a genetic screen was conducted to isolate LuxO D47E mutants that retained ATPase activity but were resistant to inhibition by compound 12. A library of random mutants was generated using error-prone PCR and introduced into a V. cholerae ΔluxO strain carrying the lux reporter. Colonies exhibiting low bioluminescence in the presence of compound 12 were selected, and sequencing of the luxO gene from these strains revealed four distinct point mutations: I211F, L215F, L242F, and V294L. These mutations were introduced individually into wild-type LuxO and LuxO D47E backgrounds, and all conferred resistance to compound 12 while preserving ATPase activity in the absence of inhibitor. Structural modeling of these residues onto the crystal structure of Aquifex aeolicus NtrC1, which shares 48% sequence identity with LuxO, revealed that all four residues map to a region immediately adjacent to the Walker B motif (D245, E246, L247, C248), a conserved element critical for ATP hydrolysis. I211 and L215 lie within the helix containing the GAFTGA domain, which mediates interaction with σ54, while L242 is positioned directly upstream of the Walker B motif, and V294 is located near the catalytic arginine residue R306. Purification and biochemical characterization of the LuxO D47E/I211F mutant confirmed that its ATPase activity was no longer inhibited by compound 12, even at concentrations up to 200 µM, whereas the wild-type LuxO D47E was fully inhibited under the same conditions. These findings indicate that compound 12 binds to a pocket formed by these residues, inducing a conformational change that allosterically disrupts the catalytic machinery of the ATPase domain without preventing ATP binding.

### Broad Spectrum Activation of Vibrio QS

To determine whether the LuxO inhibitors exhibit activity across other Vibrio species, the compounds were tested in Vibrio harveyi and Vibrio parahaemolyticus, both of which possess highly homologous LuxO orthologs. In V. harveyi, the luxO D47E mutant is dark due to constitutive repression of the lux operon, but treatment with compound 12 induced a 10,000-fold increase in bioluminescence, demonstrating that the compound can activate quorum sensing in this species. In V. parahaemolyticus, the HapR homolog OpaR controls colony opacity: opaque colonies indicate high OpaR expression (high cell density), while translucent colonies indicate low OpaR expression (low cell density). The luxO* mutant, which constitutively represses OpaR, forms translucent colonies, whereas the ΔluxO mutant forms opaque colonies. Treatment of the luxO* mutant with compound 12 or compound 11 resulted in a complete switch from translucent to opaque colony morphology, phenocopying the ΔluxO mutant. This phenotypic switch was confirmed by Western blot analysis, which showed increased OpaR expression in the presence of the compounds. These results demonstrate that the LuxO inhibitors are broadly active across Vibrio species, irrespective of the nature of their autoinducer systems, due to the high conservation of the LuxO ATPase domain.

### New Chemical Tools for Controlling Virulence in Vibrios

The ability of the LuxO inhibitors to activate quorum sensing and derepress HapR/OpaR expression was translated into functional suppression of virulence factor production. In V. cholerae luxO D47E, treatment with compound 12 led to a dose-dependent increase in HapR protein levels and a corresponding decrease in TcpA, the major subunit of the toxin co-regulated pilus, as detected by Western blot. Similarly, in V. parahaemolyticus luxO*, compound 12 treatment resulted in a marked reduction in the secretion of VopD, a key effector protein of the Type Three Secretion System. These molecular changes were functionally significant: in a cytotoxicity assay using HeLa cells, infection with the V. parahaemolyticus luxO* mutant treated with compound 12 resulted in a 70% reduction in lactate dehydrogenase release at 3 hours post-infection compared to untreated controls, indicating a substantial decrease in host cell lysis. Although cytotoxicity increased at later time points (4 hours), the overall trajectory of cell death was significantly delayed, suggesting that the inhibitors impair the initial burst of virulence required for efficient host colonization. These findings establish that inhibition of LuxO ATPase activity is sufficient to attenuate virulence in multiple pathogenic Vibrio species, validating LuxO as a viable target for anti-virulence therapy.

## Discussion Of Results

The present invention discloses a novel class of small-molecule inhibitors that target the ATPase activity of LuxO, a central response regulator in the quorum sensing pathways of pathogenic Vibrio species. These compounds, derived from the 5-thio-6-azauracil scaffold, represent the first chemical agents capable of uncompetitively inhibiting the ATPase domain of an NtrC-family response regulator, thereby activating quorum sensing and repressing virulence factor production. The mechanism of action—binding to the LuxO-ATP complex to prevent ATP hydrolysis—is unprecedented among bacterial signaling inhibitors and distinguishes these compounds from all previously reported anti-virulence agents, which have primarily targeted histidine kinases, autoinducer synthases, or receptor-ligand interactions. The identification of four resistance-conferring mutations clustered around the Walker B motif and the GAFTGA domain provides compelling structural evidence that the compounds bind to an allosteric site adjacent to the catalytic center, inducing a conformational change that disrupts the catalytic machinery without blocking ATP binding. This mode of inhibition is highly specific, as evidenced by the lack of effect on other σ54-dependent genes and the minimal inhibition of E. coli NtrC, despite high sequence conservation in the ATPase domain.

The broad-spectrum activity of these compounds across multiple Vibrio species underscores their potential as a unified therapeutic strategy against vibriosis. Unlike autoinducer mimetics or receptor agonists, which are inherently species-specific due to variations in ligand recognition, LuxO inhibitors exploit a conserved downstream node in the quorum sensing cascade, rendering them effective against strains regardless of their autoinducer profile. This is particularly significant given the global diversity of Vibrio strains and the increasing prevalence of multidrug-resistant isolates. The compounds’ favorable physicochemical properties—including water solubility, cell permeability, and low mammalian cytotoxicity—further support their translational potential. The fact that these molecules do not inhibit bacterial growth but instead disarm the pathogen by repressing virulence suggests a reduced likelihood of resistance development, as the selective pressure for mutations that restore virulence without compromising fitness is likely to be low.

The genetic screen identifying resistance mutations provides a roadmap for future structure-based drug design. The mapped residues—particularly I211, L215, L242, and V294—define a druggable pocket that can be exploited to optimize compound affinity and selectivity. Future efforts may focus on modifying the thio-azauracil core to enhance binding interactions with this pocket, potentially through introduction of hydrogen bond donors or π-stacking moieties that engage nearby aromatic residues. The observation that the tert-butyl carbinol side chain confers optimal activity suggests that the binding pocket accommodates a hydrophobic cavity with a polar terminus, a feature that may be leveraged to improve pharmacokinetics without sacrificing potency. Additionally, the ability of these compounds to inhibit LuxO in multiple Vibrio species opens the door to the development of a single therapeutic agent capable of treating cholera, seafood-borne gastroenteritis, wound infections, and septicemia caused by diverse vibrios.

The implications of this invention extend beyond the Vibrio genus. The NtrC-family of response regulators controls a wide array of bacterial processes, including nitrogen metabolism, motility, biofilm formation, and virulence in numerous pathogens. The identification of a chemical scaffold capable of selectively inhibiting the ATPase domain of LuxO provides a foundational platform for the development of inhibitors targeting other NtrC-family members. Although the compounds described herein show minimal activity against E. coli NtrC, the structural insights gained from this work—particularly the identification of a non-conserved allosteric pocket adjacent to the Walker B motif—offer a blueprint for designing broader-spectrum NtrC inhibitors. Such agents could be used to combat infections caused by pathogens such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Burkholderia cepacia, and Rhizobium species, all of which rely on NtrC-family regulators for virulence or environmental adaptation.

The discovery of these compounds also opens new avenues for probing the fundamental biochemistry of AAA+ ATPases, a superfamily of molecular machines involved in protein degradation, DNA replication, membrane trafficking, and organelle biogenesis in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes. The thio-azauracil core may serve as a starting point for the development of inhibitors targeting human AAA+ ATPases such as p97/VCP, which are implicated in neurodegenerative diseases and cancer. The fact that a bacterial ATPase inhibitor can be designed to avoid off-target effects on the human proteome suggests that selective inhibition of AAA+ ATPases is achievable, a long-standing challenge in medicinal chemistry.

In summary, the present invention provides a transformative advance in the field of anti-infective therapeutics by introducing a novel class of compounds that activate bacterial quorum sensing through the inhibition of LuxO ATPase activity. These compounds represent a new paradigm for anti-virulence therapy, offering broad-spectrum activity, low resistance potential, and a unique mechanism of action that distinguishes them from all previously described agents. The compounds are suitable for formulation into pharmaceutical compositions for the prevention and treatment of vibrio-associated diseases, and the structural insights derived from this work provide a foundation for the development of next-generation inhibitors targeting NtrC-family response regulators across the bacterial kingdom.
