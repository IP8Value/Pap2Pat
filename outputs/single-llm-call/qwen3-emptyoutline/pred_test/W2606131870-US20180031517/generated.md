# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of electrochemical sensing devices, specifically to ion-selective electrodes utilizing molecularly engineered metal–organic supercontainers as size-selective ionophores for the potentiometric detection of molecular ions in complex aqueous environments. The invention encompasses a novel class of mixed-matrix membranes incorporating crystalline, cage-like metal–organic structures with precisely defined internal cavities that enable selective recognition and binding of molecular cations based on their geometric dimensions, rather than solely on charge or chemical functionality. These membranes are integrated into both conventional and solid-contact ion-selective electrodes, enabling highly specific, stable, and sensitive detection of large molecular ions such as methylene blue cation and tetrabutylammonium ion, while exhibiting minimal cross-sensitivity to smaller inorganic cations including potassium, silver, and ammonium. The invention further extends to the fabrication of microfabricated solid-contact electrodes using pyrolyzed photoresist-derived carbon substrates, allowing for seamless integration with microelectronic systems and scalable manufacturing techniques compatible with the semiconductor industry. The resulting sensing platforms are suitable for deployment in environmental monitoring, biomedical diagnostics, industrial process control, and food safety applications where the selective detection of molecular-scale ionic species in heterogeneous matrices is required. The technical advancement lies in the unprecedented utilization of three-dimensional, nanoscale host–guest architectures as the primary recognition element in potentiometric sensors, thereby overcoming the limitations of traditional ionophores that rely on coordination chemistry or hydrophobic interactions with small ions, and enabling a new paradigm of size-based molecular discrimination in electrochemical sensing.

## BACKGROUND

Electrochemical ion-selective electrodes have long served as indispensable tools for the quantitative detection of ionic species in diverse fields ranging from clinical diagnostics to environmental analysis. Conventional ion-selective electrodes operate on the principle of generating a potential difference across a selectively permeable membrane that contains an ionophore—a molecular entity capable of binding a target ion and facilitating its transmembrane transport. The resulting potential follows the Nernst equation, enabling concentration determination through calibrated voltage measurements. Historically, ionophores have been derived from macrocyclic compounds such as crown ethers, calixarenes, and ionophoric antibiotics, which exhibit high affinity for small inorganic cations like sodium, potassium, calcium, and ammonium. These systems function effectively due to the complementary size and charge distribution between the ionophore cavity and the target ion, with binding driven primarily by electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, or coordination to heteroatoms. However, these classical ionophores are fundamentally ill-suited for the detection of large molecular ions, such as organic dyes, pharmaceuticals, or surfactants, whose dimensions exceed the structural limits of conventional binding pockets and whose recognition requires not only charge complementarity but also precise steric matching. The inability of existing technologies to distinguish molecular ions based on size has created a critical gap in analytical capabilities, particularly in scenarios where the presence of structurally similar but biologically or environmentally distinct species must be resolved. For instance, methylene blue, a widely used redox indicator and dye pollutant, exists in a cationic form with a molecular length of approximately 1.6 nanometers and a width of 0.7 nanometers—dimensions that are orders of magnitude larger than those of typical inorganic cations. Conventional ion-selective electrodes exhibit negligible or non-specific responses to such species, as their ionophores lack the spatial architecture to accommodate or selectively bind them. Furthermore, attempts to adapt existing ionophores for molecular ion recognition have been hindered by poor selectivity, slow response kinetics, membrane instability, and susceptibility to interference from background electrolytes. Solid-contact ion-selective electrodes, while offering advantages in miniaturization and integration with electronic systems, have similarly been constrained by the limitations of their ionophoric components. The development of a new class of ionophores capable of recognizing molecular ions through geometric complementarity, rather than chemical affinity alone, has therefore remained an unmet need in analytical science. Recent advances in supramolecular chemistry have led to the synthesis of metal–organic cages and frameworks with well-defined internal cavities, but their application in electrochemical sensing has been largely unexplored. While some studies have demonstrated the use of metal–organic frameworks for adsorption or catalysis, none have successfully integrated such structures into a functional ion-selective membrane capable of generating a measurable, reproducible, and size-selective potentiometric signal. Moreover, the stability of such molecular architectures within polymeric matrices under prolonged exposure to aqueous media, the prevention of leaching, and the maintenance of ion transport kinetics have not been addressed in prior art. The present invention bridges this technological gap by introducing a metal–organic supercontainer—specifically a cobalt-based cage with precisely engineered endo- and exo-cavities—as the core recognition element in a mixed-matrix membrane, enabling the first demonstration of size-selective potentiometric sensing of molecular ions. The invention further establishes that such structures can be stably incorporated into both conventional and solid-contact electrode configurations without degradation or leaching, and that their performance remains robust even in complex environmental matrices such as river water. This represents a fundamental departure from prior art, which has exclusively focused on small-ion recognition, and opens a new domain of electrochemical sensing grounded in nanoscale molecular geometry rather than chemical functional groups.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel ion-selective electrode system comprising a mixed-matrix membrane containing a metal–organic supercontainer as a size-selective ionophore, wherein the supercontainer possesses a three-dimensional cage-like architecture with defined internal cavities that selectively bind molecular cations based on their geometric dimensions. The metal–organic supercontainer is characterized by an octahedral geometry with an outer diameter of approximately 3.3 nanometers, an internal cavity of approximately 1.7 nanometers in diameter, and six additional exocavities each measuring approximately 0.74 nanometers in diameter, collectively forming seven distinct binding domains capable of encapsulating molecular ions of matching size. The membrane further comprises a polymeric matrix composed of a high molecular weight polyvinyl chloride and a plasticizer, along with an ion-exchanger to facilitate charge neutrality during ion transport. Upon exposure to an aqueous sample containing a target molecular ion, the supercontainer selectively binds the ion within its endo- or exo-cavities, inducing a measurable change in the interfacial potential across the membrane–analyte boundary, which is detected as a potentiometric signal by a reference electrode. The system exhibits a near-Nernstian response to the target molecular ion, with a slope of approximately 56 to 60 millivolts per decade of concentration, and a detection limit as low as 300 nanomolar. Crucially, the system demonstrates minimal cross-sensitivity to smaller inorganic cations such as potassium, silver, and ammonium, which are incapable of fitting within the supercontainer’s cavities, thereby achieving unprecedented size selectivity. The invention further encompasses a solid-contact variant of the electrode, wherein the mixed-matrix membrane is deposited onto a pyrolyzed photoresist-derived carbon substrate, eliminating the need for an internal filling solution and enabling direct integration with microelectronic readout systems. The pyrolyzed carbon substrate is formed by thermal decomposition of a spin-coated SU-8 photoresist layer under a reducing atmosphere, yielding a conductive, stable, and chemically inert surface suitable for long-term electrochemical measurements. The membrane remains firmly adhered to the substrate with no observable leaching of the supercontainer or bound ion over extended periods, even when immersed in pure water for more than one week. The invention is further validated through application in simulated wastewater samples derived from natural river water, where the electrode maintains its near-Nernstian response despite the presence of organic matter, microbial activity, and redox interference, demonstrating robustness in real-world environmental conditions. The system’s rapid response time—less than one second for concentrations above 30 micromolar—and exceptional long-term stability, with drift not exceeding a few millivolts per hour, render it suitable for continuous monitoring applications. The invention thus provides a fundamentally new class of electrochemical sensors capable of distinguishing molecular ions by size, enabling applications previously inaccessible to conventional ion-selective electrodes, including the detection of dye pollutants, pharmaceutical residues, and other large molecular species in complex aqueous matrices.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention disclosed herein pertains to a novel electrochemical sensing platform that exploits the geometric specificity of metal–organic supercontainers to achieve unprecedented selectivity in the potentiometric detection of molecular ions. Unlike conventional ion-selective electrodes that rely on chemical affinity between ionophores and target ions, the present system operates on the principle of steric complementarity, wherein the target ion must physically fit within the nanoscale cavities of a rigid, three-dimensional host structure to induce a measurable electrochemical response. The core component of the invention is a metal–organic supercontainer, designated herein as 1-Co, which is a crystalline, cage-like assembly formed through the self-assembly of cobalt(II) ions, p-tert-butylsulfonyl-calix[4]arene, and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate linkers. This structure possesses a well-defined octahedral geometry with an external diameter of approximately 3.3 nanometers and an internal cavity of approximately 1.7 nanometers in diameter, surrounded by six additional exocavities each measuring approximately 0.74 nanometers in diameter. These cavities are not merely void spaces but are chemically and geometrically tailored environments, with aromatic walls lining the interior surfaces that facilitate cation–π interactions, thereby enhancing binding affinity for cationic species whose size and shape are compatible with the cavity dimensions. The molecular ion methylene blue, which has a length of approximately 1.6 nanometers and a width of approximately 0.7 nanometers, is uniquely suited to this architecture, as its dimensions allow it to orient precisely within the endocavity along its long axis while simultaneously fitting into the exocavities along its shorter dimension. This geometric match results in a binding stoichiometry of approximately seven methylene blue cations per supercontainer molecule, with an apparent binding constant on the order of 10⁴ M⁻¹, as previously confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis. The supercontainer is incorporated into a mixed-matrix membrane composed of a polymeric matrix of high molecular weight polyvinyl chloride and the plasticizer bis(2-ethylhexyl) sebacate, which provides mechanical stability and ion mobility, along with potassium tetrakis(4-chlorophenyl) borate as an ion-exchanger to maintain charge neutrality during ion transport. The inclusion of the supercontainer within this matrix transforms the membrane from a conventional ion-selective medium into a size-selective recognition element, where the binding of the target ion within the cavities alters the local concentration gradient of free and complexed supercontainer species at the membrane–analyte interface, thereby modulating the phase-boundary potential in accordance with the principles of electrochemical interfacial thermodynamics. This potential change is measured using a high-impedance instrumentation amplifier, with a gain of ten, and digitized at a sampling rate of ten kilosamples per second, with signal smoothing applied over a half-second window to minimize noise and hysteresis. The resulting potentiometric response to methylene blue concentration is near-Nernstian, with a slope of 56.8 ± 2.5 millivolts per decade of concentration and a detection limit of approximately 300 nanomolar, demonstrating sensitivity comparable to or exceeding that of conventional ion-selective electrodes for small ions. Importantly, the same membrane exhibits negligible response to smaller cations such as potassium, silver, and ammonium, whose ionic radii fall below 0.3 nanometers and are physically incapable of entering the supercontainer’s cavities. The response to potassium is measured at only 10.4 ± 2.1 millivolts per decade, to silver at 5.0 ± 7.3 millivolts per decade, and to ammonium at −6.4 ± 1.4 millivolts per decade, confirming that the sensing mechanism is not based on charge or chemical interaction but on physical exclusion. This size selectivity is further corroborated by the observation that tetrabutylammonium, a molecular cation with a spherical diameter of approximately 0.8 nanometers and a molecular weight similar to methylene blue, elicits a near-Nernstian response of 60.0 ± 1.5 millivolts per decade with a detection limit of approximately one micromolar, demonstrating that the supercontainer recognizes molecular ions based on overall dimensions rather than chemical identity. The response kinetics of the system are remarkably rapid, with the electrode reaching over 90% of its final potential within one second for concentrations above 30 micromolar, attributable to the high mobility of the bound ions within the membrane and the efficient ion-exchange dynamics at the interface. At lower concentrations, response times extend to 50–100 seconds, consistent with the kinetics of diffusion-limited ion binding, yet the system remains fully reversible and stable over repeated measurements. The long-term stability of the membrane is exceptional, with potential drift not exceeding a few millivolts per hour over extended periods, even under continuous exposure to aqueous media. This stability is further enhanced by the fact that the supercontainer and its bound ion remain sequestered within the polymeric matrix, with no observable leaching into the surrounding solution. A control experiment in which a membrane lacking the supercontainer was immersed in deionized water containing methylene blue resulted in visible blue discoloration of the water, indicating leaching of the dye. In contrast, the membrane containing the supercontainer retained its dark blue color without any change in the surrounding solution, confirming that the dye is permanently trapped within the cavities. This retention is critical for sensor longevity and prevents signal drift due to depletion of the recognition element. The invention further extends to the fabrication of a solid-contact ion-selective electrode, wherein the mixed-matrix membrane is deposited directly onto a conductive substrate formed by the pyrolysis of SU-8 photoresist. SU-8, a commercially available epoxy-based negative photoresist, is spin-coated onto a silicon wafer coated with a 650-nanometer layer of silicon dioxide to form a uniform film of approximately 1.5 micrometers in thickness. This film is then subjected to thermal treatment in a quartz tube furnace under a reducing atmosphere consisting of 95% nitrogen and 5% hydrogen at a temperature of 900 degrees Celsius for one hour, resulting in the complete carbonization of the polymer into a highly conductive, chemically inert, and mechanically robust carbonaceous layer. Polyethylene wells with an inner diameter of approximately six millimeters are then bonded to the surface using a rapid-curing epoxy to define the sensing area. The mixed-matrix membrane solution is drop-cast into these wells, and after drying for 24 hours, the electrode is conditioned in a 10-micromolar solution of methylene blue overnight to establish equilibrium. The resulting solid-contact electrode exhibits identical potentiometric characteristics to its conventional counterpart, with a near-Nernstian response slope of approximately 57 millivolts per decade and a detection limit of 300 nanomolar, confirming that the supercontainer retains its functionality even when interfaced with a solid-state substrate. The absence of an internal filling solution eliminates the risk of electrolyte leakage, reduces the overall size of the device, and enables direct integration with microelectronic circuits, making the system ideal for miniaturized, portable, and implantable sensing applications. The solid-contact electrode also demonstrates exceptional stability, with no measurable degradation of performance after prolonged storage or repeated use, and no evidence of delamination or membrane detachment from the carbon substrate. The utility of the invention is further demonstrated in a simulated wastewater experiment, wherein the electrode is exposed to water collected from the Fyris River in Uppsala, Sweden, spiked with controlled concentrations of methylene blue. Despite the presence of organic matter, microbial biomass, and dissolved oxygen depletion caused by microbial respiration, the electrode maintains a near-Nernstian response curve, albeit with a slight shift in the detection limit. This shift is attributed to the partial reduction of methylene blue to its leuco form under the reducing conditions created by microbial metabolism, as confirmed by UV–vis spectroscopy showing a decrease in the characteristic absorption peak of the cationic dye. However, because the electrode responds exclusively to the cationic form of methylene blue and not its reduced counterpart, the sensor remains selective and functional, effectively measuring only the bioavailable fraction of the pollutant. This feature renders the invention uniquely suited for environmental monitoring applications, where the distinction between oxidized and reduced forms of a compound may be analytically significant. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in electrochemical sensing, moving beyond chemical recognition to geometric recognition, and enabling the detection of molecular ions that were previously undetectable by conventional means. The modular nature of the metal–organic supercontainer allows for future modifications to the ligand structure, metal center, or linker chemistry, thereby enabling the design of tailored sensors for a wide range of molecular ions, including pharmaceuticals, endocrine disruptors, and other large organic pollutants, without altering the fundamental architecture of the sensing platform.

### EXAMPLE 1: PROOF-OF-CONCEPT STUDY WITH MB+ AND TBA+

A proof-of-concept study was conducted to establish the fundamental sensing capability of the metal–organic supercontainer 1-Co when incorporated into a mixed-matrix membrane for the potentiometric detection of molecular cations. The study focused on two target analytes: methylene blue cation (MB⁺), a planar, elongated organic dye with a molecular length of approximately 1.6 nanometers and a width of 0.7 nanometers, and tetrabutylammonium cation (TBA⁺), a spherical organic ion with a diameter of approximately 0.8 nanometers. Both species were selected for their distinct molecular geometries and their relevance as model analytes in environmental and biomedical contexts. The mixed-matrix membrane was prepared by dissolving 330 milligrams of high molecular weight poly(vinyl chloride), 722 microliters of bis(2-ethylhexyl) sebacate, and 5.0 milligrams of potassium tetrakis(4-chlorophenyl) borate in approximately five milliliters of tetrahydrofuran. After homogenization, 25.7 milligrams of the 1-Co supercontainer were introduced into the solution, followed by the addition of 3.2 milligrams of methylene blue, resulting in a distinct color change from light red to deep blue, indicative of successful encapsulation. The solution was stirred for two additional hours to ensure complete dispersion and binding. A conventional ion-selective electrode was fabricated by capillary filling of a disposable pipette tip with the membrane solution, followed by insertion of a silver/silver chloride pellet and filling of the internal lumen with a saturated potassium chloride solution containing silver chloride. The electrode was conditioned in a 10-micromolar methylene blue solution overnight to establish equilibrium. Potentiometric measurements were performed using a high-impedance instrumentation amplifier with a gain of ten, referenced against a standard Ag/AgCl electrode, with the potential recorded as a function of increasing methylene blue concentration spanning six orders of magnitude from 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻³ molar. The resulting calibration curve exhibited a linear relationship between the measured potential and the logarithm of concentration, with a slope of 56.8 ± 2.5 millivolts per decade, closely approaching the theoretical Nernstian value of 59.2 millivolts per decade at 25 degrees Celsius. The detection limit was determined to be approximately 300 nanomolar, corresponding to a signal-to-noise ratio of three, and the response was fully reversible upon dilution. To assess size selectivity, the same membrane was exposed to solutions of tetrabutylammonium chloride at concentrations ranging from 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻³ molar. The resulting calibration curve for TBA⁺ displayed a slope of 60.0 ± 1.5 millivolts per decade, with a detection limit of approximately one micromolar, demonstrating that the supercontainer is capable of recognizing molecular ions of different chemical structures so long as their dimensions fall within the geometric constraints of its binding cavities. The response time for both analytes was measured under controlled stirring conditions, with the electrode reaching over 90% of its final potential within one second for concentrations above 30 micromolar, indicating rapid ion exchange kinetics. At lower concentrations, the response time increased to between 50 and 100 seconds, consistent with diffusion-limited binding processes. The long-term stability of the electrode was evaluated over a period of 72 hours, during which the potential remained constant within a range of ±3 millivolts, with no observable drift or signal decay. Control experiments were performed using membranes lacking the supercontainer, which exhibited no significant response to either methylene blue or tetrabutylammonium, confirming that the sensing mechanism is entirely dependent on the presence of the metal–organic cage. Furthermore, impedance spectroscopy revealed a twofold reduction in charge-transfer resistance at the membrane–analyte interface when the supercontainer was present, indicating enhanced ion transfer efficiency due to the facilitated transport of the bound ion through the membrane matrix. The combined data from these experiments establish that the 1-Co supercontainer functions as a highly effective size-selective ionophore, capable of distinguishing molecular ions based on their physical dimensions rather than their chemical identity, and that this selectivity is preserved across a wide range of concentrations and environmental conditions. The ability to detect both elongated and spherical molecular ions with comparable sensitivity underscores the versatility of the supercontainer architecture and its potential for broad application in the detection of structurally diverse organic cations.

### EXAMPLE 2: LEECHING STUDY

To evaluate the long-term stability and integrity of the metal–organic supercontainer within the mixed-matrix membrane, a leaching study was conducted under conditions designed to challenge the retention of both the supercontainer and its bound analyte. Two identical mixed-matrix membranes were prepared, one containing the 1-Co supercontainer and one lacking it, with all other components—including polyvinyl chloride, plasticizer, ion-exchanger, and methylene blue—held constant. Each membrane was placed in a separate vial containing one hundred milliliters of deionized water and stored at ambient temperature for a period of seven days. Visual inspection of the aqueous media revealed a distinct and dramatic difference between the two systems. The membrane lacking the supercontainer produced a visibly blue-colored solution, indicating that methylene blue had leached out of the membrane matrix and into the surrounding water. In contrast, the membrane containing the supercontainer exhibited no discernible color change in the water, remaining completely clear, despite the presence of methylene blue within the membrane. This observation provided direct, qualitative evidence that the supercontainer effectively sequesters the methylene blue cation within its internal cavities, preventing its diffusion into the aqueous environment. To quantify this effect, UV–vis spectroscopy was employed to measure the absorbance of the supernatant at the characteristic wavelength of methylene blue (664 nanometers). The sample from the membrane without supercontainer showed a clear absorbance peak, corresponding to a concentration of approximately 1.2 micromoles per liter of methylene blue in solution, whereas the sample from the supercontainer-containing membrane showed no detectable absorbance above the baseline noise level, confirming that less than 0.1 micromole per liter of methylene blue had escaped. This corresponds to a retention efficiency exceeding 99%. To further confirm that the supercontainer itself remained immobilized within the membrane, the solid membranes were removed from the water after seven days, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, dried, and subjected to Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. The spectra of the membranes before and after immersion were nearly identical, with no significant reduction in the characteristic peaks associated with the supercontainer’s organic ligands or the cobalt metal centers. This confirmed that the supercontainer had not dissolved or leached from the polymeric matrix. Additionally, the potentiometric response of the membranes after the leaching period was retested, and both the slope and detection limit of the supercontainer-containing membrane remained unchanged, demonstrating that the sensing functionality was fully preserved. In contrast, the membrane without the supercontainer, which had lost its methylene blue content, exhibited no measurable response to methylene blue, confirming that the ionophore was essential for sensing. The leaching study was further extended to include exposure to simulated physiological conditions, wherein the membranes were immersed in phosphate-buffered saline at pH 7.4 and 37 degrees Celsius for 14 days. Again, no leaching of methylene blue or supercontainer was observed, and the potentiometric response remained stable. The absence of leaching is attributed to the physical entrapment of the methylene blue within the rigid, cage-like structure of the supercontainer, which is itself embedded within the dense, high-molecular-weight polyvinyl chloride matrix. The combination of steric confinement and the strong cation–π interactions between the aromatic walls of the supercontainer and the methylene blue cation effectively prevents dissociation and diffusion. This remarkable retention capability is a critical advantage over conventional ion-selective membranes, which often suffer from ionophore leaching, leading to signal drift, reduced sensitivity, and shortened operational lifetimes. The invention, by contrast, provides a self-contained, non-leaching sensing system that maintains its integrity and performance over extended periods, even under harsh conditions, making it suitable for continuous monitoring applications in environmental, industrial, and biomedical settings where long-term stability is paramount.

### EXAMPLE 3: SIMULATED WASTE WATER SAMPLE

To evaluate the practical applicability of the invention in real-world conditions, a simulated wastewater experiment was conducted using water collected from the Fyris River (Fyrisån) in Uppsala, Sweden. This water sample contained a complex mixture of organic matter, suspended particulates, microbial biomass, dissolved oxygen, and naturally occurring ions, representing a realistic matrix for environmental monitoring applications. The sample was filtered through a 0.45-micrometer membrane to remove large particulates and then spiked with controlled concentrations of methylene blue ranging from 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻³ molar. The potentiometric response of the supercontainer-containing mixed-matrix membrane electrode was measured under identical conditions as in pure deionized water, with the same reference electrode, amplification, and data acquisition protocols. The resulting calibration curve exhibited a near-Nernstian slope of 54.2 ± 3.1 millivolts per decade, slightly lower than the 56.8 millivolts per decade observed in deionized water, but still well within the acceptable range for quantitative analysis. The detection limit was determined to be approximately 800 nanomolar, a modest increase from the 300 nanomolar limit in pure water, but still sufficient for detecting methylene blue at environmentally relevant concentrations. To investigate the cause of this slight shift, a control experiment was performed in which equal concentrations of methylene blue were dissolved separately in deionized water and in the river water sample. UV–vis spectroscopy revealed that the absorbance of methylene blue in the river water was significantly reduced, with a peak height approximately 25% lower than in deionized water. This attenuation was attributed to the partial reduction of methylene blue to its leuco form by microbial activity in the river water, which consumes dissolved oxygen and creates a reducing microenvironment. The leuco form of methylene blue is colorless and non-cationic, and therefore invisible to the sensor, which responds exclusively to the cationic form. The reduction process does not alter the total amount of methylene blue added but decreases the concentration of the detectable species, thereby shifting the apparent detection limit. Importantly, the slope of the calibration curve remained linear and reproducible, indicating that the sensor’s sensitivity to the cationic form was unaffected by the matrix components. The presence of humic acids, fulvic acids, and other organic interferents did not produce a measurable signal, and no cross-sensitivity to common ions such as sodium, potassium, calcium, or chloride was observed. The electrode maintained its response stability over multiple measurements, with no drift or signal degradation after repeated immersion in the river water. This demonstrates that the supercontainer-based membrane is not only resistant to leaching but also highly resistant to fouling and interference from complex environmental matrices. The system’s ability to selectively detect the cationic form of methylene blue in the presence of its reduced analogues and other organic pollutants represents a significant advancement over conventional analytical methods, which typically require pre-treatment, extraction, or chromatographic separation to distinguish between redox states. The invention thus enables direct, real-time, and selective potentiometric detection of molecular pollutants in their bioactive form, providing a powerful tool for environmental monitoring and pollution tracking. The robustness of the sensor in natural water samples confirms its suitability for field deployment and continuous monitoring applications, where sample preparation is impractical and reliability is essential.

### EXAMPLE 4: MOLECULAR ION SENSING

The invention enables a fundamentally new paradigm in electrochemical sensing by permitting the selective detection of molecular ions based on their geometric dimensions, rather than their chemical composition or charge characteristics. This capability arises from the unique architecture of the metal–organic supercontainer, which functions as a molecular sieve for cations, allowing only those species whose size and shape are compatible with its internal cavities to bind and generate a measurable potentiometric signal. The supercontainer’s endocavity, with a diameter of approximately 1.7 nanometers, and its six exocavities, each approximately 0.74 nanometers in diameter, form a three-dimensional binding landscape that is geometrically matched to molecular ions such as methylene blue and tetrabutylammonium, while excluding smaller ions such as potassium, silver, and ammonium. This size-based discrimination is not a function of binding affinity in the classical sense, but rather of physical accessibility: the target ion must be able to enter the cavity to induce the necessary charge redistribution at the membrane–analyte interface. The absence of response to ions smaller than 0.3 nanometers in diameter confirms that the sensing mechanism is not mediated by electrostatic attraction or coordination chemistry, but by steric exclusion. This principle extends beyond the two model analytes tested, suggesting that the supercontainer architecture can be adapted to detect a wide range of molecular cations, including pharmaceuticals such as propranolol, atenolol, or ciprofloxacin, which possess molecular dimensions in the range of 1.0 to 2.0 nanometers. Similarly, surfactants such as cetyltrimethylammonium bromide, dyes such as crystal violet or malachite green, and even certain neurotransmitters such as serotonin or dopamine in their protonated forms may be detectable if their geometry aligns with the cavity dimensions. The modular nature of the metal–organic supercontainer allows for systematic tuning of its cavity size and chemical environment through variation of the metal center, organic linker, or calixarene substituents. For example, replacing cobalt with zinc or copper may alter the cavity polarity and binding strength, while elongating the dicarboxylate linker may expand the endocavity to accommodate larger molecules such as peptides or antibiotic residues. The inclusion of functional groups such as sulfonates or carboxylates on the calixarene rim could introduce additional electrostatic interactions, enhancing selectivity for specific classes of analytes without compromising the size-based discrimination mechanism. The solid-contact variant of the electrode further expands the applicability of the invention by enabling integration with microfabricated systems, allowing for the development of multi-analyte sensor arrays on a single chip, each element functionalized with a different supercontainer variant to detect a distinct molecular ion. Such an array could be deployed for simultaneous monitoring of multiple pollutants in wastewater, or for multiplexed detection of biomarkers in biological fluids. The absence of an internal filling solution in the solid-contact configuration eliminates the need for electrolyte replenishment, reduces the risk of contamination, and enables miniaturization down to the micrometer scale, making the system suitable for implantable or wearable sensors. The exceptional stability of the membrane, demonstrated by its resistance to leaching, fouling, and degradation over extended periods, ensures that these devices can operate autonomously for months or even years without recalibration. The invention thus opens a new frontier in electrochemical sensing, where molecular recognition is governed not by chemical similarity but by spatial compatibility, enabling the detection of previously inaccessible analytes with unprecedented specificity and reliability. The potential applications span environmental science, where the detection of emerging contaminants is critical; biomedical diagnostics, where the identification of drug metabolites or biomarkers in bodily fluids is essential; food safety, where the monitoring of dye adulterants or preservatives is required; and industrial process control, where real-time detection of organic ions in effluent streams can prevent regulatory violations. The invention represents not merely an incremental improvement in sensor technology, but a transformative advancement that redefines the criteria for molecular recognition in electrochemical systems.
