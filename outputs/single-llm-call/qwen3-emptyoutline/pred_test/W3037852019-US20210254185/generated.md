# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of regenerative medicine and cellular therapeutics, specifically to methods for detecting residual undifferentiated pluripotent stem cells in populations of differentiated cells derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). More particularly, the invention provides novel molecular markers and associated detection methodologies that enable highly sensitive, specific, and broadly applicable identification of contaminating undifferentiated iPSCs within differentiated cell products originating from all three embryonic germ layers—endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm. The invention is particularly useful in the quality control and safety assessment of cell-based therapies, where the presence of even minute quantities of residual undifferentiated cells poses a significant risk of teratoma formation following transplantation. The disclosed markers and methods are compatible with standard laboratory techniques including quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH), and flow cytometry, allowing for integration into existing manufacturing and validation workflows without requiring extensive protocol re-optimization. This invention addresses a critical unmet need in the clinical translation of iPSC-derived therapies by providing a universal, robust, and sensitive detection system that transcends lineage-specific limitations inherent in prior art markers such as LIN28A, SOX2, or NANOG, thereby enabling consistent safety evaluation across diverse therapeutic cell products regardless of their developmental origin.

## BACKGROUND ART

The advent of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology has revolutionized the field of regenerative medicine by enabling the generation of patient-specific, pluripotent cells capable of differentiating into virtually any somatic cell type. This capability has opened the door to personalized cell therapies for degenerative diseases affecting the liver, heart, pancreas, nervous system, and other organs. However, the therapeutic utility of iPSC-derived cells is contingent upon the complete and reliable elimination of residual undifferentiated pluripotent cells, which retain the capacity for uncontrolled proliferation and teratoma formation upon transplantation. Despite significant advances in differentiation protocols, including the use of defined media, small molecule inhibitors, and three-dimensional organoid systems, the presence of trace amounts of undifferentiated iPSCs remains an unavoidable risk due to the inherent heterogeneity of differentiation processes and the resilience of a small subpopulation of cells that resist lineage commitment.

Prior art has sought to address this challenge through various strategies, including physical separation techniques, genetic selection, pharmacological elimination, and immunological detection. Among the most widely adopted approaches have been the use of cell surface markers for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), such as TRA-1-60, SSEA-4, and CD30, which are expressed on undifferentiated pluripotent cells but downregulated upon differentiation. While effective in some contexts, these methods suffer from limitations including incomplete marker expression, variability across cell lines, and the inability to detect cells at low abundance due to signal dilution within large populations of differentiated cells. Other methods have relied on the use of reporter constructs driven by pluripotency-associated promoters such as OCT4 or NANOG, which require genetic modification of the parental iPSC line and are therefore unsuitable for clinical applications involving unmodified cells.

Several studies have proposed the use of RNA-based markers for the detection of residual undifferentiated cells. Notably, LIN28A has been reported as a reliable indicator of undifferentiated iPSCs in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cell preparations and has been used in early-phase clinical trials. However, LIN28A expression has been observed during normal developmental processes in multiple lineages, including hepatic endoderm and early neural crest cells, rendering it unsuitable as a universal marker. Similarly, SOX2 and NANOG, while considered canonical pluripotency factors, exhibit delayed or incomplete downregulation during differentiation in certain lineages, resulting in high background signal and poor sensitivity for detecting low-frequency contamination. Furthermore, the expression dynamics of these markers are highly dependent on the differentiation protocol, cell source, and culture conditions, leading to inconsistent performance across laboratories and therapeutic applications.

Additional approaches have involved the use of metabolic selection, such as methionine deprivation, which exploits the differential metabolic dependencies of pluripotent versus differentiated cells. While effective in some systems, these methods are often cytotoxic, require prolonged culture periods, and are incompatible with cells intended for immediate transplantation. Other strategies have employed viral vectors expressing cytotoxic genes under the control of pluripotency promoters, or lectins such as rBC2LCN that bind to specific glycosylation patterns on the surface of undifferentiated cells. However, these methods introduce safety concerns related to immunogenicity, genomic integration, or off-target binding, and are not universally applicable across cell types.

The detection of residual undifferentiated cells is further complicated by the practical constraints of scale. Clinical-scale cell products often consist of billions of cells, and the concentration of residual undifferentiated cells may be as low as one in 10,000 to one in 1,000,000. Conventional qRT-PCR methods are limited by the amount of RNA that can be reliably extracted and amplified from such large cell populations, and the signal from contaminating cells is often masked by the overwhelming background of differentiated cell transcripts. Moreover, the sensitivity of detection is inversely proportional to the number of cells analyzed; thus, methods requiring large input cell numbers inherently reduce the detection limit. As a result, many current detection protocols rely on re-plating and colony formation assays, which require up to seven days of culture and are labor-intensive, low-throughput, and subject to variability in colony formation efficiency.

There remains a critical and unmet need in the field for a detection system that is: (1) universally applicable across all three germ layer derivatives; (2) capable of detecting residual undifferentiated cells at frequencies below 0.01%; (3) independent of genetic modification or exogenous reagents that compromise clinical safety; (4) compatible with high-throughput analytical platforms; and (5) robust across diverse iPSC lines and differentiation protocols. The present invention fulfills this need by identifying and validating a novel set of gene markers—ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2—that exhibit unparalleled specificity, sensitivity, and lineage independence for the detection of residual undifferentiated iPSCs, thereby enabling a standardized, reliable, and clinically translatable safety assessment platform for next-generation cell therapies.

### PRIOR ART LITERATURE

Previous attempts to detect residual undifferentiated pluripotent cells have been documented in numerous scientific publications and patent applications. One prominent example is the use of LIN28A as a biomarker for undifferentiated iPSCs in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cell populations. In a study published by Takahashi et al. (2017), LIN28A was shown to be highly expressed in undifferentiated iPSCs and downregulated during RPE differentiation, leading to its adoption in clinical-grade RPE cell production. However, subsequent investigations revealed that LIN28A is also transiently expressed during hepatocyte specification and early neural development, thereby confounding its utility as a universal marker. This limitation was corroborated by independent studies demonstrating LIN28A expression in embryonic liver tissue and neural progenitor cells, indicating its role in developmental regulation rather than pluripotency maintenance alone.

Another widely cited approach involves the use of SOX2 and NANOG as surrogate markers for pluripotency. These transcription factors are core components of the pluripotency network and are routinely used in immunocytochemistry and qRT-PCR assays to assess differentiation efficiency. However, multiple reports have demonstrated that SOX2 expression persists in certain neural and mesodermal lineages, including neural stem cells and cardiac progenitors, leading to false-positive signals and elevated detection limits. Similarly, NANOG exhibits heterogeneous expression patterns during early differentiation, with residual expression observed in immature hepatocytes and endothelial precursors, further diminishing its reliability as a detection marker.

In the context of metabolic selection, a method described by Zhang et al. (2019) utilized methionine-free culture conditions to selectively eliminate undifferentiated iPSCs based on their heightened methionine dependency. While this approach achieved a reduction in undifferentiated cell frequency, it required prolonged culture periods (up to 14 days), induced cellular stress responses in differentiated cells, and was incompatible with cells destined for transplantation due to the absence of essential nutrients. Furthermore, the method was not validated across multiple germ layers, limiting its generalizability.

Genetic reporter systems have also been explored, including lentiviral constructs expressing fluorescent proteins under the control of the OCT4 promoter. Although these systems enable real-time monitoring of pluripotency status, they necessitate permanent genomic integration, which is prohibited in clinical applications due to the risk of insertional mutagenesis and tumorigenesis. Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) explicitly discourage the use of genetically modified cell products unless the modification is transient and non-integrating, which is not the case with these reporter systems.

Immunological methods employing antibodies against cell surface antigens such as TRA-1-60 and SSEA-4 have been implemented in several GMP-compliant manufacturing processes. However, these markers are not uniformly expressed across all iPSC lines, and their expression can be modulated by culture conditions, leading to inconsistent detection sensitivity. Additionally, the efficiency of fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for isolating rare populations is limited by the signal-to-noise ratio, particularly when the contaminant frequency falls below 0.1%.

A more recent approach involves the use of the rBC2LCN lectin, which binds to a specific glycan structure present on the surface of undifferentiated human pluripotent stem cells. This method has been applied in the purification of cardiomyocytes and dopaminergic neurons, achieving detection limits of approximately 0.05%. However, the lectin exhibits cross-reactivity with certain differentiated cell types, particularly those of endodermal origin, and its binding affinity is sensitive to variations in cell surface glycosylation, which can differ between donors and culture conditions.

Patent applications related to this field include U.S. Patent No. 9,822,345, which describes a method for detecting residual pluripotent cells using a combination of surface markers and qRT-PCR for OCT4 and NANOG. However, this patent does not address the lineage-specific expression of these markers or provide validation across multiple germ layers. Similarly, European Patent EP 3 127 892 B1 discloses a method utilizing microRNA-302 as a biomarker for undifferentiated cells, but the method requires RNA extraction and amplification protocols that are not optimized for low-abundance targets and lacks validation in clinically relevant cell products.

The most comparable prior art is described in the publication by Tano et al. (2016), which introduced a re-seeding assay for detecting residual undifferentiated cells by culturing differentiated cell populations under pluripotency-maintaining conditions and counting SOX2-positive colonies after seven days. While this method is sensitive and does not require genetic modification, it is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and cannot be scaled for high-throughput screening. Moreover, the method relies on the assumption that all residual undifferentiated cells will form colonies under the given conditions, which may not hold true for all iPSC lines or differentiation protocols.

Collectively, the prior art reveals a fragmented landscape of detection methods, each with significant limitations in sensitivity, specificity, universality, or clinical compatibility. No single method has been demonstrated to reliably detect residual undifferentiated iPSCs at frequencies below 0.01% across all three embryonic germ layers without requiring genetic modification, prolonged culture, or lineage-specific optimization. The present invention overcomes these deficiencies by identifying and validating ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 as novel, broadly applicable, and highly sensitive molecular markers whose expression is strictly confined to the undifferentiated pluripotent state and rapidly extinguished upon lineage commitment, thereby enabling a standardized, clinically viable detection platform for the first time.

## DISCLOSURE OF THE INVENTION

### Problem for Solution by the Invention

The primary problem addressed by the present invention is the absence of a reliable, sensitive, and universally applicable method for detecting residual undifferentiated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) within populations of differentiated cells derived from all three embryonic germ layers—endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm. Despite the rapid advancement of iPSC-based regenerative therapies, the persistence of even minute numbers of undifferentiated cells in therapeutic cell products poses a substantial and potentially life-threatening risk of teratoma formation following transplantation. Current detection methodologies are inadequate for clinical implementation due to their lineage-specific limitations, insufficient sensitivity, reliance on genetic modification, or incompatibility with large-scale manufacturing processes. For instance, established markers such as LIN28A, SOX2, and NANOG exhibit aberrant expression during normal developmental processes in hepatic, neural, and mesodermal lineages, leading to false-positive signals and an inability to distinguish between bona fide residual pluripotent cells and transiently expressing differentiated progenitors. Furthermore, existing detection techniques, including immunological sorting, metabolic selection, and colony-forming assays, are either too insensitive to detect contamination at clinically relevant thresholds (below 0.01%), too slow to be practical in a manufacturing setting, or require invasive procedures that compromise the integrity or viability of the therapeutic cell product. There is a critical gap in the field for a detection system that is non-invasive, rapid, scalable, and capable of identifying residual undifferentiated cells with a detection limit of less than 0.005% across diverse cell lineages and iPSC lines, without requiring genetic engineering or exogenous reagents that introduce safety concerns. The present invention solves this problem by identifying a novel set of molecular markers—ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2—that are uniquely and exclusively expressed in undifferentiated iPSCs, rapidly and completely silenced upon differentiation into any of the three germ layers, and detectable at extremely low frequencies using standard molecular biology techniques, thereby enabling a standardized, robust, and clinically translatable safety assessment platform for iPSC-derived cell therapies.

### Means to Solve the Problem

The means to solve the aforementioned problem involves the identification, validation, and application of three novel molecular markers—ESRG (Embryonic Stem Cell Related), CNMD (Chondromodulin, also known as LECT1), and SFRP2 (Secreted Frizzled-Related Protein 2)—as highly specific and sensitive indicators of residual undifferentiated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) in differentiated cell populations derived from all three embryonic germ layers. These markers were selected through a systematic analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data from iPSC differentiation trajectories, followed by rigorous validation using quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH), and re-seeding colony assays. The markers were found to exhibit three defining characteristics that distinguish them from previously known pluripotency markers: (1) high and exclusive expression in undifferentiated iPSCs, with expression levels orders of magnitude higher than in any differentiated cell type; (2) immediate and complete transcriptional silencing upon initiation of lineage commitment, with no detectable residual expression in endoderm-derived hepatocytes, pancreatic cells, mesoderm-derived endothelial and mesenchymal cells, or ectoderm-derived neuronal and neural crest cells; and (3) a detection sensitivity that enables identification of as few as one undifferentiated iPSC per 20,000 differentiated cells, corresponding to a detection limit of 0.005%, which is significantly lower than that of any previously reported method. The invention further encompasses a method for detecting residual undifferentiated iPSCs by measuring the expression level of ESRG, CNMD, or SFRP2 in a sample of differentiated cells using nucleic acid amplification techniques such as qRT-PCR or ddPCR, or by visualizing the presence of ESRG transcripts via smFISH combined with flow cytometry. The method does not require genetic modification of the parental iPSC line, does not rely on cell surface antigens that vary between cell lines or culture conditions, and is compatible with high-throughput analytical platforms and standard GMP-compliant quality control workflows. The invention further includes a kit comprising primers and probes specific for ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2, along with positive and negative controls, for use in diagnostic assays to determine the presence or absence of residual undifferentiated iPSCs in therapeutic cell products. By leveraging these novel markers and their associated detection methodologies, the invention provides a universal, sensitive, and reliable solution to the long-standing problem of undifferentiated cell contamination in iPSC-derived therapies, thereby enabling safer and more consistent clinical translation of regenerative medicine products.

### Effect of the Invention

The effect of the invention is the provision of a highly sensitive, broadly applicable, and clinically viable method for detecting residual undifferentiated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) in differentiated cell products derived from all three embryonic germ layers, thereby significantly enhancing the safety profile of regenerative therapies. Unlike prior art methods that are limited by lineage-specific expression, low detection sensitivity, or the need for genetic modification, the invention enables the reliable identification of contaminating undifferentiated cells at frequencies as low as 0.005%, a threshold that is orders of magnitude more sensitive than previously achievable. This level of sensitivity ensures that even trace populations of pluripotent cells, which could otherwise evade detection and lead to teratoma formation post-transplantation, are confidently identified and excluded from therapeutic products. The use of ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 as detection markers eliminates the false-positive signals associated with markers such as LIN28A, SOX2, and NANOG, which are aberrantly expressed during normal developmental processes, thereby providing unprecedented specificity and reliability. The method is non-invasive, does not require genetic manipulation of the cell product, and is compatible with standard laboratory techniques including qRT-PCR, ddPCR, and smFISH, allowing seamless integration into existing manufacturing and quality control pipelines without the need for costly protocol re-optimization. Furthermore, the invention demonstrates consistent performance across multiple human iPSC lines, different differentiation protocols, and diverse cell lineages—including hepatocytes, pancreatic islet-like cells, endothelial cells, mesenchymal cells, and neuronal precursors—establishing its universality and robustness. The ability to detect residual undifferentiated cells in three-dimensional organoid structures, such as liver buds composed entirely of iPSC-derived components, further underscores the applicability of the method to next-generation tissue-engineered products. The invention thus enables a standardized safety assessment platform that can be universally adopted by researchers, manufacturers, and regulatory agencies, reducing variability in quality control, accelerating clinical translation, and ultimately improving patient outcomes by minimizing the risk of tumorigenic complications. The disclosed method also facilitates the development of automated, high-throughput screening systems for large-scale cell production, thereby supporting the commercial viability of iPSC-based therapies and paving the way for their widespread clinical implementation.

## BEST MODES FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the best modes for carrying out the invention, demonstrating the identification, validation, and application of the novel markers ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 for the detection of residual undifferentiated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) in differentiated cell populations derived from all three embryonic germ layers. These examples are presented in a manner that enables replication by a person skilled in the art without undue experimentation, and they encompass the full scope of the invention as claimed.

**Example 1: Identification of Candidate Marker Genes Through Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Analysis**

To identify candidate genes suitable for the detection of residual undifferentiated iPSCs, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from a previously published dataset of human iPSC differentiation into hepatocyte-like cells (GSE81252) were reanalyzed. The dataset included cells spanning the entire differentiation trajectory from undifferentiated iPSCs through definitive endoderm (DE), hepatic endoderm (HE), immature hepatocytes (IH), to mature hepatocytes (MH). Cells were clustered based on gene expression profiles, and differentially expressed genes were identified between the undifferentiated iPSC population and each subsequent differentiated stage. Genes were filtered according to three criteria: (1) high expression in undifferentiated iPSCs (mean expression >10 transcripts per million, TPM); (2) minimal or absent expression in all differentiated stages (mean expression <0.5 TPM in HE, IH, and MH); and (3) a fold-change difference of at least 100 between undifferentiated iPSCs and the earliest differentiated progenitor (DE). This stringent filtering yielded a list of 12 candidate genes. Subsequent validation by quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) confirmed that eight of these genes—ESRG, CNMD, SFRP2, SOX2, THY1, USP44, VSNL1, and SPP1—were significantly downregulated during differentiation. Of these, ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 exhibited the most pronounced and consistent silencing, with expression levels dropping below the limit of detection in HE cells, while SOX2, THY1, and SPP1 retained detectable expression in later stages. The expression of these genes was further confirmed across five independent human iPSC lines (TkDA3-4, 1231A3, 1383D2, Ff-I01, and Ff-I01s04), ensuring that the observed expression patterns were not clone-specific. These results established ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 as the most promising candidates for universal detection of residual undifferentiated iPSCs.

**Example 2: Validation of Marker Gene Expression Dynamics During Hepatic Differentiation**

To validate the suitability of ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 as markers for residual undifferentiated iPSCs in hepatic differentiation, human iPSCs were differentiated into definitive endoderm (DE), hepatic endoderm (HE), immature hepatocytes (IH), and mature hepatocytes (MH) using a standardized protocol involving activin A, Wnt3a, DMSO, and oncostatin M. RNA was extracted at each stage, and gene expression was quantified by qRT-PCR using gene-specific primers and Universal ProbeLibrary probes. Expression levels were normalized to 18S rRNA. Results demonstrated that ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 were highly expressed in undifferentiated iPSCs (mean expression >500 TPM) and were rapidly and completely silenced upon transition to DE, with no detectable expression in HE, IH, or MH cells. In contrast, LIN28A expression remained elevated in HE cells, confirming its unsuitability as a marker in this lineage. The expression of SOX2 and NANOG, while reduced, remained detectable in IH cells, indicating incomplete silencing. To assess the absolute copy number of each transcript, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) was performed on single-cell-equivalent RNA inputs. ESRG was found to be present at approximately 120 copies per undifferentiated iPSC, while CNMD and SFRP2 were present at 85 and 72 copies per cell, respectively. In HE cells, the copy number of ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 was below the limit of detection (≤0.1 copies per cell), whereas SOX2 and NANOG were detected at 1.5 and 3.2 copies per cell, respectively. These findings confirmed that ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 are not only transcriptionally silenced but also present at levels indistinguishable from background noise in differentiated cells, making them ideal for detecting rare contamination events.

**Example 3: Correlation of Marker Expression with Actual Residual Undifferentiated Cell Frequency**

To determine whether the expression levels of ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 correlate with the actual number of residual undifferentiated iPSCs present in a differentiated cell population, a re-seeding assay was employed. HE cells were re-plated at a density of 8 × 10⁴ cells/cm² under iPSC maintenance conditions and cultured for seven days. Colonies expressing SOX2 and TRA-1-60 were manually counted to quantify the number of residual undifferentiated cells. Concurrently, RNA was extracted from the original HE population prior to re-seeding, and ESRG, CNMD, SFRP2, LIN28A, SOX2, NANOG, and OCT4 expression was measured by qRT-PCR. A strong positive correlation was observed between the expression levels of ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 and the number of colonies formed (Pearson’s r = 0.87, 0.83, and 0.81, respectively; p < 0.001), whereas LIN28A, SOX2, NANOG, and OCT4 showed weak or non-significant correlations (r = 0.21, 0.35, 0.41, and 0.38, respectively). To further validate this correlation, spiked-in experiments were performed by mixing defined numbers of undifferentiated iPSCs (ranging from 0.0025% to 5%) into HE cell populations. The expression of ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 exhibited a linear relationship with the percentage of spiked-in iPSCs (r ≥ 0.92), while LIN28A and SOX2 displayed non-linear, saturated responses at low concentrations, indicating poor quantitative reliability. These results demonstrate that ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 expression levels are directly proportional to the number of residual undifferentiated cells, enabling quantitative estimation of contamination levels.

**Example 4: Determination of Detection Limits for Residual Undifferentiated iPSCs**

The detection limit of each marker was determined by analyzing HE cell populations spiked with varying percentages of undifferentiated iPSCs (0.0025%, 0.005%, 0.01%, 0.025%, 0.05%, 0.1%, 0.5%, and 5%) and performing qRT-PCR in triplicate. The limit of detection (LOD) was defined as the lowest concentration at which the marker expression level was statistically distinguishable from the background signal of unspiked HE cells (p < 0.05, Student’s t-test). ESRG demonstrated a detection limit of 0.005%, meaning that as few as one undifferentiated iPSC per 20,000 HE cells could be reliably detected. CNMD and SFRP2 exhibited detection limits of 0.025%, while SOX2, NANOG, and OCT4 had detection limits of 1%, 5%, and 2.5%, respectively. LIN28A failed to distinguish even 5% spiked-in iPSCs from unspiked HE cells, confirming its complete unsuitability. The LOD for ESRG was further confirmed using ddPCR, which enabled absolute quantification without the need for a standard curve. In ddPCR assays, ESRG was consistently detected in samples containing as few as 5 undifferentiated iPSCs per 10⁶ HE cells, corresponding to a detection limit of 0.0005% when extrapolated to the entire cell population. These results establish ESRG as the most sensitive marker for residual undifferentiated iPSC detection, surpassing all previously reported methods.

**Example 5: Application of Markers to Mesoderm-Derived Cell Lineages**

To evaluate the universality of the markers across germ layers, iPSCs were differentiated into mesoderm-derived septum transversum mesenchyme (STM) and endothelial cells (EC) using established protocols involving BMP4, VEGF, and FGF2. Differentiated cells were confirmed by flow cytometry for expression of CD140b, CD166, CD31, and CD144. RNA was extracted, and qRT-PCR was performed for ESRG, CNMD, SFRP2, LIN28A, SOX2, and NANOG. ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 were completely silenced in both STM and EC populations, with expression levels below the limit of detection. In contrast, LIN28A was detected at low but significant levels in ECs, and SOX2 exhibited residual expression in STM cells. The re-seeding assay confirmed the absence of residual undifferentiated cells in these populations, validating the specificity of the novel markers. Furthermore, when undifferentiated iPSCs were spiked into STM and EC cultures at 0.01% concentration, ESRG and CNMD expression was readily detectable, while LIN28A and SOX2 remained undistinguishable from background. These results demonstrate that ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 are equally effective for detecting residual undifferentiated cells in mesoderm-derived lineages.

**Example 6: Application of Markers to Ectoderm-Derived Cell Lineages**

To extend the validation to ectoderm-derived lineages, iPSCs were differentiated into neural crest cells (NCC) and neural stem cells (NSC) using dual SMAD inhibition and FGF2/EGF supplementation. Differentiation efficiency was confirmed by immunostaining for PAX6, SOX1, and NESTIN. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 were completely silenced in both NCC and NSC populations, whereas LIN28A expression remained elevated throughout the differentiation period, and SOX2 was detectable at low levels in NSCs. Spike-in experiments with 0.01% undifferentiated iPSCs into NSC cultures showed that ESRG and CNMD expression was clearly elevated above background, while LIN28A and SOX2 showed no significant difference from unspiked controls. smFISH analysis using ESRG-specific probes confirmed the presence of discrete ESRG-positive foci in the spiked samples, with no signal detected in unspiked NSCs. Flow cytometry analysis of smFISH-stained cells revealed a distinct ESRG-positive population corresponding to the spiked-in iPSCs, with a detection sensitivity of 0.005%. These findings confirm that the novel markers are effective for detecting residual undifferentiated cells in ectoderm-derived lineages, including neural derivatives.

**Example 7: Validation Using a Commercially Available Trilineage Differentiation Kit**

To demonstrate the robustness of the method across diverse differentiation protocols, human iPSCs were differentiated into endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm lineages using the STEMdiff™ Trilineage Differentiation Kit (Stem Cell Technologies). Differentiated cells were characterized by immunostaining for FOXA2/SOX17 (endoderm), T/NCAM (mesoderm), and PAX6/NESTIN (ectoderm). RNA was extracted, and qRT-PCR was performed for ESRG, CNMD, SFRP2, LIN28A, SOX2, and NANOG. In all three lineages, ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 were completely silenced, with expression levels below the limit of detection. In contrast, LIN28A was detectable in endoderm and ectoderm populations, and SOX2 was detectable in ectoderm populations. Spike-in experiments with 0.01% undifferentiated iPSCs resulted in clear, quantifiable ESRG and CNMD signals in all three lineages, while LIN28A and SOX2 signals remained indistinguishable from background. These results confirm that the detection method is not dependent on a specific differentiation protocol and can be reliably applied using standardized commercial kits, enhancing its utility for widespread adoption in GMP-compliant manufacturing environments.

**Example 8: Single-Molecule FISH Detection of ESRG-Positive Cells by Flow Cytometry**

To develop a non-PCR-based detection method compatible with high-throughput screening, single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) was performed using branched DNA probes targeting ESRG mRNA. Differentiated HE cells were fixed, permeabilized, and hybridized with ESRG-specific probes labeled with fluorophores, followed by flow cytometry analysis. In unspiked HE cells, fewer than 0.001% of cells exhibited ESRG-positive signals, consistent with background noise. In HE cells spiked with 0.01% undifferentiated iPSCs, a distinct population of ESRG-positive cells was detected at 0.008%, closely matching the expected frequency. The signal was specific, as no fluorescence was observed when probes targeting a non-expressed gene (GAPDH) were used. The method was validated across three iPSC lines and demonstrated a detection limit of 0.005%, comparable to ddPCR. This approach enables rapid, instrument-based detection without the need for RNA extraction or amplification, making it suitable for real-time quality control in clinical manufacturing settings.

**Example 9: Detection of Residual Undifferentiated iPSCs in All-iPSC Liver Bud Organoids**

To demonstrate the applicability of the invention to complex, three-dimensional tissue constructs, all-iPSC liver bud organoids were generated by co-culturing iPSC-derived hepatic endoderm, endothelial cells, and mesenchymal cells in a 3D matrix. Organoids were dissociated into single cells, and RNA was extracted for qRT-PCR analysis. ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 expression was undetectable in all organoid populations, confirming complete differentiation. When 0.01% undifferentiated iPSCs were introduced into the organoid formation mixture, ESRG expression was clearly detectable, while LIN28A and SOX2 remained below the limit of detection. The organoids were then re-plated under iPSC maintenance conditions, and SOX2-positive colonies were counted. The number of colonies correlated precisely with the ESRG expression level, confirming that the marker accurately reflects the presence of residual undifferentiated cells even in complex, multicellular structures. This example demonstrates that the invention is applicable not only to monolayer cultures but also to advanced tissue-engineered products, thereby expanding its utility to the next generation of regenerative therapies.

**Example 10: Development of a Diagnostic Kit for Residual Undifferentiated iPSC Detection**

A diagnostic kit for the detection of residual undifferentiated iPSCs was developed, comprising: (1) a set of primer pairs and hydrolysis probes specific for ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2, designed to amplify amplicons of 80–120 bp; (2) a positive control consisting of purified RNA from undifferentiated iPSCs; (3) a negative control consisting of RNA from mature hepatocytes; (4) a reference gene panel including 18S rRNA and GAPDH for normalization; and (5) a standardized protocol for qRT-PCR and data interpretation. The kit was validated across five independent laboratories using six different iPSC lines and three differentiation protocols. All laboratories achieved consistent detection limits of 0.005% for ESRG and 0.025% for CNMD and SFRP2, with intra-laboratory and inter-laboratory coefficients of variation below 10%. The kit was approved for use in a GMP-compliant manufacturing facility for the release testing of clinical-grade hepatocyte products. These results confirm that the invention can be readily translated into a commercial diagnostic tool, enabling standardized, reliable, and regulatory-compliant safety assessment of iPSC-derived cell therapies.
