# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Proteolytic processing of proteins is a fundamental and irreversible post-translational modification that governs a vast array of biological processes essential to cellular homeostasis, development, and organismal survival. These enzymatic cleavage events regulate critical pathways including programmed cell death, inflammatory signaling, autophagy, cell cycle progression, and intracellular trafficking. Among the most extensively studied families of proteases are the caspases, a group of evolutionarily conserved cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific endopeptidases that play central roles in apoptosis and inflammation. The human genome encodes at least ten distinct caspases, which are organized into a hierarchical network of initiators and effectors. Initiator caspases, such as caspase-8, -9, and -10, are activated through oligomerization within large multi-protein complexes formed in response to specific death signals, while effector caspases, including caspase-3, -6, and -7, are subsequently cleaved and activated by the initiators to execute downstream proteolytic events that dismantle the cell. The activation of initiator caspases is not a simple auto-processing event but rather a tightly regulated process dependent on scaffold proteins, adapter molecules, and often membrane-associated receptor complexes. For instance, caspase-8 is recruited to the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) via interactions between its death effector domains and those of the adapter protein FADD, which itself binds to the cytoplasmic tail of death receptors such as CD95 (Fas) or TRAIL receptors upon ligand engagement. Similarly, caspase-9 is activated within the apoptosome, a wheel-like complex assembled around the adaptor protein Apaf-1 in the presence of cytochrome c and dATP. These multi-component activation mechanisms are highly context-dependent, requiring precise stoichiometry, spatial organization, and temporal coordination that cannot be faithfully recapitulated using purified catalytic domains in biochemical assays.

The complexity of these activation pathways presents significant challenges for drug discovery. Traditional high-throughput screening (HTS) platforms for protease inhibitors typically rely on recombinant catalytic domains of proteases incubated with synthetic fluorogenic or chromogenic substrates. While useful for identifying direct active-site inhibitors, these systems are fundamentally limited in their ability to detect compounds that modulate protease activity indirectly—such as those that interfere with upstream activators, disrupt complex assembly, or alter subcellular localization. Furthermore, because many caspases share striking structural homology in their catalytic pockets, achieving selectivity among family members with small-molecule inhibitors has proven exceedingly difficult. Compounds designed to block caspase-3 often cross-react with caspase-7 or even caspase-6, limiting therapeutic utility and increasing the risk of off-target toxicity. The inability to screen for activators of proteases is another critical limitation of current biochemical assays, as many disease states involve pathological suppression of proteolytic activity, and restoration of function may be therapeutically beneficial. Additionally, the expression and purification of full-length, intact caspases—particularly those requiring membrane association or multi-subunit complexes for activity—remains technically challenging, often resulting in low yields, instability, or loss of native conformation. The reliance on catalytic domains further precludes the identification of allosteric modulators, which may offer greater specificity and fewer side effects than active-site binders. These shortcomings underscore the need for a cellular platform that preserves the native architecture of protease activation pathways while enabling scalable, quantitative, and reproducible readouts suitable for large-scale screening.

Yeast, particularly Saccharomyces cerevisiae, has long served as a powerful model organism for studying eukaryotic cell biology due to its genetic tractability, rapid growth, low cost, and conservation of core cellular machinery. Although yeast lacks endogenous caspases, it has been successfully employed to heterologously express mammalian proteases and their regulators, enabling functional studies in a simplified, genetically amenable system. Prior efforts have demonstrated that overexpression of certain caspases in yeast can induce cell death, a phenotype that has been exploited to screen for inhibitors of apoptosis. However, these approaches are inherently binary—cell death versus survival—and lack the sensitivity, quantifiability, and dynamic range required for HTS. Other systems have employed cleavable transcription factors fused to membrane-tethered domains, where protease-mediated cleavage liberates a transcriptional activator that drives expression of a reporter gene such as LEU2 or lacZ. While these systems have been used to define substrate specificities or identify endogenous inhibitors, they have not been systematically optimized for robust, quantitative, high-throughput screening across the entire caspase family or for reconstituting multi-component signaling networks. Moreover, no prior system has successfully integrated the full spectrum of human caspases—both initiators and effectors—with their cognate upstream activators into a single, modular, and scalable platform capable of distinguishing direct protease inhibitors from upstream modulators through counter-screening. The ability to reconstitute physiologically relevant activation pathways, including those involving NLR-family inflammasomes or death receptor complexes, in yeast has not been previously achieved in a manner that supports HTS-compatible readouts with validated statistical parameters such as Z’ factors exceeding 0.5. Furthermore, the adaptation of such systems to screen for activators of proteases, or to identify novel regulatory proteins through cDNA library screening, has remained largely unexplored. There is therefore a critical unmet need for a versatile, genetically engineered yeast-based platform that can faithfully reconstitute mammalian protease activation networks, provide quantitative, dose-responsive readouts, enable both inhibitor and activator screening, and allow for high-throughput deconvolution of compound mechanisms of action through modular counter-screens. Such a platform would represent a transformative tool for drug discovery across a broad spectrum of diseases involving dysregulated proteolysis, including neurodegeneration, autoimmune disorders, ischemic injury, and cancer.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel, genetically engineered yeast-based platform for the detection, quantification, and high-throughput screening of protease activity and modulation, with particular application to the human caspase family and autophagins. This platform enables the reconstitution of complex, multi-component mammalian protease activation pathways within the simple eukaryotic host Saccharomyces cerevisiae, allowing for the functional analysis of protease regulation in a living cellular context that preserves essential spatial, temporal, and stoichiometric relationships. The invention comprises a suite of modular genetic components, including cleavable transcription factor reporter systems, a panel of expression vectors with tunable promoter strengths and plasmid copy numbers, and plasmids encoding full-length human caspases and their upstream activators, all designed to function synergistically within yeast cells. The core innovation lies in the design of a membrane-tethered chimeric transcription factor consisting of the cytoplasmic domain of the human Fas (CD95) receptor fused to a DNA-binding domain (LexA) and a transcriptional activation domain (B42), with an intervening linker sequence containing a protease-specific cleavage motif. Upon proteolytic cleavage of this linker by an active protease, the transcription factor is liberated from the membrane, translocates to the nucleus, and activates reporter genes—LEU2 and lacZ—under the control of LexA-binding operators. This cleavage-dependent activation provides a quantitative, colorimetric, and growth-based readout that is directly proportional to protease activity. The system has been optimized for all ten human caspases, each with a distinct tetrapeptide substrate sequence (e.g., WEHD for caspase-1, DEVD for caspase-3, LETD for caspase-8, LEHD for caspase-9), enabling the simultaneous and selective detection of individual caspase activities within the same cellular environment. The platform further accommodates the reconstitution of physiologically relevant multi-protein activation complexes, including the DISC (Fas/FADD/caspase-8), the apoptosome (Apaf-1/caspase-9/caspase-3), and the NLRP1 and NLRP3 inflammasomes (NLRP1/NLRP3/Asc/caspase-1), by precisely titrating the expression levels of each component using promoters of varying strengths and plasmids with different replication origins. This precise control prevents spontaneous activation of caspases, eliminates cytotoxicity, and ensures that reporter activation occurs only upon formation of the intended multi-component complex. The system has also been successfully adapted to the autophagin family, with a cleavable reporter containing the LC3B substrate sequence enabling detection of ATG4B activity. The invention further encompasses high-throughput screening formats in 384-well microtiter plates, validated by Z’ factors exceeding 0.6 for all tested systems, and includes counter-screening assays to distinguish direct protease inhibitors from upstream modulators and to assess compound selectivity across the caspase family. Additionally, the platform supports functional cDNA library screening, allowing for the identification of novel protease activators, inhibitors, or regulatory proteins through selection for reporter gene activation. The invention thus provides a comprehensive, scalable, and broadly applicable system for the discovery of compounds and genes that modulate protease activity through diverse mechanisms, overcoming the limitations of traditional biochemical assays and enabling the identification of therapeutics targeting previously inaccessible nodes in proteolytic signaling networks.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Caspases

Caspases constitute a family of evolutionarily conserved cysteine proteases that play indispensable roles in the execution of programmed cell death and the regulation of inflammatory responses in multicellular organisms. These enzymes are synthesized as inactive zymogens, known as procaspases, which contain an N-terminal prodomain, a large catalytic subunit, and a small catalytic subunit. Activation of caspases occurs through proteolytic cleavage at specific aspartate residues, resulting in the formation of a heterotetrameric enzyme composed of two large and two small subunits. The human genome encodes at least ten distinct caspase genes, which are categorized into initiators and effectors based on their position and function within the proteolytic cascade. Initiator caspases, including caspase-1, -2, -8, -9, and -10, possess long prodomains that contain protein interaction motifs such as caspase recruitment domains (CARDs) or death effector domains (DEDs). These domains facilitate the recruitment of initiator caspases into large, multi-protein activation platforms, such as the inflammasome for caspase-1, the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) for caspase-8 and -10, and the apoptosome for caspase-9. In contrast, effector caspases, including caspase-3, -6, and -7, have short prodomains and are primarily activated by cleavage by initiator caspases. Once activated, effector caspases cleave a wide array of cellular substrates, including structural proteins, DNA repair enzymes, and inhibitors of apoptosis, leading to the systematic dismantling of the cell. Caspase-4 and caspase-5, which are human-specific homologs of caspase-1, are involved in non-canonical inflammasome activation and respond to intracellular lipopolysaccharide. Caspase-2, although classified as an initiator, exhibits functional overlap with effector caspases and can be activated by both apoptotic and non-apoptotic stimuli. The activation of initiator caspases is not a simple autocatalytic event but rather a tightly regulated process dependent on the assembly of multi-protein complexes that induce proximity-driven dimerization and trans-autocleavage. This requirement for complex formation renders the biochemical reconstitution of initiator caspase activity in vitro extremely challenging, as it necessitates the co-expression of multiple proteins in correct stoichiometry and spatial orientation. The present invention overcomes this limitation by leveraging the genetic and cellular machinery of Saccharomyces cerevisiae to reconstitute these mammalian activation complexes in a living cell, thereby preserving the physiological context required for proper caspase activation. The system enables the expression of full-length, unmodified human caspases, including their prodomains, which are essential for correct localization and interaction with upstream regulators. By expressing these caspases under the control of promoters with precisely calibrated strengths, the invention ensures that their expression levels are sufficient to permit activation upon complex assembly but insufficient to cause cytotoxicity or spontaneous activation in the absence of appropriate stimuli. This careful titration of expression levels is a critical innovation that distinguishes the present system from prior attempts, which often resulted in cell death or non-specific activation due to overexpression. The invention further enables the detection of caspase activity through a cleavable transcription factor reporter system, which provides a quantitative, amplifiable, and easily measurable output that correlates directly with protease activity. This approach allows for the functional characterization of all ten human caspases in a single, unified platform, enabling comparative studies of substrate specificity, activation kinetics, and regulatory mechanisms that were previously impossible to perform in a high-throughput manner.

### Substrate Specificity of Caspases

The substrate specificity of caspases is a defining feature that underpins their functional diversity and regulatory precision within the proteolytic cascade. Each caspase recognizes and cleaves target proteins at specific tetrapeptide sequences located N-terminal to an aspartic acid residue, which serves as the P1 position in the cleavage site. The consensus cleavage motifs for human caspases have been extensively characterized through peptide library screening and biochemical assays, revealing distinct but overlapping preferences that reflect their biological roles. Caspase-1, a key mediator of inflammation, preferentially cleaves substrates containing the tetrapeptide sequence WEHD, which is found in the precursors of interleukin-1β and interleukin-18. Caspase-4 and caspase-5, which share structural and functional homology with caspase-1, exhibit similar specificity for WEHD, enabling their detection using the same reporter construct. Caspase-3 and caspase-7, the primary effector caspases responsible for executing apoptosis, exhibit a strong preference for the sequence DEVD, which is present in numerous cellular substrates including PARP, ICAD, and lamin A. Caspase-6, another effector caspase, cleaves substrates containing the sequence TEVD, which is found in lamin A and huntingtin. Caspase-8 and caspase-10, initiator caspases of the extrinsic apoptotic pathway, recognize the sequence LETD, which is present in their own prodomains and in the protein Bid. Caspase-9, the initiator caspase of the intrinsic pathway, cleaves substrates with the sequence LEHD, which is found in caspase-3 and caspase-7. Caspase-2, although classified as an initiator, demonstrates a broader substrate tolerance and can cleave both DEVD and DEHD sequences, with DEHD being its most preferred motif. The present invention exploits these distinct substrate specificities by engineering a series of cleavable transcription factor reporters, each containing a unique tetrapeptide sequence flanked by glycine residues and inserted between the membrane-tethering domain of Fas and the LexA-B42 transcriptional activator. The inclusion of glycine residues at both termini of the cleavage motif ensures optimal accessibility for the protease while minimizing steric hindrance. The use of a single cleavage site per reporter construct was empirically determined to provide the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity, as multiple cleavage sites did not significantly enhance signal intensity but increased the risk of non-specific cleavage or structural instability. The fidelity of substrate recognition was rigorously validated by co-expressing each caspase with its cognate reporter and demonstrating that activation occurred exclusively with the matched substrate sequence. For example, caspase-1 activated only the WEHD-containing reporter, while caspase-3 activated only the DEVD-containing reporter, with no cross-reactivity observed between other caspase-reporter pairs. Even subtle variations in substrate sequence, such as the substitution of histidine for aspartic acid in the P2 position (DEHD), resulted in altered activation profiles, with caspase-2 showing enhanced activity on DEHD compared to DEVD, consistent with prior biochemical data. The system also confirmed the inability of caspases to cleave non-cleavable control constructs in which the critical aspartic acid residue was replaced with glycine (e.g., WEHG, DEVG), thereby eliminating false-positive signals arising from non-specific proteolysis. Importantly, the cleavage specificity observed in yeast mirrored the known substrate preferences established in mammalian systems, validating the fidelity of the reconstituted system. This precise discrimination among caspase substrates enables the simultaneous and independent monitoring of multiple caspase activities within the same yeast strain, a capability that is unprecedented in existing screening platforms. The ability to distinguish between closely related caspases, such as caspase-3 and caspase-7, both of which cleave DEVD, is further enhanced by the use of differential promoter strengths and plasmid copy numbers, which allow for fine-tuning of expression levels to minimize background noise and maximize signal-to-noise ratios. This level of specificity is critical for the development of selective inhibitors and for the identification of compounds that target individual caspases within complex disease contexts where multiple caspases are simultaneously activated.

### Mechanisms of Activating Upstream Initiator Caspases

The activation of upstream initiator caspases is governed by highly regulated, multi-protein complexes that assemble in response to specific cellular signals, ensuring that proteolytic cascades are triggered only under appropriate physiological or pathological conditions. Unlike effector caspases, which are activated by direct cleavage by upstream proteases, initiator caspases require the formation of large oligomeric platforms that induce proximity-driven dimerization, leading to trans-autocleavage and full enzymatic activation. The present invention provides a genetically engineered yeast system capable of reconstituting these complex activation mechanisms with remarkable fidelity, enabling the functional dissection of pathways that have been intractable to study in biochemical assays. For caspase-1, activation occurs within the inflammasome, a multiprotein complex assembled in response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns or cellular stress. The inflammasome consists of a sensor protein, such as NLRP1 or NLRP3, which contains leucine-rich repeats (LRRs) that auto-inhibit the protein in the resting state. Upon ligand binding or conformational change, the LRR domain is displaced, allowing the pyrin domain (PYD) of the sensor to interact with the PYD of the adapter protein Asc, which in turn recruits caspase-1 via homotypic CARD-CARD interactions. The present invention reconstitutes this pathway in yeast by co-expressing a gain-of-function mutant of NLRP1 or NLRP3, lacking the auto-inhibitory LRR domain, along with Asc and pro-caspase-1. The expression of each component is carefully titrated using weak promoters and low-copy plasmids to prevent spontaneous activation, ensuring that reporter gene activation occurs only when all three components are present. Similarly, the activation of caspase-9 is mediated by the apoptosome, a heptameric complex formed by Apaf-1, cytochrome c, and dATP. In yeast, this pathway is reconstituted by expressing a constitutively active mutant of Apaf-1, which bypasses the requirement for cytochrome c, along with pro-caspase-9 and the appropriate reporter. The activation of caspase-8 and caspase-10 occurs within the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), which is assembled upon ligation of death receptors such as CD95 (Fas) or TRAIL receptors. The DISC consists of the receptor, the adapter protein FADD, and the initiator caspase. FADD contains a death domain (DD) that binds to the DD of the receptor and a death effector domain (DED) that recruits the DED-containing pro-caspase-8 or -10. The present invention demonstrates that this entire pathway can be reconstituted in yeast by expressing the receptor, FADD, and the caspase under carefully controlled expression levels. Notably, the system reveals that high levels of FADD alone are sufficient to activate caspase-8 and -10, but the addition of the receptor significantly enhances activation, mimicking the physiological requirement for receptor clustering. This finding underscores the importance of stoichiometric balance in complex assembly and highlights the system’s ability to detect subtle modulations in pathway activity. The invention further extends to the activation of caspase-2 by RAIDD, a CARD-containing adapter that bridges caspase-2 to other signaling molecules. By expressing RAIDD and pro-caspase-2 under weak promoters, the system confirms that activation is strictly dependent on the presence of both components. The ability to reconstitute these diverse activation mechanisms in yeast provides a powerful tool for identifying novel regulators, screening for compounds that disrupt complex assembly, and studying the effects of disease-associated mutations. The system’s modularity allows for the sequential addition or removal of components, enabling the dissection of pathway architecture and the identification of essential versus auxiliary proteins. This level of control and precision is unattainable in traditional biochemical assays, which rely on purified components and lack the cellular context necessary for proper complex formation. The invention thus provides a uniquely powerful platform for probing the molecular mechanisms underlying caspase activation and for discovering therapeutics that target these complex assembly events.

### Phenotypes of Animal Caspases Expressed in Yeast

The heterologous expression of mammalian caspases in Saccharomyces cerevisiae produces a range of phenotypes that are directly linked to the level of expression, the intrinsic activity of the caspase, and the presence or absence of upstream activators. These phenotypes serve as critical indicators of successful reconstitution and provide a foundation for the design of the reporter system. When full-length caspases are overexpressed from strong promoters on high-copy plasmids, several distinct outcomes are observed. Caspases such as caspase-1, -2, -3, -4, -5, -6, -7, and -9, when expressed at moderate to high levels, undergo spontaneous activation due to their inherent capacity for dimerization and auto-processing. This activation leads to a dose-dependent inhibition of yeast growth, a phenotype that is consistent with prior observations and reflects the cytotoxic nature of these proteases when expressed in the absence of endogenous inhibitors. However, the degree of growth inhibition varies significantly among caspases. Caspase-3 and caspase-7, for example, cause pronounced growth suppression even at moderate expression levels, whereas caspase-1 and caspase-9 exhibit a more gradual reduction in growth, suggesting differences in their intrinsic catalytic efficiency or substrate availability in yeast. Caspase-8 and caspase-10, in contrast, produce an even more severe growth defect, often resulting in complete cell death, which is consistent with their potent pro-apoptotic activity in mammalian cells. This cytotoxicity presents a significant challenge for the development of reporter-based assays, as it precludes the use of standard growth-based readouts and renders the system unsuitable for high-throughput screening. The present invention overcomes this limitation by employing a strategy of precise expression titration, wherein caspases are expressed from weak promoters on low-copy plasmids to achieve levels that are sufficient for activation upon complex assembly but insufficient to cause cytotoxicity. For caspase-8 and caspase-10, this strategy is further refined by co-expressing the upstream activator FADD at high levels while maintaining low expression of the caspase itself, thereby ensuring that activation occurs only in the presence of the adapter protein. The use of attenuated promoters, generated through deletion mutagenesis, allows for fine-tuning of expression levels to achieve optimal signal-to-noise ratios. The resulting phenotypes are not merely cytotoxic but are instead characterized by a clear, quantifiable, and reproducible activation of reporter genes, which is dependent on the presence of both the caspase and its cognate activator. The absence of reporter activation in the absence of either component, or in the presence of catalytically inactive mutants, confirms that the observed signal is a direct consequence of proteolytic activity and not a non-specific effect of protein overexpression. Furthermore, the system demonstrates that the expression of caspases in yeast does not trigger endogenous yeast apoptotic pathways, as yeast lacks homologs of key mammalian apoptotic regulators, ensuring that the observed phenotypes are entirely attributable to the heterologous caspase activity. This specificity is critical for the reliability of the screening platform and distinguishes it from systems that rely on general cell death as an endpoint. The ability to observe and quantify these phenotypes in a controlled, reproducible manner forms the basis for the invention’s utility in high-throughput screening, functional cloning, and mechanistic studies of protease regulation.

## EXAMPLE 1

### Development and Testing of a Cleavable Reporter Gene System for Assaying Protease Activity in Living Yeast

The foundational innovation of this invention is the development of a cleavable reporter gene system that enables the quantitative, real-time detection of protease activity within living yeast cells. This system is built upon the design of a chimeric, membrane-tethered transcription factor that integrates the cytoplasmic domain of the human Fas receptor with the DNA-binding domain of LexA and the transcriptional activation domain of B42. The Fas-derived segment is truncated to remove the death domain, ensuring that the construct does not trigger endogenous yeast signaling pathways while retaining the transmembrane anchor necessary for membrane localization. Between the membrane anchor and the transcription factor, a flexible linker sequence is inserted that contains a tetrapeptide motif recognized by a specific caspase, flanked on both sides by glycine residues to optimize accessibility and cleavage efficiency. The design of this construct is based on the principle that proteolytic cleavage of the linker will release the LexA-B42 transcription factor from its membrane tether, allowing it to translocate to the nucleus and activate reporter genes under the control of LexA-binding operators. The system was implemented in the yeast strain EGY48, which contains a stably integrated LEU2 reporter gene driven by six LexA operators and a second episomal reporter, lacZ, under the control of two LexA operators. The LEU2 reporter permits growth on leucine-deficient media, while the lacZ reporter produces β-galactosidase, which can be detected colorimetrically using the substrate X-gal, resulting in blue colony formation. The system was validated by constructing a series of reporter plasmids, each containing a different tetrapeptide sequence corresponding to the known substrate preferences of human caspases: WEHD for caspase-1, DEVD for caspase-3, LETD for caspase-8, LEHD for caspase-9, TEVD for caspase-6, and DEHD for caspase-2. Control constructs were generated in which the critical aspartic acid residue at the P1 position was replaced with glycine, rendering the linker non-cleavable. These constructs were co-expressed in yeast with each of the ten human caspases, expressed from plasmids under promoters of varying strengths. The results demonstrated a highly specific pattern of activation: caspase-1 activated only the WEHD reporter, caspase-3 and caspase-7 activated only the DEVD reporter, caspase-8 and caspase-10 activated only the LETD reporter, and caspase-9 activated only the LEHD reporter. Caspase-2 activated both DEVD and DEHD reporters, consistent with its known substrate promiscuity. No activation was observed with the non-cleavable control constructs, and no cross-reactivity was detected between caspases and non-cognate reporters. The system was further validated by expressing catalytically inactive mutants of each caspase, in which the active-site cysteine was replaced with glycine or alanine; these mutants failed to activate any reporter, confirming that the observed signal was dependent on proteolytic activity. The use of a single cleavage site per reporter, rather than multiple sites, was found to provide the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity, as multiple sites did not enhance signal intensity but increased the risk of non-specific cleavage or structural instability. The system was also tested in the yeast strain EGY191, which contains two LexA operators upstream of LEU2, and yielded comparable results, demonstrating the robustness of the system across genetic backgrounds. The combination of growth-based and colorimetric readouts provides a dual-layer validation system, reducing the likelihood of false positives and enabling high-confidence identification of true activators. This reporter system represents a significant advancement over prior methods, which relied on cell death or single reporter outputs, by providing a quantitative, amplifiable, and visually discernible signal that is directly proportional to protease activity.

### A Genetic System for Monitoring Caspase 1 Activity in Yeast Cells.

The genetic system developed for monitoring caspase-1 activity in yeast cells represents a critical component of the broader invention, enabling the specific detection of this key inflammatory protease in a living cellular context. Caspase-1 is unique among the caspase family in its role as the central mediator of inflammasome-dependent cytokine maturation and pyroptosis. Its activation requires the assembly of a multi-protein complex involving sensor proteins such as NLRP1 or NLRP3 and the adapter protein Asc, making its biochemical reconstitution particularly challenging. The present system overcomes this challenge by expressing full-length human caspase-1 as a zymogen under the control of a weak promoter on a low-copy plasmid, ensuring that its expression level is insufficient to cause spontaneous activation or cytotoxicity. The caspase-1 construct is fused to a C-terminal FLAG epitope for detection by immunoblotting, allowing for confirmation of protein expression without interfering with function. The cleavable reporter system for caspase-1 contains the tetrapeptide sequence WEHD, which is the canonical substrate for this protease, inserted between the Fas transmembrane domain and the LexA-B42 transcription factor. This reporter is expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the control of a strong promoter to maximize signal output. Co-expression of caspase-1 with the WEHD reporter in yeast results in no detectable reporter activation in the absence of upstream activators, confirming that the zymogen remains inactive under basal conditions. However, when the adapter protein Asc is co-expressed at high levels using a strong promoter on a high-copy plasmid, robust activation of the LEU2 and lacZ reporters is observed, as evidenced by growth on leucine-deficient media and the development of blue coloration on X-gal plates. This activation is strictly dependent on the presence of both caspase-1 and Asc, as expression of either component alone fails to induce reporter activity. The system further demonstrates specificity by showing that other adapter proteins, such as RAIDD or Apaf-1, do not activate caspase-1, even when overexpressed. The use of a gain-of-function mutant of NLRP1, lacking the auto-inhibitory leucine-rich repeat domain, further enhances reporter activation in a manner that is dependent on Asc, thereby reconstituting the complete inflammasome pathway in yeast. The activation of the reporter is completely abolished when the active-site cysteine of caspase-1 is mutated to glycine, confirming that the signal is due to proteolytic cleavage and not non-specific effects. The system is further validated by the use of the broad-spectrum caspase inhibitor zVAD-fmk, which dose-dependently suppresses reporter activation, demonstrating that the system is pharmacologically responsive. The ability to monitor caspase-1 activity in real time, with a clear, quantifiable, and easily scalable readout, provides a powerful tool for screening compounds that modulate inflammasome assembly, identifying novel regulators of caspase-1 activation, and studying the effects of disease-associated mutations in NLRP1 or Asc. This system represents the first genetically tractable, high-throughput compatible platform for the functional analysis of caspase-1 activity in a eukaryotic cellular environment.

### Substrate Specifities of the Caspases Expressed in Yeast.

The substrate specificities of the caspases expressed in yeast were rigorously characterized using the cleavable reporter gene system, and the results demonstrated a remarkable congruence with previously established biochemical data obtained from mammalian systems. Each caspase exhibited a distinct and exclusive pattern of reporter activation, with no cross-reactivity observed between caspases and non-cognate substrates. Caspase-1, which is known to cleave interleukin-1β and interleukin-18 at the WEHD motif, activated only the WEHD-containing reporter in yeast, with no activation observed with any other substrate sequence. Similarly, caspase-4 and caspase-5, which share high sequence homology with caspase-1 and are also involved in inflammatory signaling, activated the WEHD reporter but not any other, confirming their functional similarity. Caspase-3 and caspase-7, the primary effector caspases responsible for executing apoptosis, both activated the DEVD reporter with equal efficiency, consistent with their overlapping substrate preferences in mammalian cells. Caspase-6, which cleaves lamin A and huntingtin at the TEVD site, activated only the TEVD reporter, with no activation of DEVD or other substrates, demonstrating its unique specificity. Caspase-8 and caspase-10, which initiate the extrinsic apoptotic pathway, activated only the LETD reporter, which is derived from the cleavage site in Bid and their own prodomains. Caspase-9, the initiator caspase of the intrinsic pathway, activated only the LEHD reporter, which corresponds to the cleavage site in caspase-3 and caspase-7. Caspase-2, which has been reported to cleave both DEVD and DEHD sequences, activated both the DEVD and DEHD reporters, with a slight preference for DEHD, mirroring its known substrate promiscuity. The system also confirmed that the aspartic acid residue at the P1 position is absolutely required for cleavage, as all constructs in which this residue was replaced with glycine (e.g., WEHG, DEVG, LETG) failed to activate any reporter, regardless of the caspase expressed. The use of a single cleavage site per reporter was found to be optimal, as multiple cleavage sites did not enhance signal intensity but introduced variability and potential for non-specific cleavage. The specificity of the system was further validated by the use of catalytically inactive mutants, which failed to activate any reporter, and by the use of pharmacological inhibitors, which dose-dependently suppressed reporter activation. The fidelity of the system was further confirmed by comparing the activation profiles of caspases expressed in yeast with those obtained from in vitro peptide cleavage assays, which showed near-perfect correlation. This high degree of specificity enables the simultaneous and independent monitoring of multiple caspase activities within the same yeast strain, a capability that is unmatched by any existing screening platform. The ability to distinguish between closely related caspases, such as caspase-3 and caspase-7, both of which cleave DEVD, is further enhanced by the use of differential promoter strengths and plasmid copy numbers, which allow for fine-tuning of expression levels to minimize background noise and maximize signal-to-noise ratios. This level of specificity is critical for the development of selective inhibitors and for the identification of compounds that target individual caspases within complex disease contexts where multiple caspases are simultaneously activated.

### Development of Two-component Systems Demonstrating Function of Caspase Activators in Yeast

The development of two-component systems in yeast represents a pivotal advancement in the ability to study the activation of initiator caspases in a controlled, physiologically relevant manner. These systems consist of the proform of an initiator caspase co-expressed with its cognate upstream activator, with each component expressed under carefully calibrated conditions to prevent spontaneous activation and ensure that reporter activation occurs only upon complex assembly. The invention demonstrates the successful reconstitution of five distinct two-component systems: pro-caspase-1 with Asc, pro-caspase-2 with RAIDD, pro-caspase-9 with a gain-of-function mutant of Apaf-1, pro-caspase-8 with FADD, and pro-caspase-10 with FADD. In each case, the caspase is expressed from a low-copy plasmid using a weak promoter (e.g., CYC1 or ΔGPD1), ensuring that its expression level is below the threshold required for auto-activation. The activator is expressed from a high-copy plasmid using a strong promoter (e.g., TEF or ADH), ensuring sufficient abundance to drive complex formation. The cleavable reporter system is designed to match the substrate specificity of the caspase being studied. The results show that reporter activation occurs only when both components are co-expressed, with no activation observed when either component is expressed alone. For example, pro-caspase-1 alone fails to activate the WEHD reporter, and Asc alone fails to activate the same reporter, but co-expression results in robust reporter activation. This strict dependence on both components confirms that the activation is not due to non-specific proteolysis or overexpression artifacts. The system further demonstrates specificity by showing that non-cognate activators fail to activate the caspase; for instance, Apaf-1 does not activate pro-caspase-1, and Asc does not activate pro-caspase-9. The use of catalytically inactive caspase mutants, in which the active-site cysteine is replaced with glycine or alanine, completely abolishes reporter activation, confirming that the signal is dependent on proteolytic activity. The system is further validated by the use of pharmacological inhibitors, such as zVAD-fmk, which dose-dependently suppress reporter activation. The ability to reconstitute these two-component systems in yeast provides a powerful tool for studying the molecular mechanisms of caspase activation, identifying novel regulators, and screening for compounds that disrupt or enhance complex assembly. The system’s modularity allows for the sequential addition or removal of components, enabling the dissection of pathway architecture and the identification of essential versus auxiliary proteins. This level of control and precision is unattainable in traditional biochemical assays, which rely on purified components and lack the cellular context necessary for proper complex formation. ### Development of a Plural-component System—Mammalian Protease Activating Pathways Reconstituted in Yeast

The development of plural-component systems in yeast represents a transformative leap in the ability to reconstitute complex mammalian protease activation pathways in a genetically tractable, scalable, and quantitatively measurable system. Building upon the success of two-component systems, the invention extends the platform to include three- and four-component networks that more accurately reflect the physiological architecture of caspase activation in mammalian cells. Two distinct plural-component systems were successfully reconstituted: the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) for caspase-8 and caspase-10, and the NLRP1 and NLRP3 inflammasomes for caspase-1. In this system, Fas is expressed at moderate levels from a constitutive promoter on a high-copy plasmid, FADD is expressed at low levels from a weak promoter on a low-copy plasmid, and caspase-8 or caspase-10 is expressed at low levels from a weak promoter on a low-copy plasmid. The cleavable reporter contains the LETD substrate sequence, specific for caspase-8 and -10. The system demonstrates that reporter activation occurs only when all three components are present, with no activation observed when any single component is omitted. ## Experimental Designs & Methods Used

### Vectors Encoding Caspase Cleavable Transcription Factors:

The vectors encoding caspase-cleavable transcription factors are central to the invention, providing a modular and highly specific platform for detecting protease activity in living yeast cells. Each vector is designed to express a chimeric protein consisting of three key domains: a membrane-tethering domain derived from the human Fas receptor, a protease-specific cleavage linker, and a transcriptional activation module composed of the LexA DNA-binding domain and the B42 activation domain. The Fas-derived segment is truncated to remove the cytoplasmic death domain, ensuring that the construct does not trigger endogenous yeast signaling pathways while retaining the transmembrane anchor necessary for membrane localization. The cleavage linker is inserted between the Fas transmembrane domain and the LexA-B42 transcription factor and contains a tetrapeptide sequence recognized by a specific caspase, flanked on both sides by glycine residues to optimize accessibility and cleavage efficiency. The linker sequences are designed to correspond to the known substrate preferences of human caspases: WEHD for caspase-1, DEVD for caspase-3, LETD for caspase-8, LEHD for caspase-9, TEVD for caspase-6, and DEHD for caspase-2. Control constructs are generated in which the critical aspartic acid residue at the P1 position is replaced with glycine, rendering the linker non-cleavable. These constructs are cloned into yeast expression plasmids under the control of strong constitutive promoters, such as TEF or ADH, to ensure high-level expression of the reporter protein. The plasmids are maintained at high copy number (2µ origin) to maximize signal output and are compatible with the yeast strain EGY48, which contains a stably integrated LEU2 reporter gene driven by six LexA operators and an episomal lacZ reporter under the control of two LexA operators. The use of a single cleavage site per reporter was empirically determined to provide the optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity, as multiple cleavage sites did not enhance signal intensity but introduced variability and potential for non-specific cleavage. The system is further validated by the use of catalytically inactive caspase mutants, which fail to activate any reporter, and by the use of pharmacological inhibitors, which dose-dependently suppress reporter activation. The vectors are designed to be modular, allowing for easy substitution of the cleavage linker sequence to accommodate new protease substrates, making the platform adaptable to other protease families beyond caspases. The ability to detect protease activity through a dual readout—growth on selective media and colorimetric detection of β-galactosidase—provides a high-confidence, low-background system that is ideal for high-throughput screening and functional cloning applications.

### Plasmids for Expressing Caspases and Their Upstream Activators:

The plasmids for expressing caspases and their upstream activators are engineered to provide precise control over the expression levels of each component, ensuring that protease activation occurs only upon the formation of the intended multi-protein complex. Each plasmid is derived from the pRS or pESC series of yeast expression vectors and is designed to express full-length, unmodified human caspases or their upstream activators, including their prodomains, which are essential for correct localization and interaction with binding partners. The caspases are expressed from promoters of varying strengths, ranging from weak (CYC1, ΔGPD1) to strong (TEF, ADH), and are maintained at either low copy number (CEN/ARS origin, 1–2 copies per cell) or high copy number (2µ origin, 20–50 copies per cell), depending on the desired expression level. For initiator caspases such as caspase-8 and caspase-10, which are highly cytotoxic when overexpressed, the zymogens are expressed from weak promoters on low-copy plasmids to prevent spontaneous activation and cell death. Their upstream activators, such as FADD, are expressed from strong promoters on high-copy plasmids to ensure sufficient abundance to drive complex formation. The activators are similarly expressed under controlled conditions, with adapter proteins such as Asc, RAIDD, and Apaf-1 expressed from strong promoters on high-copy plasmids to promote complex assembly. All constructs are fused to epitope tags, such as HA or FLAG, at either the N- or C-terminus to facilitate detection by immunoblotting without interfering with function. The plasmids are designed to be compatible with the yeast strain EGY48 and are selected using standard auxotrophic markers, including HIS3, URA3, TRP1, and LEU2, allowing for the co-expression of multiple plasmids within the same cell. The use of attenuated promoters, generated through deletion mutagenesis, allows for fine-tuning of expression levels to achieve optimal signal-to-noise ratios. The plasmids are constructed using standard molecular cloning techniques, including PCR amplification, restriction enzyme digestion, and ligation, and are verified by DNA sequencing. The ability to express each component at precisely calibrated levels ensures that reporter activation occurs only upon the formation of the intended complex, providing a high-fidelity system for studying protease activation in a living cellular context.

### Complex Plasmid Constructions.

The construction of complex plasmids is a critical innovation of the invention, enabling the co-expression of multiple genes within a single vector to mimic the stoichiometric and spatial organization of native protease activation complexes. These complex plasmids are designed to express two or three components simultaneously, with each gene under the control of a distinct promoter and separated by transcription termination elements to prevent read-through and ensure independent regulation. For example, a single plasmid may express pro-caspase-8 under the control of the CYC1 promoter and FADD under the control of the TEF promoter, with a transcriptional terminator between the two coding sequences. This design allows for the expression of the caspase at low levels and the activator at high levels within the same cell, eliminating the need for multiple transformations and ensuring consistent co-expression. The plasmids are constructed using a modular cloning strategy, with standardized restriction sites and linker sequences that allow for the seamless substitution of genes and promoters. The plasmids are maintained at either low-copy (CEN/ARS) or high-copy (2µ) origins, depending on the desired expression level, and are selected using auxotrophic markers such as HIS3, URA3, TRP1, or LEU2. The use of multiple markers allows for the co-expression of up to three genes on a single plasmid, enabling the reconstitution of complex pathways such as the DISC or the inflammasome. The plasmids are verified by DNA sequencing and functional testing to ensure that each component is expressed at the intended level and that the system functions as designed. The ability to construct and use complex plasmids represents a significant advancement over existing methods, which rely on the co-transformation of multiple plasmids, a process that is labor-intensive, prone to variability, and unsuitable for high-throughput screening. The invention thus provides a streamlined, reproducible, and scalable platform for studying multi-component protease activation pathways in yeast.

### Reporter Gene Assays.

The reporter gene assays developed for this invention provide a robust, quantitative, and easily scalable method for detecting protease activity in living yeast cells. The assays are based on the cleavage of a membrane-tethered transcription factor, which releases the LexA-B42 activator and enables the transcription of two reporter genes: LEU2 and lacZ. The LEU2 reporter permits growth on leucine-deficient media, providing a simple, growth-based readout that can be easily scored visually or quantified by colony counting. The lacZ reporter produces β-galactosidase, which cleaves the chromogenic substrate X-gal to produce a blue pigment, providing a colorimetric readout that can be quantified spectrophotometrically. The assays are performed in yeast strain EGY48, which contains a stably integrated LEU2 reporter gene driven by six LexA operators and an episomal lacZ reporter under the control of two LexA operators. The cleavable reporter is expressed from a high-copy plasmid under the control of a strong promoter to maximize signal output. The system is validated by the use of catalytically inactive caspase mutants, which fail to activate the reporters, and by the use of pharmacological inhibitors, which dose-dependently suppress reporter activation. The assays are performed on solid media for initial screening and validation, with yeast transformants streaked onto selective media containing X-gal and incubated at 30°C for four to six days. Growth on leucine-deficient media and blue coloration on X-gal plates are scored as positive for reporter activation. For high-throughput screening, the assays are adapted to liquid media in 384-well microtiter plates, with yeast cells suspended in selection media containing X-gal and incubated for two to three days at 30°C. Absorbance at 620 nm is measured using a microplate reader to quantify β-galactosidase activity. The use of a dual readout—growth and colorimetric detection—provides a high-confidence, low-background system that is ideal for high-throughput screening and functional cloning applications. The system is further validated by the use of control constructs, including non-cleavable linkers and catalytically inactive caspase mutants, which fail to activate the reporters, confirming that the signal is dependent on proteolytic activity.

### HTS Assays.

The high-throughput screening (HTS) assays developed for this invention represent a transformative advancement in the ability to screen large compound libraries for modulators of protease activity. The assays are adapted from the yeast-based reporter system to a 384-well microtiter plate format, enabling the rapid, automated screening of thousands of compounds in parallel. The yeast cells expressing the protease and cleavable reporter are grown in liquid selection media containing X-gal and seeded into 384-well plates at a standardized density of approximately 2×10⁵ cells per well. Compounds are added to the wells at a final concentration of 10 µM, with DMSO as a solvent control. The plates are sealed with breathable film to prevent evaporation and incubated at 30°C for 48 hours. Absorbance at 620 nm is measured using a microplate reader to quantify β-galactosidase activity. The system is validated by the use of positive and negative controls, including cells treated with the broad-spectrum caspase inhibitor zVAD-fmk and cells grown without X-gal. The Z’ factor, a statistical parameter used to assess the quality of HTS assays, is calculated for each system and consistently exceeds 0.6, indicating excellent assay robustness and suitability for high-throughput screening. The system is further validated by the use of pilot screens of the LOPAC library, which contains 1280 bioactive compounds, and demonstrates an average hit rate of approximately 0.5% per plate, consistent with the expected frequency of active compounds in a bioactive library. The ability to perform HTS in a living cellular context, with a quantitative, amplifiable, and pharmacologically responsive readout, represents a significant advancement over traditional biochemical assays, which are limited to purified components and lack the cellular context necessary for proper complex formation. The invention thus provides a uniquely powerful platform for discovering compounds that modulate protease activity through diverse mechanisms, including direct inhibition, upstream activation, and complex disruption.

### Yeast-based Counter-screen Assays.

The yeast-based counter-screen assays developed for this invention provide a powerful and systematic method for distinguishing between compounds that directly inhibit the protease and those that act upstream by disrupting complex assembly or modulating activator function. These assays are based on the use of engineered yeast strains that express the reporter system in the absence of the upstream activator or in the presence of a different protease. For example, a counter-screen strain is generated that expresses the LexA-B42 reporter system under the control of the GAL1 promoter, bypassing the need for protease cleavage entirely. This strain is used to identify compounds that directly inhibit β-galactosidase activity or alter yeast metabolism, which would produce false-positive hits in the primary screen. A second counter-screen strain expresses the caspase-1 and Asc components, allowing for the detection of compounds that inhibit caspase-1 activation but not caspase-8 activation, thereby identifying compounds with selectivity for the inflammasome pathway. A third counter-screen strain expresses caspase-3 and its activator, allowing for the identification of compounds that inhibit effector caspases. The use of these counter-screens enables the systematic deconvolution of compound mechanisms of action, allowing for the identification of true hits that modulate the intended target while eliminating false positives. The counter-screens are performed in parallel with the primary screen, using the same 384-well format and conditions, ensuring consistency and reproducibility. The ability to perform comprehensive counter-screening in a living cellular context, with a quantitative, amplifiable, and pharmacologically responsive readout, represents a significant advancement over traditional biochemical assays, which are limited in their ability to distinguish between direct and indirect modulators. The invention thus provides a uniquely powerful platform for discovering compounds that modulate protease activity through diverse mechanisms, including direct inhibition, upstream activation, and complex disruption.

### Cloning Systems for Caspase Activators.

The cloning systems developed for caspase activators are designed to enable the functional screening of cDNA libraries for novel regulators of protease activation. The system is based on the use of yeast expression vectors that contain a strong promoter (GAL1 or ADH) upstream of a multiple cloning site, allowing for the directional cloning of cDNA fragments derived from mammalian tissues. The vectors are designed to express the cDNA as a fusion protein with an HA tag for detection, and are maintained at high copy number (2µ origin) to ensure sufficient expression for activation. The cDNA libraries are constructed from mRNA isolated from human tissues such as HepG2, HeLa, liver, and placenta, using oligo(dT)-primed or random hexamer-primed reverse transcription, followed by ligation into the vector using restriction sites such as XhoI and NotI or XhoI and SfiI. The libraries are transformed into yeast strains that contain the cleavable reporter system and the pro-form of a caspase, but lack the upstream activator. For example, a strain expressing pro-caspase-8 and the LETD reporter, but lacking FADD, is used to screen for cDNA clones that restore reporter activation, thereby identifying novel activators of caspase-8. The transformants are plated on selective media containing X-gal, and blue colonies are picked and retested for reproducibility. The cDNA inserts are sequenced to identify the encoded protein, and the function of the candidate is validated by re-expression in the original strain and by testing in counter-screens. The system has been used to successfully identify known activators such as FADD, DR4, DR5, and c-FLIP, as well as novel regulators such as splice variants of caspase-8 and caspase-2. The ability to screen cDNA libraries in a living cellular context, with a quantitative, amplifiable, and pharmacologically responsive readout, represents a significant advancement over traditional biochemical assays, which are limited in their ability to identify novel regulators of complex pathways. The invention thus provides a uniquely powerful platform for discovering novel proteins that modulate protease activity through diverse mechanisms, including direct activation, complex assembly, and signal transduction.
