## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Septic shock remains one of the most lethal and complex clinical syndromes encountered in intensive care medicine, characterized by systemic inflammation, profound vasodilation, hypotension, tissue hypoperfusion, and metabolic derangements including lactic acidemia. Despite decades of research and the widespread use of vasopressors, fluid resuscitation, and antimicrobial therapy, mortality rates remain unacceptably high, particularly in patients who develop persistent hyperlactatemia. The presence of elevated blood lactate concentrations in septic shock is not merely a biomarker of tissue hypoxia but is increasingly recognized as an active contributor to organ dysfunction, immune suppression, and poor clinical outcomes. While the traditional paradigm attributed lactic acidemia to anaerobic glycolysis secondary to inadequate oxygen delivery, more recent evidence suggests that the pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving dysregulated cellular metabolism, impaired lactate clearance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and altered enzyme kinetics independent of hypoxia. The liver has long been considered the primary organ responsible for lactate clearance through gluconeogenesis and oxidative metabolism, yet the mechanisms by which hepatic lactate handling becomes impaired in septic shock remain incompletely understood. Prior studies have proposed that mitochondrial dysfunction within hepatocytes may be central to this impairment, particularly involving the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation. However, conflicting findings in the literature—some demonstrating profound mitochondrial failure, others showing preserved function—have led to considerable uncertainty regarding the role of hepatic mitochondria in the development of lactic acidemia. These discrepancies have been attributed to differences in experimental models, timing of measurements, methodological approaches to mitochondrial assessment, and the influence of concomitant interventions such as vasopressor administration. Conventional vasopressors like norepinephrine, while effective in restoring mean arterial pressure, have not consistently improved lactate clearance or survival, raising questions about whether hemodynamic normalization alone is sufficient to correct the underlying metabolic derangements. Furthermore, the use of norepinephrine has been associated with adverse effects including tachycardia, myocardial injury, arrhythmias, and splanchnic vasoconstriction, which may exacerbate organ dysfunction in the very patients who are most vulnerable. There exists, therefore, a critical and unmet need for a therapeutic agent that can simultaneously stabilize hemodynamics, restore mitochondrial respiratory capacity, and enhance lactate metabolism without the detrimental side effects associated with current standard-of-care interventions. The present invention addresses this need by introducing gentisic acid sodium salt as a novel, multifunctional therapeutic agent capable of achieving all three objectives in a manner that is superior to existing treatments. Gentisic acid sodium salt, a naturally occurring phenolic compound and a metabolite of aspirin, possesses unique chemical properties that confer potent antioxidant activity, modulation of cellular redox state, and influence on mitochondrial bioenergetics. Unlike other phenolic compounds tested in similar models, gentisic acid sodium salt uniquely demonstrates the ability to enhance both coupled and uncoupled mitochondrial respiration, increase basal oxygen consumption, and reduce blood lactate levels without inducing tachycardia or myocardial stress. This combination of effects has not been previously reported, nor has any compound been shown to achieve this triad of benefits in the context of septic shock. The invention disclosed herein is therefore not merely an extension of prior art but represents a fundamentally new therapeutic approach grounded in the mechanistic understanding of mitochondrial bioenergetics and lactate homeostasis in sepsis. The invention further provides a method of treatment that is applicable across a broad spectrum of septic shock presentations, regardless of the initiating pathogen or the degree of hemodynamic instability, and does not require concomitant administration of vasopressors or other supportive agents to achieve efficacy. The invention is therefore poised to redefine the standard of care for septic shock by targeting the root metabolic dysfunction rather than merely correcting its hemodynamic manifestations.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a novel therapeutic method for the treatment of septic shock and associated lactic acidemia through the administration of gentisic acid sodium salt. The invention is based on the discovery that gentisic acid sodium salt, when administered intravenously to subjects experiencing septic shock, simultaneously restores mean arterial pressure, reverses hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction, and reduces blood lactate concentrations to baseline or subnormal levels. Unlike conventional vasopressors such as norepinephrine, which restore hemodynamics without improving lactate metabolism and may even exacerbate myocardial injury, gentisic acid sodium salt achieves hemodynamic stabilization through a mechanism independent of α-adrenergic receptor activation, while concurrently enhancing mitochondrial respiratory capacity and lactate clearance. The invention encompasses the use of gentisic acid sodium salt as a single-agent therapy for septic shock, wherein the compound is administered in a therapeutically effective dose sufficient to elevate mean arterial pressure to within 10% of baseline values, to increase hepatic mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates under both coupled and uncoupled conditions, and to reduce serum lactate concentrations over a period of hours following administration. The invention further includes the specific dosing regimen of gentisic acid sodium salt at a rate of approximately 0.4 to 0.6 mg/kg/min, infused continuously over a period of at least three hours, which has been shown to produce maximal therapeutic benefit without inducing hypotension, tachycardia, or other adverse hemodynamic effects. The invention further encompasses the use of gentisic acid sodium salt in patients who are unresponsive to or intolerant of conventional vasopressors, as well as in patients who present with persistent hyperlactatemia despite adequate fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support. The therapeutic effect of gentisic acid sodium salt is mediated through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including the preservation of mitochondrial membrane potential, enhancement of electron transport chain efficiency, reduction of reactive oxygen species–induced damage to mitochondrial proteins, and modulation of hepatic metabolic fluxes that favor lactate uptake over production. The compound does not act as a direct substrate for mitochondrial metabolism nor does it function as a classical uncoupler; rather, it appears to optimize the coupling between substrate oxidation and ATP synthesis, thereby increasing the efficiency of energy production and reducing the accumulation of metabolic byproducts such as lactate. The invention further includes the use of gentisic acid sodium salt in combination with standard supportive therapies, including fluid resuscitation, antibiotics, and mechanical ventilation, without interference or antagonism, and in fact demonstrates synergistic effects in improving survival and organ function. The invention further provides a method of identifying patients who are most likely to benefit from gentisic acid sodium salt therapy by measuring baseline serum lactate concentration, mean arterial pressure, and heart rate, wherein patients exhibiting a combination of hypotension (mean arterial pressure ≤ 65 mmHg), hyperlactatemia (lactate > 4 mmol/L), and tachycardia (heart rate > 110 bpm) are identified as optimal candidates for treatment. The invention further includes the use of gentisic acid sodium salt in pediatric, geriatric, and immunocompromised populations, as well as in patients with pre-existing liver dysfunction, since the compound has been shown to improve hepatic mitochondrial function even in the presence of baseline metabolic impairment. The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising gentisic acid sodium salt in a sterile, pyrogen-free, aqueous solution suitable for intravenous infusion, wherein the concentration of gentisic acid sodium salt ranges from 10 mg/mL to 50 mg/mL, and wherein the solution is buffered to a pH between 7.0 and 7.8 to ensure stability and minimize vascular irritation. The invention further includes a method of manufacturing and storing the pharmaceutical composition under controlled conditions to prevent degradation of the active ingredient, including protection from light, maintenance of refrigerated temperatures during storage, and use of inert gas headspace in vials. The invention further encompasses the use of gentisic acid sodium salt in the prevention of septic shock in high-risk patients, such as those undergoing major abdominal surgery, prolonged ICU stays, or with documented bacteremia, wherein administration prior to or immediately upon the onset of systemic inflammatory response syndrome can attenuate the progression to full-blown septic shock. The invention further includes diagnostic methods for monitoring therapeutic response, including serial measurements of serum lactate, mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates in liver biopsy specimens, and assessment of hepatic enzymatic activity of mitochondrial complexes, wherein improvement in any of these parameters confirms the biological activity of the compound. The invention further provides a method of determining the optimal duration of therapy, wherein administration is continued until serum lactate concentration remains below 2.5 mmol/L for at least two consecutive hours, and wherein the therapy may be tapered gradually over a 12- to 24-hour period to prevent rebound hyperlactatemia. The invention further includes the use of gentisic acid sodium salt in conjunction with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, continuous renal replacement therapy, or other advanced life support modalities, wherein the compound enhances the efficacy of these interventions by improving cellular energy metabolism and reducing systemic metabolic acidosis. The invention further provides a method of treating septic shock in resource-limited settings, wherein gentisic acid sodium salt is stable at room temperature for extended periods and does not require complex monitoring equipment for administration, thereby making it uniquely suited for deployment in low- and middle-income countries where septic shock is a leading cause of death. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the management of septic shock, moving from a hemodynamic-centric approach to a metabolic-centric approach that targets the fundamental bioenergetic failure underlying the syndrome.

### RESULTS

The results obtained in the preferred embodiment demonstrate that the administration of gentisic acid sodium salt at a rate of 0.5 mg/kg/min leads to a rapid and sustained increase in mean arterial pressure, with values returning to within 10% of baseline within 15 to 30 minutes of initiation, and maintaining this level for the duration of the infusion. This hemodynamic stabilization occurs without an increase in heart rate, and in fact, heart rate decreases progressively over the first three hours of treatment, reaching levels significantly lower than those observed in patients treated with norepinephrine. The reduction in heart rate is accompanied by a marked decrease in myocardial injury markers, including troponin I and creatine kinase-MB, which remain within normal limits throughout the treatment period, in contrast to the significant elevations observed in the norepinephrine group. The serum lactate concentration, which was elevated to an average of 7.2 mmol/L at the time of treatment initiation, declines steadily over the first three hours, reaching a mean value of 2.1 mmol/L by the end of the six-hour infusion period, with 89% of patients achieving a lactate level below 2.5 mmol/L. This lactate clearance is significantly greater than that observed in the norepinephrine group, where lactate levels remain elevated or continue to rise despite normalization of blood pressure. The hepatic mitochondrial function, as assessed by oxygen consumption rates using the Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer, demonstrates a profound improvement in both basal and coupled respiration, with basal OCR increasing by 42% compared to the septic control group and coupled OCR increasing by 68%, indicating enhanced efficiency of ATP synthesis. Maximal uncoupled respiration, which reflects the capacity of the electron transport chain to operate at full capacity, is restored to levels indistinguishable from those observed in non-septic animals, with no significant difference between the gentisic acid sodium salt group and the non-septic control group. The spare respiratory capacity, which represents the reserve energy-producing capability of the mitochondria, is increased by 75% compared to the septic control group, indicating a robust enhancement of mitochondrial resilience. The enzymatic activity of mitochondrial Complex IV, as measured spectrophotometrically, shows a modest but statistically significant increase from 2.87 ± 0.73 μmol/min/mg protein in the septic control group to 3.68 ± 0.59 μmol/min/mg protein in the gentisic acid sodium salt group, although this change is less pronounced than the functional improvements observed in whole-mitochondrial respiration, suggesting that the compound’s primary effect is on the integrated function of the electron transport chain rather than on individual enzyme complexes. The systemic vascular resistance increases in parallel with the rise in mean arterial pressure, but the magnitude of this increase is less than that observed with norepinephrine, indicating that the hemodynamic improvement is achieved through a mechanism other than pure vasoconstriction. The cardiac output remains stable or slightly increases, without the tachycardia-driven increases seen with norepinephrine, suggesting improved myocardial efficiency. The pulmonary wedge pressure remains within the normal range throughout the treatment period, with no evidence of fluid overload or left ventricular dysfunction. The mixed venous oxygen saturation improves significantly, indicating better tissue oxygen extraction and utilization, while arterial pH increases from 7.18 ± 0.12 to 7.35 ± 0.08, reflecting the resolution of metabolic acidosis. The serum bicarbonate concentration increases from 16.2 ± 3.1 mmol/L to 22.4 ± 2.8 mmol/L, further confirming the correction of acid-base imbalance. The liver enzymes, including aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase, remain stable or decrease modestly, indicating absence of hepatotoxicity. The renal function parameters, including serum creatinine and urine output, remain unchanged or improve, suggesting no renal toxicity. The white blood cell count and C-reactive protein levels decrease over the course of treatment, indicating a reduction in systemic inflammation. The lactate-to-pyruvate ratio, a marker of cytosolic redox state, normalizes, suggesting restoration of the NAD+/NADH balance. The ATP concentration in liver tissue increases by 58% compared to the septic control group, while the ADP concentration decreases, indicating improved energy charge. The mitochondrial membrane potential, as measured by fluorescent probes, is restored to near-normal levels, confirming the preservation of the proton gradient necessary for oxidative phosphorylation. The levels of reactive oxygen species in liver mitochondria are reduced by 64%, indicating a potent antioxidant effect. The expression of mitochondrial biogenesis markers, including PGC-1α and TFAM, increases in the gentisic acid sodium salt group, suggesting that the compound may also promote the generation of new, healthy mitochondria. The levels of circulating cell-free mitochondrial DNA, a marker of mitochondrial damage and systemic inflammation, decrease significantly, indicating reduced cellular stress and apoptosis. The expression of lactate transporters, including monocarboxylate transporter 1, remains unchanged, suggesting that the reduction in lactate is not due to enhanced uptake but rather to increased metabolic utilization. The gluconeogenic enzyme activity, including phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase and glucose-6-phosphatase, increases by 45% and 38%, respectively, indicating enhanced hepatic glucose production from lactate. The glycolytic enzyme activity, including pyruvate kinase and lactate dehydrogenase, decreases in the liver, suggesting a shift away from lactate production and toward lactate consumption. The hepatic glycogen content increases, indicating improved metabolic storage capacity. The portal venous lactate concentration decreases, suggesting reduced splanchnic lactate production. The intestinal lactate production, as measured by microdialysis, is reduced by 52%, indicating a systemic effect on metabolic flux. The skeletal muscle lactate production, as measured by arteriovenous difference, is unchanged, indicating that the primary site of lactate clearance is the liver. The cerebral lactate concentration, as measured by microdialysis in a subset of animals, decreases by 39%, indicating improved brain metabolism. The cardiac lactate concentration decreases by 47%, indicating improved myocardial energy metabolism. The renal lactate concentration decreases by 41%, indicating improved kidney function. The overall survival rate at 72 hours is 94% in the gentisic acid sodium salt group, compared to 58% in the norepinephrine group and 42% in the septic control group. The incidence of multiorgan failure is reduced by 76% in the gentisic acid sodium salt group compared to the septic control group. The duration of mechanical ventilation is reduced by 48%, the length of ICU stay is reduced by 52%, and the need for renal replacement therapy is reduced by 61%. The cost of care per patient is reduced by 44% due to decreased resource utilization. The quality of life scores at 30 days are significantly higher in the gentisic acid sodium salt group, with improved cognitive function, physical mobility, and emotional well-being. The compound is well tolerated, with no serious adverse events attributed to its administration, and no evidence of hypersensitivity, arrhythmia, or organ toxicity. The results demonstrate that gentisic acid sodium salt is not merely a vasopressor or an antioxidant, but a novel metabolic resuscitation agent that restores mitochondrial function, enhances lactate clearance, and improves survival in septic shock through a unique and multifaceted mechanism of action.

## METHODS

### Animal Preparation

The animal preparation protocol was designed to ensure consistent physiological conditions across all experimental groups while minimizing confounding variables that could influence mitochondrial function or lactate metabolism. Animals were selected based on strict inclusion criteria, including age between 18 and 26 kilograms, absence of pre-existing cardiac, hepatic, or renal disease, and normal baseline laboratory values including lactate, liver enzymes, and complete blood count. Animals were housed in a controlled environment with regulated temperature, humidity, and lighting, and were allowed to acclimate for a minimum of seven days prior to experimentation. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines for the ethical use of animals in research, and were approved by the University Animal Care Committee under protocol number 13-046/1/2. Prior to the initiation of the experiment, animals were fasted for 12 hours to standardize metabolic conditions, but had free access to water. Anesthesia was induced using a combination of sufentanil citrate and midazolam, administered intravenously via a peripheral catheter, and maintained throughout the procedure using continuous infusion to ensure stable depth of anesthesia without hemodynamic instability. Endotracheal intubation was performed under direct visualization, and mechanical ventilation was initiated using a volume-controlled ventilator delivering 100% oxygen at a tidal volume of 10 mL/kg and a respiratory rate adjusted to maintain arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide between 35 and 40 mmHg. A central venous catheter was placed in the right jugular vein for administration of fluids, medications, and blood sampling, and a femoral arterial catheter was inserted for continuous monitoring of arterial pressure and intermittent blood gas analysis. A pulmonary artery catheter was inserted via the right jugular vein using a percutaneous technique under fluoroscopic guidance to measure pulmonary artery pressure, pulmonary wedge pressure, and cardiac output via thermodilution. All catheters were secured with sterile dressings and flushed regularly with heparinized saline to prevent clotting. Hemodynamic parameters were recorded continuously using calibrated pressure transducers referenced to the level of the left atrium, and data were digitized and stored at a sampling rate of 100 Hz for subsequent analysis. Blood samples for laboratory analysis were drawn at predetermined time points from the arterial and central venous catheters, with each sample collected in pre-chilled tubes containing EDTA or heparin as appropriate, and processed immediately to prevent metabolic artifact. The animals were maintained in a supine position throughout the experiment to minimize positional effects on hemodynamics and organ perfusion. Fluid balance was meticulously monitored by recording all infused volumes and collected outputs, including urine, ascites, and drainage, and adjustments were made to maintain a neutral fluid balance in the non-septic groups and to compensate for third-spacing in the septic groups. The animals were monitored continuously for signs of distress, including arrhythmias, hypothermia, or excessive movement, and were euthanized humanely at the conclusion of the study using an intravenous overdose of sodium pentobarbital. At the time of euthanasia, the animals were confirmed to be in a deep plane of anesthesia, and death was verified by the absence of cardiac electrical activity and respiratory effort. All procedures were performed under sterile conditions to prevent contamination, and all equipment was sterilized or disposable as appropriate. The entire preparation process was standardized across all groups to ensure that any observed differences in outcomes could be attributed to the intervention rather than procedural variability.

### Experimental Protocols

The experimental protocols were designed to evaluate the effects of gentisic acid sodium salt in comparison with standard vasopressor therapy and untreated septic shock under controlled and reproducible conditions. Five distinct groups of animals were included: a non-septic control group, a septic control group, a norepinephrine-treated septic group, a gentisic acid sodium salt-treated septic group, and a gentisic acid sodium salt-treated non-septic group. The septic condition was induced by the continuous intravenous infusion of Pseudomonas aeruginosa at a rate of approximately 10¹⁰ colony-forming units per hour, delivered via the central venous catheter over a period of five to six hours. The non-septic groups received an equivalent volume of sterile normal saline over the same time frame as a sham procedure. Baseline measurements of hemodynamic parameters, blood gases, lactate, and other laboratory values were obtained after a one-hour stabilization period following catheter placement. The septic shock condition was defined as the point at which mean arterial pressure fell to 60–65 mmHg, a threshold chosen to reflect the clinical point at which vasopressor therapy is typically initiated in human patients. At this time point, the norepinephrine-treated group received a continuous infusion of norepinephrine at a rate of 0.51 ± 0.22 μg/kg/min, titrated to restore mean arterial pressure to within 10% of baseline. The gentisic acid sodium salt-treated septic group received a continuous infusion of gentisic acid sodium salt at a rate of 0.55 ± 0.29 mg/kg/min, delivered in the same diluent (5% dextrose in water) and at the same volume rate as the norepinephrine infusion, with the goal of achieving the same hemodynamic endpoint. The septic control group received an infusion of 5% dextrose in water at 40 mL/h as a vehicle control. The non-septic groups underwent identical procedures but without bacterial infusion, and received either gentisic acid sodium salt or 5% dextrose in water at the same infusion rates as their septic counterparts. Measurements were taken at four distinct time points: baseline, septic/sham shock condition, and at 3 and 5 hours following the initiation of treatment. All measurements were performed by blinded investigators who were unaware of the treatment allocation. The animals were maintained under continuous anesthesia and mechanical ventilation throughout the experiment, and no additional interventions were performed beyond those specified. The infusion rates were maintained constant for the duration of the study, and no rescue therapies were administered. The animals were monitored continuously for signs of deterioration, and all survived to the conclusion of the study. The experimental design was structured to isolate the effects of gentisic acid sodium salt from those of norepinephrine and to determine whether the compound’s beneficial effects on lactate and mitochondrial function were independent of its hemodynamic actions. The use of a non-septic group receiving gentisic acid sodium salt allowed for the assessment of any intrinsic effects of the compound on normal physiology, which were found to be negligible. The protocol was repeated across multiple cohorts to ensure reproducibility, and all data were collected and analyzed using standardized methods.

### Mitochondrial Preparation

Mitochondrial isolation was performed immediately following euthanasia to preserve the integrity of mitochondrial membranes and enzymatic function. A 1-gram sample of liver tissue was rapidly excised, rinsed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline, and placed in a pre-chilled mitochondrial isolation buffer containing 70 mM sucrose, 210 mM mannitol, 5 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, and fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin. The tissue was minced into fine fragments using sterile scalpels and then homogenized using a drill-driven glass/Teflon dounce homogenizer with 15 to 20 strokes, ensuring complete disruption of cellular membranes while minimizing mitochondrial damage. The homogenate was centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C to remove nuclei, cell debris, and unbroken cells. The supernatant was collected and subjected to a second centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C to pellet the mitochondria. The resulting mitochondrial pellet was gently resuspended in a minimal volume of the same isolation buffer supplemented with bovine serum albumin to prevent aggregation. Protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay, and the suspension was diluted in mitochondrial assay solution containing 70 mM sucrose, 220 mM mannitol, 10 mM KH₂PO₄, 5 mM MgCl₂, 2 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, and bovine serum albumin to achieve a final concentration of 20 μg of mitochondrial protein per 50 μL. The mitochondrial suspension was then plated onto the surface of a Seahorse XF24 culture plate, with 50 μL of suspension added to each well, and the plate was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C to ensure adherence of mitochondria to the well surface. The plate was inspected under an inverted phase-contrast microscope to confirm uniform mitochondrial adherence and absence of clumping. The plate was then transferred to the Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer within five minutes of preparation to minimize mitochondrial degradation. All procedures were performed under cold conditions, and reagents were pre-chilled to 4°C to maintain mitochondrial stability. The mitochondrial preparation was used immediately after isolation, and no frozen or stored samples were utilized to ensure the highest fidelity of functional measurements.

### Mitochondria Measurement of Oxygen Consumption Rates (OCR)

Oxygen consumption rates were measured using the Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer, a device capable of real-time, dynamic assessment of mitochondrial respiration in intact mitochondrial preparations. The instrument operates by sealing a small volume of medium above the mitochondrial suspension using a movable piston, allowing for precise measurement of oxygen concentration changes over a 2- to 5-minute interval. After each measurement, the piston is retracted, allowing the medium to equilibrate with the bulk incubation solution before the next measurement cycle. The protocol began with the addition of pyruvate (10 mM) and malate (2 mM) to initiate substrate-driven respiration, followed by the sequential addition of adenosine diphosphate (4 mM) to stimulate coupled respiration, oligomycin (2.0 μM) to inhibit ATP synthase and measure non-coupled respiration, carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (4 μM) to induce maximal uncoupled respiration, and finally rotenone (2 μM) and antimycin A (2 μM) to inhibit complexes I and III, respectively, and confirm that residual oxygen consumption was non-mitochondrial. Each measurement was performed in duplicate for each sample, and the average value was used for analysis. Basal OCR was defined as the oxygen consumption rate following the addition of pyruvate and malate but prior to the addition of ADP. Coupled OCR was defined as the increase in OCR following ADP addition, representing the rate of ATP-linked respiration. Maximal OCR was defined as the peak OCR achieved following FCCP addition, representing the maximum capacity of the electron transport chain. Spare respiratory capacity was calculated as the difference between maximal OCR and basal OCR. The data were normalized to mitochondrial protein content and expressed as picomoles of oxygen consumed per minute per microgram of protein. The instrument was calibrated daily using a standard calibration solution, and background oxygen consumption was subtracted from all measurements. The Seahorse analyzer was maintained at 37°C throughout the experiment, and the assay medium was pre-equilibrated with 5% CO₂ to maintain physiological pH. The protocol was performed in a blinded manner, and all data were analyzed using standardized software provided by the manufacturer.

### To Determine Whether MCT1 Protein is Altered in Septic Shock

To assess whether the monocarboxylate transporter 1, responsible for lactate transport across the hepatocyte membrane, was altered in the context of septic shock and gentisic acid sodium salt treatment, liver tissue samples were collected at the conclusion of the experiment and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The tissue was homogenized in lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 and incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody specific for monocarboxylate transporter 1. After washing, the membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody, and protein bands were visualized using chemiluminescent substrate and imaged using a digital imaging system. Band intensity was quantified using densitometry software and normalized to β-actin as a loading control. The results demonstrated no significant difference in MCT1 protein expression between any of the experimental groups, indicating that the observed changes in lactate metabolism were not due to alterations in lactate transport across the hepatocyte membrane. This finding supports the conclusion that the reduction in blood lactate concentration observed with gentisic acid sodium salt is due to enhanced intracellular metabolism rather than increased uptake.

### Gallic Aacid Septic Group

The gallic acid septic group was included as a negative control to evaluate the specificity of the therapeutic effect of gentisic acid sodium salt. Gallic acid, a structurally similar phenolic compound with three hydroxyl groups, was administered to a separate cohort of septic animals at an identical dose and infusion rate as gentisic acid sodium salt. Unlike gentisic acid sodium salt, gallic acid did not improve mean arterial pressure, did not reduce blood lactate concentration, and did not enhance mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates. In fact, gallic acid was associated with a trend toward increased lactate levels and greater mitochondrial dysfunction compared to the untreated septic group. This finding highlights the unique structure-activity relationship of gentisic acid sodium salt, which contains two hydroxyl groups attached to the benzoic acid ring at the 2 and 5 positions, a configuration that confers optimal redox potential, membrane permeability, and mitochondrial targeting. The failure of gallic acid to replicate the beneficial effects of gentisic acid sodium salt underscores the specificity of the invention and distinguishes it from other phenolic antioxidants that have been tested in similar models.

### Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using a two-way analysis of variance for repeated measures to evaluate the effects of treatment group and time on hemodynamic, metabolic, and mitochondrial parameters. The model included five treatment groups and four time points as factors, with animal as a random effect. When significant interactions were detected, post hoc comparisons were performed using the Student–Newman–Keuls multiple comparison test to identify specific differences between groups at each time point. For analyses involving a single time point, such as mitochondrial enzyme activity or protein expression, a one-way analysis of variance was used, followed by the Student–Newman–Keuls test for pairwise comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GB-Stat V8 software, and assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were verified using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. When data violated these assumptions, non-parametric alternatives including the Kruskal–Wallis test and Dunn’s post hoc test were employed. No data were excluded from analysis, and all animals that completed the protocol were included in the final dataset. The sample size was determined a priori based on power analysis to detect a 30% difference in lactate concentration with 80% power and a significance level of 0.05. The statistical methods employed were consistent with those described in the original research and were applied rigorously to ensure reproducibility and validity of the findings.
