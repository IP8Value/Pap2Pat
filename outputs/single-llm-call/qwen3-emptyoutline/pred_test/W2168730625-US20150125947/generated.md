# DESCRIPTION

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The invention disclosed herein relates to a microfluidic device capable of performing precise liquid handling operations on discrete microdroplets without requiring active control over flow rates, timing of flow initiation or termination, or synchronization between multiple fluid inputs. The device operates through a series of geometrically defined modules, each engineered to exploit the dominant influence of interfacial tension forces over viscous and inertial forces under conditions of low capillary number. These modules are designed to perform elementary functions such as metering, merging, locking, delaying, and releasing droplets in a deterministic and reproducible manner, independent of the operational parameters that typically introduce variability in conventional microfluidic systems. Each module is constructed from a single continuous channel with integrated bypass structures that allow the continuous phase to flow around a trapped droplet while maintaining a pressure differential that governs the droplet’s motion. The geometry of the channel, including the relative dimensions of the main lumen, the depth and width of the bypasses, and the placement of barriers or intrusions at strategic termini, determines the behavior of the droplet under flow. For instance, when a droplet enters a metering trap whose length exceeds its own, the droplet is halted by a barrier positioned at the downstream end of the trap. This barrier extends upward to a height that matches the floor of the bypass channels, creating a localized constriction that increases the Laplace pressure required for the droplet to pass. If the droplet is shorter than the trap, the continuous phase flows unimpeded through the bypasses, and the droplet remains immobilized due to the pressure imbalance. However, if the droplet is longer than the trap, its trailing end eventually occludes the bypass entrances, preventing the continuous phase from flowing around it. This occlusion causes a buildup of hydrodynamic pressure along the length of the droplet, which overcomes the Laplace barrier at the downstream constriction, propelling the droplet forward until its rear end aligns with the entrance to the bypasses. At this precise moment, the front of the droplet encounters the barrier, and the thin liquid filament connecting the droplet to the incoming stream ruptures due to Rayleigh-Plateau instability, resulting in the release of a precisely metered volume of liquid, equal to the length of the trap, into the confined space of the module. This mechanism ensures that the volume of the metered droplet is determined solely by the physical dimensions of the trap and not by the rate at which the droplet is introduced or the pressure applied to the system. The reproducibility of this process is further enhanced by the fact that the droplet’s interface conforms to the geometry of the channel walls, minimizing variations in curvature and interfacial energy. The metering trap thus functions as a passive volumetric regulator, capable of generating consistent aliquots even when the input flow conditions are uncontrolled or fluctuate significantly. This property renders the device particularly suitable for applications where external pumps or valves are impractical, such as point-of-care diagnostics, field-based testing, or resource-limited environments.

### Example 2

The merging trap is a geometrically symmetric module designed to facilitate the controlled coalescence of two microdroplets within a confined channel while ensuring their subsequent release as a single, homogenized unit. The structure of the merging trap consists of a main channel with parallel bypass channels extending along its length, similar to the metering trap, but instead of a single downstream barrier, the trap is terminated at both ends by a series of side intrusions—protrusions that extend inward from the channel walls toward the centerline, creating a narrowing that partially obstructs the passage of the droplet. These intrusions are engineered to be shallower than the full height of the channel, allowing the continuous phase to flow through the gaps between them, thereby reducing the pressure drop across the droplet during transit. When a droplet longer than the length of the trap enters the module, it remains in motion because its front and rear ends simultaneously occlude the bypass inlets, preventing the continuous phase from bypassing it. As the droplet advances, the intrusions at the downstream end exert a mild resistance, but because the neck formed between the droplet and the intrusion is less deformed than that formed by a sharp barrier, the Rayleigh-Plateau instability is suppressed, and the droplet does not rupture. Instead, it is propelled forward with minimal disruption. When a second, shorter droplet enters the same trap from the opposite direction, it becomes trapped at the upstream intrusion because the continuous phase can flow around it via the bypasses. As the first droplet continues to move forward under pressure, it encounters the second droplet, and the intervening film of continuous phase is expelled through the bypass channels, allowing the two droplets to merge into a single, larger entity. If the merged droplet is shorter than the trap, it remains immobilized until a subsequent droplet enters and pushes it forward. If the merged droplet exceeds the length of the trap, its rear end blocks the bypass inlets, triggering a pressure buildup that forces it through the downstream intrusions and out of the trap. The merging process is inherently efficient due to the convective mixing that occurs within the merged droplet as it moves through the channel. Unlike traditional mixing methods that rely on diffusion or time-dependent homogenization, the mixing in this system is governed by the distance traveled by the droplet, not the duration of contact. This means that even at variable flow rates, the degree of homogenization remains consistent, as the droplet’s internal flow patterns are dictated by its geometry and the confinement of the channel walls. The symmetry of the merging trap allows it to function identically regardless of the direction of flow, enabling bidirectional operation and increasing the versatility of the system. Furthermore, the absence of sharp barriers reduces the risk of droplet fragmentation or unintended rupture, ensuring that the merged volume remains intact and predictable. This feature is critical for applications requiring sequential dilutions or reagent mixing, where the integrity of the combined volume must be preserved to maintain the accuracy of concentration gradients. The merging trap thus serves as a passive, geometry-driven mixer that transforms the spatial arrangement of droplets into a temporal sequence of chemical reactions, effectively encoding the logic of the process into the physical structure of the device.

### Example 3

The lock&shift trap is a specialized module designed to immobilize a single microdroplet at a defined location within the microfluidic network while permitting the continuous phase to flow freely around it, and to release the droplet only upon the arrival of a subsequent droplet. Unlike conventional trapping mechanisms that rely on external actuation or dynamic pressure modulation, the lock&shift trap achieves passive immobilization through a unique geometric configuration in which the bypass channels are connected to the main channel only at the two termini of the trap, leaving the intermediate section of the bypasses completely isolated from the main lumen. This design ensures that the continuous phase can only enter or exit the bypasses at the ends of the trap, thereby preventing any lateral flow along the length of the module. When a droplet shorter than the trap enters the module, it is pushed by the continuous phase toward the downstream end, where a barrier—either a full-height obstruction or a set of side intrusions—creates a localized pressure increase that halts the droplet’s motion. The continuous phase, unable to flow through the isolated middle section of the bypasses, is forced to circumvent the droplet via the terminal bypass connections, thereby reducing the pressure gradient along the droplet’s length and stabilizing its position. The droplet remains locked in place until a second droplet, introduced from the same direction, approaches the first. As the second droplet enters the trap, it displaces the continuous phase ahead of it, which in turn exerts a force on the trapped droplet. Because the bypasses are only accessible at the ends, the pressure generated by the incoming droplet cannot dissipate laterally and is instead transmitted directly to the front of the locked droplet. Once the force exceeds the Laplace pressure holding the first droplet in place, it is pushed forward, and the second droplet assumes its position, effectively shifting the trapped droplet one module downstream. This mechanism enables the sequential, non-contact displacement of droplets, ensuring that no two droplets ever come into direct contact with each other, thereby eliminating the risk of cross-contamination. The lock&shift trap thus functions as a passive, deterministic shift register, capable of arraying droplets in a precise spatial sequence without the need for external valves, sensors, or timing controls. The system’s reliability is further enhanced by the fact that the displacement occurs only when a new droplet is present, making the process inherently self-synchronizing. This feature is especially valuable in applications requiring the sequential delivery of reagents to immobilized samples, such as in multi-step immunoassays or enzymatic reactions, where the timing of each step must be tightly controlled but external actuation is undesirable. The trap can be configured with either a barrier or intrusions at its downstream end, allowing for flexibility in the release mechanism and compatibility with other modules in the system. When used in conjunction with metering and merging traps, the lock&shift trap enables the creation of complex, multi-stage reaction arrays where each droplet occupies a fixed position, its contents are preserved, and its release is triggered solely by the arrival of the next droplet in sequence. The result is a system that combines the precision of digital microfluidics with the simplicity of passive operation, making it ideal for integration into portable diagnostic platforms.

### Example 4

The delay trap is a geometrically simplified variant of the metering trap, designed to reduce the velocity of a microdroplet as it traverses the module without immobilizing it or altering its volume. Unlike the metering trap, which incorporates barriers to halt droplet motion, the delay trap consists of a main channel with parallel bypass channels extending along its entire length, with no obstructions at either terminus. The bypasses are fabricated to the same depth as those in the metering and merging traps, ensuring that the continuous phase can flow around the droplet with minimal resistance. However, because the droplet occupies a significant portion of the channel cross-section, the flow of the continuous phase is constrained to the narrow regions between the droplet and the channel walls, as well as within the bypass channels. This redistribution of flow reduces the pressure gradient required to propel the droplet forward, thereby decreasing its velocity relative to the bulk flow of the continuous phase. The extent of the delay is determined by the ratio of the bypass cross-sectional area to the main channel cross-section, as well as the length of the trap. A longer delay trap or a larger bypass area results in a greater reduction in droplet speed, with experimental measurements demonstrating a reduction of up to two-fold in droplet velocity under constant flow conditions. Importantly, the delay is not dependent on the droplet’s size or composition, nor is it sensitive to fluctuations in the flow rate of the continuous phase. The droplet always experiences the same hydrodynamic resistance, regardless of whether the flow is steady or variable, because the pressure drop across the droplet is governed by the fixed geometry of the channel and the interfacial tension of the liquid interface. This makes the delay trap an ideal component for introducing temporal delays into a microfluidic sequence without requiring active control over flow timing. For example, in a serial dilution system, the delay trap can be placed between metering traps to increase the time interval between successive dilution steps, allowing sufficient time for homogenization within the trapped droplet before the next dilution is introduced. The delay trap can also be used to synchronize the arrival of multiple droplets at a merging point, ensuring that two droplets meet at the same location even if they were introduced at different times or flow rates. Because the delay is purely a function of geometry, the system remains robust against variations in viscosity, surface tension, or temperature, which might otherwise affect the performance of active timing mechanisms. Furthermore, the absence of barriers or obstructions eliminates the risk of droplet rupture or unintended trapping, making the delay trap a reliable and durable component for long-term operation. Its simplicity also facilitates integration into complex networks, where multiple delay traps can be arranged in series to achieve cumulative delays of arbitrary duration. The delay trap thus serves as a passive temporal regulator, transforming spatial design into temporal control and enabling the execution of multi-step protocols without the need for external clocks, timers, or synchronized pumps.

### Example 5

The integrated serial dilutor I is a complex microfluidic architecture that combines the metering, delay, and lock&shift traps into a single, self-contained system capable of generating a precise sequence of serial dilutions without any requirement for active control over flow parameters. The device consists of three primary functional regions: a buffer metering array, a sample metering trap, and a lock&shift array. The buffer metering array comprises a series of identical metering traps, each separated by a delay trap, arranged in a linear configuration. The sample metering trap is positioned downstream of the buffer array and is structurally identical to the individual metering traps but is connected to a dedicated inlet for the sample solution. The lock&shift array, located at the far end of the system, consists of ten identical lock&shift traps, each designed to capture and hold a single droplet in a fixed position. The operation of the system proceeds in three distinct phases. In the first phase, a continuous flow of carrier liquid is introduced through a buffer inlet, while a separate stream of solvent is introduced through a dedicated solvent inlet. The carrier liquid pushes the solvent through the buffer metering array, where each metering trap captures a precisely defined volume of solvent, leaving behind a sequence of equally sized, immobilized droplets. The delay traps between each metering trap ensure that the solvent droplets are spaced apart in time, preventing premature interaction with the sample. In the second phase, the flow direction is reversed, and the carrier liquid is directed through a sample inlet, while the solvent inlet is closed. The carrier liquid now carries a droplet of the analyte solution into the sample metering trap, where a volume equal to the length of the trap is captured and immobilized. At this stage, the system contains a precisely metered droplet of sample and an array of metered solvent droplets, all held in place by the geometry of the traps. In the third phase, the carrier liquid is reintroduced through the original buffer inlet, driving the solvent droplets sequentially into the sample metering trap. As each solvent droplet enters the trap, it merges with the sample droplet, displacing an equal volume of the mixture out of the trap and into the downstream lock&shift array. The displaced droplet, now containing a diluted concentration of the original sample, is captured by the first lock&shift trap. The process repeats with each subsequent solvent droplet, producing a geometric progression of concentrations as the dilution factor is applied cumulatively. The homogenization of each diluted mixture occurs rapidly due to convective mixing within the droplet as it moves through the channel, and the final array of droplets is held in fixed positions, ready for downstream analysis. The entire sequence is executed without the need for precise control over flow rates, timing, or synchronization, as the geometry of the traps dictates the volume of each aliquot and the sequence of operations. The system’s robustness is demonstrated by its ability to function reliably even when the flow rate varies by up to threefold, with normalized standard deviations in droplet volume remaining below five percent. This level of precision, achieved without active control, distinguishes the system from conventional microfluidic approaches, such as T-junction droplet generators, where even minor fluctuations in flow lead to significant variations in droplet size. The integrated serial dilutor I thus represents a paradigm shift in microfluidic design, demonstrating that complex, multi-step liquid handling protocols can be encoded directly into the physical structure of the device, rendering them immune to operator error and environmental variability.

### Example 6

The integrated serial dilutor II, referred to herein as the DOMINO system, represents a further evolution of the passive microfluidic concept, wherein the entire process of serial dilution is achieved through the sequential reversal of flow direction, without the need for any external timing, synchronization, or flow rate control. The DOMINO system is composed of a repeating unit comprising a metering trap followed by a merging trap, arranged in a linear cascade. Each metering trap is designed to capture a fixed volume of liquid, while each merging trap is configured to receive a droplet from the upstream metering trap and merge it with a pre-placed droplet of solvent. The system is operated in three distinct steps. In the first step, a continuous flow of carrier liquid is introduced through a solvent inlet, while all other inlets and outlets are closed. The carrier liquid pushes a large volume of solvent through the entire cascade of metering traps, each of which captures a precisely metered volume of solvent, leaving behind a chain of immobilized solvent droplets. The volume of each solvent droplet is determined solely by the length of the metering trap, ensuring uniformity across the entire array. In the second step, the flow direction is reversed, and the carrier liquid is now introduced through a sample inlet, while the solvent inlet is closed. The carrier liquid carries a droplet of the analyte solution into the first metering trap, where a volume equal to twice the size of the solvent droplets is captured. The remaining volume of the sample droplet is forced into the first merging trap, where it encounters the pre-placed solvent droplet. The merging trap facilitates the coalescence of the two droplets, resulting in a single, homogenized droplet with a concentration equal to half that of the original sample. This merged droplet is then propelled forward by the continued flow of the carrier liquid into the next metering trap, where it is again split into two equal volumes: one remains trapped, and the other is passed into the next merging trap. The process repeats at each subsequent stage, with each merged droplet being halved in concentration as it progresses through the cascade. After passing through ten such stages, the system produces a sequence of ten droplets, each containing a concentration that is a power-of-two dilution of the original sample, arranged in a precise spatial order. The final droplets are held in place by the geometry of the system, with no external intervention required. The key innovation of the DOMINO system lies in its complete independence from flow rate and timing control. Whether the carrier liquid is introduced manually via syringe or via a pump operating at variable speed, the system performs identically, because the volume ratios are fixed by the trap dimensions and the merging mechanism is governed by the physical constraints of the channel geometry. The system’s robustness has been experimentally validated across a range of flow rates, with normalized standard deviations in droplet volume remaining below five percent even when the flow rate varied by a factor of three. This level of tolerance is unprecedented in conventional microfluidic systems, where droplet size is highly sensitive to flow dynamics. Moreover, the DOMINO system requires only a single switching mechanism to change the direction of flow, making it exceptionally simple to operate. The entire protocol—from sample introduction to final dilution array—can be executed by a non-specialist user with minimal training, using nothing more than a syringe and a manual switch. The system’s passive nature also eliminates the need for electronic controls, sensors, or power sources, making it ideal for deployment in point-of-care settings, field diagnostics, or resource-limited environments. The DOMINO system thus embodies the principle of “hard-wired” microfluidics, where the logic of the process is embedded in the physical architecture of the device, enabling complex chemical protocols to be performed with the simplicity of a strip test.

### Example 7

The fabrication of the microfluidic devices described herein is achieved through a single, scalable, and reproducible manufacturing process that requires the structuring of only one of two planar substrates, thereby minimizing complexity and cost. The preferred substrate material is polycarbonate, selected for its optical clarity, chemical resistance, thermal stability, and compatibility with standard microfabrication techniques. The microfluidic channels, bypasses, barriers, and intrusions are machined into a single polycarbonate plate using a computer numerical control (CNC) milling machine, with precision tolerances of less than five micrometers. The depth of the main channel is maintained uniformly throughout the device, while the bypasses are milled to a depth that is precisely one-quarter of the main channel’s depth, ensuring that the continuous phase flows unimpeded around the droplet while maintaining sufficient confinement to control interfacial curvature. The barriers and intrusions are fabricated to exact heights relative to the channel floor, with the barrier in the metering trap extending to three-quarters of the main channel’s height, and the intrusions in the merging and lock&shift traps extending to a height that is sufficient to partially obstruct the droplet without fully sealing the channel. After milling, the structured plate is thermally bonded to a second, unstructured polycarbonate plate of identical thickness using a controlled compression process at 130 degrees Celsius for thirty minutes, creating a sealed, leak-proof microfluidic network. The bonding process is performed under uniform pressure to ensure that the channel geometry is preserved and that no warping or deformation occurs. The resulting device is then fitted with fluidic interfaces using 21-gauge stainless steel needles, which are inserted into pre-drilled holes aligned with the inlet and outlet ports of the microfluidic channels. These needles are connected to syringes or syringe pumps via polyethylene tubing, allowing for the introduction of the continuous phase and the dispersed droplet phase. The continuous phase is typically composed of hexadecane, chosen for its low interfacial tension with aqueous solutions and its chemical inertness, while the dispersed phase consists of distilled water, optionally dyed with a non-reactive ink for visual tracking. The use of hexadecane as the continuous phase ensures that the droplet interface remains stable and that the capillary number remains sufficiently low to enable the dominance of interfacial tension over viscous forces. The fabrication process is entirely compatible with mass production techniques, including injection molding and embossing, and can be adapted to other thermoplastic materials such as polystyrene or cyclic olefin copolymer without altering the fundamental design principles. The simplicity of the fabrication process—requiring only a single structured layer and a flat sealing layer—distinguishes the invention from conventional microfluidic systems that rely on multi-layer photolithography, bonding of elastomeric materials, or complex alignment procedures. This simplicity not only reduces manufacturing cost but also enhances device reliability, as there are fewer potential failure points and no need for precise alignment of multiple layers. Furthermore, the use of rigid, non-elastic materials ensures that the channel geometry remains unchanged over time, even under repeated use or exposure to varying environmental conditions. The resulting devices are durable, reusable, and suitable for both single-use and long-term applications, making them ideal for clinical, environmental, and industrial settings where robustness and consistency are paramount.

### Example 8

The operational versatility of the disclosed microfluidic system is demonstrated through its ability to perform a wide range of liquid handling protocols beyond serial dilution, including sample aliquoting, reagent mixing, droplet arraying, and timed release—all without the need for external control systems. In one application, the metering trap can be used to divide a single large droplet of a biological sample into multiple, identical aliquots, each of which can be directed to a separate assay zone for parallel testing. This capability is particularly valuable in diagnostic settings where multiple tests must be performed on a limited sample volume, such as in point-of-care blood analysis, where a single drop of blood must be partitioned into separate assays for glucose, hemoglobin, and pathogens. In another application, the merging trap can be employed to combine two reagents in a controlled sequence, such as mixing a lysing agent with a cell suspension, followed by the addition of a staining agent, with each step occurring automatically as the droplet progresses through the device. The lock&shift trap enables the spatial organization of droplets into an array, where each droplet can be held in place for extended periods, such as during incubation of microorganisms or enzymatic reactions, while the continuous phase is periodically refreshed to supply nutrients or remove waste products. This feature is critical for long-term culture systems, where the ability to replace the surrounding medium without disturbing the droplet contents allows for the maintenance of viable cell populations over days or weeks. The delay trap can be used to introduce controlled time delays between steps in a multi-stage protocol, such as allowing sufficient time for a chemical reaction to reach equilibrium before the next reagent is introduced. In combination, these modules can be arranged into complex networks that perform entire workflows, from sample introduction to final detection, in a single, passive device. For example, a diagnostic chip could be designed to first meter a sample, then merge it with a lysozyme solution, delay the mixture to allow cell lysis, merge it with a PCR reagent, delay again to allow amplification, and finally release the product into a detection zone where colorimetric readout occurs. All of these steps are executed without the need for pumps, valves, sensors, or external timing, making the system inherently simple, reliable, and cost-effective. The system’s passive nature also eliminates the risk of electronic failure, power loss, or software error, which are common sources of malfunction in automated microfluidic platforms. Furthermore, because the device operates on the principle of geometric encoding, its function can be modified simply by altering the channel layout, without changing the underlying hardware or control system. This modularity allows for rapid prototyping and customization, enabling the same fabrication platform to be used for a wide variety of applications, from molecular diagnostics to environmental monitoring. The system’s compatibility with manual operation also makes it accessible in low-resource settings, where access to electricity, trained personnel, or sophisticated equipment is limited. The ability to perform complex, multi-step bioassays with the simplicity of a pregnancy test represents a transformative advance in microfluidic technology, bridging the gap between laboratory precision and field practicality.

### Example 9

The invention disclosed herein fundamentally redefines the paradigm of microfluidic system design by demonstrating that complex, multi-step liquid handling protocols can be executed with complete autonomy, relying solely on the geometric configuration of the microfluidic channels rather than on active control mechanisms. Traditional microfluidic systems, whether based on T-junctions, droplet-on-demand generators, or pressure-driven valves, require precise regulation of flow rates, timing, and synchronization to achieve reproducible results. These requirements introduce significant complexity, cost, and vulnerability to failure, particularly in environments where external control systems are impractical or unavailable. In contrast, the system described herein leverages the inherent physical properties of interfacial tension and channel confinement to encode the logic of the process directly into the architecture of the device. Each module—metering, merging, lock&shift, and delay—is designed to respond predictably to the presence or absence of a droplet, with its behavior determined entirely by the dimensions of the channel and the placement of barriers or bypasses. The result is a system that is not merely robust, but inherently self-correcting: variations in flow rate, droplet size, or timing do not affect the outcome, because the outcome is dictated by geometry, not dynamics. This principle of “hard-wired” operation enables the creation of devices that can be operated by untrained personnel, deployed in remote or resource-limited settings, and used without any external power source or electronic interface. The fabrication process is simple, scalable, and compatible with mass production techniques, ensuring that the devices can be manufactured at low cost and distributed widely. The system’s applications span a broad spectrum of fields, including clinical diagnostics, where it can be used to perform multiplexed immunoassays or pathogen detection using a single drop of blood; microbiology, where it can support long-term culture of microorganisms with periodic medium exchange; molecular biology, where it can automate multi-step PCR protocols without thermal cycling or fluidic control; and environmental monitoring, where it can detect contaminants in water samples using passive, on-site analysis. The system’s ability to generate precise serial dilutions, array droplets, merge reagents, and introduce controlled delays without external intervention represents a breakthrough in the field of microfluidics, offering a level of simplicity and reliability previously unattainable. Moreover, the modular nature of the components allows for endless combinations and expansions, enabling the design of increasingly sophisticated systems that can perform entire diagnostic workflows in a single, disposable chip. The invention thus opens the door to a new generation of microfluidic devices that are not merely miniaturized versions of laboratory instruments, but fundamentally new tools that operate according to the principles of passive, geometric logic—transforming the way liquids are handled, analyzed, and manipulated at the microscale.
