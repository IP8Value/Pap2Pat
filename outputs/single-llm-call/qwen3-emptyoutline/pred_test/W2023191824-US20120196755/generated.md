## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of molecular biology and genome stability, and more specifically to methods for identifying and mapping sites of chromosomal fragility resulting from replication stress. The invention provides novel, genome-wide techniques for detecting and localizing single-stranded DNA regions that precede and predict the formation of double-strand breaks during replication fork collapse. These methods enable the precise identification of genomic loci vulnerable to breakage under conditions of nucleotide depletion or checkpoint deficiency, without reliance on indirect markers such as phosphorylated histone variants or protein-binding assays. The invention further enables the prediction of chromosomal breakage hotspots based on the spatial and temporal dynamics of replication fork progression, thereby offering a direct, causal link between stalled replication intermediates and genomic instability. The methods described herein are applicable across eukaryotic systems, including yeast and human cells, and are particularly useful in the study of common fragile sites, cancer genomics, and disorders associated with replication stress, such as Fanconi anemia, ataxia-telangiectasia, and certain hereditary cancer syndromes.

## BACKGROUND

Genomic instability is a hallmark of cancer and a contributing factor in a wide array of human diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders and developmental syndromes. A major source of this instability arises from the failure of cells to properly manage replication stress, a condition in which DNA synthesis is impeded by a variety of endogenous and exogenous factors, including nucleotide depletion, DNA lesions, transcription-replication conflicts, and secondary DNA structures. When replication forks stall, the absence of a functional DNA damage checkpoint—particularly those mediated by the ATR/MEC1 and CHK1/RAD53 kinases—leads to the collapse of replication forks into double-strand breaks, which, if improperly repaired, result in deletions, translocations, and other chromosomal aberrations. These aberrations are frequently observed in tumor genomes and are thought to drive oncogenic transformation.

Historically, the detection of double-strand breaks has relied on indirect methods that suffer from significant limitations. For example, immunofluorescence staining for γ-H2AX, a histone variant phosphorylated in response to DNA damage, has been widely used as a surrogate marker for double-strand breaks. However, γ-H2AX foci can form in the absence of actual DNA breaks, and conversely, breaks may occur without detectable γ-H2AX accumulation, rendering this method prone to both false positives and false negatives. Similarly, chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by microarray hybridization (ChIP-chip) or sequencing (ChIP-seq) has been employed to map sites of DNA-binding proteins associated with break repair, such as Mre11 or Rad51. Yet these approaches are inherently limited by the availability and stability of such proteins at break sites, which may be transient, absent, or non-specifically recruited. Other methods, such as ssDNA enrichment using benzoyl-naphthoyl-DEAE-cellulose, have been used to detect resected ends at meiotic breaks, but these techniques are not easily adaptable to somatic cells or to breaks induced by replication stress, and they cannot distinguish between internal ssDNA gaps and true double-strand termini.

Furthermore, prior methods for mapping replication fork stalling have focused on the detection of single-stranded DNA regions formed during fork arrest, but these methods have not been rigorously correlated with the subsequent formation of double-strand breaks. In particular, no prior method has demonstrated that the spatial distribution of ssDNA formed during replication stress directly predicts the location of future chromosomal breakage. This gap in knowledge has hindered the ability to identify the precise genomic coordinates of fragile sites, to understand the sequence or structural features that predispose certain regions to instability, and to develop predictive biomarkers for genomic vulnerability.

In yeast models, the MEC1 and RAD53 genes encode key checkpoint kinases whose loss leads to massive chromosome breakage upon exposure to hydroxyurea, a ribonucleotide reductase inhibitor that depletes dNTP pools and stalls replication forks. While previous studies have shown that ssDNA accumulates at stalled forks in mec1 and rad53 mutants, no method existed to map these regions with sufficient resolution to correlate them with the exact sites of double-strand break formation. Moreover, prior techniques for detecting ssDNA required cell lysis and DNA denaturation, introducing artifacts through in vitro processing and obscuring the true in vivo topology of replication intermediates. Similarly, methods for detecting double-strand breaks relied on enzymatic digestion or physical isolation of broken DNA ends, which often failed to capture the full spectrum of breakage events or introduced biases due to differential ligation or amplification efficiencies.

Therefore, there exists a critical need in the art for a method that enables the direct, in situ, genome-wide mapping of single-stranded DNA regions formed during replication stress, and that further correlates these regions with the precise locations of subsequent double-strand break formation, without reliance on indirect markers, protein binding, or ex vivo manipulations that compromise the integrity of the DNA substrate. Such a method would provide unprecedented insight into the mechanisms of replication fork collapse, enable the identification of novel fragile genomic loci, and offer a predictive tool for assessing genomic instability in disease contexts.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, non-invasive, genome-wide method for mapping sites of chromosomal fragility by directly detecting and localizing single-stranded DNA regions formed during replication stress, and correlating these regions with the subsequent formation of double-strand breaks. The method involves embedding intact cells in agarose plugs to preserve chromosomal architecture, performing in-gel enzymatic labeling of single-stranded DNA using a strand-displacing DNA polymerase in the absence of denaturation, and quantifying the relative abundance of labeled DNA across the genome using microarray hybridization. This approach enables the detection of ssDNA gaps that are formed in vivo at stalled replication forks, without the introduction of artifacts from cell lysis, DNA extraction, or thermal denaturation. The labeled DNA is then eluted from the agarose matrix, fragmented to a uniform size, and co-hybridized with control samples labeled with a distinct fluorophore to generate a genome-wide profile of ssDNA accumulation.

The invention further provides a complementary method for mapping double-strand breaks by performing in-gel end-repair labeling of DNA termini using a DNA polymerase with 3′-5′ exonuclease activity, which processes both 3′ and 5′ overhangs to generate blunt ends suitable for labeling with fluorescently tagged nucleotides. This method allows for the direct, in situ labeling of double-strand break termini without the need for protein immunoprecipitation, ssDNA enrichment, or chemical modification of DNA ends. The labeled DNA is similarly eluted, fragmented, and co-hybridized with control samples to generate a genome-wide chromosome breakage profile.

The key innovation of the invention lies in the discovery that the spatial distribution of ssDNA formed during replication stress is not merely a byproduct of fork stalling, but is a direct and predictive precursor to the formation of double-strand breaks. By comparing the ssDNA profiles generated during replication arrest with the breakage profiles generated after recovery from arrest, the invention demonstrates that the locations of persistent ssDNA gaps precisely correspond to the sites of subsequent chromosomal breakage. This correlation is observed across multiple genetic backgrounds, including mec1 and rad53 mutants, and is independent of origin firing efficiency or chromosomal context. The invention further demonstrates that when replication forks are allowed to progress further from their origins prior to arrest, the sites of ssDNA accumulation and subsequent breakage shift accordingly, confirming that breakage is dictated by fork progression rather than by origin identity.

The invention also provides a method for predicting chromosomal fragility by identifying regions of elevated ssDNA accumulation under conditions of replication stress, even in the absence of detectable double-strand breaks. This predictive capability enables the identification of genomic loci that are at high risk for breakage under physiological or pathological conditions, such as in cancer cells with compromised checkpoints or in cells exposed to chemotherapeutic agents that deplete nucleotide pools. The method is broadly applicable to any eukaryotic organism and can be adapted to high-throughput screening platforms for drug discovery, toxicology, and personalized medicine.

In addition, the invention provides a novel method for validating the specificity of the ssDNA and breakage mapping techniques by demonstrating that internal ssDNA gaps lacking free 3′-OH termini are not efficiently labeled by the end-repair enzyme, thereby confirming that the breakage signal arises exclusively from true double-strand ends and not from non-break-associated ssDNA. The invention further validates the method using control systems, including restriction enzyme-induced breaks and HO endonuclease-induced breaks, which are accurately and specifically detected, confirming the fidelity and sensitivity of the approach.

The invention thus provides a transformative tool for understanding the molecular basis of replication stress-induced genomic instability, enabling the direct visualization of the causal chain from replication fork stalling to ssDNA formation to double-strand breakage. This method enables the precise mapping of fragile sites in human cells, the development of diagnostic biomarkers for cancer risk, and the design of therapeutic strategies aimed at protecting vulnerable genomic regions during chemotherapy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive method for mapping chromosomal fragility by directly detecting and correlating the formation of single-stranded DNA with the subsequent occurrence of double-strand breaks in eukaryotic cells undergoing replication stress. The method comprises a series of integrated, in situ procedures that preserve the native chromosomal architecture of intact cells, thereby eliminating artifacts introduced by traditional DNA extraction and denaturation techniques. The invention is based on the discovery that persistent single-stranded DNA regions formed at stalled replication forks serve as direct precursors to chromosomal breakage, and that the spatial distribution of these regions can be used to predict the location of future double-strand breaks with high precision.

The method begins with the preparation of intact cells embedded in agarose plugs. Cells are grown to mid-log phase under standard culture conditions and then synchronized at the G1/S boundary using a mating pheromone such as alpha factor. Upon release from synchronization, cells are exposed to a replication stress agent, such as hydroxyurea, which depletes intracellular deoxyribonucleotide pools and causes replication forks to stall. At defined time points—such as during the period of replication arrest and during recovery after removal of the stress agent—cells are harvested and immediately embedded in low-melting-point agarose to immobilize chromosomal DNA and prevent physical shearing or enzymatic degradation. The agarose plugs are then subjected to a series of enzymatic treatments to remove cell walls and nuclear proteins, leaving intact chromosomal DNA within a semi-solid matrix that preserves the three-dimensional organization of chromosomes.

Following preparation of the agarose plugs, two distinct labeling procedures are employed: one for the detection of single-stranded DNA and another for the detection of double-strand breaks. For single-stranded DNA mapping, the agarose plugs are equilibrated in a buffer containing Tris-HCl, magnesium chloride, and β-mercaptoethanol to maintain optimal enzymatic conditions. A labeling mix is then added directly atop each plug, containing a strand-displacing DNA polymerase, such as Klenow fragment (exo–), along with a mixture of unlabeled deoxyribonucleotides and a small proportion of a fluorescently labeled deoxyuridine triphosphate, such as Cy5-dUTP or Cy3-dUTP. The polymerase is capable of recognizing and extending from free 3′-hydroxyl termini present at the edges of single-stranded DNA gaps, displacing the downstream strand and incorporating the labeled nucleotide into the newly synthesized DNA. This process occurs without denaturation of the genomic template, ensuring that only in vivo-formed ssDNA regions are labeled. The reaction is carried out in the dark at 37°C for a duration sufficient to achieve saturation labeling, typically two hours, to ensure uniform signal across all accessible ssDNA regions.

After labeling, the agarose plugs are placed in dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cutoff of 12,000 to 14,000 Daltons and subjected to electroelution in a buffer containing 0.5× TBE at 110 volts for three hours. This process selectively elutes the labeled DNA fragments while retaining the agarose matrix and any unincorporated reagents. The eluted DNA is then subjected to sonication using a high-frequency ultrasonic device, such as a BioRuptor, to fragment the DNA into uniform pieces of approximately 500 base pairs in length. The fragmented DNA is purified using a silica-membrane-based column to remove residual proteins, salts, and unincorporated nucleotides, yielding a clean preparation of labeled ssDNA suitable for microarray hybridization.

For the detection of double-strand breaks, a parallel procedure is performed using identical agarose plug preparations. The plugs are equilibrated in a buffer containing End-Repair buffer, which includes ATP, magnesium acetate, and dithiothreitol. A labeling mix is then added, containing the End-It End-Repair enzyme mix, which comprises T4 DNA polymerase and polynucleotide kinase. The T4 DNA polymerase possesses both 3′-5′ exonuclease and 5′-3′ polymerase activities, enabling it to process DNA ends with either 3′ or 5′ overhangs, removing unpaired nucleotides and filling in recessed ends to generate blunt termini. The polymerase then incorporates fluorescently labeled dUTP at the newly formed blunt ends, effectively tagging all double-strand break termini with a detectable signal. This labeling is performed at room temperature for one hour in the dark to ensure specificity and minimize non-specific background. The labeled DNA is then electroeluted, sonicated, and purified using the same procedures as described for ssDNA labeling.

The labeled DNA samples derived from the experimental condition (e.g., cells recovering from hydroxyurea treatment) and a control condition (e.g., non-replicating G1-phase cells or untreated cells) are then mixed in equal molar quantities, with each sample labeled with a distinct fluorophore—Cy5 for the experimental sample and Cy3 for the control, or vice versa. The mixed samples are co-hybridized to a high-density DNA microarray containing oligonucleotide probes tiled across the entire genome at intervals of approximately 50 to 100 base pairs. Hybridization is performed according to standard protocols, followed by washing and scanning using a microarray scanner capable of detecting both fluorescence channels. The raw fluorescence intensities for each probe are extracted using proprietary software, and background signals are subtracted using a sliding window algorithm. The ratio of experimental to control fluorescence intensity is calculated for each probe, generating a genome-wide profile of relative ssDNA abundance or double-strand break frequency.

To enhance signal-to-noise ratio and resolve broad regions of enrichment, the raw ratio data are smoothed using a Lowess algorithm with a 6-kilobase window, a parameter chosen to match the spatial resolution of replication fork progression and the average distance between origins of replication in the organism under study. Peaks in the smoothed profile are identified using statistical thresholds based on the median and standard deviation of the entire dataset, and only those regions exceeding a defined significance threshold are considered true enrichment sites. The resulting profiles for ssDNA and breakage are then compared across multiple biological replicates to ensure reproducibility, and correlation coefficients are calculated between profiles generated under different conditions to assess the degree of spatial overlap.

The invention further includes a statistical framework for determining whether the observed correlation between ssDNA accumulation and breakage sites is significant or could arise by chance. This framework employs a random simulation test in which the positions of detected breakage sites are randomly redistributed across the genome ten thousand times, maintaining the same number of sites but altering their spatial distribution. For each simulation, the number of breakage sites falling within a 6-kilobase distance of a known origin of replication is recorded. The distribution of these randomized values forms a null model against which the observed number of breakage sites near origins is compared. A p-value is calculated as the proportion of simulations in which the number of coincident sites equals or exceeds the observed value. This test confirms that the association between breakage and ssDNA is non-random and biologically meaningful.

The invention further demonstrates that the labeling of ssDNA is specific to in vivo-formed gaps and not to nicks or other minor DNA lesions. This specificity is confirmed by performing parallel labeling experiments using Sequenase, a DNA polymerase that lacks strand-displacement activity. The resulting ssDNA profiles generated by Sequenase are nearly identical to those generated by Klenow, indicating that the labeling is not due to the enzyme’s ability to displace downstream DNA, but rather to its ability to extend from genuine ssDNA gaps. Furthermore, the end-repair labeling method is shown to be incapable of efficiently labeling internal ssDNA gaps that lack free 3′-OH termini, as demonstrated by the absence of signal at ssDNA regions in hydroxyurea-treated cells prior to breakage. This confirms that the breakage signal arises exclusively from true double-strand ends and not from non-break-associated ssDNA.

The invention also demonstrates that the timing of replication fork progression dictates the location of subsequent breakage. By synchronizing cells and adding hydroxyurea at progressively later time points after release from G1 arrest, the invention shows that when replication forks are allowed to migrate farther from their origins before stalling, the sites of ssDNA accumulation and subsequent breakage shift away from origin-proximal regions and become distributed across broader genomic intervals. This shift is reflected in the ssDNA and breakage profiles, which show a significant reduction in signal near early-firing origins and an increase in signal in regions replicated later in S phase. The correlation between ssDNA and breakage profiles remains high under these conditions, confirming that breakage is dictated by the position of the stalled fork, not by the identity of the origin itself.

The invention further validates the method using control systems in which double-strand breaks are introduced at known genomic locations. For example, when cells are treated with the restriction enzyme BamHI or FspI while embedded in agarose, the end-repair labeling method accurately detects the known cleavage sites with high specificity and sensitivity. Similarly, when cells are engineered to contain irreparable HO endonuclease cut sites, the method detects strong, localized signals precisely at these sites upon induction of HO expression, confirming the method’s ability to detect true double-strand breaks in vivo. These control experiments establish the fidelity, sensitivity, and specificity of the method and demonstrate its applicability beyond replication stress to other forms of DNA damage.

The invention further demonstrates that the method is applicable to temperature-sensitive mutants that exhibit replication stress and chromosome breakage in the absence of external chemical agents. For example, in a mec1-4 temperature-sensitive mutant shifted to the restrictive temperature, the method detects elevated levels of ssDNA at replication termination zones—regions previously identified as replication slow zones—well before the onset of detectable chromosome breakage. This temporal precedence confirms that ssDNA formation is not merely a consequence of breakage, but a causal precursor. The detection of ssDNA at these sites under physiological conditions, without chemical perturbation, underscores the broad relevance of the method to endogenous replication stress and its potential to identify fragile sites in human cells under normal growth conditions.

The method is further validated by indirect end-labeling Southern blot analysis, which confirms the presence of double-strand breaks at the same genomic loci identified by the microarray-based method. This orthogonal validation ensures that the microarray signals are not artifacts of hybridization bias or probe design, but reflect genuine DNA breakage events. The method is also shown to be quantitative, with signal intensity correlating with the number of breaks per cell, as demonstrated by dose-response experiments with varying concentrations of hydroxyurea.

The invention further provides a computational pipeline for analyzing the data generated by the method, including software for normalization, peak calling, correlation analysis, and statistical testing. This pipeline is designed to be compatible with standard bioinformatics platforms and can be adapted for use with next-generation sequencing data, enabling the method to be scaled to higher resolution and applied to organisms with larger genomes, including humans.

The invention also provides a method for identifying genomic features associated with fragility, such as repetitive elements, transcriptionally active regions, or late-replicating domains. By overlaying the breakage profiles with annotated genomic features, the invention reveals that breakage sites are significantly enriched near replication termination zones, regions with low origin density, and sequences prone to forming secondary structures. This enables the prediction of fragile sites in uncharacterized genomes and provides a mechanistic basis for understanding why certain genomic regions are more susceptible to instability.

The invention further enables the identification of novel therapeutic targets by revealing which replication fork-associated proteins or enzymes are responsible for ssDNA generation. For example, by combining the method with RNA interference or chemical inhibitors, the invention can be used to determine whether inhibition of specific nucleases, helicases, or polymerases alters the pattern of ssDNA accumulation and breakage, thereby identifying potential targets for chemoprotection.

The method is also applicable to human cells, including cancer cell lines with defective ATR or CHK1 pathways. By adapting the agarose plug preparation and labeling conditions to mammalian cell types, the invention enables the mapping of common fragile sites in primary human cells, lymphoblastoid lines, and tumor biopsies. This application has profound implications for cancer diagnostics, as it allows for the identification of genomic regions that are most vulnerable to breakage in response to chemotherapy, enabling the prediction of treatment-induced genomic instability and the development of strategies to protect these regions.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, robust, and broadly applicable method for mapping chromosomal fragility by directly detecting and correlating the formation of single-stranded DNA with the subsequent occurrence of double-strand breaks. The method is non-invasive, highly specific, and capable of high-resolution genome-wide mapping. It establishes a causal link between replication fork stalling and chromosomal breakage, provides a predictive tool for identifying fragile sites, and enables the development of new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for diseases associated with genomic instability.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the method for mapping single-stranded DNA and chromosome breakage was applied to yeast strains deficient in the MEC1 checkpoint kinase. The strain MEC1, which possesses a functional DNA damage checkpoint, and the isogenic mec1-1 mutant, which lacks this checkpoint, were synchronized in G1 phase using alpha factor pheromone. Following synchronization, cells were released into S phase in the presence of 200 mM hydroxyurea for one hour to induce replication fork stalling. After this period, cells were washed and transferred to fresh medium lacking hydroxyurea to allow for a one-hour recovery phase. At each time point—G1 (control), HU 1hr (during arrest), and R 1hr (after recovery)—cells were harvested and embedded in agarose plugs to preserve chromosomal integrity.

For single-stranded DNA mapping, agarose plugs were equilibrated in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 5 mM MgCl₂, and 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol. A labeling mix containing 0.24 mM each of dATP, dCTP, and dGTP, 0.12 mM dTTP, 0.12 mM Cy5-dUTP, 250 μg/ml random hexamers, and 150 units of Klenow fragment (exo–) was added directly atop each plug. The plugs were incubated at 37°C in the dark for two hours to allow for strand-displacing synthesis at ssDNA gaps. After labeling, the plugs were placed in dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cutoff of 12,000–14,000 Daltons and electroeluted in 0.5× TBE buffer at 110 volts for three hours at room temperature. The eluted DNA was then sonicated using a BioRuptor to reduce fragment size to approximately 500 base pairs and purified using a Qiagen PCR Cleanup Kit.

For chromosome breakage mapping, a parallel set of plugs was prepared and equilibrated in End-Repair buffer. A labeling mix containing 1 mM ATP, 0.24 mM each of dATP, dCTP, and dGTP, 0.12 mM dTTP, 0.12 mM Cy3-dUTP, and 3 μl of End-It End-Repair enzyme mix was added to each plug. The plugs were incubated at room temperature in the dark for one hour to allow for end-repair and labeling of double-strand break termini. The labeled DNA was similarly electroeluted, sonicated, and purified.

The ssDNA-labeled DNA from the HU 1hr and R 1hr samples was mixed with Cy3-labeled DNA from the G1 control sample, and the breakage-labeled DNA from the R 1hr sample was mixed with Cy3-labeled DNA from the G1 control sample. All mixtures were co-hybridized to Agilent G4493A yeast 4x44K microarrays according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After hybridization, slides were washed, scanned, and analyzed using Agilent Feature Extraction software. Background-subtracted fluorescence ratios were calculated for each probe, and the data were smoothed using a Lowess algorithm with a 6-kilobase window.

The ssDNA profile generated from the HU 1hr sample revealed strong enrichment signals near known origins of replication, particularly those classified as late-firing or checkpoint-controlled origins. The profile from the R 1hr sample was nearly identical, with a correlation coefficient of 0.93, indicating that the ssDNA gaps persisted after removal of hydroxyurea. In contrast, the breakage profile from the R 1hr sample of mec1-1 cells showed significant enrichment at multiple discrete loci across the genome, with peak intensities comparable to those observed at known restriction enzyme cut sites. The spatial correlation between the ssDNA profile from the HU 1hr sample and the breakage profile from the R 1hr sample was high, with a correlation coefficient of 0.64, indicating that the sites of ssDNA accumulation directly predicted the sites of subsequent breakage.

To validate these findings, a control experiment was performed using the rad53K227A mutant, which is known to accumulate ssDNA at all origins. The ssDNA profile generated using the in-gel labeling method was nearly identical to that obtained using prior methods, but with improved resolution and signal-to-noise ratio, confirming the reliability of the new technique. Furthermore, when the same labeling procedure was performed using Sequenase polymerase instead of Klenow, the resulting ssDNA profile was virtually indistinguishable, demonstrating that the labeling was due to extension from genuine ssDNA gaps and not due to strand displacement activity.

The breakage profile was further validated using indirect end-labeling Southern blot analysis at a specific locus on chromosome II at 620 kb. A strong signal was detected at this site in the mec1-1 R 1hr sample, confirming the presence of a double-strand break at the exact location predicted by the microarray data.

In contrast, wild-type cells subjected to the same treatment showed no significant ssDNA accumulation or breakage at any genomic locus, demonstrating that the checkpoint pathway effectively prevents both ssDNA persistence and breakage formation.

These results demonstrate that the method accurately detects ssDNA formation and its correlation with chromosome breakage, and that this correlation is specific to checkpoint-deficient cells undergoing replication stress.

### Example 2

In this example, the method was extended to investigate whether the location of chromosome breakage is determined by the progression of replication forks rather than by the identity of the origin of replication. To test this hypothesis, mec1-1 cells were synchronized in G1 phase with alpha factor and then released into S phase. Five aliquots of the culture were treated with hydroxyurea at different time points after release: 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 minutes. Each aliquot was incubated in hydroxyurea for one hour and then transferred to hydroxyurea-free medium for a one-hour recovery period. The samples were designated T0-HU, T5-HU, T10-HU, T15-HU, and T20-HU for the arrest phase, and T0-R, T5-R, T10-R, T15-R, and T20-R for the recovery phase.

Contour-clamped homogeneous electric field (CHEF) gel electrophoresis was performed on DNA extracted from each sample to assess replication fork progression. As expected, cells treated with hydroxyurea at early time points (T0-HU to T10-HU) exhibited a near-complete loss of linear chromosome signal, indicating that replication forks were stalled and chromosomes remained in a branched, replication-trapped state. In contrast, the T20-HU sample showed partial reappearance of linear chromosomes, indicating that many cells had completed replication before hydroxyurea was added, allowing forks to terminate and chromosomes to resolve.

Following CHEF analysis, the T0-R and T20-R samples were subjected to the in-gel ssDNA and breakage labeling procedures as described in Example 1. The ssDNA profiles from the T0-HU and T20-HU samples were highly similar, showing strong enrichment near early-firing origins. However, the breakage profiles from the T0-R and T20-R samples were markedly different. The T0-R sample showed breakage enrichment primarily near early-firing origins, consistent with the observation that forks had not progressed far before stalling. In contrast, the T20-R sample showed a broad, diffuse pattern of breakage across regions replicated later in S phase, with reduced signal near early origins. The correlation coefficient between the T0-R and T20-R breakage profiles was only 0.17, indicating that the spatial distribution of breakage had shifted dramatically.

To quantify this shift, a statistical analysis was performed using the random simulation test. The number of breakage sites within 6 kb of known origins was counted for each sample. In the T0-R sample, 41.6% of breakage sites were located within 6 kb of checked origins, a value significantly higher than expected by chance (P = 0.0032). In contrast, in the T20-R sample, only 12.1% of breakage sites were located near checked origins, and this value was not statistically significant (P = 0.8276). This result demonstrates that when replication forks are allowed to progress farther from their origins before stalling, the sites of breakage shift away from origin-proximal regions and become distributed across the genome in proportion to fork progression.

The ssDNA profiles from T0-HU and T20-HU were again highly correlated with their respective breakage profiles, with correlation coefficients of 0.74 and 0.65, respectively. This confirms that ssDNA formation precedes and predicts breakage regardless of the distance the fork has traveled. Furthermore, a weak negative correlation (coefficient = -0.25) was observed between the T0-HU ssDNA profile and the T20-R breakage profile, indicating that regions that were previously enriched for ssDNA near origins were protected from breakage when forks were allowed to migrate away from them.

These results provide definitive evidence that chromosome breakage is not determined by the intrinsic properties of replication origins, but rather by the position of the stalled replication fork. The invention thus demonstrates that the location of chromosomal fragility is a dynamic consequence of replication fork progression and that the detection of ssDNA can be used to predict where breakage will occur, even under conditions where forks have traveled significant distances from their origins.

To further validate the specificity of the breakage labeling, the method was applied to a strain containing three irreparable HO endonuclease cut sites on chromosomes II, III, and VI. Upon induction of HO expression, strong, localized signals were detected at all three sites in the breakage profile, confirming that the method is capable of detecting true double-strand breaks with high fidelity. No signal was detected in the absence of HO induction, demonstrating the specificity of the labeling.

In summary, this example demonstrates that the invention provides a powerful tool for mapping the causal relationship between replication fork progression, ssDNA accumulation, and chromosome breakage. The method reveals that breakage sites are not fixed by origin location, but are instead determined by the spatial and temporal dynamics of replication, offering a new paradigm for understanding genomic instability.
