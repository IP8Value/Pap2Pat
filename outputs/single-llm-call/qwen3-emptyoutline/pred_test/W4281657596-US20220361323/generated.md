## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of microfabricated neural interfaces and more specifically to systems and methods for fabricating three-dimensional microelectrode arrays with high spatial resolution using two-photon laser lithography integrated with thin-film microfabrication techniques. The invention further relates to the fabrication of complex, biomimetic, and porous electrode geometries that enable improved neural recording and stimulation performance in both acute and chronic implantation scenarios. The disclosed methods are particularly suited for applications in neuroscience research, neuroprosthetics, and bioelectric medicine, where high-density, minimally invasive, and mechanically stable electrode arrays are required for interfacing with neural tissue at the cellular level. The invention encompasses the integration of three-dimensional polymer structures with metallic conductive pathways on flexible or rigid substrates, the precise removal of insulating layers using femtosecond laser ablation, and the subsequent electrochemical characterization and implantation of such devices in living organisms. The invention further extends to the use of these electrode arrays for recording single-unit action potentials and local field potentials in small animal models, including songbirds and rodents, and to the potential adaptation of these fabrication techniques for retinal, cortical, and peripheral nerve interfaces in human clinical applications.

## BACKGROUND

Neural interfacing technologies have long sought to achieve high-fidelity, chronic recordings and stimulation of neural activity with minimal tissue damage and immune response. Traditional microfabricated electrode arrays, primarily based on planar silicon or flexible polyimide substrates, have enabled significant advances in electrophysiology, yet remain fundamentally limited by their two-dimensional geometry. These planar configurations constrain electrode contacts to a single plane, resulting in suboptimal contact with complex, three-dimensional neural architectures and limiting the spatial resolution of recorded signals. Furthermore, the mechanical mismatch between rigid silicon probes and soft neural tissue often leads to chronic inflammation, glial scarring, and signal degradation over time. While advances in flexible substrates and microneedle arrays have improved tissue compatibility, the ability to fabricate electrodes with intricate, non-planar geometries at micron-scale resolution has remained elusive using conventional photolithographic techniques.

Existing methods for creating three-dimensional electrode structures have relied on techniques such as electroplating, deep reactive ion etching, or multi-layer stacking, all of which impose significant limitations on geometric complexity, aspect ratio, and material compatibility. These approaches are often incompatible with flexible substrates, require multiple alignment steps that introduce positional error, and lack the resolution necessary to fabricate features smaller than 10 microns. Moreover, the integration of three-dimensional structures with underlying metal traces has historically required complex, multi-step processes involving sacrificial layers, selective etching, and post-fabrication assembly, each of which introduces variability and reduces yield. The removal of insulating layers such as parylene C from the tips of three-dimensional structures has further posed a significant challenge, as traditional methods—including UV laser ablation with photomasks or focused ion beam milling—are either too slow, too destructive, or lack the spatial precision required for complex topographies.

Recent efforts to overcome these limitations have explored the use of two-photon polymerization for fabricating microscale scaffolds and neural probes. However, prior systems have been limited by slow printing speeds, poor mechanical stability of printed structures, lack of real-time feedback during fabrication, and incompatibility with standard microfabrication workflows. Additionally, no prior system has demonstrated the seamless integration of two-photon lithography with automated surface localization, in situ fluorescence imaging, and femtosecond laser ablation for tip opening—all within a single, unified platform. The ability to print complex, high-aspect-ratio structures directly onto thin-film substrates, followed by conformal metalization and precise, athermal removal of insulation at the electrode tip, has not been previously achieved. Similarly, the fabrication of macroporous three-dimensional electrode geometries with internal metalization, enabling increased surface area without increasing tissue displacement, has not been demonstrated in a manner compatible with flexible substrates or high-throughput wafer-scale processing.

Furthermore, while biomimetic designs—such as those inspired by the mosquito proboscis—have been proposed to reduce insertion forces and improve tissue penetration, no prior system has enabled the rapid, high-resolution printing of such geometries as part of a fully integrated, wafer-scale fabrication process. The mechanical properties of commonly used photoresists, such as OrmoComp®, have also limited the ability to fabricate long, slender electrodes capable of penetrating deep brain structures without buckling. The absence of a scalable method to fabricate electrodes with sub-20-micron cross-sections, sharp tips, and tunable stiffness has hindered the development of chronic implants capable of stable, single-unit recordings in densely packed neural tissue.

There is therefore a critical need in the art for a novel fabrication platform that enables the high-resolution, high-throughput, and fully automated production of three-dimensional microelectrode arrays with customizable geometries, integrated metallic traces, and precisely defined recording surfaces. Such a platform must be compatible with standard thin-film processes, capable of operating on both rigid and flexible substrates, and able to produce electrodes with mechanical properties optimized for minimally invasive insertion and long-term stability. The invention disclosed herein fulfills this need by integrating a high-speed two-photon lithography system with real-time imaging, automated substrate alignment, conformal metalization, and femtosecond laser ablation into a single, cohesive fabrication workflow that overcomes the fundamental limitations of prior art.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel system and method for fabricating three-dimensional microelectrode arrays with micron-scale resolution using a unified platform that integrates two-photon laser lithography, thin-film microfabrication, and femtosecond laser ablation. The invention enables the automated production of complex, high-aspect-ratio electrode structures directly on flexible or rigid substrates, with integrated conductive traces, precisely defined recording surfaces, and customizable geometries optimized for neural interfacing. The method begins with the deposition and patterning of a sacrificial metal layer on a substrate, followed by the direct printing of three-dimensional polymer structures using a two-photon lithography system that incorporates real-time fluorescence imaging to ensure precise surface localization and substrate alignment. The printed structures are then conformally metalized using a non-directional sputtering process, after which a thin insulating layer is deposited over the entire device. A femtosecond laser ablation system, co-aligned with the lithography system, is then used to remove the insulating layer from the electrode tips with micron-scale precision, exposing the underlying metal without inducing thermal damage or redeposition. The devices are subsequently singulated and packaged for implantation.

The three-dimensional structures are fabricated from a biocompatible, glass-like photopolymer, such as OrmoComp®, doped with a fluorescent marker to enable real-time monitoring of polymerization during printing. The lithography system employs a resonant scan mirror to achieve printing speeds orders of magnitude faster than conventional galvanometer-based systems, enabling the fabrication of complex arrays in minutes rather than hours. The system further incorporates an automated surface-finding algorithm that uses fluorescence intensity at the substrate interface to determine the exact position of the substrate surface, ensuring consistent electrode height and adhesion across the entire array. Following metalization, the insulating layer is removed using a high-pulse-energy, 1035 nm femtosecond laser that raster-scans a thin volume encompassing all electrode tips, with ablation monitored via emitted ultraviolet fluorescence, allowing for real-time calibration and control of the ablation depth.

The invention further encompasses the fabrication of porous three-dimensional electrode geometries, wherein internal voids are created during the printing process and subsequently metalized to form conductive networks within the structure. These porous electrodes exhibit significantly increased surface area compared to planar or solid counterparts, enabling enhanced charge storage capacity and reduced impedance without increasing the footprint of the device. The method further enables the fabrication of biomimetic electrode geometries, such as needle-like tips inspired by the mosquito proboscis, which reduce insertion forces and permit penetration through the dura mater without the need for surgical removal. The resulting devices are compatible with standard neural recording systems, including Intan and Open Ephys, and have been demonstrated to record single-unit action potentials and local field potentials with high signal-to-noise ratios in awake, behaving animals.

The invention further provides a scalable, wafer-scale fabrication process that enables batch production of multiple electrode arrays on a single substrate, with each array independently customizable in geometry, height, spacing, and tip configuration. The entire process—from substrate preparation to final singulation—is fully automated and algorithmically defined, permitting rapid iteration and customization for specific experimental or clinical applications. The resulting electrodes are mechanically robust, chemically stable, and biocompatible, making them suitable for both acute and chronic neural interfacing in small animal models and, with further scaling, in human applications such as visual prostheses, cortical stimulators, and peripheral nerve interfaces. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in neural interface technology by enabling the fabrication of previously inaccessible electrode geometries with unprecedented precision, speed, and reproducibility.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for the fabrication of three-dimensional microelectrode arrays with high spatial resolution, mechanical stability, and precise electrical functionality. The invention integrates a suite of complementary fabrication technologies—including two-photon laser lithography, thin-film microfabrication, conformal metalization, and femtosecond laser ablation—into a single, automated workflow that enables the production of complex, customizable neural interfaces with micron-scale fidelity. The method is designed to be compatible with standard semiconductor and flexible electronics manufacturing processes, allowing for high-throughput, wafer-scale production while preserving the ability to tailor individual electrode geometries for specific neuroanatomical targets. The resulting devices are capable of high-fidelity neural recording and stimulation, with performance characteristics that surpass those of conventional planar or rigid microelectrodes.

The fabrication process begins with the preparation of a substrate, which may be composed of a silicon wafer with a thermally grown oxide layer or a flexible polyimide film. In one embodiment, a prime-grade 75 mm silicon wafer with 300 nm of thermal oxide is used as the base substrate. A layer of polyimide, such as HD MicroSystems PI2611, is spin-coated onto the substrate and cured at elevated temperature in a nitrogen atmosphere to achieve a final thickness of approximately six micrometers. This polyimide layer serves as a flexible, biocompatible substrate that provides mechanical compliance during implantation and reduces stress-induced tissue damage. An adhesion promoter, such as VM652, is applied to the edges of the wafer prior to spin coating to ensure uniform adhesion and prevent delamination during subsequent processing steps. Following curing, a sacrificial layer of chromium is deposited onto the polyimide surface using direct current sputtering at a pressure of approximately three millitorr. The chromium layer, which is approximately five hundred nanometers thick, is patterned using standard photolithography to define the locations of electrical traces and electrode anchor points. A photoresist, such as AZ-1512, is spin-coated onto the wafer and exposed through a custom photomask written using a direct-write laser lithography system. The exposure dose is calibrated to approximately seventy millijoules per square centimeter using a mercury arc lamp, followed by development in AZ 300 MIF to reveal the chromium pattern. The exposed chromium is then etched using a room-temperature ceric ammonium nitrate/nitric acid etchant, which selectively removes the chromium without attacking the underlying polyimide or oxide layers. The remaining photoresist is removed via sonication in acetone, leaving behind a precisely defined pattern of chromium traces and anchor pads.

Prior to the three-dimensional printing step, the substrate undergoes a plasma cleaning procedure to enhance the adhesion of the photopolymer to the chromium surface. The substrate is placed in a plasma etcher operating at a pressure of three hundred millitorr with a radiofrequency power of one hundred watts for a duration of ninety seconds. This step removes organic contaminants and activates the surface to promote covalent bonding between the polymer and the underlying metal. Following plasma treatment, the substrate is mounted onto a precision translation stage within a two-photon lithography system. The lithography system employs a mode-locked titanium-sapphire laser emitting at a wavelength of 780 nanometers, with a pulse width of one hundred femtoseconds, a repetition rate of eighty megahertz, and a peak power of approximately forty milliwatts. The laser beam is focused through a twenty-fold immersion objective lens with a numerical aperture of 0.7, enabling sub-micron resolution polymerization within a liquid photopolymer. The photopolymer is a hybrid formulation based on the commercially available OrmoComp® photoresist, a glass-like, biocompatible ORMOCER® material, which has been modified by the addition of a photoinitiator (2,4,6-trimethyl benzoyl phosphine oxide), a stabilizing agent (3,5-di-tert-butyl-4-hydroxytoluene), and a fluorescent dye (fluorescein). The inclusion of fluorescein enables real-time optical feedback during printing, as the dye emits fluorescence upon excitation by the infrared laser, allowing for the visualization of polymerization dynamics and structural integrity.

The three-dimensional electrode structures are designed using standard computer-aided design software and exported as STL files, which are then voxelized and translated into a series of three-dimensional scan trajectories by the printer’s control software. The printer incorporates a resonant scan mirror system that enables high-speed lateral movement of the laser focus, achieving printing speeds that are one to two orders of magnitude faster than conventional galvanometer-based systems. This speed enhancement is critical for the practical fabrication of large-area electrode arrays, as each sixteen-channel array can be printed in approximately ten minutes. During printing, the microscope objective is immersed in the liquid photopolymer, and the substrate is translated vertically to control the depth of polymerization. The system employs an automated surface-finding algorithm that utilizes the fluorescence signal emanating from the photopolymer at the substrate interface to determine the exact position of the substrate surface with micron-scale accuracy. This feature ensures that all electrode structures are printed with consistent height and optimal adhesion to the underlying chromium layer, eliminating the variability introduced by manual alignment or mechanical stage calibration. The algorithm continuously monitors the fluorescence intensity as the objective approaches the substrate, identifying the point of maximum signal as the surface plane. Once the surface is located, the printer automatically adjusts the vertical position of the substrate to maintain a fixed distance between the focal plane and the surface, ensuring uniform electrode height across the entire array.

Upon completion of the printing process, the substrate is immersed in a developer solution, such as Ormodev, for a duration of twelve hours to dissolve the un-polymerized photopolymer. The substrate is then rinsed in isopropanol to remove residual developer and dried under a stream of nitrogen. To enhance the mechanical stability and degree of crosslinking within the polymerized structures, the substrate is subjected to a post-cure step using ultraviolet light at a wavelength of 395 nanometers for ten minutes at an intensity of 2.8 milliwatts per square millimeter. This step increases the rigidity of the polymer, reducing the likelihood of structural deformation during subsequent processing. The resulting three-dimensional structures are composed of a dense, glass-like network with a modulus of approximately one gigapascal, enabling the fabrication of slender, high-aspect-ratio electrodes that resist buckling during insertion.

Following the printing and curing steps, the substrate undergoes a second plasma cleaning procedure to prepare the surface for metalization. The plasma treatment, performed under identical conditions as the first, removes any residual organic contamination and enhances the adhesion of the metallic layers to the polymer surfaces. The substrate is then transferred to a sputtering system, where a thin layer of titanium (approximately fifteen nanometers) is deposited as an adhesion layer, followed by a thicker layer of platinum (approximately two hundred nanometers) as the primary conductive material. The sputtering is performed under non-directional conditions, with pressures of three millitorr for titanium and ten millitorr for platinum, to ensure conformal coverage of the complex three-dimensional geometries. This step simultaneously metalizes the underlying chromium traces and the overlying polymer structures, creating a continuous electrical pathway from the electrode tips to the contact pads. The chromium sacrificial layer is then removed by immersion in a heated chromium etchant at sixty degrees Celsius for fifteen minutes. During this process, the wafer is agitated and transferred between multiple fresh etchant baths to ensure complete removal of chromium flakes and debris. The substrate is then thoroughly rinsed in deionized water to eliminate residual etchant and dried under nitrogen.

To electrically isolate the metal traces and prevent short circuits, a layer of parylene C is deposited over the entire device using a vacuum deposition system. The parylene C layer is deposited to a thickness of approximately three micrometers, providing a biocompatible, pinhole-free insulating barrier. However, the recording surfaces of the electrodes must be exposed to enable electrical interfacing with neural tissue. This is accomplished using a femtosecond laser ablation system, which is co-aligned with the two-photon lithography system to ensure precise spatial registration. The ablation system employs a 1035 nanometer wavelength, high-pulse-energy fiber laser (Coherent Monaco) capable of delivering up to forty microjoules per pulse at a repetition rate of one megahertz. The laser is raster-scanned across a thin volume—two to three micrometers in thickness—that encompasses the tips of all electrode structures in the array. The ablation process is athermal, meaning that the ultra-short pulse duration prevents thermal diffusion into the surrounding material, thereby avoiding melting, redeposition, or collateral damage to the underlying metal or polymer. The ablation is monitored in real time using photomultiplier tubes that detect the ultraviolet fluorescence emitted during the ablation of parylene C. This emitted light serves as a feedback signal, allowing the system to calibrate the laser power and scan parameters to achieve precise, uniform removal of the insulation layer without over-ablation. The entire ablation process for a sixteen-channel array is completed in less than one minute, enabling high-throughput processing.

Following ablation, the devices are singulated from the wafer using the same 1035 nanometer laser, which is programmed to trace the outline of each individual device, cutting through the parylene C and polyimide layers. The wafer is then submerged in warm water to release the individual devices, which float free due to the hydrophilic nature of the polyimide. Each device is then bonded to a commercial connector, such as an Omnetics connector, using anisotropic conductive film (ACF), which provides both electrical and mechanical connection between the device pads and the connector pins. The ACF is applied using a thermal press at a controlled temperature and pressure, ensuring reliable contact without damaging the delicate electrode structures. The resulting device is fully functional, with each electrode tip exposed and electrically connected to an external recording system via the flex cable.

In an alternative embodiment, the invention enables the fabrication of macroporous three-dimensional electrodes for enhanced charge injection and stimulation. In this configuration, the initial substrate is prepared with a pre-patterned metal layer consisting of titanium and platinum, which is then electrically isolated using a layer of SU-8 negative photoresist. Openings are patterned in the SU-8 to expose regions of the underlying metal, which will serve as electrical contact points for the subsequent three-dimensional structures. A sacrificial chromium layer is deposited and patterned to define the locations of the porous structures. The photopolymer is then printed within these openings, forming a lattice of interconnected pillars or pyramids with internal voids ranging from forty to four hundred square micrometers in cross-sectional area. The printed structures are metalized using the same non-directional sputtering process, resulting in conformal metalization of both the exterior and interior surfaces of the porous geometry. Scanning electron microscopy and focused ion beam sectioning confirm the presence of continuous metal pathways within the interior voids, indicating that the sputtering process achieves complete internal coating. These porous electrodes exhibit a five-fold increase in surface area compared to solid pyramids of equivalent footprint, resulting in significantly higher charge storage capacity and lower impedance. The internal porosity also permits the potential for tissue ingrowth, which may stabilize the electrode against micromotion and reduce fibrotic encapsulation over time.

The invention further enables the fabrication of biomimetic electrode geometries inspired by natural structures, such as the mosquito proboscis. In this configuration, the three-dimensional structures are designed with a sharp, tapered tip resembling the needle-like morphology of the mosquito’s feeding apparatus. The tip geometry is characterized by a bevel angle of approximately twenty-four degrees and a terminal radius of less than one micron, which minimizes the force required for tissue penetration. These structures are printed directly on the substrate using the same lithography system and demonstrate the ability to penetrate the dura mater of songbird brains without requiring surgical removal of the dura. This capability significantly reduces surgical time, minimizes tissue trauma, and lowers the risk of post-operative inflammation. The mechanical strength of the OrmoComp® polymer, combined with the optimized geometry, allows these electrodes to withstand insertion forces that exceed those required for penetration, while maintaining structural integrity during implantation.

The resulting devices have been tested in vivo in both zebra finches and C57BL/6J mice. In zebra finches, the devices were implanted into the HVC region of the songbird brain using a custom micromanipulator and suction-based holding system. High signal-to-noise ratio single-unit action potentials were recorded on multiple channels, demonstrating the ability of the electrodes to resolve individual neurons with high fidelity. In mice, the devices were implanted into the olfactory bulb, where they recorded local field potentials that were temporally locked to the breathing cycle, as measured by an intranasal thermistor. The recorded signals faithfully replicated the aperiodic rhythm of inhalation and exhalation, confirming the high temporal resolution and sensitivity of the electrodes. Electrochemical characterization using cyclic voltammetry and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy demonstrated mean impedances of approximately two hundred kilo-ohms at one kilohertz, with charge storage capacities that exceeded those of conventional planar electrodes by a factor of two or more. The devices remained stable over extended recording periods, with no evidence of signal degradation or mechanical failure.

The fabrication process described herein is fully scalable and compatible with standard microfabrication facilities. The use of wafer-scale processing enables the simultaneous production of dozens of electrode arrays on a single substrate, reducing per-device cost and increasing throughput. The algorithmic nature of the printing and ablation processes allows for rapid iteration and customization of electrode geometries, enabling researchers to tailor device parameters—such as height, spacing, tip shape, and porosity—to specific neuroanatomical targets or experimental requirements. The integration of real-time fluorescence imaging and automated feedback control ensures high reproducibility across batches, eliminating the variability inherent in manual fabrication methods. The resulting devices are mechanically robust, chemically inert, and biocompatible, making them suitable for both acute and chronic implantation scenarios.

The invention further enables the development of next-generation neural interfaces for human applications. The ability to fabricate high-density, three-dimensional electrode arrays with sub-20-micron features opens the door to volumetric recording and stimulation in cortical, retinal, and peripheral nerve tissues. The reduced cross-sectional area of the electrodes minimizes the reactive tissue response, potentially extending the longevity of chronic implants. The incorporation of porous geometries and biomimetic tips may further enhance long-term stability by promoting vascular integration and reducing fibrotic encapsulation. The compatibility of the fabrication process with flexible substrates allows for the development of conformable interfaces that can adapt to curved or irregular tissue surfaces, such as the cortex or retina. The method is also amenable to integration with wireless electronics, microfluidic delivery systems, and optogenetic stimulation modules, enabling the creation of multifunctional neural interfaces that combine recording, stimulation, and modulation in a single device.

In summary, the invention provides a novel, integrated platform for the fabrication of three-dimensional microelectrode arrays with unprecedented precision, speed, and versatility. The combination of high-speed two-photon lithography, automated surface localization, conformal metalization, and femtosecond laser ablation enables the production of complex, customizable, and mechanically robust neural interfaces that overcome the fundamental limitations of prior art. The resulting devices enable high-fidelity neural recording and stimulation in small animal models and hold significant promise for translation to human clinical applications in neuroprosthetics, neuromodulation, and brain-computer interfaces.
