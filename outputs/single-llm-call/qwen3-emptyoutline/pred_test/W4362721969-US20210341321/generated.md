# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of microfluidic sensing technologies, specifically to integrated, non-invasive, and highly sensitive flow rate sensors for microfluidic channels that operate via the mechanical deformation of a thin, rigid membrane coupled with microwave resonant circuitry. The invention is particularly suited for applications requiring real-time, localized measurement of fluid flow rates in microscale environments where conventional flow sensors are impractical due to size constraints, thermal interference, or compositional variability of the fluid. The sensor is designed to function reliably across a broad spectrum of fluid types—including aqueous solutions, organic solvents, and gases—without recalibration, leveraging the mechanical resilience and consistent elastic properties of a silicon nitride membrane. The integration of microwave resonators directly onto the membrane surface enables contactless, high-resolution detection of minute dimensional changes induced by fluidic forces, thereby transforming mechanical deformation into an electrical signal with exceptional fidelity. This technology is especially advantageous in biomedical microsystems such as organ-on-a-chip platforms, digital microfluidics, lab-on-a-chip diagnostic devices, and precision drug delivery systems, where dynamic flow control is critical to maintaining cellular viability, ensuring reproducible chemical gradients, and enabling accurate pharmacological screening. The invention distinguishes itself from prior art by eliminating the need for moving parts, avoiding thermal dependencies inherent in resistive or thermocouple-based sensors, and overcoming the slow recovery times and hysteresis limitations of elastomeric materials such as polydimethylsiloxane. Furthermore, the invention introduces a novel secondary sensing modality based on the detection of periodic membrane pulsations under steady-state flow conditions, which provides an additional, complementary channel for flow rate quantification that is inherently self-calibrating and insensitive to ambient temperature fluctuations or fluid composition changes.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION (PRIOR ART)

Prior art in the domain of microfluidic flow sensing has predominantly relied on two categories of sensing principles: active thermal sensors and passive mechanical transducers. Thermal flow sensors, widely commercialized and commonly employed in microfluidic systems, operate by measuring the heat dissipation from a localized heater placed between two temperature sensors along a fluidic channel. While these devices offer reasonable accuracy for single-component fluids such as water or buffer solutions, their performance degrades significantly when the thermal properties of the fluid vary—such as in mixtures of solvents, biological media containing proteins or cells, or during phase transitions. The reliance on thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity renders these sensors inherently non-universal and prone to drift under changing fluid compositions, a critical limitation in applications involving dynamic cell culture environments or multi-phase reagent delivery. Moreover, the presence of a heater introduces localized heating, which can alter the physiological state of sensitive biological samples, induce convection artifacts, or trigger unwanted chemical reactions, thereby compromising experimental integrity.

Passive mechanical sensors, on the other hand, have sought to circumvent thermal limitations by exploiting fluid-induced mechanical deflections. One prominent approach involves the use of cantilever structures with perforated geometries, wherein fluid flow exerts drag forces on the cantilever, causing measurable deflection correlated to flow rate. Although such systems have demonstrated sensitivities on the order of 1 nL/min, they require the fluid to pass directly through the sensing element, introducing flow obstruction, increased pressure drop, and potential clogging risks—particularly problematic in systems handling suspensions or viscous biological fluids. Another class of passive sensors employs elastomeric membranes, typically fabricated from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), whose deformation under flow is detected via optical or capacitive means. While these sensors offer non-invasive operation and reasonable sensitivity down to 0.5 µL/min, their fundamental material properties impose severe operational constraints. PDMS exhibits significant viscoelastic relaxation, resulting in recovery times on the order of several minutes after flow cessation, rendering it unsuitable for rapid, repeated measurements. Furthermore, PDMS is permeable to small molecules and gases, leading to unintended diffusion of analytes, swelling under prolonged exposure to organic solvents, and long-term mechanical drift due to creep. These material instabilities preclude reliable long-term operation in complex biological or chemical environments.

Electrical sensing modalities operating in the radio frequency (RF) domain have also been explored, primarily through capacitive coupling between electrodes placed adjacent to deformable membranes. However, these systems have been limited by low signal-to-noise ratios, poor spatial resolution, and insufficient bandwidth to capture transient or oscillatory behaviors. Many prior RF-based sensors rely on large electrode separations or indirect coupling mechanisms that diminish sensitivity and introduce parasitic capacitances, thereby reducing the precision of capacitance change detection. Additionally, these systems typically lack the integration of resonant circuitry necessary to convert minute capacitance variations into measurable frequency shifts with high fidelity. While phase-locked loop (PLL) techniques have been applied in other contexts for frequency tracking, their application to microfluidic membrane deformation has not been previously demonstrated, nor has any prior system combined microwave resonators with a rigid, non-elastomeric membrane to achieve both high sensitivity and rapid mechanical recovery.

Another category of prior art includes Coriolis flow meters, which detect the lateral force induced on an oscillating channel by flowing fluid. These sensors offer wide dynamic ranges and are insensitive to fluid composition, but their implementation at the microscale is severely hindered by the complexity of fabricating oscillating microchannels, the high power consumption required to maintain mechanical resonance, and the thermal loading introduced by the driving actuators. Moreover, their sensitivity diminishes at low flow rates due to the quadratic dependence of the Coriolis force on velocity, making them unsuitable for the low-flow regimes critical in cellular microenvironments. Capacitive sensors based on drag force measurements have also been reported, but they require direct fluid contact with the sensing element, thereby introducing the same clogging and fouling issues as cantilever-based systems.

Notably, no prior invention has demonstrated the use of a silicon nitride membrane—known for its high Young’s modulus, low thermal expansion coefficient, chemical inertness, and exceptional mechanical durability—as the primary transducing element in a microwave-based flow sensor. Silicon nitride has been employed in microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) for pressure sensing and resonant mass detection, but never in the context of continuous, real-time flow rate measurement in microfluidic channels. Furthermore, the discovery that steady fluid flow through a microchannel can induce sustained, periodic oscillations in a thin silicon nitride membrane—a phenomenon previously observed only in optical studies and never harnessed for quantitative sensing—has no precedent in the literature. The prior art has treated membrane deformation as a static or quasi-static phenomenon; none have recognized or exploited the emergence of dynamical bistability and self-sustained oscillations under constant flow conditions as a viable, high-sensitivity sensing mechanism. The integration of microwave resonators directly onto the membrane surface to detect both static deflections and dynamic pulsations with sub-kilohertz resolution, coupled with the use of a PLL-based phase-tracking system to convert frequency and phase shifts into real-time flow rate data, constitutes a fundamental departure from all existing approaches. The absence of any prior system that combines these elements—rigid ceramic membrane, integrated microwave resonator, phase-locked loop detection, and dual-mode sensing (static deformation and dynamic pulsation)—demonstrates the novelty and non-obviousness of the present invention.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a microfluidic flow rate sensor that detects fluid flow through a microchannel by measuring mechanical deformations of a thin, rigid silicon nitride membrane integrated with a microwave resonator. The sensor operates without moving parts, requires no thermal actuation, and is insensitive to fluid composition, enabling reliable operation across a wide range of liquids and gases. The membrane, fabricated to a thickness of approximately 220 nanometers, forms a compliant yet robust structural element of the microfluidic channel and deflects under the influence of fluid shear forces and pressure gradients. This deflection alters the capacitance of a coplanar waveguide microwave resonator patterned directly onto the membrane surface, resulting in a measurable shift in the resonant frequency of the circuit. The resonant frequency shift is detected in real time using a phase-locked loop system that maintains the resonator at a fixed phase condition, converting any deviation into an error signal proportional to the flow rate. This primary sensing mechanism enables continuous, high-bandwidth flow rate monitoring with a sensitivity of less than 0.5 µL/min and a temporal resolution of several seconds, suitable for dynamic flow control in microfluidic systems.

In addition to the static deflection-based sensing modality, the invention introduces a novel secondary sensing mechanism based on the detection of periodic membrane pulsations induced by steady fluid flow. Under constant flow conditions, the silicon nitride membrane exhibits self-sustained oscillations characterized by a distinct pulsation period that is inversely correlated with the flow rate. These oscillations arise from a nonlinear fluid-structure interaction wherein the membrane deflects under flow, temporarily increasing the channel cross-section and reducing hydraulic resistance, which in turn increases flow velocity, causing the membrane to snap back, thereby initiating a cyclic feedback loop. The period of these pulsations, ranging from approximately 7 seconds at low flow rates (e.g., 5 µL/min) to less than 1 second at higher flow rates (e.g., 120 µL/min), is detected by monitoring the phase response of the microwave resonator, which tracks the membrane’s rhythmic deformation with high fidelity. This secondary mechanism provides a complementary, self-calibrating method of flow rate determination that is entirely independent of absolute capacitance values or baseline drift, making it exceptionally robust against environmental noise, temperature fluctuations, and fluid composition changes. The combination of these two sensing modalities—static deflection and dynamic pulsation—enables the sensor to operate effectively across an unprecedented dynamic range, from sub-microliter-per-minute flows to flows exceeding 100 µL/min, all within a single, monolithic device architecture.

The sensor is fabricated using standard microfabrication techniques, including photolithography, inductively coupled plasma etching, and gold thin-film deposition, enabling batch production and seamless integration into existing microfluidic platforms. The silicon nitride membrane is formed by etching through a silicon substrate and underlying silicon dioxide layer, leaving a freestanding ceramic membrane that is mechanically stable, chemically inert, and resistant to swelling, creep, or degradation under prolonged exposure to biological fluids, organic solvents, or pressurized gases. The microwave resonator, designed with a 50-ohm impedance to match standard measurement equipment, is patterned directly onto the membrane surface using a coplanar waveguide geometry, with the signal and ground electrodes narrowing to a 20-micrometer gap directly over the membrane to maximize sensitivity. The entire system is enclosed by a bonded polydimethylsiloxane microchannel, ensuring fluid containment while preserving the membrane’s mechanical freedom. The sensor requires no external calibration for different fluids, as its operation is based solely on mechanical deformation and electromagnetic coupling, not on fluid-specific thermal or electrical properties. The invention further includes a custom electronic readout system comprising a phase-locked loop controller, a lock-in amplifier, and heterodyne frequency conversion circuitry, which enables continuous, real-time tracking of resonance frequency and phase shifts with sub-kilohertz resolution. The system is capable of simultaneous acquisition of both static deflection data and pulsation frequency data, allowing for cross-validation and enhanced reliability in critical applications. The invention thus provides a unified, multi-modal, and highly robust solution for microfluidic flow sensing that overcomes the fundamental limitations of all prior art, enabling precise, long-term, and composition-independent flow control in next-generation biomedical and analytical microsystems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention comprises a microfluidic flow rate sensor that integrates a thin, rigid silicon nitride membrane with a microwave resonator to detect fluid flow through a microchannel via mechanical deformation. The sensor is constructed on a silicon wafer substrate comprising a 500-micrometer-thick silicon base, a 2-micrometer-thick silicon dioxide intermediate layer, and a 220-nanometer-thick silicon nitride top layer. The silicon nitride layer serves as the primary transducing element, chosen for its exceptional mechanical properties, including a Young’s modulus exceeding 200 gigapascals, low thermal expansion coefficient, high fracture toughness, and complete chemical inertness toward aqueous, organic, and gaseous media. The fabrication process begins with photolithographic patterning on the backside of the wafer to define an etch window, followed by inductively coupled plasma etching to remove silicon and silicon dioxide material from beneath the designated membrane region. The wafer is then subjected to a prolonged potassium hydroxide wet etch, which selectively removes the remaining silicon substrate while preserving the silicon nitride membrane, resulting in a freestanding, ultra-thin ceramic membrane with dimensions ranging from 600 to 1500 micrometers in length and 300 to 500 micrometers in width. This membrane is mechanically compliant under fluidic pressure but exhibits negligible creep, hysteresis, or viscoelastic relaxation, ensuring that deformation is purely elastic and immediately reversible upon flow cessation.

On the front side of the wafer, a coplanar waveguide microwave resonator is patterned using photolithography and gold thin-film deposition. The resonator consists of a central signal electrode and two ground electrodes, arranged in a planar configuration with a nominal gap of 400 micrometers between the signal and ground lines. However, over the region of the silicon nitride membrane, this gap is narrowed to 20 micrometers to create a localized sensing region where the electric field is maximally sensitive to membrane deflection. A 100-nanometer-thick layer of gold is deposited via electron-beam evaporation and patterned to form the electrodes, with the entire structure designed to exhibit a characteristic impedance of 50 ohms to ensure optimal matching with standard microwave measurement equipment. The microwave resonator is tuned to resonate at frequencies between 2.0 and 3.7 gigahertz, depending on the membrane dimensions and geometry, with the first resonant mode being the primary operational mode. The resonator functions as a lumped-element RLC circuit, where the capacitance is determined by the distance between the signal and ground electrodes, and any change in membrane position alters this capacitance, thereby shifting the resonant frequency. The resonator’s quality factor exceeds 1000 under ambient conditions, ensuring sharp resonance peaks that allow for precise frequency tracking even in the presence of minor environmental perturbations.

To enclose the microfluidic channel, a negative photoresist mold is fabricated using SU-8 resist, and polydimethylsiloxane is cast onto the mold and cured at 80 degrees Celsius. The cured PDMS microchannel, typically 300 micrometers in width and 150 micrometers in depth, is then plasma-treated and bonded directly onto the front surface of the wafer, aligned precisely over the silicon nitride membrane and the narrowed gap of the microwave resonator. This bonding process creates a sealed, fluid-tight enclosure in which the membrane remains free to deflect without mechanical constraint, while the PDMS provides structural support and fluid containment. The microchannel is connected to a pressure-driven fluid delivery system comprising a programmable pressure pump capable of delivering precise flow rates from 0.1 µL/min to 120 µL/min, with a reference thermal flow sensor placed upstream to provide an independent calibration standard. The fluid, whether aqueous, organic, or gaseous, flows through the channel and exerts shear stress and pressure gradients on the underside of the silicon nitride membrane, inducing a measurable deflection perpendicular to the plane of the membrane.

The deflection of the membrane alters the distance between the signal and ground electrodes of the microwave resonator, thereby changing the capacitance of the system. This change in capacitance results in a proportional shift in the resonant frequency of the microwave circuit, which is detected using a custom-built electronic measurement system. The system employs a phase-locked loop controller based on a proportional-integral feedback algorithm, which continuously adjusts the frequency of a signal generator to maintain the phase of the resonator at a fixed reference value—typically zero degrees. Any deviation from this reference phase, caused by a change in resonant frequency due to membrane deformation, is converted into an error signal that is proportional to the magnitude of the frequency shift. This error signal is then digitized and recorded at a sampling rate of 20 Hz, providing a real-time, continuous output that is directly correlated to the flow rate. The baseline frequency noise of the system is approximately 1 kilohertz, corresponding to a short-term sensitivity of 0.5 µL/min, and the system exhibits a temporal response time of several seconds, enabling rapid feedback control in dynamic flow scenarios. Importantly, the system does not require recalibration when switching between different fluids, as the sensing mechanism is purely mechanical and electromagnetic, and thus independent of fluid thermal, electrical, or optical properties.

In addition to the static deflection-based sensing modality, the invention exploits a previously unreported secondary sensing mechanism: the emergence of periodic membrane pulsations under steady-state fluid flow. When fluid flows through the microchannel at a constant rate, the silicon nitride membrane does not remain in a static deflected state but instead undergoes rhythmic, self-sustained oscillations. These oscillations manifest as periodic transitions between two stable deformation states, with the membrane alternately bulging into the channel and snapping back to its original position. The period of these oscillations, referred to as the pulse interval, is found to be inversely proportional to the flow rate. For example, at a flow rate of 5 µL/min, the pulse interval is approximately 14 seconds, whereas at 10 µL/min, it decreases to approximately 7 seconds. This relationship holds across multiple device geometries, with different membrane sizes and channel widths yielding distinct but consistent scaling laws. The pulsation frequency increases monotonically with flow rate, spanning from less than 0.1 Hz at the lowest measurable flow rates to over 1 Hz at the upper limits of the sensor’s dynamic range. The origin of this phenomenon lies in a nonlinear feedback loop: as the membrane deflects under flow, the effective cross-sectional area of the channel increases, reducing hydraulic resistance and causing a transient increase in flow velocity, which in turn enhances the force acting on the membrane, driving it further into the channel. Once the membrane reaches a critical deflection, the increased channel volume reduces the pressure gradient, causing the membrane to snap back rapidly, restoring the original geometry and initiating the cycle anew. This bistable behavior is not induced by any external actuator or mechanical component; it arises spontaneously due to the coupling between fluid dynamics and membrane elasticity, and it is entirely reproducible across multiple devices and fluid types.

To detect these pulsations, the same microwave resonator used for static deflection sensing is employed. The phase response of the resonator, which is continuously monitored by the phase-locked loop system, exhibits a periodic modulation synchronized with the membrane’s oscillations. Each pulse corresponds to a distinct phase shift in the resonator’s output, and the time between consecutive phase peaks is measured to determine the pulsation period. The phase signal is filtered and analyzed using a custom LabVIEW program that identifies peak-to-peak intervals with sub-millisecond precision, even in the presence of minor signal noise. This secondary sensing modality offers several critical advantages over the static deflection method. First, it is inherently self-calibrating: because the pulsation period depends only on the fluid-structure interaction dynamics and not on absolute capacitance values, it is immune to baseline drift, temperature fluctuations, or long-term material degradation. Second, it provides a direct, discrete measurement of flow rate that does not require prior calibration against a reference sensor, making it ideal for closed-loop systems where external calibration is impractical. Third, the pulsation frequency increases with flow rate, meaning that at higher flow rates, the update rate of the sensor is faster, while at lower flow rates, the longer pulsation periods provide higher signal-to-noise ratios due to the extended observation window. This adaptive temporal resolution allows the sensor to optimize its performance across the entire dynamic range without requiring hardware modifications.

The sensor has been tested with a wide variety of fluids, including deionized water, phosphate-buffered saline, cell culture media containing serum, ethanol-water mixtures, and pressurized air. In all cases, the static deflection method produced consistent frequency shifts proportional to flow rate, and the pulsation frequency exhibited the same monotonic relationship with flow rate regardless of fluid composition. For gaseous flows, the sensor demonstrated operation under pressures up to 4 bar, with the membrane exhibiting no permanent deformation, fatigue, or rupture, even after thousands of pressure cycles. The ability to measure both liquid and gas flows using the same device architecture is a unique feature of the invention, as prior art sensors are typically limited to one phase or require separate calibration for each fluid type. The sensor’s robustness is further evidenced by its performance over extended periods: continuous operation for more than 72 hours under constant flow conditions revealed no measurable drift in either the static deflection response or the pulsation frequency, confirming the long-term stability of the silicon nitride membrane and the integrity of the microwave circuit.

The electronic readout system is designed to simultaneously acquire both the static deflection signal and the pulsation frequency signal. The phase-locked loop controller maintains a constant phase condition, while the error signal from the controller is fed into a lock-in amplifier operating at the resonant frequency of the circuit. To overcome the frequency limitations of the lock-in amplifier, an external heterodyne circuit is employed, which down-converts the microwave signal to a lower intermediate frequency compatible with the amplifier’s bandwidth. The output of the lock-in amplifier is then digitized and processed by a microcontroller running custom algorithms that extract both the mean frequency shift (for static deflection) and the autocorrelation of the phase signal (for pulsation period). The system outputs two independent flow rate estimates, which can be averaged for improved accuracy or used independently depending on the application. For high-precision applications requiring absolute calibration, the static deflection signal can be used in conjunction with a reference sensor during initial setup, after which the pulsation frequency can serve as a self-calibrating, drift-free measurement. For applications demanding real-time feedback control, the pulsation frequency provides a rapid, high-bandwidth signal that can be used to adjust pump pressure or valve states with minimal latency.

The sensor’s scalability is demonstrated through the fabrication of three distinct device geometries, each optimized for a different flow regime. The first device, with a membrane size of 1.5 mm by 0.3 mm and a resonant frequency of 3.66 GHz, is designed for ultra-low flow rates in the range of 0.1 to 3.5 µL/min. This device exhibits the highest sensitivity to static deflection and the longest pulsation periods, making it ideal for applications such as single-cell perfusion or slow drug infusion. The second device, with a membrane size of 0.8 mm by 0.35 mm and a resonant frequency of 2.36 GHz, operates in the range of 5 to 50 µL/min and is suitable for organ-on-a-chip systems requiring moderate flow rates with high temporal resolution. The third device, with a membrane size of 3 mm by 1 mm and a resonant frequency of 2.08 GHz, is optimized for high-flow applications up to 120 µL/min, such as high-throughput screening or large-volume perfusion systems. All three devices share the same fabrication process, the same microwave resonator design, and the same electronic readout system, demonstrating the modularity and reproducibility of the invention.

The invention further includes a method for integrating the sensor into larger microfluidic networks. Multiple sensors can be fabricated on a single chip, each with a different membrane geometry, to provide multi-point flow monitoring across a complex channel network. The sensors can be multiplexed using a single signal generator and phase-locked loop system, with frequency division or time-division multiplexing employed to isolate the signals from each sensor. Alternatively, each sensor can be equipped with its own dedicated readout circuit for parallel operation. The sensor can also be combined with other microfluidic components such as valves, mixers, and droplet generators, enabling closed-loop control of fluid delivery based on real-time flow measurements. The absence of thermal elements, moving parts, or consumable components ensures that the sensor can be sterilized, reused, or integrated into disposable devices without degradation in performance.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in microfluidic flow sensing by replacing thermal and elastomeric sensing principles with a rigid, ceramic membrane and microwave resonant detection. It eliminates the need for fluid-specific calibration, overcomes the slow recovery times of elastomers, avoids the thermal artifacts of resistive sensors, and introduces a novel, self-sustaining oscillatory sensing mechanism that is both robust and highly sensitive. The combination of static deflection and dynamic pulsation sensing provides dual-mode operation that enhances reliability, extends dynamic range, and enables unprecedented control over microfluidic systems. The sensor’s compatibility with standard microfabrication techniques ensures scalability and cost-effectiveness, while its mechanical and chemical durability guarantees long-term operation in the most demanding biological and chemical environments. This invention therefore provides a comprehensive, integrated, and universally applicable solution for microfluidic flow rate measurement that supersedes all prior art in performance, versatility, and reliability.
