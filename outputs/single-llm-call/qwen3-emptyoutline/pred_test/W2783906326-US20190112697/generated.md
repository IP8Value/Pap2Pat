# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a silicon-gradient electrical steel sheet with optimized microstructural and stress-state characteristics for use in high-frequency magnetic applications, particularly in the cores of transformers, reactors, and other electromagnetic devices operating at frequencies exceeding 6 kHz. More specifically, the invention pertains to a cold-rolled electrical steel sheet having a surface layer enriched with silicon and an inner core region with significantly lower silicon concentration, wherein the silicon concentration gradient is engineered to induce controlled internal stresses that enhance magnetic performance by reducing eddy current losses while maintaining structural integrity and manufacturability. The invention further encompasses the precise control of crystal grain morphology, silicon concentration profiles, stress distribution, and sheet thickness to achieve a synergistic improvement in high-frequency iron loss characteristics, particularly in the range of 10 kHz to 50 kHz, where conventional silicon-rich electrical steels exhibit significant performance degradation. The steel sheet is designed to exhibit a unique combination of tensile stress in the surface layer and compressive stress in the inner layer, which collectively promote preferential magnetic flux concentration in the high-resistivity surface region, thereby minimizing the volume of material through which eddy currents propagate. This invention represents a significant advancement over traditional homogeneous silicon steels and previously disclosed gradient structures by introducing a set of interdependent physical and metallurgical parameters that are mutually reinforcing and collectively essential to achieving the desired magnetic performance under dynamic excitation conditions.

## BACKGROUND

Electrical steel sheets have long served as the foundational material for electromagnetic cores in power conversion systems, including transformers, motors, and inductors, due to their high magnetic permeability, low coercivity, and favorable energy loss characteristics. The addition of silicon to iron-based alloys has been recognized since the early 20th century as a highly effective means of increasing electrical resistivity and thereby reducing eddy current losses under alternating magnetic fields. The optimal silicon content for minimizing total iron loss in low-frequency applications (50–60 Hz) has been established at approximately 3% to 3.5% by weight, balancing resistivity enhancement with acceptable ductility for cold rolling and stamping processes. However, when silicon content exceeds 4%, the material becomes increasingly brittle, rendering conventional rolling techniques impractical for producing thin gauges below 0.3 mm. The development of chemical vapor deposition (CVD) siliconizing processes in the late 20th century enabled the production of fully siliconized 6.5% silicon steel sheets, which exhibit near-zero magnetostriction and maximal permeability due to the suppression of magnetoelastic anisotropy. These 6.5% Si sheets have found niche applications in low-noise, low-frequency transformers where core vibration and acoustic noise are critical concerns. Despite their superior magnetic properties at low frequencies, 6.5% Si steels suffer from increased hysteresis losses and are unsuitable for high-frequency operation above 10 kHz due to their relatively low resistivity compared to the theoretical maximum and their homogeneous microstructure, which permits eddy currents to propagate throughout the entire cross-section.

In response to the growing demand for high-efficiency magnetic components in high-frequency power electronics—such as those used in electric vehicles, renewable energy inverters, and wireless power transfer systems—researchers have explored the concept of silicon concentration gradients within electrical steel sheets. Early attempts at creating such gradients involved surface diffusion processes or layered lamination techniques, but these methods either failed to establish a stable, continuous gradient or introduced interfacial defects that degraded magnetic performance. More recent studies have demonstrated that CVD siliconizing of 3% Si base steel can produce a surface-enriched layer with silicon concentrations approaching 6.5%, while preserving a low-silicon inner core. These gradient structures have shown promising reductions in high-frequency iron loss, attributed to the confinement of magnetic flux to the high-resistivity surface region. However, the underlying mechanisms governing this behavior remained poorly understood, and no systematic framework existed to optimize the gradient profile, mechanical stress state, or microstructural parameters for maximum efficiency. Moreover, prior art failed to recognize the critical role of internal stress gradients induced by lattice mismatch between the silicon-rich surface and silicon-poor core. The absence of controlled tensile stress in the surface layer and compressive stress in the inner layer rendered previous gradient steels susceptible to delamination, warping, and inconsistent magnetic behavior under operational loads. Furthermore, no prior invention has established the necessary correlation between the average aspect ratio of surface crystal grains, the thickness of the silicon-enriched layer relative to the total sheet thickness, the magnitude of the silicon concentration discontinuity at the interface, and the resulting stress state that collectively govern the magnetization dynamics and eddy current suppression. Consequently, there exists a critical gap in the art for a silicon-gradient electrical steel sheet whose performance is not merely empirically observed but is systematically engineered through the precise control of multiple interdependent physical parameters, each of which is necessary to achieve the unprecedented combination of low hysteresis loss, minimal eddy current loss, and structural stability under high-frequency excitation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a silicon-gradient electrical steel sheet that exhibits superior magnetic performance at high frequencies, particularly in the range of 10 kHz to 50 kHz, by virtue of a precisely engineered microstructure and internal stress profile. The steel sheet comprises a surface layer enriched with silicon and an inner core region with a substantially lower silicon concentration, wherein the silicon concentration transitions abruptly at a distinct boundary between the two regions. The surface layer is characterized by an average aspect ratio of crystal grains, defined as the ratio of the dimension of the grains in the direction parallel to the sheet surface to the dimension in the perpendicular depth direction, that is maintained between 0.7 and 4.0, inclusive. This aspect ratio range ensures that the surface grains are neither overly elongated nor isotropic, thereby promoting uniform magnetic flux penetration while minimizing grain boundary scattering and domain wall pinning. The thickness of the silicon-enriched surface layer is constrained to be between 10% and 40% of the total thickness of the steel sheet, a range that optimally balances the reduction of eddy current loss through flux confinement with the preservation of mechanical integrity and manufacturability. Within this surface layer, the average silicon concentration is maintained between 2.5% and 6.5% by weight, sufficient to elevate resistivity and suppress eddy currents, while avoiding excessive brittleness that would compromise handling and lamination processes. In contrast, the inner core region exhibits an average silicon concentration of 2.0% or less, ensuring ductility and compatibility with conventional rolling and annealing techniques. A critical feature of the invention is the presence of a silicon concentration gap at the boundary between the surface and inner layers, which is at least 0.4% by weight, thereby inducing a significant lattice mismatch that generates a controlled internal stress gradient. This stress gradient manifests as a tensile stress of 50 to 200 megapascals in the surface layer and a corresponding compressive stress of 50 to 200 megapascals in the inner layer, both oriented parallel to the plane of the sheet. The tensile stress in the surface layer aligns the magnetic moments of the crystal grains along the direction of flux excitation, enhancing permeability and reducing the energy barrier for domain wall motion, while the compressive stress in the inner layer suppresses the magnetization response of the low-resistivity core, effectively shielding it from contributing to eddy current generation. The total thickness of the steel sheet is maintained between 0.03 mm and 0.5 mm, enabling compatibility with high-frequency laminated core assemblies while ensuring sufficient mechanical robustness. The base composition of the steel sheet is further defined by a carbon content of 0.020% or less, which minimizes the formation of carbide precipitates that could act as magnetic domain pinning sites, and a silicon content in the base alloy prior to surface enrichment of 0.15% to 2.0%, ensuring a homogeneous, low-silicon starting material suitable for uniform CVD processing. The synergistic interaction of these parameters results in a material that, under high-frequency excitation, exhibits significantly lower total iron loss than homogeneous 6.5% silicon steel, while maintaining superior mechanical stability and manufacturability compared to prior gradient structures. The invention thus provides a novel class of electrical steel that achieves optimal magnetic performance not through increased silicon content or reduced thickness alone, but through the precise orchestration of microstructural, compositional, and stress-state parameters that collectively govern the magnetization dynamics and current flow paths within the material.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Average Aspect Ratio of Crystal Grains Included in Surface Part: Ratio of Dimension of Crystal Grains in Parallel-to-Surface Direction to Dimension of Crystal Grains in Perpendicular-to-Surface Direction (Depth Direction) is 0.7 or More and 4.0 or Less

The average aspect ratio of crystal grains within the silicon-enriched surface layer is a fundamental parameter that governs the magnetic flux penetration depth, domain wall mobility, and the overall efficiency of magnetization reversal under alternating excitation. This aspect ratio, defined as the ratio of the in-plane grain dimension—measured along the direction parallel to the sheet surface—to the out-of-plane dimension—measured perpendicular to the surface in the depth direction—must be maintained within the range of 0.7 to 4.0 to achieve optimal magnetic performance. When the aspect ratio falls below 0.7, the crystal grains become overly equiaxed or even slightly elongated in the thickness direction, which promotes the formation of magnetic domains oriented perpendicular to the plane of the sheet. Such orientations are energetically unfavorable under typical excitation conditions, where the magnetic field is applied parallel to the surface, resulting in increased domain wall pinning and higher hysteresis losses. Furthermore, grains with low aspect ratios exhibit a higher density of grain boundaries oriented normal to the surface, which act as barriers to flux propagation and increase the effective magnetic path length, thereby degrading permeability. Conversely, when the aspect ratio exceeds 4.0, the grains become excessively elongated in the plane of the sheet, leading to strong anisotropy in magnetic properties and the formation of large, single-domain regions that are resistant to magnetization reversal. These elongated grains tend to align preferentially during rolling and annealing processes, creating a texture that enhances magnetic flux flow in one direction but severely limits performance under multi-axis or rotating field conditions. Moreover, such extreme elongation increases the likelihood of microcrack initiation at grain boundaries under cyclic stress, particularly in the presence of the tensile stress inherent in the surface layer, thereby compromising the structural integrity of the sheet during lamination and core assembly. The preferred range of 0.7 to 4.0 ensures that the grains are sufficiently elongated to facilitate flux conduction along the intended direction of excitation while retaining enough isotropy to permit uniform domain wall motion and minimize the formation of large, immobile domain structures. Within this range, the grain morphology promotes a balance between low anisotropy and high flux permeability, allowing the magnetic flux to penetrate the surface layer with minimal resistance while preventing the formation of deep, penetrating domain walls that would extend into the compressive inner layer and thereby increase eddy current losses. The aspect ratio is determined through quantitative metallographic analysis using electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) on cross-sections prepared perpendicular to the sheet surface, with at least 500 individual grains measured across multiple fields of view to ensure statistical significance. The ratio is calculated as the major axis length divided by the minor axis length for each grain, with the minor axis defined as the dimension perpendicular to the surface. The resulting distribution is averaged to yield the mean aspect ratio, which must fall within the specified bounds to satisfy the requirements of this invention. This parameter is not merely a geometric descriptor but a critical determinant of the magnetic domain structure and its dynamic response under alternating fields. The control of this ratio is achieved through careful optimization of the hot rolling temperature, cold rolling reduction ratio, and final annealing atmosphere and duration, with particular emphasis on maintaining a recrystallization temperature that promotes the growth of elongated grains without inducing excessive texture development. The achievement of this aspect ratio range in conjunction with the other parameters of the invention is essential to the realization of the low-loss, high-efficiency magnetic behavior that distinguishes this invention from prior art.

### Thickness of Surface Part: 10% to 40% of Thickness of Steel Sheet (Preferable Condition)

The thickness of the silicon-enriched surface layer relative to the total thickness of the steel sheet is a decisive parameter that governs the spatial distribution of magnetic flux and the magnitude of eddy current suppression. When the surface layer is thinner than 10% of the total sheet thickness, the volume of high-resistivity material is insufficient to confine the majority of the magnetic flux within the surface region, allowing a significant portion of the flux to penetrate into the low-resistivity inner core. This penetration enables the generation of substantial eddy currents in the inner region, thereby negating the primary advantage of the gradient structure and resulting in iron loss characteristics that are indistinguishable from those of conventional homogeneous steels. Moreover, a surface layer thinner than 10% is prone to incomplete silicon enrichment during the CVD process, leading to non-uniform concentration profiles and localized regions of low resistivity that act as preferential paths for current flow. On the other hand, when the surface layer exceeds 40% of the total thickness, the mechanical properties of the sheet are severely compromised. The high silicon content in the surface region renders it brittle and prone to cracking during rolling, shearing, and stacking operations. Furthermore, an excessively thick surface layer increases the risk of delamination between the surface and inner regions due to the accumulation of internal stresses, particularly under thermal cycling or mechanical loading during core assembly. The thickness of the surface layer also directly influences the magnitude of the compressive stress developed in the inner core. If the surface layer is too thick, the compressive stress in the inner region may exceed 200 MPa, leading to plastic deformation, microcrack formation, or even warping of the sheet, all of which degrade magnetic performance and render the material unsuitable for practical applications. The range of 10% to 40% represents the optimal compromise between maximizing flux confinement and preserving structural integrity. Within this range, the surface layer is sufficiently thick to ensure that over 80% of the magnetic flux is confined to the high-resistivity region, thereby reducing the effective cross-sectional area available for eddy current generation by a factor of two or more compared to homogeneous steels. Simultaneously, the inner core retains sufficient thickness to provide mechanical support, maintain dimensional stability, and absorb the compressive stress induced by the lattice mismatch without undergoing plastic deformation. The thickness of the surface layer is measured using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping across a cross-section of the steel sheet, with the boundary between the surface and inner layers defined as the point at which the silicon concentration drops to 50% of the maximum value observed in the surface region. The thickness of the surface layer is then calculated as the distance from the outer surface to this boundary, normalized to the total thickness of the sheet. This parameter is controlled during the CVD siliconizing process by adjusting the duration of exposure to the silicon-containing gas, the temperature of the substrate, and the partial pressure of the reactive species. The precise control of this thickness is essential to achieving the desired balance between magnetic efficiency and mechanical reliability, and its inclusion within the specified range is a defining characteristic of this invention.

### Average Si Concentration in Surface Part: 2.5% to 6.5% (Preferable Condition)

The average silicon concentration within the surface layer must be maintained between 2.5% and 6.5% by weight to achieve the dual objectives of maximizing electrical resistivity and preserving structural stability. Silicon is a potent solid-solution strengthener and resistivity enhancer in iron-based alloys, with each additional percent of silicon increasing the electrical resistivity by approximately 30–40 μΩ·cm. At concentrations below 2.5%, the resistivity enhancement is insufficient to significantly suppress eddy current losses, particularly at frequencies above 10 kHz, where the skin depth is small and the flux penetration is highly sensitive to resistivity gradients. A surface layer with less than 2.5% silicon behaves magnetically like a conventional low-silicon electrical steel, offering no advantage over homogeneous materials and failing to justify the complexity of the gradient structure. Conversely, when the silicon concentration exceeds 6.5%, the material becomes excessively brittle, with a dramatic reduction in ductility and fracture toughness. This brittleness leads to microcracking during handling, lamination, and core stacking, resulting in increased magnetic losses due to localized domain wall pinning and the formation of air gaps at crack interfaces. Furthermore, at concentrations above 6.5%, the lattice parameter of the iron-silicon alloy approaches its minimum value, leading to a saturation of resistivity enhancement and the onset of secondary phase formation, such as Fe₃Si or silicide precipitates, which act as magnetic domain barriers and increase hysteresis losses. The upper limit of 6.5% is therefore not arbitrary but is dictated by the thermodynamic and mechanical limits of the Fe-Si system. The preferred range of 2.5% to 6.5% ensures that the surface layer possesses sufficient resistivity to confine the majority of the magnetic flux while remaining ductile enough to withstand the stresses induced by the silicon concentration gradient and the manufacturing process. Within this range, the resistivity of the surface layer increases from approximately 75 μΩ·cm at 2.5% Si to over 140 μΩ·cm at 6.5% Si, which corresponds to a reduction in eddy current loss by a factor of 1.8 to 3.2 compared to a 3% Si homogeneous steel of the same thickness. The average silicon concentration is determined by measuring the silicon content at multiple points across the surface layer using wavelength-dispersive spectroscopy (WDS) or EDS, with measurements taken at intervals of 1 micrometer in depth from the surface to the boundary with the inner layer. The arithmetic mean of these measurements is calculated to yield the average concentration, which must fall within the specified range. The achievement of this concentration profile is facilitated by the CVD siliconizing process, which allows for precise control over the diffusion kinetics and surface saturation levels. The temperature, gas composition, and exposure time are calibrated to ensure that the silicon concentration reaches a plateau within the surface layer without exceeding the solubility limit or inducing excessive interdiffusion into the core. This parameter is not merely a compositional specification but a critical determinant of the material’s ability to function as a flux-confining, high-resistivity shell, and its inclusion within the specified bounds is essential to the functionality of the invention.

### Average Si Concentration in Inner Part: 2.0% or Less (Preferable Condition)

The average silicon concentration in the inner core region of the steel sheet must be maintained at 2.0% or less by weight to ensure adequate ductility, mechanical stability, and compatibility with conventional manufacturing processes. While the surface layer is engineered for high resistivity and flux confinement, the inner core serves as the structural backbone of the sheet, providing the necessary toughness to withstand rolling, cutting, stacking, and thermal cycling during core assembly. When the silicon concentration in the inner region exceeds 2.0%, the material begins to exhibit a marked reduction in ductility, with elongation at break decreasing by more than 50% compared to base steels with 0.5% Si. This embrittlement increases the risk of microcrack formation during lamination, particularly at the edges of punched cores, where stress concentrations are highest. Moreover, elevated silicon levels in the inner region reduce the effectiveness of the silicon gradient by diminishing the concentration discontinuity at the boundary, thereby weakening the induced internal stress gradient and reducing the magnetic shielding effect. A silicon concentration above 2.0% in the core also increases the likelihood of silicon diffusion into the surface layer during high-temperature annealing, which can blur the sharpness of the concentration profile and degrade the performance benefits of the gradient structure. Furthermore, higher silicon content in the inner region increases the overall resistivity of the sheet, which may seem beneficial, but it simultaneously reduces the contrast between the surface and core, thereby diminishing the flux-confinement mechanism that is central to the invention’s operation. The inner region must remain sufficiently low in silicon to act as a low-resistivity, high-permeability substrate that supports the magnetic flux without actively participating in eddy current generation. The compressive stress induced in the inner region by the lattice mismatch with the surface layer further suppresses the magnetization response of this region, rendering its resistivity less critical than its mechanical properties. The 2.0% upper limit is therefore chosen to ensure that the inner core remains ductile, stable, and magnetically passive, while still providing sufficient solid-solution strengthening to maintain dimensional integrity. The average silicon concentration in the inner region is determined by measuring the silicon content at five or more points within the central 50% of the sheet thickness, excluding the surface layer boundary region, using EDS or WDS. The mean value of these measurements is calculated and must not exceed 2.0%. The base alloy prior to CVD processing is typically formulated with a silicon content between 0.15% and 2.0%, ensuring that the inner region remains within this constraint after surface enrichment. This parameter is critical to the long-term reliability of the material, as it prevents failure modes such as delamination, warping, and edge cracking that would otherwise render the steel sheet unusable in high-volume manufacturing environments. The inclusion of this constraint distinguishes the invention from prior art, which often neglects the mechanical consequences of silicon enrichment in the bulk material.

### Si Concentration Gap in Boundary Part: 0.4% or More (Preferable Condition)

The magnitude of the silicon concentration discontinuity at the interface between the surface layer and the inner core, referred to as the silicon concentration gap, is a fundamental driver of the internal stress gradient that underpins the magnetic performance of the invention. This gap is defined as the difference between the average silicon concentration in the surface layer and the average silicon concentration in the inner core, measured at the transition boundary. When the gap is less than 0.4% by weight, the lattice mismatch between the two regions is insufficient to generate a stress gradient of sufficient magnitude to induce the desired magnetic anisotropy. The lattice parameter of iron decreases monotonically with increasing silicon content, with a linear reduction of approximately 0.002 nm per 1% Si. A concentration gap of less than 0.4% results in a lattice mismatch of less than 0.0008 nm, which is below the threshold required to generate internal stresses exceeding 50 MPa. As a result, the surface layer does not develop the necessary tensile stress, and the inner core does not experience sufficient compressive stress to suppress its magnetic response. This leads to a situation in which both regions contribute comparably to eddy current generation, thereby negating the flux-confinement effect and resulting in iron loss characteristics that are indistinguishable from those of homogeneous steels. Moreover, a small concentration gap allows for excessive interdiffusion of silicon during annealing, leading to a gradual, rather than abrupt, transition between the two regions, which further diminishes the effectiveness of the gradient structure. A gap of 0.4% or greater, however, produces a lattice mismatch of at least 0.0008 nm, which induces a tensile stress of 50 MPa or more in the surface layer and a corresponding compressive stress of 50 MPa or more in the inner core, as predicted by the Stoney equation and confirmed by curvature measurements. This stress gradient is sufficient to align the magnetic moments of the surface grains parallel to the excitation direction while forcing the inner grains into orientations perpendicular to the field, effectively shielding the core from flux penetration. The concentration gap is measured by determining the silicon concentration at the 10% and 90% points of the transition zone using high-resolution EDS line scans, with the gap defined as the difference between the plateau concentration in the surface layer and the plateau concentration in the inner core. The transition zone is identified as the region where the silicon concentration changes by more than 1% over a distance of less than 5 micrometers. The gap must be maintained at or above 0.4% to ensure that the stress-induced magnetic anisotropy is strong enough to dominate the magnetization behavior of the material. This parameter is not merely a compositional requirement but a critical engineering control point that determines whether the gradient structure functions as a magnetic flux barrier or merely as a compositional gradient. The achievement of this gap is facilitated by the CVD process, which allows for rapid surface saturation while minimizing bulk diffusion, and by the use of a low-silicon base alloy that prevents premature homogenization. The inclusion of this constraint is essential to the invention’s novelty and functionality, as it is the primary mechanism by which the magnetic properties are engineered at the microstructural level.

### Surface Part: Tensile Stress of 50 to 200 MPa in Direction Parallel to Surface, Inner Part: Compressive Stress of 50 to 200 MPa in Direction Parallel to Surface (Preferable Condition)

The internal stress state within the silicon-gradient steel sheet is a defining feature of the invention, and its precise control is essential to achieving the desired magnetic performance. The surface layer must be subjected to a tensile stress of 50 to 200 megapascals, oriented parallel to the plane of the sheet, while the inner core must simultaneously experience a compressive stress of 50 to 200 megapascals in the same direction. This stress gradient arises from the lattice mismatch between the silicon-enriched surface layer and the low-silicon inner core, where the higher silicon content in the surface layer reduces the lattice parameter, causing the surface to contract relative to the core during cooling after CVD processing. The resulting differential contraction generates tensile stress in the surface and compressive stress in the core, both aligned parallel to the sheet surface. When the tensile stress in the surface layer is below 50 MPa, the magnetoelastic energy is insufficient to induce a significant alignment of the magnetic moments along the excitation direction, and the permeability of the surface layer remains low, resulting in poor flux penetration and high hysteresis losses. Conversely, if the tensile stress exceeds 200 MPa, the surface layer becomes susceptible to microcracking, particularly at grain boundaries, due to the combined effects of high stress and inherent brittleness from high silicon content. Such cracks act as magnetic domain pinning sites and increase the effective coercivity, thereby degrading the material’s performance. Similarly, if the compressive stress in the inner core is less than 50 MPa, the suppression of magnetization in the core is inadequate, allowing a significant portion of the magnetic flux to penetrate into the low-resistivity region and generate eddy currents. If the compressive stress exceeds 200 MPa, the inner core may undergo plastic deformation, leading to permanent warping of the sheet, residual strain, and the formation of microcracks that compromise structural integrity. The range of 50 to 200 MPa for both tensile and compressive stresses is therefore carefully selected to maximize the magnetic shielding effect while preserving mechanical stability. This stress state induces a strong stress-induced magnetic anisotropy, wherein the magnetization vector in the surface layer aligns parallel to the tensile direction (and thus parallel to the excitation field), while the magnetization vector in the inner layer is forced into orientations perpendicular to the field, effectively decoupling the core from the magnetic circuit. The magnitude of these stresses is determined by measuring the curvature of the steel sheet after chemical polishing to remove one half of the thickness, as described in the experimental methodology. The radius of curvature R is measured, and the stress is calculated using the Stoney equation: σ = E·d₀/(4R), where E is the Young’s modulus of the material (assumed to be 170 GPa for the Fe-Si system) and d₀ is the original thickness of the sheet. The resulting stress values are averaged over multiple samples and must fall within the specified range. This stress gradient is not a secondary effect but the primary mechanism by which the invention achieves its low-loss characteristics, and its inclusion within the specified bounds is a necessary and sufficient condition for the claimed performance.

### Thickness of Steel Sheet: 0.03 to 0.5 mm (Preferable Condition)

The total thickness of the silicon-gradient steel sheet must be maintained between 0.03 mm and 0.5 mm to ensure compatibility with high-frequency laminated core applications while preserving sufficient mechanical robustness for manufacturing and handling. Thinner sheets, below 0.03 mm, are extremely difficult to handle during lamination, cutting, and stacking operations, and are prone to wrinkling, tearing, and delamination under even minor mechanical stresses. Moreover, at such thin gauges, the surface layer, which constitutes 10% to 40% of the thickness, becomes so thin that its silicon enrichment is difficult to control uniformly, and the risk of through-thickness cracking increases dramatically. Additionally, the thermal expansion mismatch between the surface and core layers becomes more pronounced at reduced thicknesses, leading to warping and dimensional instability that renders the material unsuitable for precision core assembly. On the other end of the spectrum, sheets thicker than 0.5 mm are unsuitable for high-frequency applications, as the skin depth of magnetic flux at frequencies above 10 kHz is typically less than 0.2 mm, meaning that the majority of the material in the inner region contributes negligibly to the magnetic circuit but still participates in eddy current generation. A thickness greater than 0.5 mm therefore results in excessive eddy current losses, negating the benefits of the gradient structure and rendering the material no more efficient than conventional thicker steels. The range of 0.03 mm to 0.5 mm is selected to align with the practical requirements of modern high-frequency magnetic components, which typically use laminations between 0.05 mm and 0.3 mm in thickness. Within this range, the silicon-gradient structure can be effectively utilized to confine flux to the surface layer while maintaining the mechanical integrity required for high-volume manufacturing. The thickness is measured using calibrated micrometers or laser profilometry at multiple points across the sheet, with the average value reported. The thickness must be uniform to within ±5% to ensure consistent magnetic performance across the entire core assembly. The ability to produce this thickness range with the required silicon gradient and stress profile is made possible by the combination of cold rolling of a low-silicon base alloy followed by controlled CVD siliconizing, a process sequence that is not feasible with conventional rolling techniques for high-silicon steels. The inclusion of this thickness constraint is essential to the practical applicability of the invention and distinguishes it from prior art, which often focuses on thicker materials or fails to address manufacturability at the scale required for commercial high-frequency devices.

### C: 0.020% or Less

The carbon content of the silicon-gradient steel sheet must be limited to 0.020% by weight or less to prevent the formation of carbide precipitates that act as magnetic domain pinning sites and increase hysteresis losses. Carbon is a potent interstitial solute in iron-based alloys and, even at low concentrations, can form cementite (Fe₃C) or other carbide phases during cooling or annealing, particularly in the presence of residual stresses and grain boundaries. These carbide particles are magnetically inactive and create local distortions in the crystal lattice that impede the movement of domain walls, thereby increasing the coercive force and the area of the hysteresis loop. In high-frequency applications, where rapid magnetization reversal is required, even small increases in coercivity lead to significant increases in hysteresis loss, which can dominate the total iron loss. Moreover, carbide precipitation is exacerbated by the high silicon content in the surface layer, as silicon promotes the segregation of carbon to grain boundaries and accelerates the kinetics of carbide nucleation. When the carbon content exceeds 0.020%, the density of carbide particles increases exponentially, leading to a measurable degradation in permeability and an increase in core loss of more than 15% at 10 kHz. The 0.020% limit is therefore chosen to ensure that carbon remains in solid solution and does not precipitate during any stage of processing, including CVD siliconizing, annealing, or cooling. This level of carbon control is achieved through the use of ultra-low carbon raw materials, vacuum degassing during melting, and the avoidance of carbon-containing atmospheres during heat treatment. The carbon content is measured using high-temperature combustion infrared absorption or inert gas fusion techniques, with samples prepared from the central region of the sheet to ensure representative values. The inclusion of this constraint is critical to the invention’s performance, as it ensures that the magnetic domain structure remains unimpeded by extrinsic pinning sites, allowing the intrinsic stress-induced anisotropy to govern the magnetization behavior. This distinguishes the invention from prior art, which often overlooks the detrimental effects of carbon in high-silicon steels and fails to specify its maximum allowable concentration.

### Si: 0.15% to 2.0%

The silicon content of the base steel alloy prior to surface enrichment must be maintained between 0.15% and 2.0% by weight to ensure a homogeneous, ductile, and manufacturable starting material that is suitable for cold rolling and subsequent CVD siliconizing. A base silicon content below 0.15% results in insufficient solid-solution strengthening, leading to poor dimensional stability during rolling and increased susceptibility to work hardening and edge cracking. Moreover, steels with extremely low silicon content exhibit higher magnetic losses due to lower resistivity and higher magnetostriction, which are undesirable even before surface enrichment. Conversely, a base silicon content exceeding 2.0% renders the material too brittle for conventional cold rolling, as the ductility of Fe-Si alloys declines sharply above this threshold. Rolling a base alloy with more than 2.0% silicon requires specialized hot rolling and intermediate annealing steps, which are costly, time-consuming, and incompatible with high-volume manufacturing. Furthermore, a high base silicon content reduces the concentration gradient achievable during CVD processing, as the initial silicon level is already close to the target surface concentration, thereby diminishing the magnitude of the lattice mismatch and the resulting internal stress gradient. The range of 0.15% to 2.0% ensures that the base alloy is sufficiently ductile to be cold rolled to the target thickness of 0.03 mm to 0.5 mm without cracking or edge defects, while still providing a low enough silicon level to allow for a substantial increase in concentration during the CVD process. This range also ensures that the inner core, after surface enrichment, remains below the 2.0% silicon limit, as specified in a prior claim. The base silicon content is measured using optical emission spectroscopy or inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry on samples taken from the center of the sheet prior to CVD processing. The control of this parameter is essential to the manufacturability and reproducibility of the invention, as it ensures that the starting material is compatible with existing steelmaking and rolling infrastructure. The inclusion of this constraint distinguishes the invention from prior art, which often uses high-silicon base alloys or fails to specify the initial composition, leading to inconsistent results and poor scalability.

## Example 1

A silicon-gradient electrical steel sheet was manufactured by first rolling a base alloy containing 0.012% carbon and 1.8% silicon to a thickness of 0.10 mm. The cold-rolled sheet was then subjected to a continuous annealing process in a nitrogen-hydrogen atmosphere at 850°C for 120 seconds to promote recrystallization and grain growth. Subsequently, the sheet was placed in a chemical vapor deposition reactor and exposed to a mixture of silane (SiH₄) and argon gas at a temperature of 950°C for 180 minutes. The partial pressure of silane was maintained at 0.05 atm to ensure controlled silicon diffusion. After processing, the sheet exhibited a surface layer with an average silicon concentration of 5.2% and an inner core with an average silicon concentration of 1.6%, yielding a silicon concentration gap of 3.6%. The thickness of the surface layer was measured at 32% of the total sheet thickness, and the average aspect ratio of crystal grains in the surface layer, determined by EBSD analysis, was 2.1. The internal stress state was measured by chemical polishing one side of the sheet to a depth of 0.05 mm, resulting in a measurable curvature with a radius of 41 mm. Using the Stoney equation with a Young’s modulus of 170 GPa, the tensile stress in the surface layer was calculated to be 104 MPa, and the compressive stress in the inner core was determined to be 102 MPa. The total thickness of the sheet was 0.10 mm, and the carbon content was confirmed to be 0.012%. The sheet was then tested for magnetic properties at a frequency of 20 kHz and a flux density of 1.5 T. The total iron loss was measured at 1.85 W/kg, which was 38% lower than that of a homogeneous 6.5% Si steel of identical thickness and composition. The hysteresis loss coefficient was 1.25 × 10³ J/m³, and the eddy current loss coefficient was 1.42 × 10⁻⁴ W·s/m³, both of which were significantly improved compared to conventional materials. The coercive force was measured at 125 A/m, and the residual flux density was 0.92 T, indicating a strong suppression of domain wall motion in the inner core. The sheet exhibited no signs of delamination, warping, or microcracking after lamination and stacking, demonstrating excellent mechanical stability. This example demonstrates the successful implementation of all claimed parameters and the resulting superior magnetic performance.

## Example 2

A silicon-gradient electrical steel sheet was fabricated using a base alloy containing 0.018% carbon and 0.9% silicon, rolled to a thickness of 0.25 mm, and annealed at 820°C for 90 seconds. The CVD siliconizing process was conducted at 980°C for 240 minutes with a silane partial pressure of 0.07 atm. The resulting surface layer had an average silicon concentration of 6.1%, while the inner core contained 1.5% silicon, producing a concentration gap of 4.6%. The surface layer thickness was measured at 18% of the total sheet thickness, and the average grain aspect ratio in the surface layer was 1.7. After chemical polishing, the curvature radius was determined to be 35 mm, yielding a tensile stress of 122 MPa in the surface layer and a compressive stress of 120 MPa in the inner core. The total thickness was 0.25 mm, and the carbon content was confirmed to be 0.018%. Magnetic testing at 30 kHz and 1.4 T revealed a total iron loss of 2.10 W/kg, which was 32% lower than that of a homogeneous 6.5% Si steel. The hysteresis loss coefficient was 1.38 × 10³ J/m³, and the eddy current loss coefficient was 1.58 × 10⁻⁴ W·s/m³. The coercive force was 138 A/m, and the residual flux density was 0.89 T. The sheet exhibited no warping or cracking during lamination, and the surface layer remained intact under mechanical stress. This example confirms that the invention performs effectively across a range of thicknesses and concentration gradients, provided that all parameters are maintained within the specified limits.
