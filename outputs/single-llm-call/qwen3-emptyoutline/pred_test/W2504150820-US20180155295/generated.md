## FIELD

The present invention resides in the field of medicinal chemistry and molecular pharmacology, specifically in the discovery and characterization of novel small-molecule modulators of the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), a class I G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) implicated in immune cell trafficking, stem cell homing, cancer metastasis, and inflammatory diseases. The invention encompasses a set of structurally distinct chemical entities that function as either agonists or antagonists of CXCR4, identified through a dual in silico screening strategy integrating ligand-based pharmacophore modeling and structure-based molecular docking. These compounds exhibit unprecedented pharmacological profiles, including the first documented small-molecule agonists of CXCR4 capable of inducing receptor internalization, downstream signaling via ERK phosphorylation, and functional chemotaxis—biological responses previously attributed exclusively to the endogenous ligand SDF-1α. The invention further includes methods for identifying, synthesizing, and utilizing these compounds as pharmacological tools, diagnostic agents, or therapeutic candidates for modulating CXCR4-mediated signaling pathways in human disease contexts.

## BACKGROUND

The C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) is a seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor that binds with high affinity to its natural ligand, stromal cell-derived factor-1α (SDF-1α or CXCL12). This receptor-ligand pair plays a pivotal role in embryonic development, hematopoietic stem cell mobilization, lymphocyte trafficking, and organogenesis. In pathological contexts, aberrant CXCR4 signaling has been strongly associated with the metastatic spread of numerous cancers—including breast, prostate, lung, and hematological malignancies—as well as with chronic inflammatory conditions, HIV-1 entry into host cells, and autoimmune disorders. Consequently, CXCR4 has emerged as a compelling therapeutic target, and its modulation has been the subject of extensive pharmaceutical research over the past two decades.

To date, the majority of clinically and preclinically explored CXCR4 modulators have been antagonists. The most prominent example is AMD3100 (plerixafor), a bicyclam compound approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for the mobilization of hematopoietic stem cells in patients with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma and multiple myeloma. Other antagonists, including small molecules such as IT1t, TN14003, and BL-8040, have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models of cancer metastasis and inflammation. These compounds typically bind within the transmembrane helical bundle of CXCR4, interacting with key residues such as Asp171, Glu288, and Tyr255, and function by sterically or allosterically preventing SDF-1α binding or receptor activation.

Despite the success of CXCR4 antagonists, the pharmacological landscape of this receptor remains significantly underexplored. No small molecule agonist of CXCR4 had been previously reported prior to the present invention. While SDF-1α is a potent and efficacious natural agonist, its large peptide structure (72 amino acids) renders it unsuitable for oral administration, limits its bioavailability, and increases its susceptibility to proteolytic degradation. The absence of a synthetic, drug-like agonist has hindered efforts to probe the physiological roles of CXCR4 activation in vivo, to distinguish between G protein– and β-arrestin–mediated signaling pathways, or to develop novel therapeutic strategies that exploit receptor activation rather than inhibition.

Moreover, existing screening methodologies for CXCR4 modulators have been largely constrained by reliance on binding assays using radiolabeled SDF-1α or functional assays measuring calcium flux in the presence of the endogenous ligand. These approaches inherently bias discovery toward compounds that compete with SDF-1α for the orthosteric binding site, thereby excluding potential allosteric modulators or ligands that activate the receptor through distinct binding modes. Furthermore, the structural complexity of CXCR4’s ligand-binding pocket—characterized by a large, solvent-exposed cavity of approximately 2049 Å³—has posed significant challenges for structure-based drug design, as conventional docking algorithms struggle to accurately predict binding poses for small molecules within such expansive and flexible binding environments.

Virtual screening efforts have historically employed either ligand-based methods, such as pharmacophore modeling derived from known antagonists, or structure-based methods using crystallographic data from CXCR4 bound to peptide inhibitors or small molecules like IT1t. However, these approaches have been limited by the scarcity of high-resolution structural data, the conformational heterogeneity of the receptor, and the lack of consensus regarding the critical pharmacophoric elements required for agonism. As a result, previous screening campaigns have uniformly failed to identify any small molecule capable of mimicking the full spectrum of SDF-1α–induced biological responses, including receptor internalization, ERK phosphorylation, and chemotaxis.

The present invention overcomes these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel, integrated computational strategy that synergistically combines ligand-based pharmacophore modeling with dual-algorithm structure-based docking to interrogate a diverse library of drug-like compounds. This approach, which does not rely on prior assumptions regarding agonist pharmacophores or orthosteric competition, has yielded the first-ever identified small-molecule agonists of CXCR4. These compounds exhibit distinct chemical scaffolds from known antagonists, bind to a region of the receptor that partially overlaps with but is not identical to the SDF-1α binding site, and activate downstream signaling cascades with efficacy comparable to the native ligand. The invention further provides a method for differentiating between agonist and antagonist chemotypes based on specific molecular interactions within the binding pocket, enabling rational design of next-generation modulators with tailored signaling profiles.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel small-molecule compounds that modulate the activity of the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), including the first identified small-molecule agonists of CXCR4 capable of inducing receptor internalization, phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and chemotaxis of CXCR4-expressing cells. These compounds are structurally distinct from known CXCR4 ligands and exhibit pharmacological profiles that recapitulate the full spectrum of biological responses elicited by the endogenous ligand SDF-1α, thereby enabling unprecedented experimental and therapeutic applications.

The invention comprises a set of chemical entities selected from a library of approximately 13,000 drug-like compounds, identified through a dual virtual screening strategy integrating ligand-based pharmacophore modeling and structure-based molecular docking. The ligand-based component of the strategy involved the construction of a five-point pharmacophore model derived from a cluster-analyzed set of 162 known CXCR4 antagonists, comprising two hydrophobic features, two hydrogen bond acceptor features, and one positive ionizable feature. This model was used to screen the compound library for molecules exhibiting high conformational fit and low energy states, yielding a subset of 26 candidate structures. Concurrently, the structure-based component employed two orthogonal docking algorithms—Surflex and Glide—to dock the same compound library into a refined crystal structure of CXCR4 (PDB: 3ODU), with docking scores and interaction patterns with key residues (including Glu288, Tyr255, Asp171, and His281) used to prioritize compounds for experimental validation.

Fifteen compounds selected from the intersection of both screening methods were subjected to functional validation using a calcium flux assay in human melanoma cells (C8161) expressing endogenous CXCR4. Four of these compounds—NUCC-388, NUCC-392, NUCC-397, and NUCC-54120—demonstrated potent antagonism of SDF-1α–induced calcium mobilization, with IC50 values ranging from 0.3 μM to 3 μM. Notably, two other compounds—NUCC-390 and NUCC-398—induced a robust increase in intracellular calcium concentration in the absence of SDF-1α, a response that was fully inhibited by the selective CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100, confirming their activity as agonists. Further characterization of NUCC-390 revealed that it induces phosphorylation of ERK1/2, triggers internalization of CXCR4-YFP fusion receptors in HEK293 cells, and stimulates chemotaxis of C8161 cells in a Boyden chamber assay with efficacy equivalent to SDF-1α. Importantly, NUCC-390 does not compete with SDF-1α for binding to CXCR4, indicating a distinct binding mode that does not involve direct orthosteric occlusion.

The invention further provides a method for distinguishing between agonist and antagonist chemotypes based on specific hydrogen bonding interactions within the CXCR4 binding pocket. Molecular modeling demonstrates that agonists such as NUCC-390 consistently form a hydrogen bond with the phenolic hydroxyl group of Tyr255, whereas antagonists such as NUCC-388 and NUCC-54120 form a hydrogen bond with the carboxylate side chain of Glu288. This structural distinction provides a predictive framework for rational design of novel modulators with desired functional outcomes.

The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising one or more of the identified compounds, optionally in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or other therapeutic agents. The compounds are suitable for administration via oral, intravenous, subcutaneous, or inhalation routes and are useful for treating conditions associated with CXCR4 dysregulation, including cancer metastasis, stem cell mobilization, inflammatory disorders, and HIV infection. In particular, the agonists disclosed herein are useful for enhancing stem cell homing to regenerative niches, promoting tissue repair, or modulating immune cell trafficking in autoimmune contexts, while the antagonists are useful for inhibiting metastatic dissemination or blocking viral entry.

The invention further provides a method for identifying additional CXCR4 modulators by applying the pharmacophore model or the interaction signature (Tyr255 vs. Glu288 hydrogen bonding) to new chemical libraries using computational screening platforms. The invention also includes synthetic routes for preparing the disclosed compounds, analytical methods for their characterization, and kits for detecting CXCR4 activity in biological samples using the disclosed compounds as probes.

## DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this disclosure, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below, unless otherwise indicated.

“CXCR4” refers to the human C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4, a class A G protein-coupled receptor encoded by the CXCR4 gene located on chromosome 2q22.1, which mediates cellular responses to the chemokine SDF-1α (CXCL12) through activation of heterotrimeric G proteins, primarily of the Gi/o family, and subsequent recruitment of β-arrestins. CXCR4 is expressed on a variety of cell types including hematopoietic stem cells, lymphocytes, monocytes, neurons, and certain cancer cells.

“Agonist” refers to a compound that binds to CXCR4 and activates downstream signaling pathways, resulting in a biological response that mimics or enhances the effect of the endogenous ligand SDF-1α. An agonist may be a full agonist, producing a maximal response equivalent to SDF-1α, or a partial agonist, producing a submaximal response even at saturating concentrations. Agonists of CXCR4 may activate G protein–dependent pathways (e.g., calcium mobilization, inhibition of adenylate cyclase) and/or β-arrestin–dependent pathways (e.g., receptor internalization, ERK phosphorylation).

“Antagonist” refers to a compound that binds to CXCR4 and inhibits or prevents activation of the receptor by SDF-1α or other agonists, without eliciting a detectable biological response on its own. Antagonists may act competitively by occupying the orthosteric binding site or non-competitively by binding to an allosteric site and stabilizing an inactive conformation of the receptor.

“Pharmacophore” refers to an abstract representation of the essential steric and electronic features of a molecule that are necessary for its biological activity, including hydrogen bond acceptors, hydrogen bond donors, hydrophobic regions, aromatic rings, and ionizable groups, arranged in a three-dimensional spatial configuration. In the context of this invention, the pharmacophore consists of two hydrophobic features, two hydrogen bond acceptor features, and one positive ionizable feature arranged in a specific spatial orientation.

“Docking score” refers to a computational metric generated by molecular docking software that estimates the binding affinity of a ligand to a protein target, typically expressed in units of kcal/mol or as a normalized score derived from the scoring function of the docking algorithm. In this invention, docking scores from Glide and Surflex were used to prioritize compounds for experimental validation, with thresholds set at Glide scores below −6.0 and Surflex total scores above 6.0.

“Conformational energy” refers to the internal energy of a ligand molecule in a specific three-dimensional conformation, calculated using a molecular mechanics force field, and expressed in kilocalories per mole (kcal/mol). In this invention, conformers with energies less than 5 kcal/mol above the global minimum were considered for screening to ensure biologically relevant geometries.

“ChemBridge GPCR-focused library” refers to a proprietary collection of approximately 13,000 drug-like small molecules curated for structural diversity and relevance to G protein-coupled receptor targets, obtained from ChemBridge Corporation, San Diego, California. These compounds were selected based on compliance with Lipinski’s Rule of Five, absence of reactive or toxic functional groups, and synthetic tractability.

“ERK phosphorylation” refers to the post-translational modification of extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1 and 2 (ERK1/2) via dual phosphorylation at threonine and tyrosine residues, a hallmark of activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascade downstream of CXCR4. Detection of phosphorylated ERK by Western blot or immunoassay serves as a functional readout of receptor activation.

“Receptor internalization” refers to the process by which a cell removes a G protein-coupled receptor from the plasma membrane and sequesters it into intracellular vesicles, typically via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, following agonist binding. In this invention, internalization is quantified by fluorescence microscopy of CXCR4-YFP fusion proteins expressed in HEK293 cells.

“Chemotaxis” refers to the directed migration of cells along a concentration gradient of a chemoattractant. In this invention, chemotaxis is measured using a Boyden chamber assay in which CXCR4-expressing cells migrate through a porous membrane toward a chemoattractant gradient, with migration quantified by cell counting or fluorescent labeling.

“Pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” refers to a non-toxic, inert substance used as a diluent, stabilizer, or vehicle for the administration of a compound, including but not limited to water, saline, dextrose, glycerol, ethanol, polyethylene glycol, cyclodextrins, liposomes, and buffers. The carrier must be compatible with the compound and suitable for the intended route of administration.

“Therapeutically effective amount” refers to the quantity of a compound sufficient to elicit a desired biological response in a subject, such as inhibition of cancer cell metastasis, enhancement of stem cell homing, or reduction of inflammatory cell infiltration, without causing unacceptable toxicity or adverse effects.

“Compound” refers to any small organic molecule, whether naturally occurring or synthetically produced, having a molecular weight less than 1,500 Daltons, capable of modulating CXCR4 activity. Compounds of the invention include, but are not limited to, NUCC-390, NUCC-398, NUCC-388, NUCC-392, NUCC-397, and NUCC-54120, as well as their analogs, derivatives, salts, prodrugs, and stereoisomers.

“Prodrug” refers to a biologically inactive derivative of a compound that is converted in vivo into the active form through metabolic processes such as hydrolysis, oxidation, or enzymatic cleavage. Prodrugs may be designed to improve solubility, bioavailability, or tissue penetration.

“Salt” refers to a compound formed by the reaction of an acidic or basic functional group of a compound with a pharmaceutically acceptable acid or base, including but not limited to hydrochloride, sulfate, phosphate, acetate, citrate, maleate, fumarate, mesylate, and tosylate salts.

“Isomer” refers to any stereoisomer, enantiomer, diastereomer, tautomer, or conformational isomer of a compound disclosed herein, including racemic mixtures, enriched enantiomers, and resolved optically pure forms.

“Computer-readable medium” refers to any tangible, non-transitory storage device capable of storing digital data, including hard drives, solid-state drives, optical discs, flash memory, and RAM, used to store the pharmacophore model, docking parameters, or screening algorithms of the invention.

“Virtual screening” refers to the computational process of evaluating a library of chemical compounds for potential biological activity using in silico methods, including pharmacophore filtering, molecular docking, machine learning scoring, or similarity searching, prior to experimental validation.

“Structural diversity” refers to the degree of chemical variation among a set of compounds, assessed by molecular fingerprinting, scaffold analysis, or principal component analysis, such that the compounds represent distinct chemotypes and minimize structural redundancy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention discloses a novel class of small-molecule modulators of the CXCR4 receptor, identified through an integrated computational and experimental platform that overcomes the historical limitations of ligand- and structure-based screening methods. The invention provides not only a set of pharmacologically validated compounds but also a predictive framework for rational design of future modulators based on specific molecular interactions within the CXCR4 binding pocket. The compounds of the invention are structurally distinct from all previously reported CXCR4 ligands and exhibit unprecedented pharmacological profiles, including the first documented small-molecule agonism of CXCR4, which recapitulates the full spectrum of biological responses induced by the endogenous ligand SDF-1α.

The discovery process began with the selection of a chemically diverse library of approximately 13,000 drug-like compounds from the ChemBridge GPCR-focused library. This library was chosen for its high degree of structural novelty, compliance with drug-like properties, and synthetic accessibility, ensuring that any identified hits would be amenable to medicinal chemistry optimization. Each compound in the library was subjected to conformational sampling using the ConFirm/CatConf module of Discovery Studio 3.1, with a modified version of the CHARMm force field employed to refine the generated conformers. A poling technique was applied to bias conformational sampling toward energetically accessible geometries that were not local minima, thereby increasing the likelihood of identifying bioactive conformations that might be missed by standard sampling protocols. This process generated approximately 100 low-energy conformers per compound, with an energy cutoff of 10 kcal/mol above the global minimum, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the conformational space relevant to receptor binding.

Following conformational sampling, a ligand-based pharmacophore model was constructed using a training set of 10 CXCR4 antagonists selected from a cluster-analyzed set of 162 compounds sourced from the ChEMBL database (version 13). These compounds, which exhibited IC50 values ranging from 1 nM to 10 μM, were grouped into five distinct clusters based on structural similarity using hierarchical clustering algorithms implemented in Discovery Studio 3.1. Two compounds from each cluster were selected to form the training set, ensuring broad chemical representation across the antagonist landscape. The common feature pharmacophore modeling tool was then applied to generate ten distinct pharmacophore hypotheses, each representing a possible arrangement of key molecular features necessary for antagonist activity. The default parameters of the software were retained to avoid biasing the model toward any specific chemotype. The resulting hypotheses were tested against a separate test set of 10 antagonists, also selected from the five clusters, to evaluate their predictive power. One hypothesis emerged as superior, consistently fitting all 10 test compounds with a perfect fit value of 100%. This optimal pharmacophore consisted of two hydrophobic features, two hydrogen bond acceptor features, and one positive ionizable feature, each assigned a weight of 1, resulting in a cumulative fit score that directly correlated with binding affinity. This model was then applied to screen the entire ChemBridge library, yielding 26 compounds with a fit value exceeding 85% and a conformational energy below 5 kcal/mol.

Simultaneously, a structure-based virtual screening campaign was conducted using two orthogonal docking engines: Glide version 6.5 (Schrödinger, LLC) and Surflex (Sybyl-X, Certara, Inc.). The crystal structure of CXCR4 bound to the small molecule IT1t (PDB code 3ODU) was selected as the structural template due to its high resolution and well-defined ligand-binding pose. The protein structure was prepared using Prime version 3.8 (Schrödinger) to correct protonation states, optimize side-chain orientations, replace B-factors with OPLS charges, and eliminate non-physiological torsions. A 12 Å³ grid box centered on the centroid of the co-crystallized ligand was generated for Glide docking, while a ligand-based protomol was constructed in Sybyl using the extracted IT1t molecule as a template to define the active site volume. The 20 compounds from the training and test sets were first docked to validate the docking protocols. Both algorithms successfully reproduced the experimentally observed binding poses of the known antagonists, with Glide scores and Surflex total scores correlating with their reported potencies. The van der Waals radii of nonpolar ligand atoms were scaled to 0.8 to account for desolvation effects, and the maxkeep and maxref parameters were set to 5000 and 400, respectively, to ensure thorough sampling. The GPCR-focused library was then prepared using the Lig-Prep module of the Schrödinger suite, with ionization states and tautomers generated at physiological pH (7.4). Docking was performed using identical parameters, and compounds with Glide scores below −6.0 and Surflex total scores above 6.0 were retained for further analysis. A consensus set of 22 compounds was identified as hits that were ranked favorably by both algorithms, indicating robust agreement between the two docking methodologies. These compounds were then filtered for drug-like properties, including absence of reactive functional groups, metabolic liabilities, structural redundancy, and synthetic intractability. Nine compounds were selected for purchase based on commercial availability and structural novelty.

A total of 15 compounds—six from the ligand-based screen and nine from the structure-based screen—were subjected to functional validation using a calcium flux assay in C8161 human melanoma cells, which endogenously express high levels of CXCR4. Cells were loaded with the fluorescent calcium indicator fura-2 and stimulated twice with SDF-1α to establish baseline responsiveness. Test compounds were added prior to the second stimulation. Compounds that reduced the magnitude of the second calcium response were classified as antagonists, while those that elicited a calcium transient in the absence of SDF-1α were classified as potential agonists. Four compounds—NUCC-388, NUCC-392, NUCC-397, and NUCC-54120—demonstrated potent antagonism, with IC50 values of 0.3 μM, 1 μM, 3 μM, and 1 μM, respectively. Two compounds—NUCC-390 and NUCC-398—induced a robust calcium response indistinguishable in kinetics and amplitude from that elicited by SDF-1α. This response was completely abolished by pre-treatment with AMD3100, confirming that the observed activity was mediated specifically through CXCR4. Further experiments demonstrated that NUCC-390 did not compete with SDF-1α for binding to CXCR4, as determined by a radioligand displacement assay using 125I-labeled SDF-1α, indicating a non-orthosteric binding mode. This finding was corroborated by molecular docking simulations, which revealed that NUCC-390 binds in a region adjacent to, but distinct from, the SDF-1α binding pocket, engaging residues such as Tyr255, Phe189, and Trp252, while avoiding direct interaction with Glu288.

To confirm agonist activity beyond calcium mobilization, additional functional assays were performed. Treatment of C8161 cells with NUCC-390 resulted in a dose-dependent increase in phosphorylated ERK1/2, as detected by Western blot analysis, mirroring the signaling profile induced by SDF-1α. Receptor internalization was assessed in HEK293 cells stably expressing a CXCR4-YFP fusion protein. Treatment with NUCC-390 for two hours induced pronounced translocation of the receptor from the plasma membrane to intracellular vesicles, a hallmark of agonist-induced endocytosis. This internalization was fully inhibited by AMD3100, confirming its dependence on CXCR4 activation. Chemotaxis assays using a Boyden chamber demonstrated that NUCC-390 stimulated directional migration of C8161 cells with efficacy comparable to SDF-1α, confirming its ability to elicit a physiologically relevant functional response. Importantly, NUCC-390 and NUCC-398 had no effect on HEK293 cells, which express negligible levels of CXCR4, ruling out off-target effects.

The molecular basis for the distinction between agonist and antagonist activity was elucidated through analysis of the docked poses of the active compounds. Agonists consistently formed a hydrogen bond between a nitrogen-containing moiety (either an amine or an amide) and the hydroxyl group of Tyr255, a residue located in transmembrane helix 6. In contrast, antagonists formed a hydrogen bond with the carboxylate side chain of Glu288, located in transmembrane helix 7. This single residue interaction pattern provided a clear structural determinant for functional outcome: interaction with Tyr255 favored receptor activation, while interaction with Glu288 favored receptor inhibition. This insight enabled the development of a predictive rule for future compound design: compounds capable of forming a hydrogen bond with Tyr255 are likely to function as agonists, whereas those forming a hydrogen bond with Glu288 are likely to function as antagonists. This rule was validated across the entire set of 15 tested compounds and has since been applied to screen additional chemical libraries with high predictive accuracy.

The compounds of the invention are represented by the following structural classes: NUCC-390, a substituted pyridine derivative with a central amide linker and a terminal piperazine ring; NUCC-398, a fused tricyclic scaffold containing a pyrazolo[1,5-a]pyridine core; NUCC-388, a benzimidazole derivative with a sulfonamide substituent; NUCC-392, a quinoline-based structure with a tertiary amine side chain; NUCC-397, a benzothiazole analog with a morpholine group; and NUCC-54120, a pyrimidine derivative with a piperidine tail. Each of these scaffolds is chemically distinct from previously reported CXCR4 ligands, including AMD3100, IT1t, and BL-8040, and exhibits novel physicochemical properties, including moderate lipophilicity (cLogP 2.5–4.2), molecular weights between 380 and 520 Da, and acceptable solubility profiles. The compounds are stable under physiological conditions, show no reactivity with glutathione or cytochrome P450 isoforms in preliminary metabolic assays, and are amenable to scalable synthesis via standard organic chemistry techniques.

Oral formulations may include tablets, capsules, or suspensions containing the compound in combination with excipients such as microcrystalline cellulose, lactose, starch, magnesium stearate, or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose. Parenteral formulations may be prepared as sterile solutions or lyophilized powders in isotonic saline, dextrose, or phosphate-buffered saline, optionally containing stabilizers such as albumin, polysorbate 80, or citrate buffer. The compounds may also be encapsulated in liposomes, nanoparticles, or polymeric micelles to enhance bioavailability, prolong half-life, or target specific tissues.

For example, NUCC-390 may be used to selectively activate G protein– or β-arrestin–mediated signaling in cell-based assays, enabling the identification of downstream effectors specific to each pathway. The compounds may also be used in animal models to study the physiological consequences of CXCR4 activation, such as stem cell homing to bone marrow, cardiac repair after myocardial infarction, or neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease. In therapeutic applications, the antagonists may be used to inhibit cancer metastasis, block HIV entry, or mobilize hematopoietic stem cells, while the agonists may be used to enhance tissue regeneration, promote immune cell recruitment to sites of injury, or modulate neural plasticity.

Such kits may comprise one or more of the disclosed compounds as positive or negative controls, along with reagents for measuring calcium flux, ERK phosphorylation, receptor internalization, or chemotaxis. ### EXPERIMENTAL

The experimental procedures described herein were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the ethical use of biological materials and in compliance with Good Laboratory Practice standards. All chemicals were purchased from commercial vendors and used without further purification unless otherwise specified. The ChemBridge GPCR-focused library was obtained as a 10 mM stock solution in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at −20°C in aliquots to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles. All compounds were reconstituted in DMSO immediately prior to use, and final concentrations in biological assays were achieved by dilution in assay buffer to maintain a maximum DMSO concentration of 0.1% (v/v), which was shown to have no effect on cell viability or calcium signaling.

Conformational sampling of the 13,000-compound library was performed using the ConFirm/CatConf module of Discovery Studio 3.1 (Accelrys, Inc., San Diego, CA). The CHARMm force field was modified to include reparameterized torsional terms for nitrogen-containing heterocycles and sulfonamide groups, which are prevalent in the library. The poling technique employed a biasing potential that penalized conformations within 1.5 Å of local energy minima, encouraging sampling of higher-energy, extended geometries that are more likely to adopt bioactive conformations upon receptor binding. A total of 100 conformers per compound were generated, with a maximum energy cutoff of 10 kcal/mol above the lowest-energy conformer. Conformers were then filtered for drug-like properties using Lipinski’s Rule of Five, with molecular weight ≤500, cLogP ≤5, hydrogen bond donors ≤5, and hydrogen bond acceptors ≤10. Compounds violating these criteria were excluded from further analysis.

The pharmacophore model was built using the common feature pharmacophore modeling tool in Discovery Studio 3.1. The training set of 10 antagonists was aligned using the maximum common substructure algorithm, and the software was instructed to generate hypotheses with a minimum of four and a maximum of six features. Hydrophobic features were defined as aromatic rings or aliphatic chains with a radius of 1.8 Å, hydrogen bond acceptor features were defined as oxygen or nitrogen atoms with lone pairs oriented within 30° of the optimal vector, and the positive ionizable feature was defined as a nitrogen atom with a formal charge of +1 at physiological pH. The model was validated using a leave-one-out cross-validation procedure, and the final hypothesis was selected based on its ability to correctly classify all 10 test compounds with a fit value ≥85%.

For structure-based docking, the CXCR4 crystal structure (PDB: 3ODU) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank and processed using Prime version 3.8 (Schrödinger, LLC). The structure was validated for missing atoms, incorrect protonation states, and non-physiological rotamers. The histidine residues at positions 113, 281, and 284 were protonated at the δ-nitrogen, as required for hydrogen bonding with SDF-1α. The asparagine and glutamine side chains were optimized for hydrogen bonding geometry, and the B-factors were replaced with OPLS2005 charges. The ligand IT1t was removed, and a 12 Å³ grid box was generated centered on its centroid. For Glide docking, the XP (extra precision) mode was employed with a grid scale of 0.8 for van der Waals radii, and the maxkeep parameter was set to 5000 to retain the top 5000 poses per compound. For Surflex docking, the GeomX mode was selected to optimize pose prediction, and the protomol was generated using the default parameters with a 0.8 Å probe radius. The total score, a composite metric derived from −logKd, was used to rank compounds, with a threshold of 6.0 used to define hits.

The calcium imaging assay was performed using C8161 human melanoma cells maintained in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Cells were seeded in 96-well black-walled plates at a density of 15,000 cells per well and loaded with 5 μM fura-2 AM in HEPES-buffered saline (HBS) containing 1 mM CaCl₂ for 45 minutes at 37°C. After washing, cells were imaged using a FlexStation 3 plate reader (Molecular Devices) with excitation wavelengths of 340 nm and 380 nm and emission at 510 nm. SDF-1α was added at a concentration of 100 nM, and test compounds were added 5 minutes prior to the second stimulation. Responses were quantified as the ratio of fluorescence at 340/380 nm, and the peak amplitude of the second response was normalized to the first. Compounds that reduced the second response by more than 50% were classified as antagonists; those that elicited a response greater than 20% of the SDF-1α maximum in the absence of SDF-1α were classified as agonists.

Western blot analysis for ERK phosphorylation was performed by lysing C8161 cells in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay, and 20 μg of total protein was separated by SDS-PAGE on a 10% gel and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk in TBS-T and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against phospho-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) and total ERK1/2 (Cell Signaling Technology). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and developed using chemiluminescent substrate. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software, and the ratio of phospho-ERK to total ERK was calculated.

Receptor internalization was assessed in HEK293 cells stably expressing CXCR4-YFP. Cells were grown on glass-bottom dishes and treated with 1 μM SDF-1α, 1 μM NUCC-390, or 1 μM AMD3100 for 2 hours at 37°C. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, washed, and imaged using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 710) with a 63× oil objective. Fluorescence intensity in the plasma membrane versus cytosol was quantified using Fiji software, and internalization was defined as a >40% reduction in membrane fluorescence and a >30% increase in cytosolic puncta.

Chemotaxis assays were performed using 5 μm pore Transwell inserts (Corning). C8161 cells (1×10⁵) were suspended in serum-free medium and placed in the upper chamber. SDF-1α (100 nM) or NUCC-390 (1 μM) was placed in the lower chamber. After 4 hours at 37°C, migrated cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope. Each condition was performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as the mean number of migrated cells ± standard deviation.

Radioligand binding assays were performed using human Chem-1 cells expressing CXCR4. Cells were incubated with 0.1 nM 125I-SDF-1α in the presence of increasing concentrations of NUCC-390 (1 nM to 10 μM) for 2 hours at 4°C. Bound radioactivity was separated by filtration through GF/C filters and quantified using a gamma counter. Non-specific binding was determined in the presence of 10 μM unlabeled SDF-1α. Percent inhibition was calculated relative to control, and IC50 values were determined using nonlinear regression analysis.

All data were analyzed using IGOR Pro 6.12 (WaveMetrics, Inc.) for dose-response curve fitting and GraphPad Prism 9.0 for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was determined using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, with p < 0.05 considered significant. All experiments were performed in triplicate or greater, and results are reported as mean ± SEM.

### Annotated Database Creation

The annotated database used in the present invention was constructed from the ChemBridge GPCR-focused library, a proprietary collection of approximately 13,000 small molecules curated for relevance to G protein-coupled receptor targets. Each compound in the library was assigned a unique identifier, chemical structure, and physical property profile, including molecular weight, cLogP, hydrogen bond donor and acceptor counts, rotatable bond count, and polar surface area. The database was further annotated with information regarding synthetic accessibility, commercial availability, and structural novelty as determined by comparison with the PubChem and ChEMBL databases. Each compound was assigned a purity score based on analytical LC/MS data, with only those exhibiting >95% purity and a mass ion consistent with the expected molecular formula included in the screening set. The database was structured as a relational file system compatible with Discovery Studio 3.1, with each compound linked to its corresponding 3D conformer ensemble generated via the ConFirm/CatConf module. The conformer ensemble for each compound included 100 low-energy geometries, each with a calculated energy in kcal/mol relative to the global minimum, and each conformer was tagged with its torsional angles, dihedral distributions, and hydrogen bonding potential. The database was further annotated with a flag indicating whether the compound contained any structural alerts for toxicity, such as nitro groups, alkyl halides, Michael acceptors, or reactive esters, and compounds flagged as high-risk were excluded from subsequent analysis. The database was also annotated with a synthetic complexity score derived from the number of synthetic steps required to produce the compound, estimated using retrosynthetic analysis algorithms embedded in the Synthia software platform. Compounds with a complexity score exceeding 8 were deprioritized to ensure that hits could be rapidly synthesized and optimized. The annotated database thus served as a comprehensive, multi-dimensional resource for virtual screening, integrating structural, energetic, pharmacokinetic, and synthetic criteria into a single searchable framework. This level of annotation was critical to the success of the invention, as it allowed for the elimination of false positives and the prioritization of compounds with high translational potential.

### EXAMPLE 2

In this example, the pharmacophore model derived from the 162 CXCR4 antagonists was applied to a second, independent library of 5,000 compounds obtained from the Enamine REAL database. This library was selected for its high structural diversity and inclusion of novel, patentable scaffolds not present in the ChemBridge library. The same five-point pharmacophore model—comprising two hydrophobic features, two hydrogen bond acceptor features, and one positive ionizable feature—was used to screen this new library. The screening was performed using the same energy cutoff of 5 kcal/mol and a minimum fit value of 85%. This resulted in the identification of 18 new compounds with high pharmacophore fit. These compounds were purchased and tested in the calcium flux assay using C8161 cells. Three of these compounds—designated ENM-102, ENM-147, and ENM-211—demonstrated potent antagonism, with IC50 values of 0.7 μM, 1.2 μM, and 0.9 μM, respectively. Molecular docking of these compounds into the CXCR4 binding site revealed that all three formed a hydrogen bond with Glu288, consistent with the antagonist classification rule established in the invention. This example demonstrates the generalizability of the pharmacophore model and the predictive power of the Tyr255/Glu288 interaction rule beyond the original screening library, validating the invention as a platform technology for the discovery of novel CXCR4 modulators.

### EXAMPLE 3

In this example, the structure-based docking protocol was applied to a library of 2,000 compounds from the ZINC15 database, which contains commercially available, diverse, and drug-like molecules. The same CXCR4 crystal structure (PDB: 3ODU) and docking parameters (Glide XP, Surflex GeomX) were employed. The top 50 compounds from each algorithm were selected, and a consensus set of 15 compounds was identified. These compounds were purchased and tested in the calcium flux assay. Two compounds—ZINC-789 and ZINC-912—exhibited agonist activity, inducing calcium transients with EC50 values of 0.5 μM and 1.8 μM, respectively. Both compounds were found to form a hydrogen bond with Tyr255 in the docking pose, while avoiding interaction with Glu288. This example confirms that the structure-based docking protocol, when combined with the interaction rule, can identify novel agonists from external chemical libraries, further validating the invention’s predictive framework. Additionally, ZINC-789 was found to be more metabolically stable than NUCC-390 in human liver microsome assays, suggesting its potential as a lead for further optimization.

### EXAMPLE 4

In this example, the agonist NUCC-390 was tested in a panel of 12 other GPCRs, including CXCR7, CCR5, CCR2, S1PR1, LPA1, and β2-adrenergic receptor, to assess selectivity. Cells expressing each receptor were challenged with the appropriate endogenous ligand in the presence of 10 μM NUCC-390. Binding assays using radiolabeled ligands for each receptor confirmed no displacement at concentrations up to 10 μM. This example demonstrates that the invention provides not only novel modulators but also highly selective tools for probing CXCR4 biology without confounding off-target effects.

### EXAMPLE 5

In this example, NUCC-390 was tested in vivo in a murine model of ischemic myocardial infarction. Mice underwent permanent ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery and were administered NUCC-390 (1 mg/kg, intravenously) or vehicle control immediately after surgery. Fourteen days later, cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography. Mice treated with NUCC-390 exhibited significantly improved ejection fraction (48% vs. 32% in controls), reduced infarct size, and increased infiltration of CXCR4-expressing stem cells into the border zone of the infarct. Histological analysis confirmed increased capillary density and reduced fibrosis. These effects were abolished by co-administration of AMD3100, confirming CXCR4 dependence. This example demonstrates the therapeutic potential of the invention’s agonists in promoting tissue repair and regeneration.

### EXAMPLE 6

In this example, NUCC-388 was tested in a murine model of metastatic breast cancer. MDA-MB-231 cells expressing luciferase were injected intravenously into female nude mice. Treatment with NUCC-388 (5 mg/kg, intraperitoneally, daily) for 21 days resulted in an 85% reduction in lung metastases compared to vehicle controls, as quantified by bioluminescence imaging. Histological analysis confirmed a marked reduction in tumor burden in the lungs and spleen. No signs of toxicity or weight loss were observed. This example demonstrates the therapeutic potential of the invention’s antagonists in inhibiting cancer metastasis.

### EXAMPLE 7

In this example, a computational model was trained using machine learning to predict agonist or antagonist activity based on the hydrogen bonding pattern with Tyr255 and Glu288. A dataset of 50 known CXCR4 modulators, including the compounds of the invention, was encoded using molecular fingerprints and docking-derived interaction features. A random forest classifier was trained to predict functional outcome. The model achieved an accuracy of 96%, with a sensitivity of 95% for agonists and a specificity of 97% for antagonists. This model was then deployed to screen a virtual library of 10 million compounds, predicting 1,200 potential agonists and 850 potential antagonists. The top 20 of each class were synthesized and tested, and 18 of the 20 agonists and 19 of the 20 antagonists were confirmed experimentally. This example demonstrates that the invention’s interaction rule can be translated into a highly accurate predictive algorithm, enabling high-throughput discovery of novel CXCR4 modulators on a global scale.
