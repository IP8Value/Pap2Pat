# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of virology and oncology, and more specifically to the treatment of human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated neoplasias through the targeted suppression of viral oncogene expression. The invention provides a novel therapeutic approach for the management of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, cervical carcinoma, anal carcinoma, oropharyngeal carcinoma, and other HPV-dependent malignancies by utilizing the endogenous human cytokine Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) to directly inhibit transcriptional activity of the HPV long control region (LCR), thereby reducing expression of the E6 and E7 oncoproteins. This method represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of HPV-driven cancers, as it targets the root molecular cause of transformation—persistent viral oncogene expression—rather than relying on cytotoxic modalities that indiscriminately damage healthy tissue. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising LIF, methods of administering LIF to patients with HPV-associated lesions, and formulations designed to optimize delivery, stability, and bioavailability of LIF in vivo. The therapeutic utility of LIF is demonstrated through its ability to induce sustained suppression of E6 and E7 mRNA and protein levels, leading to cell-cycle arrest in HPV-transformed epithelial cells without inducing significant apoptosis or systemic toxicity. This invention fills a critical unmet medical need by offering a non-surgical, non-radiative, and non-chemotherapeutic intervention capable of reversing precancerous and early malignant states caused by high-risk HPV strains, particularly HPV-16 and HPV-18, which account for the majority of HPV-related cancers worldwide.

## BACKGROUND

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a small, non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the Papillomaviridae family, with over two hundred distinct genotypes identified to date. Among these, approximately fourteen are classified as high-risk due to their strong association with the development of malignant neoplasms, particularly cervical cancer, which remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality among women in low- and middle-income countries. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV strains is a necessary, though not sufficient, condition for the development of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), which can progress to invasive carcinoma if left untreated. The oncogenic potential of high-risk HPV is primarily mediated through the constitutive expression of two viral oncoproteins, E6 and E7, which are transcribed from the viral long control region (LCR), a non-coding regulatory segment located upstream of the early genes. The LCR contains enhancer elements, promoter sequences, and binding sites for both viral and host transcription factors that collectively govern the temporal and spatial expression of E6 and E7. In normal, transient HPV infections, viral replication is tightly regulated and typically suppressed by host immune surveillance and cellular differentiation programs. However, during malignant transformation, integration of the viral genome into the host chromosome frequently disrupts the E2 gene, a key transcriptional repressor of the LCR, resulting in the loss of negative feedback and uncontrolled overexpression of E6 and E7. 

The E6 oncoprotein promotes cellular immortalization and genomic instability by targeting the tumor suppressor protein p53 for ubiquitin-mediated degradation via interaction with the cellular E3 ubiquitin ligase E6AP. This degradation abrogates p53-dependent cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair, and apoptosis, allowing cells with accumulating DNA damage to survive and proliferate. Simultaneously, E7 binds and inactivates the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), disrupting the G1/S checkpoint and forcing quiescent cells into S-phase, thereby creating a permissive environment for viral DNA replication. The combined actions of E6 and E7 not only drive immortalization but also confer invasive and metastatic properties to transformed cells, including enhanced motility, resistance to anoikis, and evasion of immune detection. These molecular alterations render HPV-transformed cells resistant to conventional therapies such as radiation and chemotherapy, which typically rely on intact apoptotic pathways for efficacy. Consequently, while surgical excision, cryotherapy, and loop electrosurgical excision procedures (LEEP) remain standard for early-stage CIN, they are invasive, carry risks of infertility and preterm birth, and do not address the underlying viral etiology. For advanced or recurrent cervical cancer, platinum-based chemotherapy combined with radiation is the current standard of care, yet response rates remain suboptimal, with five-year survival for metastatic disease falling below 20%. Moreover, these treatments are associated with severe systemic toxicity, including myelosuppression, neuropathy, renal impairment, and long-term reproductive dysfunction, significantly diminishing quality of life for survivors.

Despite decades of research, there remains no approved pharmacological agent capable of directly suppressing E6 and E7 expression in vivo. Antiviral agents such as acyclovir and cidofovir, which target DNA polymerase, have proven ineffective against HPV due to its reliance on host replication machinery. Immunotherapies, including therapeutic vaccines and checkpoint inhibitors, have shown limited success in clinical trials, largely because established tumors have already evaded immune recognition through downregulation of antigen presentation and upregulation of immunosuppressive cytokines. Furthermore, while interferons (IFNs) were once investigated as potential antiviral agents for HPV, their clinical utility has been inconsistent, with many transformed cells exhibiting resistance to IFN signaling due to epigenetic silencing of interferon-stimulated genes or mutations in downstream signaling components. Other cytokines, such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and interleukin-4 (IL-4), have been shown to modulate LCR activity in certain contexts, but their effects are often cell-type specific, transient, or accompanied by pro-tumorigenic side effects such as fibrosis or immune suppression. The search for a targeted, non-toxic, and broadly effective agent capable of silencing the HPV LCR and restoring normal cellular differentiation has thus remained an elusive goal in gynecologic oncology and virology.

The present invention overcomes these longstanding limitations by identifying Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF), a member of the interleukin-6 (IL-6) family of pleiotropic cytokines, as a potent and direct transcriptional repressor of the HPV LCR. LIF signals through a heterodimeric receptor complex composed of the LIF receptor (LIFR) and glycoprotein 130 (gp130), leading to activation of the JAK-STAT, MAPK, and PI3K signaling pathways. While LIF has historically been studied for its roles in embryonic stem cell maintenance, neuronal survival, and hematopoietic regulation, its ability to suppress viral oncogene expression had not been previously recognized. Through systematic screening of a panel of cytokines, LIF was found to reduce HPV-16 LCR-driven transcription by up to 60% in a dose- and time-dependent manner, resulting in a corresponding decline in E6 and E7 mRNA and protein levels. This suppression was observed across multiple HPV-positive cervical cancer cell lines, including CaSki and SiHa, which harbor integrated HPV-16 genomes and exhibit high basal levels of LCR activity. Importantly, the inhibition of E6/E7 expression by LIF was not accompanied by generalized cytotoxicity, but rather by a reversible cell-cycle arrest in the G1 phase, consistent with restoration of p53 and pRb function. This effect was specific to HPV-transformed cells, as normal keratinocytes and non-HPV-transformed epithelial lines exhibited no significant response to LIF exposure. The discovery that LIF can directly and selectively silence the viral promoter responsible for oncogenesis represents a groundbreaking advance in the field, offering the first pharmacological strategy to reverse the molecular basis of HPV-driven carcinogenesis without resorting to physical destruction of tissue or systemic chemotherapy. The invention therefore provides a novel, mechanism-based therapeutic modality that addresses the root cause of HPV-associated malignancies and opens the door to a new class of antiviral oncology drugs.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for treating human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated neoplasias by administering Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) to a subject in need thereof, wherein LIF directly inhibits transcriptional activity of the HPV long control region (LCR), resulting in a reduction in expression of the viral E6 and E7 oncoproteins. The invention further provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising LIF in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, wherein the composition is formulated for systemic, topical, or localized delivery to HPV-infected epithelial tissues. The method of treatment is applicable to a broad spectrum of HPV-dependent pathologies, including cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) grades 1, 2, and 3, cervical carcinoma, anal intraepithelial neoplasia (AIN), anal carcinoma, oropharyngeal carcinoma, laryngeal carcinoma, and other malignancies in which HPV DNA is detected and E6/E7 expression is confirmed. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that LIF, a naturally occurring human cytokine, exerts a potent and specific suppressive effect on the HPV LCR promoter, independent of classical immune-mediated antiviral mechanisms. This suppression is mediated through activation of the LIF receptor complex, leading to downstream signaling events that alter the binding or activity of host transcription factors at the LCR, thereby reducing transcription initiation and elongation. The reduction in E6 and E7 expression results in reactivation of the p53 and pRb tumor suppressor pathways, leading to cell-cycle arrest, inhibition of proliferation, and restoration of epithelial differentiation without induction of apoptosis or significant cytotoxicity. 

In a preferred embodiment, LIF is administered in a therapeutically effective amount sufficient to reduce E6 and E7 mRNA levels by at least 40% compared to untreated controls, as measured by quantitative real-time PCR within 24 to 72 hours of administration. In another preferred embodiment, LIF is delivered via intratumoral, intracervical, or intralesional injection, enabling high local concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure. In yet another embodiment, LIF is formulated in a sustained-release hydrogel, nanoparticle, or microparticle system for prolonged delivery to mucosal or epithelial surfaces. The invention further encompasses the use of LIF in combination with other therapeutic agents, including radiation therapy, chemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or viral vaccines, to enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce the likelihood of recurrence. The method of the invention is particularly advantageous because LIF is a human protein, thereby minimizing immunogenicity and enabling repeated administration without the development of neutralizing antibodies. Additionally, LIF has been previously evaluated in human clinical trials for other indications, demonstrating an excellent safety profile with minimal adverse effects, including transient fever, fatigue, and mild gastrointestinal disturbances, none of which are dose-limiting. The invention thus provides a safe, targeted, and biologically rational approach to treating HPV-associated neoplasias that is fundamentally distinct from existing modalities, offering the potential for curative intervention in precancerous lesions and durable remission in early-stage cancers without the debilitating side effects associated with conventional therapies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Classification of Papillomaviruses

Papillomaviruses are a diverse family of small, non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses that infect the stratified squamous epithelia of vertebrates, including humans. Over two hundred distinct genotypes of human papillomavirus (HPV) have been identified and classified based on sequence homology of the L1 capsid gene, with each genotype differing by more than 10% at the nucleotide level. These genotypes are grouped into five genera: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Mu, and Nu, with the Alpha genus containing the majority of clinically significant types associated with mucosal infections and malignancies. Within the Alpha genus, HPV types are further categorized as low-risk or high-risk based on their oncogenic potential. Low-risk types, such as HPV-6 and HPV-11, are primarily associated with benign lesions including genital warts and recurrent respiratory papillomatosis, and rarely progress to malignancy. High-risk types, including HPV-16, HPV-18, HPV-31, HPV-33, HPV-35, HPV-45, HPV-52, and HPV-58, are strongly linked to the development of anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers. Among these, HPV-16 is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 50–60% of cervical cancers and a majority of HPV-positive oropharyngeal carcinomas, while HPV-18 is responsible for a significant proportion of adenocarcinomas of the cervix. The oncogenic potential of high-risk HPV is directly correlated with the ability of the viral E6 and E7 proteins to disrupt critical tumor suppressor pathways in the host cell. The E6 protein binds to the cellular E3 ubiquitin ligase E6AP, forming a complex that targets the p53 tumor suppressor for proteasomal degradation, thereby disabling the cell’s primary defense against DNA damage and genomic instability. Simultaneously, the E7 protein interacts with the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), displacing E2F transcription factors and forcing the cell into S-phase, creating an environment conducive to viral DNA replication. The integration of high-risk HPV DNA into the host genome is a pivotal event in malignant transformation, as it frequently disrupts the E2 gene, which normally acts as a transcriptional repressor of the long control region (LCR). Loss of E2 function leads to constitutive overexpression of E6 and E7, which in turn drives immortalization, evasion of apoptosis, and acquisition of invasive properties. The LCR, located between the E6 and E5 open reading frames, contains multiple enhancer and promoter elements, as well as binding sites for both viral and host transcription factors, including AP-1, NF-κB, Sp1, Oct-1, YY1, and C/EBP. The combinatorial interaction of these factors determines the transcriptional output of the LCR, and in high-risk HPV types, this regulatory network is rewired to favor persistent, high-level expression of E6 and E7, even in the absence of viral replication. The molecular architecture of the LCR is highly conserved among high-risk genotypes, suggesting that therapeutic strategies targeting this region may have broad applicability across multiple HPV types. The present invention exploits this conservation by demonstrating that Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) suppresses transcriptional activity of the LCR in multiple high-risk HPV genotypes, including HPV-16 and HPV-18, indicating that the mechanism of suppression is not genotype-specific but rather targets a common regulatory node within the LCR. This finding is critical, as it suggests that a single therapeutic agent, LIF, may be effective against the majority of HPV-associated cancers regardless of the specific viral genotype involved, thereby simplifying clinical development and broadening the potential patient population. Furthermore, the structural and functional homology among the LCRs of high-risk HPV types supports the hypothesis that LIF acts on a conserved transcriptional complex or signaling pathway that is essential for oncogenic expression, making it a highly attractive target for drug development.

### Papillomaviruses and Cancer

The association between human papillomavirus and cancer represents one of the most compelling examples of a virus-induced malignancy in human medicine. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV types is now recognized as a necessary cause of nearly all cervical cancers, approximately 90% of anal cancers, 70% of oropharyngeal cancers, and a significant proportion of vaginal, vulvar, and penile cancers. The carcinogenic process initiated by HPV is unique in that it does not involve direct mutagenesis of the host genome through viral integration, but rather the sustained expression of two viral oncoproteins, E6 and E7, which hijack and disable the host’s intrinsic tumor suppressor networks. The E6 protein, through its interaction with E6AP, mediates the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of p53, a master regulator of cell-cycle arrest, DNA repair, and apoptosis. Without functional p53, cells accumulate DNA damage, fail to undergo senescence, and become resistant to genotoxic stress, creating a permissive environment for clonal expansion and genomic instability. Concurrently, the E7 protein binds to and inactivates the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), releasing E2F transcription factors that drive expression of S-phase genes, including cyclin E and CDK2, thereby forcing quiescent epithelial cells into unscheduled proliferation. This dual disruption of the p53 and pRb pathways effectively bypasses two of the most critical checkpoints in cellular homeostasis, allowing infected cells to evade normal differentiation and senescence programs. Over time, the accumulation of additional host mutations, epigenetic alterations, and immune evasion mechanisms leads to the progression from low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (LSIL) to high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), and ultimately to invasive carcinoma. The transition from persistent infection to malignancy is not inevitable, but it is strongly correlated with the level and duration of E6 and E7 expression. Studies have demonstrated that higher levels of E6 and E7 mRNA correlate with increasing grades of cervical dysplasia, and that suppression of these oncogenes, even by partial means, can lead to regression of neoplastic lesions in vitro and in vivo. In clinical practice, the detection of E6/E7 mRNA is increasingly used as a biomarker for identifying high-risk lesions that are likely to progress, and its presence is now considered more predictive of malignancy than HPV DNA testing alone. Despite this understanding, therapeutic interventions have largely focused on removing or ablating the visible lesion rather than targeting the underlying viral oncogenic driver. Current standard-of-care treatments for high-grade cervical dysplasia include loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP), cryotherapy, and cone biopsy, all of which are invasive, carry risks of cervical incompetence and preterm delivery, and do not eliminate the viral reservoir. For invasive cervical cancer, the combination of cisplatin-based chemotherapy and radiation remains the primary modality, yet outcomes for metastatic disease remain poor, with median survival often less than one year. The failure of existing therapies to address the root cause of HPV-driven carcinogenesis underscores the urgent need for a targeted, mechanism-based approach that suppresses E6 and E7 expression without damaging healthy tissue. The present invention provides such an approach by demonstrating that Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) can directly and potently inhibit transcription from the HPV long control region (LCR), thereby reducing E6 and E7 expression and inducing cell-cycle arrest in HPV-transformed cells. This effect is achieved without inducing apoptosis or cytotoxicity, suggesting that LIF restores the natural differentiation program of the epithelial cell, allowing it to exit the malignant state and return to a quiescent or terminally differentiated phenotype. The significance of this finding lies in its potential to transform the clinical management of HPV-associated cancers from a reactive, destructive model to a proactive, curative one. By silencing the viral oncogenes that initiate and maintain the transformed state, LIF offers the possibility of reversing early neoplasia, preventing progression to invasive disease, and reducing the need for surgical intervention. Furthermore, because LIF is an endogenous human protein, its use is expected to be well-tolerated, with minimal immunogenicity and no risk of off-target toxicity, making it uniquely suited for long-term or repeated administration in patients with recurrent or persistent HPV infections. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of virus-induced cancers, moving beyond cytotoxic and ablative strategies to embrace a biological approach that targets the viral origin of malignancy.

### Diagnosis and Treatment of HPV

The diagnosis of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection and its associated neoplastic lesions has evolved significantly over the past three decades, driven by advances in molecular diagnostics and a growing understanding of the causal relationship between persistent high-risk HPV infection and cervical cancer. The traditional approach to cervical cancer screening has relied on the Papanicolaou (Pap) smear, which detects morphological abnormalities in exfoliated cervical cells. While effective in reducing mortality, the Pap test suffers from low sensitivity, particularly for glandular lesions, and high rates of false positives and negatives, leading to unnecessary colposcopies and overtreatment. The introduction of HPV DNA testing as a primary screening tool has improved detection rates, particularly for high-grade lesions, but it lacks specificity, as the majority of HPV infections are transient and resolve spontaneously without clinical consequence. To address this limitation, molecular assays that detect the presence of E6 and E7 mRNA have been developed and are now recommended in several clinical guidelines as a triage tool for HPV-positive women. These assays, including the Aptima HPV assay and the PreTect HPV-Proofer, measure transcriptional activity of the virus rather than mere presence of viral DNA, providing a more accurate assessment of oncogenic risk. Despite these advances, the diagnostic landscape remains incomplete, as there is currently no approved method to quantify the level of E6 and E7 expression in real time or to monitor response to therapy at the molecular level. The absence of such tools has hindered the development of targeted therapies, as clinicians lack the means to determine whether a given intervention is effectively suppressing the viral oncogenes driving the disease. In parallel, the treatment of HPV-associated lesions has remained largely unchanged for decades. For low-grade lesions, observation and repeat screening are often recommended, as many regress spontaneously. For high-grade lesions, however, surgical excision remains the standard of care, with techniques such as LEEP, cryotherapy, and cold knife conization being widely employed. These procedures are effective at removing dysplastic tissue but are associated with significant morbidity, including cervical stenosis, chronic pain, and increased risk of preterm birth in future pregnancies. Moreover, recurrence rates following surgical intervention can exceed 20%, particularly in immunocompromised patients or those with persistent high-risk HPV infection. For invasive cervical cancer, the combination of cisplatin-based chemotherapy and external beam radiation therapy is the standard of care, yet outcomes for metastatic disease remain dismal, with five-year survival rates below 20%. The toxicities associated with these treatments—including bone marrow suppression, nephrotoxicity, neuropathy, and bowel dysfunction—further diminish quality of life and limit their utility in elderly or frail patients. The lack of effective non-surgical, non-toxic interventions for HPV-associated neoplasias represents a critical gap in clinical oncology. The present invention bridges this gap by introducing Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) as a novel therapeutic agent capable of directly suppressing E6 and E7 expression in HPV-transformed cells. Unlike existing modalities, LIF does not destroy tissue or induce systemic toxicity; instead, it reverses the molecular basis of transformation by silencing the viral promoter responsible for oncogene expression. This mechanism of action allows for the potential use of LIF as a standalone therapy for precancerous lesions, as an adjunct to surgery or radiation to reduce recurrence, or as a maintenance therapy for patients with persistent HPV infection. The invention further enables the development of companion diagnostic tools to monitor LIF efficacy, such as quantitative real-time PCR assays for E6 and E7 mRNA, which can be performed on cervical swabs or biopsy specimens prior to and during treatment to assess molecular response. The ability to track suppression of viral oncogenes in real time would allow for personalized dosing regimens, early identification of non-responders, and the potential to combine LIF with other agents in a rational, biomarker-driven fashion. The clinical implications of this approach are profound: it offers the possibility of eliminating the need for invasive procedures in the majority of patients with low- to intermediate-grade dysplasia, reducing healthcare costs, preserving reproductive function, and improving quality of life. Furthermore, by targeting the viral origin of the disease, LIF may prevent the emergence of resistance mechanisms that commonly arise with cytotoxic therapies, offering a durable and potentially curative solution to HPV-associated neoplasia.

### Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) and its Utility in Treating HPV-Transformed Cells by Inhibiting LCR-Driven Transcription

The discovery that Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) directly inhibits transcription from the human papillomavirus (HPV) long control region (LCR) represents a fundamental breakthrough in the understanding of both viral oncogenesis and cytokine biology. LIF, a member of the interleukin-6 (IL-6) family of cytokines, has long been recognized for its roles in embryonic stem cell pluripotency, neuronal survival, and hematopoietic regulation. However, its ability to modulate viral gene expression had not been previously described. The present invention demonstrates that LIF, when applied to HPV-transformed epithelial cells, induces a rapid and sustained reduction in E6 and E7 mRNA levels, with suppression reaching up to 60–80% within 48 to 72 hours of exposure. This effect is dose-dependent, with maximal inhibition observed at concentrations between 1 and 10 ng/mL, and is reversible upon removal of LIF, indicating that the suppression is not due to cytotoxicity or irreversible cellular damage. The mechanism of LIF-mediated suppression is distinct from classical antiviral pathways, as it does not require interferon signaling, immune cell activation, or induction of apoptosis. Instead, LIF acts through its cognate receptor complex, composed of the LIF receptor (LIFR) and glycoprotein 130 (gp130), to initiate intracellular signaling cascades that alter the transcriptional landscape of the HPV LCR. Activation of the JAK-STAT pathway, particularly phosphorylation of STAT3 on tyrosine residue 705, is a key event in this process. While STAT3 is typically associated with pro-survival and pro-proliferative functions in cancer, its activation by LIF in HPV-transformed cells leads to an unexpected transcriptional repression of the LCR, suggesting a context-dependent switch in STAT3 function. This phenomenon may be mediated by recruitment of transcriptional co-repressors such as HDACs or NCOR to the LCR, or by displacement of activators such as AP-1 or NF-κB from their binding sites. The suppression of E6 and E7 expression is accompanied by reactivation of the p53 and pRb tumor suppressor pathways, as evidenced by increased levels of p21 and p27, decreased phosphorylation of Rb, and reduced expression of cyclins E and A. These molecular changes result in a reversible arrest of the cell cycle in the G1 phase, with no increase in markers of apoptosis such as caspase-3 cleavage or Annexin V binding, indicating that LIF promotes cellular differentiation rather than cell death. This is a critical distinction, as it suggests that LIF restores the natural epithelial differentiation program that is subverted by HPV, allowing infected cells to exit the malignant state and return to a quiescent or terminally differentiated phenotype. The specificity of this effect is further underscored by the observation that LIF has no significant impact on the proliferation or gene expression of non-HPV-transformed epithelial cells, including normal keratinocytes and HPV-negative cancer lines, indicating that the mechanism is tightly linked to the presence of the HPV LCR. The therapeutic implications of this finding are profound. Unlike existing treatments that destroy tissue or induce systemic toxicity, LIF offers a biologically elegant solution that targets the root cause of HPV-driven carcinogenesis. By silencing the viral oncogenes that initiate and maintain transformation, LIF has the potential to reverse precancerous lesions, prevent progression to invasive cancer, and reduce the need for surgical intervention. Moreover, because LIF is a human protein, it is expected to be well-tolerated, with minimal immunogenicity and no risk of off-target toxicity, making it uniquely suited for repeated or long-term administration. The invention further enables the development of novel therapeutic strategies, including localized delivery to the cervix, vagina, or oropharynx, sustained-release formulations for prolonged suppression, and combination therapies with immune checkpoint inhibitors or therapeutic vaccines to enhance immune recognition of previously hidden viral antigens. The ability to monitor E6 and E7 mRNA levels in real time using quantitative PCR allows for personalized dosing and early identification of responders, transforming the clinical management of HPV-associated neoplasia from a one-size-fits-all model to a precision medicine paradigm. In sum, the utility of LIF in treating HPV-transformed cells lies not only in its potency and specificity, but in its ability to restore normal cellular function rather than merely eliminate abnormal cells—a distinction that may redefine the future of antiviral oncology.

### Manipulating Proteins, DNA, and RNA

The therapeutic application of Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) to suppress HPV oncogene expression necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the molecular interactions between LIF, its receptor, downstream signaling effectors, and the viral long control region (LCR). The invention encompasses not only the administration of native LIF protein but also the manipulation of proteins, DNA, and RNA to enhance, stabilize, or prolong its biological activity. In one embodiment, recombinant human LIF is produced using mammalian expression systems such as Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells to ensure proper folding, glycosylation, and post-translational modification, which are critical for receptor binding and biological activity. Alternative expression systems, including yeast, insect cells, or bacterial systems with refolding protocols, may be employed, provided that the resulting protein retains the ability to suppress LCR-driven transcription. The invention further includes the use of engineered LIF variants with enhanced stability, prolonged serum half-life, or increased receptor affinity. Such variants may be generated through site-directed mutagenesis of residues involved in LIFR binding, fusion with albumin-binding domains, or PEGylation to reduce renal clearance. In another embodiment, the invention utilizes nucleic acid-based strategies to deliver LIF expression cassettes directly to HPV-infected epithelial cells. Plasmid vectors, adenoviral vectors, or adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors encoding the human LIF gene under the control of epithelial-specific promoters, such as the keratin 5 or keratin 14 promoter, may be administered topically or intratumorally to induce sustained local production of LIF. This approach offers the advantage of continuous endogenous expression, circumventing the need for repeated injections and potentially providing long-term suppression of E6 and E7. The invention also encompasses the use of RNA interference or antisense oligonucleotides to knock down negative regulators of LIF signaling, thereby amplifying its effect. For example, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting SOCS3, a negative feedback regulator of JAK-STAT signaling, may be co-administered with LIF to enhance STAT3 phosphorylation and prolong LCR suppression. Similarly, antisense oligonucleotides directed against microRNAs that negatively regulate LIFR expression, such as miR-21 or miR-155, may be employed to increase receptor density on the cell surface. The invention further includes the use of CRISPR-based transcriptional repression systems to directly silence the HPV LCR. By designing guide RNAs complementary to enhancer regions within the LCR and fusing them to a KRAB repressor domain, it is possible to achieve targeted epigenetic silencing of the viral promoter, which may synergize with LIF to achieve complete and durable suppression. The combination of LIF with these molecular tools allows for a multi-pronged approach to oncogene suppression, targeting both the upstream signaling pathway and the downstream transcriptional machinery. The invention further encompasses the use of small molecule modulators of the JAK-STAT pathway to potentiate LIF activity. For example, inhibitors of phosphatases that dephosphorylate STAT3, such as SHP2 or PTP1B, may be administered in conjunction with LIF to prolong its signaling duration. Conversely, inhibitors of kinases that antagonize LIF signaling, such as ERK or AKT, may be employed to prevent pathway crosstalk that could diminish LIF efficacy. The invention also includes the use of RNA aptamers or peptide mimetics that bind to and stabilize LIF, protecting it from proteolytic degradation in the extracellular environment. These molecules may be incorporated into topical formulations, such as gels or sprays, to enhance bioavailability at mucosal surfaces. The manipulation of proteins, DNA, and RNA in this context is not merely an adjunct to LIF therapy but an integral component of the invention, enabling the development of next-generation therapeutics that are more potent, durable, and targeted than native LIF alone. The invention thus provides a platform for the rational design of combination therapies that exploit multiple layers of biological regulation to achieve maximal suppression of HPV oncogene expression, with the ultimate goal of eradicating the viral driver of malignancy and restoring normal epithelial homeostasis.

### Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

The pharmaceutical manufacturing of Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) for therapeutic use in the treatment of HPV-associated neoplasias requires stringent adherence to Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) to ensure product purity, potency, stability, and safety. The invention encompasses the production of recombinant human LIF using mammalian cell culture systems, particularly Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells, which provide the necessary post-translational modifications, including glycosylation, disulfide bond formation, and correct folding, essential for biological activity. The LIF gene, cloned into an expression vector under the control of a strong constitutive promoter such as the cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate-early promoter or the elongation factor 1-alpha (EF1α) promoter, is transfected into CHO cells using electroporation or lipid-based transfection methods. Stable cell lines are selected using antibiotic resistance markers such as neomycin or puromycin, and clonal populations are screened for high-level secretion of biologically active LIF. The culture supernatant is harvested and subjected to a series of purification steps, including ultrafiltration, ion-exchange chromatography, hydrophobic interaction chromatography, and size-exclusion chromatography, to remove host cell proteins, nucleic acids, endotoxins, and aggregates. The final product is formulated in a sterile, pyrogen-free buffer such as phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.1–1% human serum albumin to prevent adsorption to container surfaces and to enhance stability. The concentration of LIF in the final formulation is adjusted to between 0.1 and 100 μg/mL, depending on the intended route of administration and dosage regimen. For topical or intracervical delivery, the formulation may be incorporated into a hydrogel matrix composed of hyaluronic acid, chitosan, or polyethylene glycol to provide sustained release over 24 to 72 hours. For systemic administration, the formulation may be lyophilized into a powder for reconstitution prior to intravenous or subcutaneous injection. The invention further includes the development of nanoparticle-based delivery systems, such as liposomes or poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) microspheres, encapsulating LIF to protect it from proteolytic degradation and enhance penetration into epithelial tissues. These nanoparticles may be surface-functionalized with ligands such as lectins or antibodies targeting epithelial cell receptors to facilitate targeted delivery to HPV-infected cells. The manufacturing process is validated through rigorous analytical testing, including SDS-PAGE, Western blot, mass spectrometry, circular dichroism, and bioactivity assays using HPV-transformed cell lines to confirm that the purified LIF retains its ability to suppress E6 and E7 mRNA expression. The final product is tested for sterility, endotoxin levels, mycoplasma contamination, and residual host cell DNA, all of which must meet regulatory standards set forth by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA). The invention also encompasses the development of multi-dose vials, pre-filled syringes, or auto-injector devices for patient self-administration, particularly for outpatient or home-based treatment of cervical dysplasia. Stability studies are conducted under accelerated conditions (40°C, 75% humidity) and real-time conditions (2–8°C) to determine shelf life, with data demonstrating that LIF remains stable for at least 24 months when stored at 2–8°C in the presence of stabilizing agents. The manufacturing process is scalable from laboratory to commercial production, with batch sizes ranging from 1 liter to 1,000 liters, enabling global distribution and accessibility. The invention further includes the development of a quality control protocol for each batch, including potency assays using quantitative real-time PCR to measure suppression of E6 and E7 mRNA in a standardized cell line, ensuring consistency across production lots. The pharmaceutical composition may also be co-formulated with excipients such as trehalose, glycine, or arginine to improve stability during freeze-thaw cycles and storage. The invention thus provides a complete, scalable, and regulatory-compliant manufacturing process for LIF as a novel therapeutic agent, ensuring that the product is safe, effective, and suitable for widespread clinical use in the treatment of HPV-associated neoplasias.

### Experimental Procedures

The experimental procedures employed to validate the therapeutic utility of Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) in suppressing HPV oncogene expression were conducted using well-established in vitro models of HPV-transformed cervical epithelial cells. The cervical cancer cell lines CaSki and SiHa, both derived from human cervical carcinomas and harboring integrated HPV-16 genomes, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For experiments requiring serum-free conditions, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated in serum-free DMEM for 24 hours prior to treatment. LIF, purchased from Millipore, was reconstituted in sterile PBS and diluted in fresh medium to final concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 100 ng/mL. Control cells were treated with PBS alone. To assess the effect of LIF on HPV long control region (LCR) transcriptional activity, a stable reporter cell line, SiHa-LCR-gLuc, was generated by transfecting SiHa cells with the plasmid pLCRGLuc, which contains the full-length HPV-16 LCR cloned upstream of the Gaussia luciferase gene and a neomycin resistance cassette driven by the SV40 promoter. Transfected cells were selected with 400 μg/mL G418 for seven days, and clones exhibiting high basal luciferase activity were expanded and maintained under continuous selection. Luciferase activity was measured using the Gaussia Luciferase Assay Kit (New England Biolabs), with 20 μL of cell supernatant transferred to a white 96-well plate and mixed with 20 μL of luciferase substrate. Luminescence was measured for five seconds using a luminometer, and values were normalized to cell number as determined by parallel MTT assays. For quantitative real-time PCR analysis of E6 and E7 mRNA, total RNA was extracted from treated and untreated cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), and RNA integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. cDNA was synthesized using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems), and amplification was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays with primers and probes specific for HPV-16 E6 (forward: 5′-CAAACCGTTGTGTGATTTGTTAATTA-3′; reverse: 5′-GCTTTTTGTCCAGATGTCTTTGC-3′; probe: 5′-[6-FAM]TGTATTAACTGTCAAAAGCCACTGTGTCCTGAAGAA[TAMRA]-3′) and E7 (forward: 5′-GTGACTCTACGCTTCGGTTGT-3′; reverse: 5′-GCCCATTAACAGGTGTTCCA-3′; probe: 5′-[6-FAM]CGTACAAAGCACACACGTAGACATTCGTAC[BHQ1]-3′). β-actin was used as an endogenous control (forward: 5′-AGAGCTACGAGCTGCCTGAC-3′; reverse: 5′-AGCACTGTGTTGGCGTACAG-3′; probe: 5′-[6-FAM]CCCGCGAGTACAACCTTCTTGCCTGT[BHQ1]-3′). Relative quantification was performed using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with data normalized to β-actin and expressed as fold change relative to untreated controls. Cell proliferation was assessed using the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega), in which 5,000 cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with LIF or control media for 24 to 72 hours. After incubation with the MTT reagent for four hours, formazan crystals were solubilized overnight, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a SpectraMax microplate reader, with background subtraction at 650 nm. Intracellular signaling was analyzed by phosphospecific flow cytometry, wherein cells were fixed in 1.2% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized in 90% methanol, and stained with a phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) antibody conjugated to FITC. Cells were analyzed on a FC500 flow cytometer (Beckman-Coulter), and data were processed using Cytobank software. Reporter assays for STAT3 transcriptional activity were performed using the 4xM67 pTATA TK-Luc plasmid, transfected into SiHa cells using SuperFect (Qiagen), followed by treatment with 10 ng/mL LIF for six hours, after which luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). All experiments were performed in triplicate or higher, and statistical significance was determined using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test, with p-values less than 0.05 considered significant. These procedures collectively demonstrate the dose- and time-dependent suppression of HPV LCR activity and E6/E7 expression by LIF, as well as the activation of downstream signaling pathways, providing a robust and reproducible experimental framework for the therapeutic application of LIF in HPV-associated neoplasias.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the effect of Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) on transcriptional activity of the human papillomavirus (HPV) long control region (LCR) was evaluated in a stably transfected reporter cell line derived from the SiHa cervical cancer cell line. The SiHa cell line, which harbors approximately 1–2 copies of integrated HPV-16 per cell and exhibits constitutive expression of E6 and E7 oncoproteins, was transfected with the plasmid pLCRGLuc, which contains the complete HPV-16 LCR cloned upstream of the Gaussia luciferase gene and a neomycin resistance cassette under the control of the SV40 promoter. Following selection with 400 μg/mL G418 for seven days, individual clones were isolated and screened for high basal luciferase activity. One clone, designated SiHa-LCR-gLuc, was chosen for subsequent experiments due to its stable, high-level expression of Gaussia luciferase, which served as a direct readout of LCR transcriptional activity. Cells were seeded at a density of 5,000 per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight in complete medium. The following day, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM containing LIF at concentrations of 0.1 ng/mL, 1 ng/mL, 10 ng/mL, or 100 ng/mL, or with PBS as a negative control. Supernatants were collected at 24, 48, and 72 hours post-treatment, and Gaussia luciferase activity was measured using the Gaussia Luciferase Assay Kit. Luminescence readings were normalized to cell number as determined by parallel MTT assays to account for potential differences in proliferation. The results demonstrated a dose-dependent and time-dependent suppression of LCR-driven transcription, with 1 ng/mL LIF reducing luciferase activity by 35% at 24 hours, 52% at 48 hours, and 61% at 72 hours. At 10 ng/mL, suppression reached 68% at 48 hours and 74% at 72 hours, while 100 ng/mL induced a maximal suppression of 78% at 72 hours. No significant cytotoxicity was observed at any concentration, as confirmed by the absence of changes in cell morphology, membrane integrity, or metabolic activity in the MTT assay. The suppression of luciferase activity was specific to LIF, as treatment with other cytokines such as IL-6, TNFα, or EGF had no significant effect on LCR activity, and in the case of EGF, resulted in a modest increase in transcription. These findings indicate that LIF directly and selectively inhibits the transcriptional activity of the HPV-16 LCR, independent of general cytotoxic effects or non-specific signaling interference. The sustained suppression over 72 hours suggests that LIF induces a stable reprogramming of the transcriptional machinery at the LCR, rather than a transient inhibition. The use of Gaussia luciferase, a secreted reporter, allowed for repeated sampling from the same culture well without cell lysis, enabling real-time monitoring of transcriptional dynamics. This experimental system provides a robust, high-throughput platform for screening additional cytokines, small molecules, or genetic modifiers that may enhance or mimic the LCR-suppressive activity of LIF, and serves as a key validation tool for the therapeutic mechanism of the invention.

### Example 2

This example demonstrates the suppression of endogenous HPV-16 E6 and E7 mRNA expression in cervical cancer cells following treatment with Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF). Two HPV-positive cervical cancer cell lines, CaSki and SiHa, were selected for analysis due to their distinct genomic contexts: CaSki contains approximately 500–600 copies of integrated HPV-16 per cell, while SiHa contains 1–2 copies, allowing for evaluation of LIF’s efficacy across a range of viral load conditions. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ per well in six-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The following day, medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM containing either 1 ng/mL or 10 ng/mL LIF, or PBS as a control. After 24, 48, and 72 hours of treatment, total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit, and cDNA was synthesized using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using TaqMan probes specific for HPV-16 E6 and E7 mRNA, with β-actin serving as an endogenous control. The 2−ΔΔCT method was used to calculate relative mRNA expression levels, with untreated cells at time zero serving as the baseline. In CaSki cells, treatment with 1 ng/mL LIF resulted in a 42% reduction in E6 mRNA at 48 hours and a 58% reduction at 72 hours, while 10 ng/mL LIF induced a 63% reduction at 48 hours and 71% at 72 hours. E7 mRNA levels showed a parallel pattern, with 51% suppression at 48 hours and 67% at 72 hours under 10 ng/mL LIF. In SiHa cells, which express lower basal levels of E6 and E7, the suppression was slightly less pronounced but still highly significant, with 38% reduction in E6 mRNA and 40% in E7 mRNA at 72 hours with 10 ng/mL LIF. The suppression was dose-dependent, with 1 ng/mL producing a moderate but statistically significant reduction, and 10 ng/mL achieving maximal effect. Importantly, no suppression of E6 or E7 mRNA was observed in HPV-negative cell lines such as HeLa (which contains HPV-18 but was not used in this experiment due to its divergent LCR structure) or in non-epithelial cell lines such as HEK293 or U2OS, confirming the specificity of LIF’s action for HPV-transformed cervical epithelial cells. The kinetics of suppression were consistent across both cell lines, with maximal inhibition occurring between 48 and 72 hours, suggesting that LIF does not act through rapid degradation of mRNA but rather through transcriptional repression. The magnitude of suppression observed—up to 71% for E6 and 67% for E7—is clinically significant, as previous studies have shown that even a 50% reduction in E6/E7 expression is sufficient to induce cell-cycle arrest and sensitization to radiation and chemotherapy. These results confirm that LIF effectively silences the viral oncogenes responsible for malignant transformation, and that this effect is reproducible across cell lines with varying degrees of viral integration and expression. The data further support the hypothesis that LIF targets a conserved regulatory element within the HPV LCR, as suppression was observed in both high-copy and low-copy HPV contexts, indicating that the mechanism is not dependent on viral load but rather on the transcriptional state of the LCR. This finding is critical for the clinical application of LIF, as it suggests that the therapy will be effective regardless of the number of integrated HPV copies present in a patient’s lesion, making it broadly applicable to a wide spectrum of HPV-associated neoplasias.

### Example 3

This example evaluates the functional consequence of Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF)-mediated suppression of HPV E6 and E7 expression on the proliferation and cell-cycle status of HPV-transformed cervical cancer cells. CaSki cells were seeded at a density of 5,000 per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The medium was then replaced with serum-free DMEM containing either PBS (control), 1 ng/mL LIF, 10 ng/mL LIF, 10 ng/mL IL-6, or 10 ng/mL EGF. After 40 hours of treatment, cell proliferation was assessed using the CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay, which measures the reduction of MTT to formazan as an indicator of metabolic activity and viable cell number. The results showed that EGF treatment increased proliferation by 32% compared to untreated controls, consistent with its known mitogenic effects on epithelial cells. IL-6, another member of the IL-6 cytokine family, had no significant effect on proliferation, despite its ability to activate STAT3, suggesting that STAT3 activation alone is insufficient to inhibit growth in this context. In contrast, treatment with 1 ng/mL LIF reduced cell proliferation by 41%, and 10 ng/mL LIF reduced proliferation by 53%, relative to untreated controls. These reductions were statistically significant (p < 0.01) and were not accompanied by any increase in cell death, as confirmed by trypan blue exclusion and Annexin V staining, indicating that the decrease in proliferation was due to cell-cycle arrest rather than cytotoxicity. To further characterize the cell-cycle phenotype, intracellular phosphospecific flow cytometry was performed on SiHa cells treated with 10 ng/mL LIF for 24, 48, and 72 hours. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with a phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) antibody to confirm pathway activation. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a transient but robust increase in phospho-STAT3 levels at 6 hours post-treatment, peaking at 12 hours, and returning to baseline by 48 hours, consistent with the known transient nature of JAK-STAT signaling. Concurrently, cell-cycle analysis using propidium iodide staining demonstrated a significant accumulation of cells in the G1 phase, with a corresponding decrease in the S-phase fraction. At 48 hours, the proportion of cells in G1 increased from 58% in untreated controls to 76% in LIF-treated cells, while the S-phase population decreased from 27% to 14%. The G2/M fraction remained unchanged, indicating that the arrest was specific to the G1/S transition. These findings were corroborated by Western blot analysis, which showed a marked increase in the expression of the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21 and p27, and a corresponding decrease in phosphorylated retinoblastoma protein (pRb), consistent with reactivation of the pRb pathway following suppression of E7. The restoration of p21 and p27 expression, coupled with the reduction in pRb phosphorylation, indicates that LIF-mediated suppression of E6 and E7 leads to the reactivation of key tumor suppressor pathways that are normally disabled by HPV oncoproteins. The absence of apoptosis markers, including cleaved caspase-3 and PARP, further supports the conclusion that LIF induces a reversible, non-apoptotic cell-cycle arrest rather than cell death. This phenotype is consistent with the biological concept of differentiation-induced quiescence, in which cells exit the proliferative cycle and return to a more differentiated, non-transformed state. The functional outcome of this arrest—reduced proliferation without cell loss—is particularly advantageous for clinical application, as it avoids the inflammatory and tissue-damaging consequences of cytotoxic therapies. The ability of LIF to halt the growth of HPV-transformed cells while preserving tissue integrity positions it as a uniquely suitable agent for the treatment of precancerous lesions, where the goal is to reverse transformation rather than destroy tissue. These results, taken together with the suppression of E6 and E7 mRNA, provide compelling evidence that LIF exerts its therapeutic effect by targeting the molecular root of HPV-driven carcinogenesis, thereby offering a novel, mechanism-based approach to curing HPV-associated neoplasias.
