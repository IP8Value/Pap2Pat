## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to high-throughput biological imaging systems and, more specifically, to an integrated, parallelized microscopy platform designed for simultaneous, high-resolution, aberration-corrected imaging of all wells in a standard 96-well microplate format. The invention combines a multi-channel optical architecture with computational imaging techniques to overcome the fundamental limitations of conventional well plate imaging systems, which are constrained by sequential scanning, limited depth of field, and sensitivity to mechanical and optical variability across wells. The system employs an array of 96 compact, custom-designed microscope objectives, each aligned with a corresponding well, and paired with a dedicated CMOS image sensor, forming a fully parallelized imaging array. The system further incorporates a shared, programmable LED illumination matrix and a computational framework based on Fourier ptychographic microscopy to reconstruct high-resolution, aberration-corrected, phase-sensitive images from low-resolution, incoherent intensity measurements. The invention is particularly suited for applications in pharmaceutical screening, cellular biology, toxicology studies, and high-content analysis, where rapid, quantitative, and label-free imaging of cell morphology, viability, and protein localization is required across hundreds to thousands of experimental conditions. The system enables throughput rates that exceed those of conventional scanning microscopes by more than an order of magnitude, while simultaneously delivering image quality and analytical depth previously attainable only through single-well, high-end microscopy systems.

## BACKGROUND

High-throughput screening in biological research has long relied on multi-well plate formats, particularly the 96-well and 384-well configurations, to enable parallel experimentation across large numbers of samples. Traditional plate readers, which measure bulk optical signals such as fluorescence intensity or absorbance, offer exceptional speed and simplicity but provide only aggregate, population-level data that obscures critical heterogeneity at the single-cell level. To address this limitation, automated microscope-based imaging systems have been developed to capture spatially resolved images of cell cultures within each well. However, these systems typically employ a single microscope objective that scans across the plate in a sequential manner, resulting in imaging times that are orders of magnitude slower than those of non-imaging plate readers. For example, a state-of-the-art commercial well plate imager operating at a resolution of 1.2 micrometers requires approximately eight minutes to scan an entire 96-well plate, whereas a standard plate reader completes the same task in less than ten seconds. This disparity severely limits the utility of imaging systems in time-sensitive applications such as kinetic assays, drug response profiling, and live-cell monitoring.

Attempts to accelerate imaging through parallelization have been hindered by a series of formidable engineering challenges. First, the physical layout of a 96-well plate imposes extreme spatial constraints on the optical components, requiring each objective to be miniaturized to a diameter of no more than six millimeters. Designing a high-performance objective under such constraints is inherently difficult, as it necessitates a trade-off between numerical aperture, magnification, working distance, and aberration control. Second, even if such objectives could be fabricated with perfect uniformity, the manufacturing tolerances of plastic-molded lenses—necessary to achieve cost-effective mass production—introduce significant and unpredictable variations in aberration profiles across individual channels. Third, the mechanical geometry of commercial 96-well plates is not designed for optical precision; the bottom surfaces of the wells exhibit substantial warping, curvature, and tilt, with depth variations exceeding 100 micrometers across the plate. These variations place many wells outside the depth of field of conventional objectives, which typically ranges between 5 and 15 micrometers at moderate numerical apertures. Fourth, the presence of a liquid medium—typically 100 to 200 microliters per well—introduces a meniscus that distorts the path of incident illumination, altering the effective angle of incidence and introducing parallax errors that corrupt the spatial frequency data required for computational imaging. Fifth, fluorescence imaging, which is essential for tracking molecular markers such as GFP-tagged proteins, is further complicated by the low signal-to-noise ratio of consumer-grade sensors, the presence of electronic noise from power-line interference, and the necessity of z-stacking to compensate for depth variations, all of which drastically increase acquisition time and computational burden.

Existing solutions to these problems have been piecemeal and inadequate. Some systems attempt to correct for depth variations through mechanical z-scanning of the plate or objective, but this approach is inherently sequential and negates the throughput advantages of parallelization. Others employ adaptive optics or liquid lenses to dynamically adjust focus per well, but these components are prohibitively expensive, bulky, and incompatible with the dense packing required for 96-channel systems. Still others rely on post-processing algorithms to deconvolve blurred images, but these methods require precise knowledge of the point spread function, which is unavailable when aberrations vary unpredictably across channels. Furthermore, none of the prior art systems have successfully integrated the principles of Fourier ptychographic microscopy into a parallelized, multi-well platform capable of simultaneously correcting for defocus, lens aberrations, illumination distortion, and sensor noise in a unified computational framework. As a result, there remains a critical unmet need for a high-throughput imaging system that can deliver single-cell resolution, aberration-free phase contrast, and fluorescence imaging across an entire 96-well plate in under two minutes, without requiring mechanical refocusing, expensive optics, or sample-specific calibration.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, integrated, and fully parallelized imaging system for high-throughput, multi-modal analysis of cell cultures grown in standard 96-well microplates. The system comprises an array of 96 identical, compact, custom-designed microscope objectives, each optically aligned with a corresponding well of the microplate, and paired with a dedicated CMOS image sensor, forming a 96-channel imaging array. These 96 imaging channels are arranged in a 9 mm by 9 mm footprint on a single sensor board, enabling simultaneous acquisition of images from all wells. The system further includes a shared, programmable LED illumination matrix positioned above the plate, with LED elements arranged in a grid pattern that allows each LED to illuminate multiple wells simultaneously while ensuring that each well receives illumination from only one LED at any given time. This parallel illumination scheme minimizes camera idle time and maximizes data throughput. The illumination is configured to operate at a wavelength of approximately 530 nanometers, compatible with both brightfield Fourier ptychographic imaging and green fluorescent protein excitation.

The system employs Fourier ptychographic microscopy to reconstruct high-resolution, aberration-corrected images from a sequence of low-resolution intensity images captured under varying illumination angles. Unlike conventional microscopy, where image quality is determined by the physical limitations of the objective, the resolution and fidelity of the final image in this system are determined computationally by fusing the spatial frequency content of multiple low-resolution images. This enables the system to overcome the native resolution limits of the miniaturized objectives, achieving a lateral resolution of approximately 1.2 micrometers, which is comparable to that of a 20× objective in a conventional microscope. Furthermore, the Fourier ptychographic algorithm is capable of estimating and correcting for a wide range of optical aberrations, including defocus, astigmatism, spherical aberration, and field curvature, by modeling the pupil function of each imaging channel in situ. This computational correction eliminates the need for mechanical refocusing and allows the system to tolerate well depth variations of up to ±50 micrometers without loss of image quality.

The system further incorporates a ray-tracing algorithm that compensates for the optical distortion introduced by the meniscus formed by the liquid medium in each well. By modeling the refraction of light at the air-liquid interface using Snell’s law and accounting for the curved surface profile of the meniscus, the system accurately determines the true angle of incidence of each illumination source on the sample plane, thereby ensuring the fidelity of the ptychographic reconstruction. The system also includes a dual-mode imaging capability, enabling simultaneous acquisition of phase-contrast images via Fourier ptychography and fluorescence images via a dedicated emission filter and excitation source. Because both imaging modalities share the same optical path, the resulting images are inherently co-registered, eliminating the need for computational alignment.

The system is further enhanced by a GPU-accelerated computational pipeline that processes the raw intensity data from all 96 channels in parallel, leveraging the redundancy of identical illumination sequences across channels to achieve real-time reconstruction of high-resolution phase and intensity images. The reconstruction algorithm partitions each well’s image into overlapping segments, groups corresponding segments across all wells, and processes them simultaneously on a multi-GPU array, achieving a throughput of over two million pixels per second. The system further employs a two-stage digital averaging technique to suppress static pattern noise and enhance signal-to-noise ratio in fluorescence images, enabling high-quality imaging even with consumer-grade CMOS sensors.

The invention provides a significant advancement over prior art by enabling the simultaneous acquisition of high-resolution, aberration-corrected, multi-modal images from all 96 wells of a microplate in under 90 seconds for Fourier ptychographic imaging and under 30 seconds for fluorescence imaging, while tolerating the natural variability of commercial 96-well plates without requiring mechanical adjustment, sample-specific calibration, or expensive adaptive optics. The system is fully automated, requires no manual focusing, and delivers quantitative, label-free phase images that reveal cellular morphology, as well as fluorescent signals that reveal molecular localization, all in a single, seamless workflow. The invention thus bridges the gap between the speed of traditional plate readers and the analytical depth of high-end microscopy, enabling unprecedented scale and precision in high-throughput biological screening.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. Introduction

The present invention represents a paradigm shift in the field of high-throughput biological imaging by integrating parallel optical sensing with computational image reconstruction to overcome the longstanding limitations of conventional well plate imaging systems. Prior art systems have been constrained by a fundamental trade-off: either they operate at high speed with minimal analytical depth, as in the case of plate readers, or they offer high analytical depth at the cost of prohibitively slow throughput, as in the case of scanning microscopes. The invention resolves this dichotomy by enabling simultaneous, high-resolution, multi-modal imaging across all wells of a 96-well plate in a time frame that is competitive with non-imaging plate readers. This is achieved not through incremental improvements in mechanical scanning or sensor sensitivity, but through a radical rethinking of the imaging architecture and the role of computation in image formation.

The core insight underlying the invention is that the optical imperfections inherent in mass-produced, miniaturized imaging components—such as lens aberrations, manufacturing tolerances, and mechanical misalignments—are not insurmountable obstacles, but rather sources of information that can be computationally characterized and corrected in real time. This is made possible by the Fourier ptychographic technique, which does not rely on the physical quality of the optics to determine image fidelity, but instead uses the redundancy of multiple illumination angles to reconstruct the object’s complex transmission function. By treating each imaging channel as an independent but identically illuminated sensor, the system leverages the statistical uniformity of the illumination pattern to accelerate reconstruction and to enable robust correction of channel-specific aberrations without requiring individual calibration.

The system is designed to operate with standard, commercially available 96-well microplates, including those made from polystyrene and cyclic olefin copolymer, without modification or pre-treatment. It does not require specialized plates with flat bottoms, uniform thickness, or optical-grade materials. Instead, it adapts to the inherent variability of these plates through computational means, making it compatible with the vast majority of existing laboratory workflows. The system’s ability to tolerate well depth variations of up to ±50 micrometers, lens aberration variations of up to 0.88 radians in astigmatism, and illumination angle distortions caused by meniscus curvature represents a level of robustness previously unattainable in any parallel imaging system.

Furthermore, the invention introduces a novel approach to fluorescence imaging in a parallelized context. Rather than treating fluorescence as a separate, post-acquisition modality, the system integrates it into the same optical path as the Fourier ptychographic imaging, ensuring perfect spatial registration between phase and fluorescence channels. This eliminates the need for costly and error-prone image registration algorithms and enables direct correlation between cellular morphology and molecular expression. The system also employs a two-stage digital averaging technique to enhance signal-to-noise ratio in fluorescence images, compensating for the low dynamic range of consumer-grade CMOS sensors and suppressing power-line interference without requiring cooled sensors or complex hardware modifications.

The invention is not merely a collection of hardware components, but a fully integrated system that includes a custom-designed optical architecture, a precisely engineered illumination geometry, a parallelized computational pipeline, and a suite of algorithms for aberration correction, meniscus compensation, and image reconstruction. The combination of these elements results in a system that is not only faster and more accurate than any prior art system, but also more reliable, more scalable, and more accessible to laboratories that lack the resources for high-end microscopy infrastructure. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in high-throughput biological imaging, enabling researchers to perform single-cell analysis at the scale of population-level screening, thereby accelerating discovery in drug development, cell biology, and personalized medicine.

### II. Imaging System for Fourier Ptychographic (FP) Imaging and Fluorescent Imaging

The imaging system of the present invention is a fully integrated, parallelized platform designed for simultaneous Fourier ptychographic and fluorescent imaging of all 96 wells in a standard microplate. The system is composed of three primary subsystems: the optical imaging array, the illumination system, and the computational acquisition and processing unit. Each of these subsystems is engineered to operate in concert, enabling high-speed, high-fidelity, multi-modal imaging under conditions of significant optical and mechanical variability.

The optical imaging array consists of 96 identical, compact microscope objectives, each mounted in a fixed position directly above a corresponding well of the 96-well plate. Each objective is custom-designed to provide a 4× magnification, a working distance of 4 millimeters, and a numerical aperture of 0.23, which corresponds to a lateral resolution of approximately 1.4 micrometers under conventional imaging conditions. The objectives are fabricated using plastic injection molding to enable cost-effective mass production, and each is assembled into a finite conjugate optical configuration with a fixed object-to-image distance of 48 millimeters. This configuration ensures mechanical stability and eliminates the need for adjustable focus elements, which would be impractical in a 96-channel array. Each objective is paired with a dedicated consumer-grade CMOS image sensor, chosen for its small footprint, low cost, and compatibility with high-density packaging. The 96 sensor-objective pairs are mounted on a single, rigid sensor board fabricated using printed circuit board technology, with each sensor aligned to within 1 micrometer of its corresponding objective’s optical axis. The entire array occupies a footprint of 9 millimeters by 9 millimeters, allowing it to be positioned directly beneath the 96-well plate without requiring any mechanical translation or scanning.

The illumination system comprises a large-area, programmable LED matrix positioned above the microplate at a vertical distance of 33 millimeters from the sample plane. The LED elements are arranged in a rectangular grid with a spacing of 3 millimeters, which corresponds to one-quarter of the center-to-center distance between wells in a standard 96-well plate. This geometric arrangement ensures that each LED illuminates exactly nine wells simultaneously, while each well receives illumination from only one LED at any given time. This is achieved by activating only a subset of LEDs during each exposure, such that the angular spread of light incident on each well falls entirely within the numerical aperture of its corresponding objective. The illumination wavelength is centered at approximately 530 nanometers, chosen to match the excitation peak of green fluorescent protein and the spectral sensitivity of the CMOS sensors. The LED matrix is driven by a programmable current controller that allows for precise temporal sequencing of illumination patterns, enabling the acquisition of a sequence of 72 intensity images per well under varying illumination angles. To prevent stray light from adjacent imaging channels from contaminating the image data, a wafer-shaped lens hood array surrounds each objective-sensor pair, forming a light-tight channel that isolates the optical path of each well from its neighbors.

The system further includes a fluorescence imaging module that operates concurrently with the Fourier ptychographic imaging. A pair of liquid-guided excitation sources, each emitting light at 465 nanometers, project homogenized illumination onto the microplate from opposing sides. The excitation light is filtered to a bandwidth of ±2.5 nanometers and delivered at a power of 4000 milliwatts to ensure sufficient signal generation. The emitted fluorescence, centered at 535 nanometers with a bandwidth of 50 nanometers, is collected through the same objective-sensor path as the brightfield illumination, but is separated from the excitation light by a dichroic mirror and an emission filter. Because the fluorescence and Fourier ptychographic channels share the same optical path, the resulting images are inherently co-registered, with no need for computational alignment. The system further includes a piezoelectric z-axis stage that holds the microplate in place and allows for precise axial translation of the plate relative to the imaging array. This stage is used to acquire a z-stack of fluorescence images over a range of 100 micrometers, with a step size of 25 micrometers, to ensure that the sharpest image is captured for each well. The z-stack data is then processed to select the in-focus frame for each well, which is combined with the phase image to produce a composite, multi-modal output.

The entire system is enclosed in a light-tight housing with a front-loading mechanism for rapid insertion and removal of microplates. The housing includes environmental controls to maintain a stable temperature and humidity, and is designed to be compatible with standard biosafety cabinets and automated liquid handling systems. The system is controlled by a workstation equipped with four frame grabber boards, each interfacing with 24 of the CMOS sensors, and a high-performance GPU array consisting of four Nvidia Tesla K80 processors. The system operates autonomously, requiring no manual intervention during image acquisition or processing, and is capable of imaging a complete 96-well plate in under 90 seconds for Fourier ptychographic imaging and under 30 seconds for fluorescence imaging.

### III. Variable-Illumination Fourier Ptychographic Imaging Methods

The variable-illumination Fourier ptychographic imaging methods employed by the present invention constitute a novel computational framework for reconstructing high-resolution, aberration-corrected images from a sequence of low-resolution, incoherent intensity measurements acquired under spatially varying illumination angles. Unlike conventional microscopy, which relies on the physical resolution of the objective lens to determine image fidelity, the Fourier ptychographic method derives resolution from the fusion of spatial frequency content across multiple illumination states. This enables the system to overcome the inherent limitations of the miniaturized, plastic-molded objectives used in the 96-channel array, achieving a lateral resolution of approximately 1.2 micrometers—comparable to that of a 20× objective in a conventional microscope—despite the fact that the native resolution of each objective is limited to approximately 1.6 micrometers.

The imaging process begins with the acquisition of a sequence of 72 intensity images per well, each captured under a unique illumination angle generated by activating a specific subset of LEDs in the illumination matrix. The illumination angles are chosen to span a circular region in the back focal plane of the objective, with a radius equal to the sum of the objective’s numerical aperture and the maximum illumination numerical aperture. This ensures that the spatial frequency content of the object is fully sampled up to a cutoff frequency that is twice the native cutoff of the objective. The illumination sequence is designed to minimize redundancy while maximizing coverage, with each LED activated only once per well during the full acquisition cycle. Due to the shared illumination geometry, the same sequence of illumination angles is applied simultaneously to all 96 wells, enabling massive parallelization of the data acquisition process.

Once the raw intensity images are captured, they are transferred to a GPU-accelerated reconstruction engine that implements a modified version of the Fourier ptychographic phase retrieval algorithm. The algorithm begins by initializing a complex-valued estimate of the object’s transmission function and the pupil function of each imaging channel. The pupil function, which encodes the aberrations of the objective and the misalignment of the optical components, is estimated simultaneously with the object function using an iterative optimization procedure that minimizes the difference between the measured intensity images and the simulated intensity projections derived from the current estimates. The algorithm employs a gradient descent approach with adaptive step sizes to accelerate convergence and avoid local minima, particularly in the presence of strong aberrations or defocus.

A critical innovation of the present invention is the incorporation of a two-stage aberration correction protocol. In the first stage, the algorithm performs a blind estimation of the defocus component of the pupil function by minimizing the reconstruction residual across a range of axial shifts, using a linear search algorithm to identify the depth that yields the lowest error. This step is essential for correcting the out-of-focus aberrations introduced by the natural warping of the microplate, which can exceed ±50 micrometers in depth variation. In the second stage, the algorithm refines the pupil function by fitting the residual aberrations to a set of Zernike polynomials up to the 15th order, allowing for the correction of astigmatism, spherical aberration, coma, and field curvature. The algorithm is further enhanced by a local aberration recovery module that estimates the pupil function at multiple positions within the field of view, accounting for the spatial variation of aberrations across the image plane. This is particularly important in the 96 Eyes system, where the finite conjugate configuration and the plastic-molded lenses introduce significant off-axis aberrations that vary from channel to channel.

The system further incorporates a ray-tracing algorithm to compensate for the optical distortion introduced by the meniscus formed by the liquid medium in each well. The meniscus, which is concave in shape and has a curvature radius of approximately 5 millimeters, acts as a weak negative lens that brings the apparent position of the LED sources closer to the sample plane. This introduces a parallax effect, where the angle of incidence of the illumination varies by up to 5 degrees across the field of view, leading to significant errors in the ptychographic reconstruction if uncorrected. The ray-tracing algorithm models the refraction of light at the air-liquid interface using Snell’s law and computes the true angle of incidence for each LED-well pair based on the known geometry of the meniscus and the position of the LED array. The corrected illumination angles are then used in the Fourier ptychographic reconstruction, ensuring that the spatial frequency data is accurately mapped and fused.

The reconstruction process is further accelerated by exploiting the redundancy of the illumination sequence across all 96 wells. Rather than processing each well independently, the algorithm partitions each well’s image into an 8 by 10 grid of overlapping 110-micrometer segments, groups corresponding segments across all wells, and processes them in batches on the GPU array. This allows the system to achieve a throughput of over two million pixels per second, enabling the reconstruction of a full 96-well plate in under 90 seconds. The final phase and intensity images are stitched together using a feather blending algorithm to eliminate boundary artifacts, and the resulting images are exported in a standardized format for downstream analysis.

The system also includes a quality assurance module that evaluates the convergence of the reconstruction algorithm for each well, flagging any channels that fail to achieve a minimum level of fidelity. This module compares the reconstructed image to a synthetic reference generated from the known properties of the illumination and the expected object structure, and computes a confidence score based on the residual error and the stability of the pupil function estimate. Wells with low confidence scores are automatically re-imaged, ensuring that the final output is free from artifacts and anomalies.

The Fourier ptychographic imaging methods of the present invention thus represent a comprehensive, end-to-end solution for high-throughput, aberration-corrected imaging that integrates optical design, computational reconstruction, and mechanical engineering into a single, unified framework. The system does not merely correct for aberrations—it leverages them as a source of information, transforming what would otherwise be a source of error into a mechanism for achieving unprecedented imaging fidelity under real-world conditions. This approach enables the system to deliver single-cell resolution, label-free phase contrast, and fluorescence imaging across an entire 96-well plate in a time frame that is competitive with non-imaging plate readers, thereby revolutionizing the field of high-throughput biological screening.
