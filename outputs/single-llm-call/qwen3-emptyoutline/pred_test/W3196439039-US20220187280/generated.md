# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of molecular biosensors and neurodegenerative disease research, specifically to genetically encoded death indicators (GEDIs) for the precise, real-time, and irreversible detection of neuronal death in live cells and living organisms. These biosensors are engineered to detect the catastrophic loss of intracellular calcium homeostasis as a universal and definitive marker of neuronal demise, independent of the underlying cell death pathway. The invention further encompasses the use of such indicators in longitudinal, single-cell imaging studies of neurodegeneration in vitro and in vivo, enabling high-throughput quantification of neuronal survival and death kinetics across diverse experimental models including primary neuronal cultures, induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons, and live zebrafish larvae. The GEDIs are designed for compatibility with standard fluorescence microscopy platforms, including spinning disk confocal, widefield, and two-photon systems, and are adaptable to multi-color imaging setups through variant spectral profiles and subcellular targeting motifs. The invention also includes kits and methods for automated survival analysis, statistical modeling of neurodegenerative progression, and therapeutic screening based on the temporal dynamics of neuronal death.

## BACKGROUND

Neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, frontotemporal dementia, and Alzheimer’s disease are characterized by the progressive and irreversible loss of neurons, leading to the deterioration of motor, cognitive, and behavioral functions. Despite decades of research, the mechanisms underlying selective neuronal vulnerability remain poorly understood, largely due to the lack of tools capable of accurately and unambiguously identifying the precise moment of neuronal death in live systems. Traditional methods for assessing neuronal viability—including vital dye staining with propidium iodide, ethidium homodimer, or Hoechst, biochemical assays for caspase activation, TUNEL staining for DNA fragmentation, and morphological evaluation of neurite retraction or soma shrinkage—are inherently limited by delayed signal onset, non-specificity, toxicity, or subjectivity. These methods often fail to distinguish between neurons that are functionally impaired but still viable and those that have crossed an irreversible threshold of demise, thereby obscuring the critical pre-mortem events that drive neurodegeneration.

Fluorescent biosensors based on genetically encoded calcium indicators (GECIs), such as GCaMP and CEPIA variants, have revolutionized the study of neuronal activity by enabling real-time visualization of intracellular calcium dynamics with high temporal and spatial resolution. These sensors are typically engineered to respond to physiological fluctuations in calcium concentration associated with synaptic transmission, action potentials, or intracellular signaling events. However, their utility in detecting neuronal death is compromised by their sensitivity to normal calcium transients, which can produce confounding signals indistinguishable from those arising from pathological calcium overload. Moreover, while some calcium indicators have been adapted to detect organelle-specific calcium fluxes, none have been designed to respond specifically to the massive, sustained, and irreversible influx of extracellular calcium that occurs upon plasma membrane rupture—a hallmark event in many forms of neuronal death.

The inability to detect the exact moment of neuronal death with high fidelity has hindered the development of accurate models of neurodegenerative progression. Population-level assays, such as bulk viability measurements or endpoint immunohistochemistry, average out heterogeneity in neuronal responses and mask the existence of subpopulations with differential susceptibility to stressors. Longitudinal imaging studies that rely on morphological degradation as a proxy for death are prone to misclassification, as neurons may exhibit prolonged periods of structural disintegration before complete loss of fluorescence, and debris can persist for days after the actual point of death. Furthermore, in vivo models, particularly in zebrafish larvae, face additional challenges due to tissue opacity, movement artifacts, and the presence of endogenous autofluorescence, which further complicate the interpretation of fluorescence-based death markers. While some studies have attempted to overcome these limitations by using anesthetics to suppress neuronal activity and thereby reduce confounding calcium signals, such interventions alter the physiological state of the nervous system and may interfere with disease-relevant phenotypes such as hyperexcitability, which is implicated in several neurodegenerative conditions.

There is therefore a critical unmet need for a biosensor that can unambiguously, sensitively, and non-invasively report the irreversible loss of calcium homeostasis as a definitive and pathway-agnostic marker of neuronal death, without being confounded by physiological activity, without requiring exogenous dyes, and without introducing toxicity or photodamage during long-term imaging. Such a tool would enable the precise temporal mapping of neuronal demise, the identification of neuroprotective or neurotoxic subpopulations, the quantification of degenerative kinetics across species and disease models, and the development of high-throughput screens for therapeutics that delay or prevent neuronal death. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel class of genetically encoded biosensors—genetically encoded death indicators (GEDIs)—that are specifically engineered to detect the catastrophic rise in cytosolic calcium concentration that accompanies plasma membrane failure, thereby providing an acute, quantitative, and universally applicable readout of neuronal death.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides genetically encoded death indicators (GEDIs) that detect the irreversible loss of calcium homeostasis in neurons as a definitive marker of neuronal death. These biosensors are derived from engineered calcium indicators originally designed to detect calcium fluxes within intracellular organelles, but are reconfigured to localize to the cytosol and exhibit minimal response to physiological calcium transients while producing a robust, sustained, and irreversible increase in fluorescence upon exposure to pathologically elevated extracellular calcium concentrations. The GEDIs are fused to a self-cleaving P2a peptide and a second fluorescent protein, enabling pseudo-ratiometric quantification of the death signal independent of expression level, transfection efficiency, or photobleaching. This design allows for the unambiguous discrimination of live neurons—characterized by low red-to-green or red-to-blue fluorescence ratios—from dead neurons, which exhibit a dramatic and permanent increase in the ratio due to the influx of extracellular calcium and consequent saturation of the calcium-binding domain.

The invention encompasses multiple variants of GEDIs, including RGEDI-P2a-EGFP, RGEDI-P2a-3xBFP, GC150-P2a-mApple, RGEDI-NLS-P2a-EGFP-NLS, and GC150-NLS-P2a-mApple-NLS, each engineered for distinct experimental applications such as compatibility with green fluorescent biosensors, nuclear localization for improved signal resolution in dense tissue, or enhanced calcium affinity for in vivo detection in environments with limited extracellular calcium. The GEDIs are functional in a wide range of neuronal systems, including primary rodent cortical neurons, human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived motor neurons, and live zebrafish larvae, and are compatible with standard transfection, viral transduction, and transgenic expression methods. The invention further provides a method for determining a quantitative GEDI threshold, derived empirically from the distribution of fluorescence ratios in live versus experimentally induced dead neurons, which enables automated, high-throughput classification of neuronal death without manual intervention.

The invention also includes methods for longitudinal, single-cell survival analysis using GEDIs in combination with automated microscopy platforms, allowing for the generation of Kaplan–Meier survival curves, cumulative risk-of-death profiles, and hazard ratios that quantify the temporal dynamics of neurodegeneration across diverse disease models, including overexpression of mutant huntingtin, alpha-synuclein, TDP43, and SOD1, as well as excitotoxic and toxin-induced injury. In vivo applications of the invention demonstrate that certain GEDI variants, particularly GC150-based sensors, can detect neuronal death in unanesthetized zebrafish larvae without suppressing endogenous neuronal activity, thereby enabling the study of neurodegeneration in physiologically relevant contexts. The invention further provides kits comprising plasmids, viral vectors, or transgenic constructs encoding GEDIs, along with instructions for use in neuronal culture, imaging, and survival analysis, and may be combined with other biosensors for multiplexed detection of calcium dynamics, mitochondrial function, or proteolytic activity.

The GEDIs represent a transformative advance in the field of neurodegeneration research by providing the first biosensor capable of defining the precise moment of neuronal death with single-cell resolution, high temporal fidelity, and pathway independence. This capability enables unprecedented insight into the sequence of events leading to neuronal demise, facilitates the discovery of neuroprotective mechanisms, and provides a robust, quantitative, and scalable platform for the preclinical evaluation of therapeutic candidates aimed at delaying or preventing neuronal death.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this disclosure, the term “neuron” refers to any excitable cell of the nervous system capable of generating and transmitting electrical and chemical signals, including but not limited to cortical neurons, motor neurons, dopaminergic neurons, hippocampal neurons, cerebellar granule cells, and induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neuronal subtypes. The term “neuronal death” refers to the irreversible cessation of neuronal function and integrity, characterized by the permanent loss of plasma membrane integrity, uncontrolled influx of extracellular calcium, collapse of ionic gradients, and eventual disintegration of cellular structures. Neuronal death may occur via multiple pathways, including necrosis, excitotoxicity, apoptosis, autophagic cell death, or mixed mechanisms, and is distinguished from transient dysfunction, reversible stress, or physiological quiescence.

The term “genetically encoded death indicator” or “GEDI” refers to a recombinant protein construct comprising a calcium-binding sensor domain derived from a genetically encoded calcium indicator, modified to lack organelle-targeting sequences and engineered to exhibit minimal fluorescence response to physiological calcium transients while producing a pronounced and irreversible increase in fluorescence upon exposure to pathologically elevated cytosolic calcium concentrations indicative of plasma membrane rupture. The GEDI is fused to a self-cleaving peptide sequence and a second fluorescent protein to enable pseudo-ratiometric quantification of the death signal. The GEDI may be expressed under the control of a neuron-specific promoter and may be further modified with nuclear localization signals, endoplasmic reticulum retention motifs, or other targeting sequences to alter subcellular localization.

The term “pseudo-ratiometric” refers to the ratio of fluorescence intensity between the GEDI sensor and a co-expressed, constitutively expressed, and non-responsive fluorescent protein, such as EGFP, mApple, or 3xBFP, which serves as an internal reference for normalization of expression level, transfection efficiency, and photobleaching. Unlike true ratiometric sensors that measure emission shifts within a single fluorophore, pseudo-ratiometric sensors rely on the independent expression of two spectrally distinct proteins, where one is responsive to the biological event of interest and the other is not, thereby enabling quantitative comparison across cells and time points.

The term “calcium homeostasis” refers to the maintenance of intracellular calcium concentration within a narrow physiological range, typically between 50 and 100 nanomolar in resting neurons, through the coordinated action of calcium channels, pumps, exchangers, and intracellular buffers. The loss of calcium homeostasis, as detected by GEDIs, occurs when extracellular calcium enters the cytosol in uncontrolled amounts due to plasma membrane rupture, resulting in sustained cytosolic calcium concentrations exceeding 1 micromolar, which is incompatible with cellular viability.

The term “irreversible commitment to death” refers to the point at which a neuron has undergone a biochemical or biophysical change that renders recovery impossible, even if the initiating insult is removed. This state is defined in the present invention by the persistent elevation of the GEDI ratio above a predetermined threshold, which corresponds to the loss of plasma membrane integrity and the uncontrolled influx of extracellular calcium.

The term “longitudinal imaging” refers to the repeated acquisition of fluorescence images from the same individual neuron or set of neurons over a period of hours to days, enabling the temporal tracking of dynamic biological processes such as degeneration and death. Longitudinal imaging is performed using automated microscopy systems capable of returning to the same spatial coordinates across multiple time points, with minimal phototoxicity and environmental perturbation.

The term “high-throughput” refers to the ability to analyze large numbers of neurons or samples simultaneously, typically in multi-well plate formats such as 96-well or 384-well plates, using automated image acquisition and analysis pipelines that require minimal manual intervention.

The term “cumulative risk-of-death” or “CRD” refers to a statistical measure derived from survival analysis that quantifies the probability of neuronal death over time, accounting for the timing and number of death events within a population. CRD is calculated using Cox proportional hazards models and Kaplan–Meier estimators based on the time at which the GEDI ratio exceeds the predetermined death threshold.

The term “neuron-specific promoter” refers to a DNA sequence that drives the expression of a transgene preferentially or exclusively in neurons, including but not limited to the human synapsin 1 (hSyn1) promoter, the mouse neurofilament light chain (NFL) promoter, the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II alpha (CaMKIIα) promoter, the vesicular glutamate transporter 1 (vGlut1) promoter, and the neuroD promoter.

The term “self-cleaving peptide” refers to a short amino acid sequence derived from viral sources, such as porcine teschovirus-1 2A (P2a), that mediates ribosomal skipping during translation, resulting in the production of two separate polypeptides from a single open reading frame without the need for proteolytic cleavage. The P2a peptide is used in the present invention to ensure equimolar expression of the GEDI sensor and the reference fluorescent protein.

The term “transgenic organism” refers to an organism, such as a zebrafish, mouse, or fly, that has been genetically modified to stably express a transgene, such as a GEDI construct, in its germline and in subsequent generations. Transgenic lines may be generated using techniques such as Tol2 transposon-mediated integration, CRISPR/Cas9 knock-in, or pronuclear injection.

The term “in vivo” refers to experiments conducted within a living organism, as opposed to in vitro, which refers to experiments conducted in an artificial environment outside the living organism, such as in cell culture dishes or microfluidic devices.

The term “extracellular calcium” refers to calcium ions present in the extracellular fluid surrounding cells, which in physiological conditions is maintained at a concentration of approximately 1 to 2 millimolar. In the context of neuronal death, the influx of extracellular calcium into the cytosol is a key event detected by GEDIs.

The term “pathway-agnostic” refers to the ability of a biosensor to detect cell death regardless of the molecular mechanism initiating the death process, including apoptosis, necrosis, necroptosis, autophagy, or excitotoxicity, so long as the final common pathway involves loss of plasma membrane integrity and uncontrolled calcium influx.

### Vectors

The invention encompasses a family of recombinant expression vectors designed for the efficient delivery and stable expression of genetically encoded death indicators (GEDIs) in a variety of cellular and organismal systems. Each vector is constructed to contain a neuron-specific promoter operably linked to a nucleic acid sequence encoding a GEDI construct, which comprises a calcium-binding sensor domain fused to a self-cleaving peptide and a second fluorescent protein. The calcium-binding sensor domain is derived from a genetically encoded calcium indicator such as RCEPIA or GCaMP6-150, modified by deletion of endoplasmic reticulum retention signals and optimization of calcium-binding affinity to render the sensor unresponsive to physiological calcium transients while maintaining high sensitivity to pathological calcium overload.

The preferred vector backbone is a mammalian expression plasmid containing a cytomegalovirus enhancer and a human synapsin 1 promoter to ensure robust and neuron-specific expression. The GEDI coding sequence is inserted downstream of the promoter in a single open reading frame, followed by a sequence encoding the porcine teschovirus-1 2A (P2a) self-cleaving peptide, which is in turn followed by the coding sequence of a reference fluorescent protein such as enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP), monomeric apple (mApple), or triple-tagged blue fluorescent protein (3xBFP). The entire cassette is flanked by restriction sites for cloning flexibility and includes a polyadenylation signal derived from the bovine growth hormone gene to ensure proper mRNA processing.

Alternative vectors include lentiviral and adeno-associated viral (AAV) constructs, which are packaged into viral particles for transduction of primary neurons, induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons, or in vivo applications in rodents and zebrafish. Lentiviral vectors are particularly useful for stable, long-term expression in dividing and non-dividing cells, while AAV serotypes such as AAV9 and AAV-PHP.eB are optimized for efficient transduction of neurons in the central nervous system. For in vivo transgenesis, the GEDI cassette is cloned into Tol2 transposon-based vectors for integration into the zebrafish genome, enabling the generation of stable transgenic lines expressing GEDIs under the control of neuron-specific enhancers such as neuroD or mnx1.

The vectors may further contain additional elements to enhance expression, including woodchuck hepatitis virus post-transcriptional regulatory element (WPRE), introns, or synthetic enhancer sequences. The plasmid backbone may also include antibiotic resistance genes such as ampicillin or kanamycin for bacterial selection, and fluorescent markers such as mCherry or BFP for visual confirmation of transfection efficiency. The vectors are designed to be compatible with standard molecular cloning techniques, including restriction enzyme digestion, Gibson assembly, and Gateway recombination, and are validated for functionality in a range of host systems, including rat, mouse, and human primary neurons, as well as zebrafish embryos.

The invention further includes vectors encoding nuclear-localized variants of GEDIs, in which a nuclear localization signal (NLS) derived from the SV40 large T antigen is fused to both the N-terminus and C-terminus of the GEDI cassette, ensuring accumulation of the sensor in the nucleus. This design enhances signal-to-noise ratio in dense tissue environments and facilitates automated segmentation in three-dimensional imaging datasets. The nuclear-localized variants are particularly useful for in vivo applications in zebrafish larvae and for studies requiring high-resolution imaging of individual neurons within complex neural circuits.

### Cells

The genetically encoded death indicators (GEDIs) of the present invention are functional in a wide variety of neuronal cell types, including primary neurons derived from embryonic rodent cortex, spinal cord, hippocampus, and midbrain, as well as human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons of multiple subtypes, including cortical neurons, motor neurons, dopaminergic neurons, and GABAergic interneurons. The GEDIs are expressed in these cells via transient transfection using lipid-based reagents such as Lipofectamine 2000 or 3000, electroporation, or nucleofection, or via stable transduction using lentiviral or adeno-associated viral vectors. In all cases, the GEDIs produce a consistent and reproducible response to neuronal death, characterized by a rapid and irreversible increase in the pseudo-ratiometric fluorescence signal.

In primary rat cortical neurons, the GEDIs are expressed at 4 to 6 days in vitro and respond to cytotoxic insults such as sodium azide, glutamate excitotoxicity, or mechanical disruption with a rapid increase in the GEDI ratio, which remains elevated for the duration of the imaging period. The GEDI signal precedes the appearance of morphological signs of death such as neurite fragmentation, soma shrinkage, or loss of EGFP fluorescence, and is more sensitive than traditional dyes such as propidium iodide or ethidium homodimer, which require more extensive membrane damage for uptake. The GEDI signal is also unaffected by the presence of calcium chelators in the extracellular medium, as long as calcium is present, and is abolished when extracellular calcium is removed, confirming that the signal is dependent on calcium influx rather than intracellular calcium release.

In human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived motor neurons, the GEDIs are expressed following differentiation protocols that yield mature, electrophysiologically active neurons expressing markers such as HB9, ISL1, and ChAT. These neurons exhibit a robust GEDI response when exposed to disease-relevant stressors such as mutant SOD1 expression, TDP43 aggregation, or oxidative stress, enabling the quantification of survival kinetics in patient-derived cells. The GEDI signal in these cells is stable over periods of up to 14 days, allowing for longitudinal monitoring of neurodegeneration in the absence of exogenous toxins. The GEDI ratio is normalized to the reference fluorescent protein to account for variability in transfection efficiency and expression level, ensuring accurate comparison across individual neurons and across experimental replicates.

The GEDIs are also functional in non-neuronal cell types such as HEK293 cells, where they similarly detect loss of membrane integrity and calcium influx, indicating that the underlying mechanism of detection is not neuron-specific but rather reflects a universal feature of cell death. However, the neuron-specific promoters used in the preferred embodiments ensure that expression is restricted to neuronal populations in mixed cultures or in vivo systems, minimizing background signal from glial or non-neural cells.

The invention further includes methods for co-expressing GEDIs with other biosensors, such as mitochondrial membrane potential indicators, reactive oxygen species sensors, or caspase activity reporters, enabling multiplexed detection of multiple cellular events during the degenerative process. The GEDIs are compatible with standard fluorescence filters and do not exhibit spectral overlap with commonly used indicators such as GCaMP, R-GECO, or FRET-based sensors, allowing for simultaneous imaging without crosstalk.

### Animals

The genetically encoded death indicators (GEDIs) of the present invention are functional in live animals, particularly in zebrafish larvae, which are transparent, genetically tractable, and amenable to high-throughput longitudinal imaging. Transgenic zebrafish lines expressing GEDIs under the control of neuron-specific promoters such as neuroD or mnx1 are generated by microinjection of Tol2 transposon-based constructs into fertilized eggs at the one-cell stage, followed by selection of founder lines that transmit the transgene to offspring. These transgenic lines exhibit stable, heritable expression of GEDIs in spinal cord and brain neurons, with no observable developmental defects or behavioral abnormalities.

In zebrafish larvae, the GEDIs are used to detect neuronal death induced by genetic ablation using the nitroreductase (NTR)/metronidazole (MTZ) system, which converts the prodrug MTZ into a cytotoxic compound specifically in cells expressing NTR. The GC150-P2a-mApple variant, which has a higher affinity for calcium than the RGEDI variant, is particularly effective in this system, producing a clear and sustained increase in fluorescence ratio within 24 hours of MTZ exposure, even in the absence of pharmacological immobilization. This allows for the study of neurodegeneration in a physiologically intact nervous system, preserving endogenous neuronal activity and circuit function.

The invention further includes methods for imaging GEDI-expressing zebrafish larvae using automated spinning disk confocal microscopy in multi-well plates, enabling the simultaneous tracking of hundreds of individual neurons across multiple animals over several days. The larvae are immobilized in low-melting-point agarose to prevent movement artifacts, but are not anesthetized, allowing for the preservation of natural neural activity. The GEDI signal is quantified using custom image analysis pipelines that segment individual neurons, track them over time, and calculate the GEDI ratio at each time point. Neurons are classified as dead when the GEDI ratio exceeds a predetermined threshold derived from control experiments.

The invention also encompasses the use of GEDIs in rodent models of neurodegeneration, including transgenic mice expressing mutant huntingtin, alpha-synuclein, or TDP43. In these models, GEDIs are delivered via stereotactic injection of AAV vectors into specific brain regions such as the striatum, motor cortex, or spinal cord. Longitudinal imaging is performed using cranial windows or through thinned skull preparations, enabling the visualization of neuronal death in the intact brain over weeks to months. The GEDI signal is used to quantify the rate of neuronal loss, identify neuroprotective subpopulations, and assess the efficacy of therapeutic interventions such as small molecule inhibitors, gene therapies, or immunotherapies.

The use of GEDIs in live animals provides a powerful platform for correlating neuronal death with behavioral outcomes, such as motor deficits, sensory impairments, or cognitive decline. For example, in zebrafish larvae, the timing of neuronal death detected by GEDI can be directly correlated with the loss of swimming behavior, providing a quantitative link between cellular pathology and functional impairment. This capability enables the development of biomarkers for disease progression and the validation of therapeutic efficacy in preclinical models.

### Methods of Use

The methods of use of the genetically encoded death indicators (GEDIs) encompass a broad range of applications in neuroscience and drug discovery, including the detection of neuronal death in live-cell imaging, the quantification of neurodegenerative progression, the identification of neuroprotective subpopulations, and the high-throughput screening of therapeutic compounds. The GEDIs are expressed in neurons via transfection, transduction, or transgenesis, and their fluorescence is monitored over time using automated microscopy systems capable of repeated imaging of the same cells or animals.

In vitro, the GEDIs are used in primary neuronal cultures or induced pluripotent stem cell-derived neurons plated in multi-well plates. Neurons are transfected with GEDI constructs and imaged at regular intervals—ranging from every 30 minutes to every 24 hours—using a fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filter sets for the GEDI and reference fluorophore. Image analysis software automatically segments individual neurons, tracks them across time points, and calculates the GEDI ratio. A death threshold is determined empirically by comparing the GEDI ratio in control neurons to neurons treated with a cytotoxic agent such as sodium azide or glutamate. Neurons with a GEDI ratio above this threshold are classified as dead, and survival curves are generated using Kaplan–Meier analysis.

The GEDIs are particularly useful for identifying subpopulations of neurons with differential susceptibility to neurodegenerative stressors. For example, in cultures exposed to low concentrations of glutamate, a small fraction of neurons survive for extended periods while the majority die rapidly. These resistant neurons can be tracked over time to identify molecular or morphological features that correlate with resilience, such as altered calcium buffering capacity, enhanced mitochondrial function, or increased expression of neuroprotective genes. The GEDI signal provides a precise temporal marker for the onset of death, enabling the correlation of pre-mortem phenotypes with survival outcomes.

In vivo, the GEDIs are used in zebrafish larvae and rodent models to monitor neuronal death in real time within the intact nervous system. In zebrafish, larvae are immobilized in agarose and imaged using a spinning disk confocal microscope, with images acquired every 6 to 24 hours over several days. The GEDI signal is used to quantify the rate of neuronal loss following genetic ablation, toxin exposure, or genetic mutation. In rodent models, GEDIs are delivered via viral vectors and imaged through cranial windows or via fiber-optic probes, allowing for longitudinal monitoring of neurodegeneration in deep brain structures.

The GEDIs are also used in high-throughput drug screening assays, in which neurons are plated in 96-well or 384-well plates, transfected with GEDIs, and exposed to libraries of small molecules, siRNAs, or CRISPR guide RNAs. After 24 to 72 hours, the GEDI ratio is measured in each well using a high-content imaging system, and wells with a significantly lower proportion of dead neurons are identified as potential neuroprotective hits. This approach enables the rapid identification of compounds that delay or prevent neuronal death, with minimal false positives due to the specificity and irreversibility of the GEDI signal.

The GEDIs are further used in combination with other biosensors to study the sequence of events leading to neuronal death. For example, GEDIs can be co-expressed with mitochondrial membrane potential sensors, lysosomal pH indicators, or caspase activity reporters to determine whether calcium influx occurs before or after mitochondrial dysfunction, lysosomal rupture, or caspase activation. This multiplexed approach enables the dissection of complex cell death pathways and the identification of upstream drivers of neurodegeneration.

The GEDIs are also used in behavioral studies to correlate neuronal death with functional deficits. In zebrafish larvae, the timing of neuronal death detected by GEDI is compared to the loss of swimming response to tactile stimuli, providing a direct link between cellular pathology and behavior. In rodent models, GEDI-based quantification of neuronal loss in motor cortex or spinal cord is correlated with gait analysis, grip strength, or rotarod performance, enabling the validation of therapeutic interventions at both cellular and behavioral levels.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Primary rat cortical neurons were isolated from embryonic day 18–19 Long-Evans rats and cultured in Neurobasal medium supplemented with B27, GlutaMAX, and penicillin/streptomycin. At 4 days in vitro, neurons were transfected with a plasmid encoding the human synapsin 1 promoter driving the expression of RGEDI-P2a-EGFP using Lipofectamine 2000. Transfection efficiency was assessed by fluorescence microscopy, with approximately 15–20% of neurons expressing the construct. Neurons were imaged at 3-hour intervals for up to 72 hours using a Nikon Ti-E inverted microscope equipped with a Yokogawa CSU-W1 spinning disk confocal unit, an Andor iXon EMCCD camera, and appropriate filter sets for red (543/22 excitation, 617/73 emission) and green (488/10 excitation, 525/50 emission) fluorescence.

To validate the specificity of the GEDI signal, neurons were subjected to three distinct stimuli: (1) brief electrical field stimulation (3 seconds at 30 Hz) to evoke physiological calcium transients, (2) exposure to 2% sodium azide to induce rapid necrotic cell death, and (3) exposure to 0.1 mM glutamate to induce excitotoxic death. In response to electrical stimulation, neurons expressing GCaMP6f exhibited a rapid and transient increase in fluorescence, whereas neurons expressing RGEDI-P2a-EGFP showed no significant change in fluorescence ratio. In contrast, upon exposure to sodium azide, RGEDI-P2a-EGFP-expressing neurons exhibited a rapid and sustained increase in the red-to-green fluorescence ratio, which remained elevated for the duration of the imaging period. The increase in GEDI ratio occurred within 1–2 minutes of toxin exposure and was irreversible, even after washout of the toxin.

The GEDI threshold for death was determined by measuring the fluorescence ratio in 100 neurons before and after sodium azide treatment. The mean GEDI ratio in live neurons was 0.032 ± 0.005, while the mean ratio in dead neurons was 0.187 ± 0.012. The death threshold was calculated using the equation: (mean dead − mean live) × 0.25 + mean live, yielding a threshold of 0.072. Neurons with a GEDI ratio above this threshold were classified as dead. When applied to a separate set of 200 neurons undergoing spontaneous death over 48 hours, the GEDI threshold correctly classified 98.7% of neurons as live or dead, with only 1.3% false positives and no false negatives.

To assess the temporal relationship between GEDI signal and other death markers, neurons were co-stained with propidium iodide and TUNEL. Propidium iodide staining appeared 6–12 hours after the GEDI signal had already exceeded the death threshold, while TUNEL staining appeared 18–24 hours later. Caspase-3/7 activity, as measured by PhiPhiLux, was also detected after the GEDI signal, confirming that the GEDI reports an early and irreversible step in the death cascade preceding downstream biochemical events.

### Example 2

To evaluate the ability of GEDIs to detect neuronal death in the context of neurodegenerative disease, rat cortical neurons were co-transfected with RGEDI-P2a-EGFP and plasmids encoding disease-associated proteins: mutant huntingtin exon 1 with 97 glutamine repeats (HttEx1Q97), wild-type alpha-synuclein, or human TDP43. Neurons were imaged every 6 hours for 168 hours using automated microscopy. The GEDI ratio was quantified in each neuron, and the death threshold of 0.072, previously established in Example 1, was applied to classify neurons as live or dead.

In neurons expressing HttEx1Q97, the proportion of dead neurons increased steadily over time, with 15% of neurons dead at 24 hours, 42% at 48 hours, 68% at 72 hours, and 85% at 168 hours. Control neurons expressing only RGEDI-P2a-EGFP showed a baseline death rate of less than 5% over the same period. Similar trends were observed in neurons expressing alpha-synuclein and TDP43, with death rates of 72% and 78% at 168 hours, respectively. The cumulative risk-of-death (CRD) was calculated using Cox proportional hazards modeling, yielding hazard ratios of 1.83 for HttEx1Q97, 1.73 for alpha-synuclein, and 1.77 for TDP43, all significantly greater than 1 (p < 0.001).

To validate the accuracy of GEDI-based classification, neurons were manually scored as live or dead by an observer blinded to the GEDI data, based on morphological criteria such as soma shrinkage, neurite fragmentation, and loss of EGFP fluorescence. The agreement between manual scoring and GEDI classification was 94.2%, with the majority of discrepancies arising from neurons that were classified as live by morphology but had a GEDI ratio above the threshold. These neurons were later confirmed to be dead at subsequent time points, demonstrating that GEDI provides an earlier and more accurate detection of death than morphology alone.

### Example 3

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) derived from a healthy control donor and from a patient carrying the SOD1 D90A mutation were differentiated into motor neurons using a dual-SMAD inhibition protocol. At day 19 of differentiation, neurons were transfected with RGEDI-P2a-EGFP and imaged every 12 hours for 120 hours using automated microscopy. The GEDI ratio was quantified, and a death threshold of 0.072 was applied.

In control neurons, the death rate remained low throughout the imaging period, with only 8% of neurons classified as dead by 120 hours. In SOD1 D90A neurons, the death rate was significantly higher, with 32% of neurons classified as dead by 120 hours. The cumulative risk-of-death curve showed a hazard ratio of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.11–1.44, p < 0.0001), indicating a 26% increased risk of death in mutant neurons compared to controls. This result was consistent with prior observations of axonal degeneration and reduced survival in SOD1 D90A motor neurons, but provided the first quantitative, time-resolved measurement of neuronal death in this model.

To confirm that the GEDI signal was not confounded by differences in transfection efficiency or expression level, the mean EGFP fluorescence intensity was measured in live and dead neurons. No significant difference was observed, confirming that the GEDI ratio was a reliable indicator of death independent of expression level. The GEDI signal preceded morphological signs of degeneration by an average of 18 hours, demonstrating its utility as an early biomarker of disease progression in human neurons.

### Example 4

To test the ability of GEDIs to detect neuronal death in vivo, transgenic zebrafish larvae were generated by microinjection of a Tol2 transposon-based construct encoding the neuroD promoter driving expression of GC150-P2a-mApple. Embryos were raised to 72 hours post-fertilization, immobilized in low-melting-point agarose, and imaged every 12 hours for 96 hours using a spinning disk confocal microscope. To induce neuronal death, larvae were exposed to 10 μM metronidazole (MTZ), which activates the nitroreductase (NTR) protein expressed in motor neurons.

In control larvae exposed to DMSO, no increase in GC150 signal was observed, and motor neurons retained normal morphology and fluorescence over the entire imaging period. In MTZ-treated larvae, a rapid and sustained increase in the GC150/mApple fluorescence ratio was observed in motor neurons within 24 hours, with 68% of neurons exceeding the death threshold by 48 hours. The increase in GEDI ratio was irreversible and occurred before visible signs of axon retraction or soma fragmentation. The GEDI signal was specific to NTR-expressing neurons, as non-transgenic neurons in the same larvae showed no change in fluorescence.

To confirm that the GEDI signal was not due to physiological calcium transients, larvae were treated with 4-methyl-N-(phenylmethyl)benzenesulfonamide (BTS), a muscle contraction blocker that preserves neuronal activity. GC150-expressing neurons in BTS-treated larvae showed no increase in fluorescence in response to acetic acid-induced swimming behavior, confirming that the sensor does not respond to normal calcium dynamics. In contrast, GCaMP7-expressing neurons in the same larvae showed robust calcium transients during swimming, demonstrating that GC150 is uniquely suited for death detection in active neural circuits.

### Example 6

To expand the utility of GEDIs for multiplexed imaging, a variant encoding RGEDI-P2a-3xBFP was generated and tested in rat cortical neurons. Neurons were transfected with RGEDI-P2a-3xBFP and imaged alongside neurons expressing GCaMP6f-P2a-mRuby. Upon exposure to sodium azide, RGEDI-P2a-3xBFP exhibited a rapid and sustained increase in the blue-to-red fluorescence ratio, comparable in kinetics and magnitude to RGEDI-P2a-EGFP. The peak response rate (τ) was not significantly different between the two variants (p = 0.44, ANOVA), confirming that the spectral shift did not affect sensor performance.

To test compatibility with green fluorescent biosensors, neurons expressing RGEDI-P2a-3xBFP were co-transfected with a green fluorescent calcium sensor (GCaMP6s) and imaged simultaneously. The blue and red channels of RGEDI-P2a-3xBFP did not overlap with the green channel of GCaMP6s, allowing for clear discrimination of both death and calcium activity signals. In contrast, neurons expressing RGEDI-P2a-EGFP showed significant spectral overlap with GCaMP6s, limiting their utility in multiplexed experiments.

The nuclear-localized variant RGEDI-NLS-P2a-EGFP-NLS was also tested in zebrafish larvae. Neurons expressing this construct showed enhanced signal intensity in the nucleus, with reduced background from cytoplasmic autofluorescence. The death threshold was unchanged, and the kinetics of the GEDI response were identical to the cytoplasmic variant, confirming that nuclear localization does not alter sensor function but improves signal resolution in dense tissue. These results demonstrate that the GEDI platform is highly modular and can be adapted for a wide range of experimental needs.
