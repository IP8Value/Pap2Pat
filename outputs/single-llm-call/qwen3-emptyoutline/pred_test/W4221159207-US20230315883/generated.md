## BACKGROUND

The challenge of identifying matching records across separate datasets while preserving the confidentiality of non-matching records has become increasingly critical in domains such as healthcare, finance, law enforcement, and social services, where data sharing is restricted by legal, ethical, or regulatory constraints. Traditional entity resolution techniques rely on direct comparison of identifying fields such as names, addresses, or social security numbers, but these approaches are infeasible when the datasets reside in separate organizational silos with strict data governance policies. Even when identifying fields exist, they are often subject to typographical errors, formatting inconsistencies, or intentional obfuscation, rendering exact matching methods ineffective. The need to identify approximate matches—such as recognizing that “John Doe,” “Jon P. Doe,” and “J. Doe” refer to the same individual—has led to the adoption of local sensitive hashing (LSH) in non-private settings, where similar records are mapped to similar hash values to enable efficient clustering and comparison. However, applying LSH directly in a distributed environment exposes the underlying data to significant privacy risks, as an adversary with access to the hash outputs may perform offline brute-force attacks, reverse-engineer sensitive information, or infer patterns about non-matching records based on the distribution of hash values. 

Private set intersection (PSI) protocols have emerged as a cryptographic solution for securely computing the overlap between two datasets without revealing any additional information beyond the intersection itself. These protocols, often based on public-key cryptography such as Diffie-Hellman key exchange or homomorphic encryption, ensure that each party learns only the identities of matching records and nothing else about the other party’s dataset. While PSI is highly effective for exact matching, it cannot be directly applied to approximate matching scenarios because it requires identical inputs to produce identical outputs, and thus fails to accommodate the inherent noise and variability present in real-world data fields. Attempts to combine LSH with PSI have been proposed in prior work, but these efforts either rely on a trusted third party to mediate the comparison, introduce prohibitive computational overhead, fail to provide rigorous security analysis, or suffer from scalability limitations that render them impractical for datasets exceeding tens of thousands of records. Moreover, many existing approaches use Bloom filters or locality-preserving hashes, which are vulnerable to reconstruction attacks due to their data-dependent nature and lack of formal privacy guarantees. The absence of a practical, scalable, and provably secure protocol that enables fuzzy record linkage without third-party involvement has created a significant gap in the ability of organizations to collaborate on cross-institutional data analysis while complying with privacy regulations such as HIPAA, GDPR, and CCPA. 

Furthermore, existing solutions do not adequately address the trade-off between accuracy, performance, and privacy leakage. The parameters governing the LSH function—such as the number of bands and the number of min-hashes per band—directly influence the probability of false positives and false negatives, yet prior methods offer no systematic framework for optimizing these parameters under real-world constraints. Additionally, the computational burden of performing cryptographic operations on large datasets has been a major bottleneck, particularly when each record must be processed individually rather than in bulk. The lack of efficient methods for weighting the influence of different data fields—such as giving greater importance to names over zip codes in a matching algorithm—has further limited the practical utility of prior approaches. Finally, no prior solution provides a mechanism for estimating the similarity between matched records without fully revealing the underlying data, nor does any existing protocol offer a way to prevent an adversary from inferring the absence of records in the other party’s dataset based on the structure of the transmitted hashes. These deficiencies have hindered the adoption of privacy-preserving record linkage in large-scale, real-world applications, despite the clear demand for such capabilities in public health surveillance, fraud detection, and identity verification systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel and highly efficient privacy-preserving record linkage protocol that enables two parties to identify similar records across their respective datasets without revealing any information beyond the identities of the matched records and the total size of the opposing dataset. The protocol combines local sensitive hashing with a private set intersection mechanism in a manner that ensures linear time complexity, strong security guarantees against semi-honest adversaries, and minimal privacy leakage. Each party independently preprocesses its dataset by extracting k-shingles from quasi-identifying fields, applying a weighted hashing technique to adjust the influence of each field, and computing a series of min-hash signatures grouped into bands. These band signatures are then encrypted using a cryptographic key and transmitted to the other party via a secure communication channel. A private set intersection protocol is executed over the encrypted signatures to determine which signatures are shared between the two parties, thereby identifying records that are likely to correspond to the same entity. The party that initiated the protocol then maps the intersected signatures back to their original record identifiers to determine the set of matching records. 

To prevent an adversary from inferring the absence of records in the opposing dataset, the protocol employs a permutation mechanism that randomly reorders the encrypted signatures before transmission, ensuring that the relative position of any record’s signatures cannot be used to deduce the presence or absence of neighboring records. The protocol further includes a novel weighted shingle hashing technique that achieves the same statistical properties as duplicating shingles multiple times, but without the associated computational overhead, thereby significantly improving performance. The invention also provides a method for estimating the Jaccard similarity between matched records without revealing the underlying shingles, by conducting a secondary private set intersection over the shingle sets of only the matched pairs, thus preserving privacy while enabling quality control. The protocol is designed to be modular, allowing for the substitution of different cryptographic primitives for the private set intersection component without compromising the core privacy guarantees. The entire process operates in linear time with respect to the number of records, making it scalable to datasets containing millions of entries. The invention further includes a formal security analysis demonstrating that, under the decisional Diffie-Hellman and one-more-Diffie-Hellman assumptions, an adversary cannot distinguish the encrypted signatures from random values, cannot reconstruct any non-matching records, and cannot determine the size of the intersection beyond what is explicitly revealed. The protocol is implemented in a manner that supports secure communication via TLS 1.3, prevents key reuse to mitigate man-in-the-middle attacks, and includes mechanisms for deduplication and field normalization to ensure consistency across datasets. The invention enables organizations to perform accurate, scalable, and privacy-compliant record linkage for the first time at a practical scale, without relying on third parties, without exposing sensitive data, and without sacrificing performance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a method and system for performing privacy-preserving record linkage between two parties holding private datasets, wherein the goal is to identify records that correspond to the same real-world entity without revealing any information about non-matching records or the detailed content of the datasets beyond what is necessary to determine the matches. The invention operates in a two-party setting, with a sender party and a receiver party, each possessing a dataset of records that contain quasi-identifying fields such as names, addresses, dates of birth, and other attributes that may vary slightly between records due to data entry errors, formatting differences, or intentional obfuscation. The protocol is designed to be executed over a secure communication channel, and all cryptographic operations are performed locally by each party without the involvement of any external or trusted third party. The core innovation lies in the integration of local sensitive hashing with private set intersection in a manner that preserves the privacy of non-matching records while enabling efficient identification of approximate matches.

The protocol begins with a preprocessing phase in which each party independently normalizes the records in its dataset to ensure consistency in formatting, case, punctuation, and whitespace. This normalization step ensures that variations such as “John Doe” and “john doe” are treated as equivalent prior to hashing. Following normalization, each record is decomposed into a set of overlapping substrings, known as k-shingles, where k is a predefined integer representing the length of each substring. For example, if a record contains the name “John Smith” and k is set to 3, the resulting shingles would include “Joh,” “ohn,” “hn “, “n S,” “ Sm,” “Smi,” “mit,” “ith,” and “th.” These shingles are extracted from all quasi-identifying fields in the record, and each field is assigned a weight that reflects its relative importance in determining identity. Fields that are more indicative of identity, such as full names or social security numbers, are assigned higher weights, while fields with lower discriminative power, such as zip codes or city names, are assigned lower weights. The weights are applied using a novel hashing technique that avoids the computational inefficiency of duplicating shingles multiple times. Instead of physically replicating shingles according to their weight, the hash value of each shingle is transformed using a non-linear function that alters its probability distribution such that the likelihood of a shingle being selected as the minimum hash value in a min-hash operation is proportional to its weight. This transformation is mathematically equivalent to generating w independent hash values and selecting the minimum, where w is the weight of the shingle, but it requires only a single hash computation and a simple arithmetic operation, resulting in a substantial performance improvement.

Once the weighted shingles are generated for each record, the protocol proceeds to compute a local sensitive hash signature for each record using a series of min-hash functions. Specifically, P different min-hash functions are applied to the set of weighted shingles associated with a record, where P is a configurable parameter. The outputs of these min-hash functions are divided into B bands, each containing R min-hash values, such that P equals the product of B and R. Within each band, the R min-hash values are concatenated into a single string, which is then hashed again using a collision-resistant cryptographic hash function to produce a single band signature. The final local sensitive hash output for a record is therefore an ordered list of B band signatures, each representing a probabilistic summary of the record’s shingle content. This structure ensures that records with similar shingle content are likely to share at least one common band signature, while records with dissimilar content are unlikely to do so. The choice of B and R parameters allows for fine-tuning of the trade-off between false positive and false negative rates: increasing the number of bands increases the probability of detecting true matches but also increases communication overhead, while increasing the number of min-hashes per band reduces the probability of false positives by requiring a higher degree of similarity for a match to occur.

After computing the band signatures for all records in its dataset, each party encrypts these signatures using a secret key derived from a cryptographic key exchange protocol, such as elliptic curve Diffie-Hellman over the NIST P-256 curve. The encryption is performed by raising each band signature to the power of the party’s private key modulo a large prime, resulting in a set of encrypted signatures that are computationally indistinguishable from random values under the decisional Diffie-Hellman assumption. To prevent an adversary from inferring the absence of records in the opposing dataset, the sender party applies a secret permutation to the ordered list of encrypted signatures before transmitting them to the receiver. This permutation is applied at two levels: first, an intra-permutation reorders the B band signatures within each record’s group, and second, an inter-permutation reorders the entire sequence of record groups. This dual permutation ensures that the spatial relationship between records is completely obscured, so that even if an adversary observes the encrypted signatures and knows the order of records in its own dataset, it cannot determine whether a particular record in the opposing dataset is missing or whether adjacent records are correlated. The receiver party then performs the same encryption process on its own band signatures and transmits the resulting encrypted signatures to the sender party.

Once both parties have exchanged their encrypted signatures, a private set intersection protocol is executed to determine which encrypted signatures are shared between the two datasets. The protocol used in this invention is a unidirectional variant of the Diffie-Hellman-based private set intersection, in which the sender party computes the intersection by raising the receiver’s encrypted signatures to the power of its own secret key and comparing the results with its own encrypted signatures. If the values match, this indicates that the corresponding band signatures originated from records that share at least one common band. The security of this step relies on the computational hardness of the discrete logarithm problem and the one-more-Diffie-Hellman assumption, which together ensure that an adversary cannot generate new encrypted signatures in the name of the other party or reverse-engineer the original band signatures from the transmitted values. The intersection result is a set of encrypted signatures that correspond to records that are likely to match. The sender party then maps these intersected encrypted signatures back to their original record identifiers by reversing the permutation applied during transmission, thereby identifying the specific records in its dataset that correspond to matching records in the receiver’s dataset. The receiver party learns only the total number of records in the sender’s dataset and the number of matches, but gains no information about the content of the sender’s records or the identities of non-matching records.

To further enhance privacy and enable quality control, the invention includes a mechanism for estimating the Jaccard similarity between matched records without revealing the underlying shingles. After the initial private set intersection is complete, the sender party may request a secondary private set intersection over the shingle sets of only the matched record pairs. In this secondary protocol, the sender party provides the shingle set of its own record, and the receiver party provides the shingle set of its corresponding record. The private set intersection is then performed to compute the size of the intersection of the two shingle sets and the size of the receiver’s shingle set, from which the Jaccard index can be computed without revealing the individual shingles. This allows the sender party to estimate the degree of similarity between matched records and to filter out matches with low confidence, without exposing any additional information about non-matching records. The secondary protocol is executed only for a small subset of the matched records, ensuring that the additional computational and communication overhead remains negligible compared to the primary protocol.

The protocol is implemented in a manner that supports secure communication via Transport Layer Security version 1.3, which provides mutual authentication, encryption, and integrity protection for all messages exchanged between the parties. To prevent man-in-the-middle attacks and key reuse vulnerabilities, each party generates a fresh cryptographic key pair for every session, and the keys are discarded immediately after the protocol completes. The protocol also includes a deduplication step prior to execution, in which duplicate records within each party’s dataset are identified and removed based on exact matches across all quasi-identifying fields, to prevent the sender from inferring information about the structure of the receiver’s dataset by observing multiple identical matches. The entire process is designed to operate in linear time with respect to the number of records, with the dominant computational cost arising from the cryptographic operations during the private set intersection phase. The communication cost is proportional to the number of band signatures, which is determined by the number of records multiplied by the number of bands, and is optimized by selecting the smallest number of bands that achieves the desired false positive rate.

The invention further includes a method for empirically determining the optimal values of B and R based on the characteristics of the datasets and the desired balance between accuracy and performance. By analyzing the cumulative probability distribution of the LSH match function under varying Jaccard thresholds, the parties can select parameter values that minimize the number of false positives while maintaining acceptable performance. For example, increasing the number of min-hashes per band reduces the probability of false positives by requiring a higher degree of similarity for a match, while decreasing the number of bands reduces the communication overhead and computational cost. The invention demonstrates that it is possible to achieve a false positive rate of less than one in ten thousand records while maintaining execution times under one hour for datasets containing one million records, a performance level that is orders of magnitude faster than prior solutions using homomorphic encryption or garbled circuits. The protocol has been implemented in C++ using the OpenSSL library for cryptographic operations and has been tested on real-world datasets, including the North Carolina Voter Register and synthetic datasets generated to mimic real-world data distributions. The results confirm that the protocol identifies all true matches with high precision, while maintaining strict privacy guarantees and achieving practical execution times even on modest hardware configurations.

The security of the protocol is formally established under the semi-honest adversary model, in which both parties follow the protocol correctly but attempt to infer additional information from the messages they receive. Under this model, the invention guarantees that the sender learns only the set of matching records and the size of the receiver’s dataset, while the receiver learns only the size of the sender’s dataset. No information about non-matching records, the distribution of shingles, or the structure of the datasets beyond the number of matches is revealed. The use of the permutation mechanism ensures that even if an adversary knows the order of records in its own dataset, it cannot infer the presence or absence of specific records in the opposing dataset. The cryptographic encryption ensures that the band signatures are computationally indistinguishable from random values, preventing any offline brute-force attacks. The use of the weighted shingle hashing technique ensures that the influence of different fields is accurately modeled without introducing unnecessary computational overhead. The modular design of the protocol allows for the substitution of alternative cryptographic primitives for the private set intersection component, such as homomorphic encryption or oblivious transfer, without altering the core privacy guarantees or the structure of the local sensitive hashing component. The invention thus provides a complete, scalable, and secure solution for privacy-preserving record linkage that is suitable for deployment in real-world environments where data privacy is paramount and computational resources are constrained.
