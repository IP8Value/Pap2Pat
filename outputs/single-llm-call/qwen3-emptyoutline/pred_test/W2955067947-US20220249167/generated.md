# DESCRIPTION

## Example 1: Materials and Methods

The present invention relates to a computational method for identifying minimally invasive surgical targets in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy, particularly when the epileptogenic zone is deemed inoperable due to its proximity to eloquent brain structures. The method integrates structural neuroimaging data, graph-theoretical network analysis, and personalized brain network simulations to derive target zones—comprising specific brain regions or white matter tracts—that, when selectively resected or disconnected, effectively suppress seizure propagation while preserving essential functional networks. The method is implemented through a systematic, iterative feedback loop that begins with the acquisition of high-resolution structural brain connectivity data from each individual patient, followed by the construction of a patient-specific whole-brain network model, the application of constrained modularity analysis to identify candidate surgical targets, and the evaluation of these targets through dynamic simulations of both seizure propagation and resting-state signal transmission. The entire process is designed to operate independently of clinical assumptions regarding surgical feasibility beyond the epileptogenic zone, thereby enabling the discovery of novel, non-traditional intervention points that are otherwise overlooked in conventional presurgical planning.

Structural brain connectivity data is obtained from diffusion MRI and T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging scans acquired using a 3 Tesla MRI scanner, with standardized protocols ensuring high spatial fidelity and reproducibility across subjects. The raw imaging data is processed using a validated pipeline known as SCRIPTS, which segments the brain into 84 anatomically defined regions, including 68 cortical regions derived from the Desikan-Killiany atlas and 16 subcortical structures such as the thalamus, hippocampus, amygdala, and cerebellar lobules. Each region is treated as a single node in the network model, and the strength of structural connectivity between any two nodes is quantified as the number of streamline fibers connecting them, normalized by the total number of streamlines in the brain to account for inter-subject variability in total fiber count. The physical distance between each pair of connected nodes is also computed from the tractography data to determine the conduction delay of neural signals, which is incorporated into the network model as a time-delay parameter. This results in a weighted, directed, and time-delayed adjacency matrix that fully characterizes the patient’s structural connectome.

The structural network is then imported into The Virtual Brain (TVB) platform, a validated open-source simulation environment capable of modeling large-scale neural dynamics. Within TVB, each brain region is represented by a six-dimensional Epileptor model, a phenomenological neural population model that reproduces the characteristic dynamics of seizure onset, propagation, and termination. The Epileptor model comprises five state variables and six parameters, including an excitability parameter (x0) that determines whether a node behaves as an epileptogenic zone, a propagation zone, or a non-involved region. For the purposes of this invention, the excitability parameter is set to -1.6 for all clinically identified epileptogenic zones, which is known to induce spontaneous, self-sustained oscillations consistent with ictal activity. For all other brain regions, the excitability parameter is calibrated to a range between -2.150 and -2.095, corresponding to the propagation zone phenotype, based on the patient’s individual structural connectivity profile. This calibration ensures that, in the absence of intervention, seizure activity originating from the epileptogenic zone will propagate to a large proportion of the brain, simulating the worst-case scenario in which the epileptogenic zone is highly connected and the surrounding tissue is highly susceptible to recruitment. This conservative assumption is critical to the method’s robustness, as it ensures that any identified target zone must be capable of suppressing propagation even under extreme network conditions.

To simulate the baseline dynamics of the epileptic brain, zero-mean Gaussian white noise with a standard deviation of 0.0003 is added to the second and fourth state variables of each Epileptor, replicating the stochastic background neural activity observed in vivo. This noise induces spontaneous interictal spikes in all nodes, providing a realistic baseline against which seizure propagation can be measured. The coupling between nodes is implemented through a permittivity-based coupling mechanism that models the extracellular field effects of slow synaptic dynamics, with coupling weights derived from the streamline-based connectivity matrix and time delays derived from the physical distance between nodes. The entire network is integrated over time using a fourth-order Runge-Kutta solver with a time step of 0.1 milliseconds, and simulated for a total duration of 10,000 seconds to ensure convergence to a stable dynamic regime and to capture multiple seizure-like events.

Following the construction of the dynamic network model, a constrained modularity analysis is performed to identify candidate target zones. Modularity analysis, based on Newman’s spectral algorithm, partitions the network into non-overlapping modules by maximizing the modularity coefficient Q, which quantifies the density of within-module connections relative to between-module connections. In this invention, the standard algorithm is modified to incorporate surgical constraints by enforcing that any node identified as an epileptogenic zone, or any node designated as inoperable due to its functional importance, must remain within the same module as its immediate neighbors. This is achieved by modifying the group membership variables during the spectral partitioning step: if an inoperable node and its adjacent nodes are assigned to different modules by the algorithm, their group memberships are forcibly realigned to match the majority assignment among the inoperable node and its direct neighbors. This constraint ensures that the inoperable zone does not become a bridge between modules, thereby preventing the algorithm from selecting it as a target for resection or disconnection. The resolution parameter α, which controls the granularity of the modular decomposition, is systematically swept from 0.5 to 1.5 in increments of 0.25, generating multiple modular structures for each patient. A higher resolution parameter yields a greater number of smaller modules, while a lower parameter results in fewer, larger modules. This parameter sweep is essential to the method’s flexibility, as it allows the identification of multiple candidate target zones that vary in number, location, and anatomical specificity, thereby providing clinicians with a spectrum of surgical options tailored to individual patient needs.

Within each modular structure, the module containing the epileptogenic zone is further subdivided into sub-modules using the same constrained modularity algorithm. The target zones are then defined as the set of nodes and edges that connect the sub-module containing the epileptogenic zone to all other sub-modules or modules. Nodes that serve as bridges between the epileptogenic sub-module and the rest of the network are designated as target nodes for resection, while the white matter tracts connecting these nodes to other modules are designated as target edges for disconnection. This dual approach allows the method to generate two distinct classes of surgical interventions: focal resection of critical hub regions or selective disconnection of key fiber tracts. The selection between resection and disconnection can be guided by clinical factors such as the accessibility of the target region, the risk of postoperative deficits, and the patient’s prior surgical history. Importantly, the method does not require the epileptogenic zone to be resected; instead, it focuses exclusively on interrupting the propagation pathways that enable seizures to spread beyond the epileptogenic zone, thereby preserving the integrity of the seizure-generating region while mitigating its clinical impact.

The modularity analysis is implemented in a custom MATLAB-based computational framework that automates the entire process, including the application of constraints, the parameter sweep, and the extraction of target nodes and edges. The output of this stage is a list of candidate target zones, each associated with a specific modular structure and resolution parameter. These candidate zones are then evaluated for their effectiveness in suppressing seizure propagation and their safety in preserving normal brain function through a series of in silico simulations.

For effectiveness evaluation, the network model is simulated under two conditions: one in which the original structural connectivity is intact, and another in which the target nodes are removed or the target edges are severed. The removal of a target node is modeled by setting all incoming and outgoing connection weights to zero, effectively isolating the node from the network. The disconnection of a target edge is modeled by setting the corresponding weight in the adjacency matrix to zero, while preserving all other connections. The seizure propagation dynamics are then compared between the two conditions by measuring the suppression ratio, defined as the percentage reduction in the number of brain regions recruited into seizure activity following the removal or disconnection of the target zone. The recruitment of a region into seizure activity is determined by detecting sustained oscillations in the first state variable of the Epileptor model that exceed a predefined amplitude threshold for a minimum duration of five seconds. The suppression ratio is calculated as the difference between the number of recruited regions in the pre-intervention simulation and the number of recruited regions in the post-intervention simulation, divided by the number of recruited regions in the pre-intervention simulation, multiplied by 100. A suppression ratio exceeding 80% is considered clinically significant, as it corresponds to a substantial reduction in the spatial extent of seizure spread, which is known to correlate with improved postoperative outcomes, particularly in terms of loss of consciousness and automatisms.

For safety evaluation, a separate set of simulations is performed to assess the impact of each candidate target zone on the brain’s capacity for normal signal transmission. Rather than modeling seizure dynamics, these simulations employ a generic two-dimensional neural oscillator model to replicate the transient, damped oscillatory responses observed in resting-state functional networks following external perturbation. Each oscillator is tuned to operate in close proximity to a supercritical Andronov-Hopf bifurcation, such that it remains quiescent at rest but generates a transient oscillation when perturbed by an external stimulus. The oscillators are coupled via the same structural connectivity matrix used in the Epileptor model, ensuring that the propagation of signals through the network is governed by the same anatomical constraints. To evaluate the integrity of eight well-established resting-state networks—including the default mode network, the visual network, the somatomotor network, the memory network, the ventral stream network, the dorsal attention network, the working memory network, and the auditory-phonological network—a brief electrical pulse of 2.5 seconds is applied to a specific cortical region previously identified in the literature as being optimally suited to elicit each network. The stimulation site for each network is selected based on empirical evidence from prior studies that demonstrated the ability of focal stimulation to reproduce the spatial and temporal signature of the corresponding resting-state network.

The spatiotemporal response pattern elicited by each stimulation is recorded across all 84 brain regions for a duration of 10 seconds following stimulus onset. The resulting time series are then subjected to mode-level cognitive subtraction analysis, a technique that identifies the dominant modes of network activation by performing principal component analysis on the covariance matrix of the response signals. The first three principal components are retained, as they capture the majority of the variance in the response trajectory. The response signals before and after the removal or disconnection of the target zone are then projected onto these principal components to reconstruct the spatial and temporal profile of the network activation. The similarity between the pre- and post-intervention response patterns is quantified using a similarity coefficient, defined as one minus the normalized deviation of the post-intervention power from the pre-intervention power, averaged across all brain regions. The similarity coefficient ranges from 0 to 1, where a value of 1 indicates perfect preservation of the response pattern, and a value below 0.75 is interpreted as a clinically significant disruption of the resting-state network. If the mean similarity coefficient across all stimulation sites falls below 0.75, the candidate target zone is deemed unsafe and is excluded from further consideration.

In cases where multiple nodes or edges are identified as candidate targets, the method identifies the critical node—the single node whose removal produces the most significant reduction in similarity coefficient—by iteratively removing each candidate node in isolation and re-evaluating the similarity coefficient for all eight resting-state networks. The node that yields the lowest mean similarity coefficient is designated as the critical node and is added to the list of inoperable zones. This updated list of inoperable zones is then fed back into the modularity analysis, triggering a new round of modular decomposition and target zone derivation. This feedback loop continues iteratively until a target zone is identified that achieves a suppression ratio above 80% while maintaining a mean similarity coefficient above 0.75 across all resting-state networks. The final output of the method is a set of one or more optimized target zones, each comprising a combination of resectable brain regions and disconnectable fiber tracts, that are guaranteed to suppress seizure propagation without compromising essential functional networks.

All simulations are performed on a high-performance computing cluster with dedicated memory and processing resources to ensure computational accuracy and reproducibility. The entire workflow, from image preprocessing to final target zone optimization, is fully automated and requires no manual intervention beyond the initial input of clinical data and the definition of epileptogenic and inoperable zones. The method is designed to be scalable and generalizable, with the capacity to accommodate additional constraints, such as the preservation of specific functional pathways or the avoidance of vascular structures, through the extension of the constraint module. The output of the method is presented in a clinically interpretable format, including 3D anatomical visualizations of the target zones overlaid on the patient’s structural MRI, a quantitative summary of the suppression ratio and similarity coefficient, and a ranked list of alternative target zones with their associated trade-offs between efficacy and safety. This output is intended to serve as a decision-support tool for neurosurgeons, enabling them to select the optimal surgical strategy based on individual patient anatomy, clinical priorities, and risk tolerance.

## Example 2: Target Zone Derivation

The derivation of target zones in accordance with this invention is a multi-stage, patient-specific process that begins with the identification of the epileptogenic zone and the imposition of surgical constraints based on clinical and anatomical considerations, and culminates in the extraction of a set of optimal surgical targets that maximize seizure suppression while minimizing functional disruption. Unlike conventional approaches that focus exclusively on the resection of the epileptogenic zone itself, this invention shifts the paradigm by treating the epileptogenic zone as a fixed, non-modifiable entity and instead seeks to identify the minimal set of intervening nodes and edges that, when altered, prevent the seizure activity from escaping the epileptogenic region and engaging widespread cortical and subcortical networks. This approach is particularly valuable in cases where the epileptogenic zone is located within or adjacent to eloquent cortex, such as the primary motor, sensory, language, or memory areas, where resection would inevitably lead to permanent neurological deficits.

The derivation process is initiated by defining the epileptogenic zone based on clinical data, including intracranial electroencephalography, structural MRI abnormalities, and metabolic imaging such as FDG-PET. In this invention, the epileptogenic zone is not treated as a single point or region but as a set of one or more anatomically contiguous brain regions that have been clinically determined to generate spontaneous seizure activity. For example, in a patient with mesial temporal lobe epilepsy, the epileptogenic zone may consist of the hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, and entorhinal cortex. In a patient with frontal lobe epilepsy, the epileptogenic zone may include the orbitofrontal cortex and the anterior cingulate. These regions are explicitly marked as inoperable in the computational model, ensuring that they are never selected as targets for resection or disconnection. This constraint is fundamental to the method’s novelty, as it forces the algorithm to search for alternative intervention points outside the epileptogenic zone, thereby expanding the surgical horizon beyond the traditional boundaries of resective epilepsy surgery.

Once the epileptogenic zone is defined, the method applies a series of constraints to the modularity analysis to ensure that the derived target zones are both anatomically plausible and surgically feasible. The first constraint is the enforcement of modular cohesion around the epileptogenic zone: during the spectral partitioning step of the modularity algorithm, any node identified as epileptogenic is prohibited from acting as a bridge between modules. This is accomplished by modifying the group membership variables of the epileptogenic node and its immediate neighbors to ensure they are assigned to the same module, regardless of the eigenvalue-based assignment. This constraint prevents the algorithm from selecting the epileptogenic zone as a hub connecting disparate functional networks, which would render it unsuitable as a target but also ensures that the surrounding tissue remains functionally integrated with the epileptogenic region. The second constraint is the exclusion of known eloquent regions from consideration as target zones. These regions are identified through preoperative functional mapping, including task-based fMRI, resting-state fMRI, and, when available, direct cortical stimulation during awake craniotomy. Regions such as Brodmann areas 4, 3, 1, 2, 44, 45, 41, 42, 22, 17, 18, 19, and the posterior cingulate cortex are flagged as inoperable and are treated identically to the epileptogenic zone, with their group memberships forcibly aligned with their neighbors during modularity analysis.

The modularity analysis is then performed across a range of resolution parameters, from 0.5 to 1.5 in increments of 0.25, to generate multiple modular decompositions of the brain network. Each decomposition yields a different number of modules, ranging from as few as four to as many as twelve, depending on the parameter value. At low resolution parameters, the network is partitioned into large, coarse modules that encompass broad functional systems such as the default mode network or the sensorimotor network. At high resolution parameters, the network is subdivided into smaller, more localized modules that correspond to fine-grained anatomical regions such as individual gyri or subcortical nuclei. The use of multiple resolution parameters is critical, as it allows the method to identify target zones at multiple spatial scales, from broad disconnection of major fiber tracts to precise resection of small hub regions. For example, a target zone derived at a low resolution parameter may consist of a single white matter tract connecting the temporal lobe to the frontal lobe, whereas a target zone derived at a high resolution parameter may consist of three small cortical nodes in the inferior frontal gyrus that serve as conduits for seizure spread from the temporal lobe to the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.

Within each modular structure, the module containing the epileptogenic zone is further subdivided into sub-modules using the same constrained modularity algorithm. This secondary subdivision is necessary because, in many cases, the epileptogenic zone occupies a large portion of a single module, making it impossible to isolate it from the rest of the network without directly targeting the zone itself. By subdividing the epileptogenic module into sub-modules, the method identifies the minimal set of connections that link the sub-module containing the epileptogenic zone to the rest of the brain. These connections are then designated as the target zones. The target nodes are the cortical or subcortical regions that serve as endpoints of these connections, and the target edges are the white matter tracts that physically link them. The distinction between target nodes and target edges is not arbitrary but reflects two distinct surgical strategies: resection, which involves the removal of a brain region, and disconnection, which involves the severing of a fiber tract. The method generates both types of targets simultaneously, allowing the clinician to choose the most appropriate intervention based on the patient’s individual anatomy and risk profile.

For example, in a patient with bilateral temporal lobe epilepsy and an epileptogenic zone located in the right parahippocampal gyrus, the modularity analysis may reveal that the epileptogenic sub-module is connected to the contralateral temporal lobe, the thalamus, and the posterior cingulate cortex via three distinct fiber tracts. The target nodes may include the right posterior cingulate cortex, the left superior temporal gyrus, and the right thalamus, while the target edges may include the cingulum bundle, the inferior longitudinal fasciculus, and the anterior thalamic radiation. The method then evaluates each of these candidates for their effectiveness in suppressing seizure propagation and their safety in preserving resting-state networks. In this case, the posterior cingulate cortex may be found to be highly effective in suppressing propagation but is also identified as a critical node for the default mode network, leading to its exclusion as a target. The thalamus may be found to be equally effective but is deemed too risky due to its role in arousal and consciousness. The superior temporal gyrus, however, may be found to suppress propagation with minimal impact on resting-state networks, making it the optimal target for resection.

The derivation process is not limited to a single solution. Because the modularity analysis is performed across multiple resolution parameters and because the constraint enforcement allows for multiple valid modular decompositions, the method generates a portfolio of candidate target zones for each patient. In one patient, the method may identify seven distinct target node options and nine distinct target edge options, each corresponding to a different modular structure and resolution parameter. These options are ranked according to their suppression ratio and similarity coefficient, and presented to the clinician as a decision tree. The clinician may then select the option that best balances the goals of seizure control and functional preservation. For instance, a patient who is a candidate for minimally invasive surgery may prefer a disconnection strategy involving a single fiber tract, even if it has a slightly lower suppression ratio, because it avoids the risks associated with cortical resection. Conversely, a patient with a history of failed resection surgery may prefer a multi-node resection strategy that offers a higher suppression ratio, even if it requires a more extensive procedure.

The derivation of target zones is further enhanced by the inclusion of anatomical and physiological plausibility filters. For example, any target node that lies within a region known to have low excitability, such as the cerebellum or the brainstem, is automatically flagged for review, as these regions are unlikely to play a significant role in seizure propagation despite their high connectivity. Similarly, any target edge that corresponds to a fiber tract with a low streamline count or a long conduction delay is given a lower priority, as it is unlikely to be a major conduit for rapid seizure spread. These filters are not hard constraints but rather weighting mechanisms that influence the ranking of candidate targets, ensuring that the final recommendations are grounded in both network topology and neurobiological reality.

The output of the target zone derivation stage is a comprehensive list of candidate surgical targets, each annotated with its anatomical location, its network role (hub, bridge, connector), its suppression ratio, its similarity coefficient, and its rank among all candidate options. The list is accompanied by a 3D visualization of the brain network, with the target nodes and edges highlighted in color-coded overlays that distinguish between resection and disconnection targets. The visualization is interactive, allowing the clinician to rotate the brain, zoom in on specific regions, and toggle between different candidate target zones. The list and visualization are exported in a standardized format compatible with surgical planning software, such as BrainVoyager, Slicer, or the TVB platform itself, enabling seamless integration into the preoperative workflow.

The derivation of target zones is not a static process but a dynamic one that evolves in response to the constraints imposed by the patient’s unique anatomy and clinical history. The method does not assume that there is a single “best” target zone for any given patient; rather, it recognizes that surgical decision-making is inherently multidimensional and that the optimal target zone is the one that best aligns with the patient’s values, the surgeon’s expertise, and the available surgical technology. By generating a diverse set of viable options, the method empowers clinicians to make informed, personalized decisions rather than relying on rigid, one-size-fits-all protocols.

## Example 3: Systematic Analysis According to an Epileptogenic Zone Location

The systematic analysis of target zone derivation across varying locations of the epileptogenic zone demonstrates the robustness, generalizability, and clinical relevance of the proposed method. In this analysis, the epileptogenic zone is systematically moved to every possible brain region in a cohort of seven patients, with each location treated as a hypothetical inoperable zone, and the target zones are derived using the full computational pipeline described in the preceding sections. This approach allows for the identification of consistent, recurrent patterns in the location and topology of target zones that are independent of the specific clinical presentation of any individual patient, thereby revealing the underlying network architecture that governs seizure propagation in the epileptic brain. The results of this analysis provide not only a validation of the method’s predictive power but also a clinically actionable atlas of high-yield surgical targets that can guide intervention in cases where the epileptogenic zone is uncertain or multifocal.

For each patient, the epileptogenic zone is iteratively placed in each of the 84 brain regions, resulting in 84 distinct simulations per patient and a total of 588 simulations across the cohort. In each simulation, the modularity analysis is performed with the designated region marked as inoperable, and the resulting target zones are recorded. The frequency with which each brain region or fiber tract is selected as a target zone across all 588 simulations is then computed, yielding a cumulative map of the most frequently derived target nodes and edges. This map reveals that certain regions are consistently selected as target zones regardless of the location of the epileptogenic zone, indicating that they serve as universal hubs in the propagation network. The most frequently derived target node across all simulations is the right postcentral gyrus, which appears as a target in over 80% of simulations. This region, which corresponds to the primary somatosensory cortex, is not typically considered a primary epileptogenic zone but is consistently identified as a critical conduit for seizure spread, particularly from temporal and frontal lobe origins. The second most frequent target node is the left supramarginal gyrus, followed by the right superior frontal gyrus, the left precentral gyrus, and the right thalamus. These regions are all part of the fronto-parietal network, which has been previously implicated in the loss of consciousness during temporal lobe seizures, further validating the method’s ability to identify clinically relevant targets.

The most frequently derived target edges are the connections between the left supramarginal gyrus and the left postcentral gyrus, the connection between the right superior frontal gyrus and the right precentral gyrus, and the connection between the right thalamus and the right posterior cingulate cortex. These fiber tracts correspond to the superior longitudinal fasciculus, the corticospinal tract, and the cingulum bundle, respectively—white matter pathways known to be involved in interhemispheric and cortico-subcortical communication. The consistency with which these tracts are identified as target zones across diverse epileptogenic zone locations underscores their role as critical bottlenecks in seizure propagation. Importantly, these findings are not artifacts of high connectivity alone; while the nodes with the highest degree (i.e., the highest number of connections) are often selected as target zones, the final target zones identified after the safety feedback loop are not strongly correlated with node strength. This indicates that the method does not simply select the most connected regions but rather identifies those that are most critical for propagation within the modular architecture of the epileptic brain.

The analysis also reveals a striking pattern in the identity of critical nodes—the nodes whose removal most severely disrupts resting-state networks and therefore must be excluded from consideration. Across all seven patients, the superior frontal gyrus is identified as the critical node in over 90% of simulations, regardless of the location of the epileptogenic zone. This region, which includes Brodmann areas 8, 9, and 10, is known to be a central hub in the default mode network, the working memory network, and the dorsal attention network, and is frequently involved in the generation of interictal spikes and the modulation of consciousness. The fact that it is consistently identified as a critical node, even when the epileptogenic zone is located in the occipital or cerebellar regions, suggests that its role in network integration is so fundamental that its removal would compromise multiple functional systems simultaneously. This finding has profound clinical implications: it implies that the superior frontal gyrus should be considered a universal contraindication for resection in epilepsy surgery, not because it is epileptogenic, but because it is a keystone node in the brain’s functional architecture.

The systematic analysis further demonstrates that the method is capable of identifying region-specific patterns of target zone derivation. For example, when the epileptogenic zone is located in the temporal lobe, the target zones are predominantly concentrated in the contralateral temporal lobe, the insula, and the thalamus, reflecting the known propensity of temporal lobe seizures to propagate along the limbic circuit. When the epileptogenic zone is located in the frontal lobe, the target zones shift toward the supplementary motor area, the precentral gyrus, and the corpus callosum, consistent with the rapid interhemispheric spread characteristic of frontal lobe seizures. When the epileptogenic zone is located in the occipital lobe, the target zones are primarily located in the parietal lobe and the posterior cingulate cortex, reflecting the strong connectivity between visual and default mode networks. These region-specific patterns are not only consistent with the known anatomy of seizure propagation but also provide a predictive framework for identifying target zones in patients with unclear or multifocal epileptogenic zones.

The method also reveals that the optimal target zone is not always the one that achieves the highest suppression ratio. In many cases, a target zone with a slightly lower suppression ratio but a significantly higher similarity coefficient is preferred, as it offers a better balance between efficacy and safety. For example, in one patient, the removal of the right precuneus achieved a suppression ratio of 94%, but the similarity coefficient dropped to 0.68, indicating a severe disruption of the default mode network. In contrast, the removal of the left inferior parietal lobule achieved a suppression ratio of 88% but maintained a similarity coefficient of 0.82, indicating minimal disruption to resting-state networks. The method correctly identifies the latter as the superior option, demonstrating its ability to prioritize functional preservation over maximal seizure suppression.

The cumulative results of the systematic analysis are presented as a 3D probability map, where each brain region and fiber tract is colored according to its frequency of selection as a target zone across all simulations. This map serves as a clinical atlas that can be used to guide surgical planning in real-world cases. For instance, if a patient presents with a suspected epileptogenic zone in the right temporal lobe but the exact location is uncertain, the surgeon can consult the atlas and prioritize the resection or disconnection of the right postcentral gyrus, the left supramarginal gyrus, and the cingulum bundle, as these are the most frequently derived targets across all locations. Similarly, if a patient has a history of postoperative memory deficits following anterior temporal lobectomy, the atlas can be used to avoid the resection of the left posterior cingulate cortex, which is identified as a critical node for memory network integrity.

The systematic analysis also provides insight into the limitations of conventional surgical approaches. In many cases, the target zones identified by the method are located far from the epileptogenic zone, sometimes in the contralateral hemisphere or in subcortical structures that are rarely targeted in standard epilepsy surgery. This suggests that the current paradigm of resecting the epileptogenic zone and its immediate surroundings may be fundamentally incomplete, as it ignores the network-level mechanisms that govern seizure spread. The method demonstrates that effective seizure control can be achieved without directly touching the epileptogenic zone, opening the door to entirely new classes of surgical interventions that are less invasive, more precise, and more broadly applicable.

The results of the systematic analysis are not limited to a single cohort or imaging modality. The method is designed to be generalizable across different patient populations, different imaging platforms, and different definitions of the epileptogenic zone. It has been validated across seven patients with diverse etiologies, including mesial temporal sclerosis, focal cortical dysplasia, and tuberous sclerosis, and has produced consistent results in each case. The method is also compatible with alternative network models, including those based on functional connectivity or dynamic causal modeling, and can be extended to incorporate additional constraints such as vascular anatomy, cortical thickness, or myelin content. The systematic analysis thus establishes the method not as a tool for a single patient or a single condition, but as a foundational framework for the future of personalized epilepsy surgery.

The clinical translation of these findings is immediate and profound. The identification of a universal set of target zones that are consistently effective across diverse epileptogenic zone locations suggests that a standardized surgical protocol can be developed based on network topology rather than lesion localization. This would represent a paradigm shift in epilepsy surgery, moving from a focus on the “where” of seizure onset to a focus on the “how” of seizure spread. The method enables the surgeon to ask not “Where is the seizure coming from?” but “How is the seizure spreading?” and to answer that question with precision, personalization, and predictability. The result is a new standard of care—one that is not bound by the limitations of current imaging or surgical technology, but that is guided by the intrinsic architecture of the brain itself.
