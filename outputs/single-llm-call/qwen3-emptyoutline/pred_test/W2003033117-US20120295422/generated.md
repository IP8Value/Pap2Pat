# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of light-emitting diodes based on III-nitride semiconductors has undergone substantial evolution over the past several decades, driven by the demand for energy-efficient solid-state lighting solutions capable of replacing traditional incandescent and fluorescent technologies. Gallium nitride (GaN) and its alloys, particularly indium gallium nitride (InGaN), have emerged as the dominant materials for the fabrication of blue and green light-emitting devices due to their direct bandgap tunability across the visible spectrum. Despite significant advances in epitaxial growth techniques, device architecture, and manufacturing scalability, the commercial viability of these devices remains constrained by two interrelated challenges: high production costs and suboptimal internal quantum efficiency, particularly in the green spectral region. The cost structure of GaN-based LEDs is heavily influenced by the substrate upon which the epitaxial layers are grown. Historically, sapphire and silicon carbide (SiC) have served as the primary substrates due to their thermal stability and lattice compatibility with GaN. However, sapphire is electrically insulating, necessitating complex lateral current injection schemes and limiting thermal dissipation, while SiC, though conductive and thermally robust, remains prohibitively expensive due to its difficult crystal growth and machining processes. These factors contribute significantly to the overall device cost, with substrate expenses accounting for between 25% and 35% of the total manufacturing cost in commercial production.

Silicon, by contrast, presents a compelling alternative substrate material owing to its low cost, large wafer availability, well-established fabrication infrastructure, and favorable thermal and electrical properties. The semiconductor industry’s decades-long investment in silicon processing technology enables the integration of GaN-based LEDs into existing CMOS-compatible manufacturing lines, offering a clear pathway to economies of scale. However, the implementation of GaN epilayers on silicon substrates introduces a host of technical challenges stemming from the large lattice mismatch (approximately 17%) and significant thermal expansion coefficient difference (approximately 40%) between GaN and silicon. These disparities lead to the formation of high-density threading dislocations, wafer bowing, and cracking during growth and post-growth cooling, which severely degrade the structural and optical quality of the active region. To mitigate these effects, researchers have developed sophisticated buffer layer architectures, typically involving low-temperature nucleation layers, aluminum nitride (AlN) or aluminum gallium nitride (AlGaN) interlayers, and graded composition transition layers designed to gradually accommodate strain and reduce defect density. While these approaches have enabled the successful growth of crack-free GaN layers on silicon, the integrity of the subsequent quantum well structures—particularly those containing high indium content necessary for green emission—remains vulnerable to thermal degradation during the growth of overlying barrier and contact layers.

The active region of modern GaN-based light-emitting diodes typically consists of multiple quantum wells (MQWs) composed of alternating thin layers of InGaN and GaN. The efficiency of these devices is critically dependent on the homogeneity of indium incorporation within the quantum wells and the abruptness of the interfaces between the wells and barriers. Achieving high indium content in InGaN is inherently difficult due to the lower growth temperature required to incorporate indium, which conflicts with the higher temperatures needed to grow high-quality GaN barrier layers. This thermal mismatch creates a kinetic challenge: when the growth temperature is increased to deposit the GaN barrier immediately following the InGaN well, residual thermal energy can induce surface diffusion, phase segregation, and partial decomposition of the InGaN layer, resulting in localized regions of indium depletion and compositional inhomogeneity. Such inhomogeneities manifest as non-radiative recombination centers, broadened photoluminescence linewidths, reduced internal quantum efficiency, and undesirable spectral shifts. Furthermore, the lack of a well-defined interface between the well and barrier leads to wavefunction overlap reduction, diminished carrier confinement, and increased electron leakage, all of which compromise device performance. These issues are particularly acute in green-emitting LEDs, where indium mole fractions exceeding 20% are required to achieve the desired emission wavelength, making the quantum wells even more susceptible to thermal instability.

Previous efforts to address these challenges have focused on modifying growth parameters such as ammonia flow rates, precursor partial pressures, growth rates, and temperature ramp profiles. Some approaches have employed pulsed growth techniques, surface passivation layers, or post-growth annealing treatments to improve indium retention. However, none of these methods have provided a robust, scalable, and universally applicable solution that simultaneously ensures structural integrity, compositional uniformity, and abrupt interface formation without introducing additional process complexity or compromising throughput. The introduction of a thin, in-situ grown GaN layer immediately following the InGaN quantum well and prior to the barrier layer has been suggested in limited contexts as a potential protective measure, but its thickness has not been systematically optimized, nor has its role in preserving indium content and interface abruptness been rigorously quantified across multiple characterization modalities. Consequently, there remains a critical unmet need in the art for a method of fabricating high-efficiency green InGaN/GaN multiple quantum well structures on silicon substrates that reliably prevents thermal degradation of the quantum wells during subsequent growth steps, enhances indium homogeneity, sharpens the well-barrier interface, and thereby significantly improves the optical performance of the resulting light-emitting device—all while maintaining compatibility with low-cost, large-area silicon substrate technology.

## SUMMARY

The present invention relates to a novel method for fabricating high-efficiency green light-emitting diodes based on gallium nitride and its alloys, wherein the structural and optical quality of indium gallium nitride quantum wells grown on silicon substrates is substantially enhanced through the precise control of a thin gallium nitride cap layer inserted between each quantum well and its subsequent barrier layer. The invention is directed to a layered semiconductor structure and a corresponding epitaxial growth process that enables the preservation of indium content within the quantum wells during the thermal transition from well growth to barrier growth, thereby achieving a more homogeneous indium distribution, an abrupt and well-defined quantum well/barrier interface, and a significant improvement in photoluminescence intensity and spectral narrowness. The key innovation lies in the recognition that a gallium nitride cap layer, grown at the same temperature as the underlying InGaN quantum well and with a thickness between 12 angstroms and 35 angstroms, acts as a thermally protective barrier that mitigates surface decomposition and indium segregation during the subsequent temperature ramp to the higher barrier growth temperature. This cap layer is not merely a passive structural element but an active component in the epitaxial sequence that fundamentally alters the kinetic and thermodynamic behavior of the indium atoms at the quantum well surface during the critical phase transition between growth stages.

In one embodiment, the invention comprises a semiconductor light-emitting device comprising a silicon substrate, a buffer layer stack grown on the substrate to accommodate lattice and thermal mismatch, a plurality of alternating indium gallium nitride quantum well layers and gallium nitride barrier layers, wherein each quantum well layer is immediately followed by a gallium nitride cap layer having a thickness of at least 12 angstroms and not exceeding 35 angstroms, and wherein the cap layer is grown at the same temperature as the quantum well layer prior to increasing the growth temperature to form the subsequent barrier layer. The cap layer is grown using metalorganic chemical vapor deposition with trimethylgallium and ammonia as precursors, under conditions that ensure conformal, defect-free, and stoichiometric growth without incorporating indium. The quantum well layers are grown at a temperature ranging from 720°C to 780°C, and the barrier layers are grown at a temperature 100°C to 140°C higher than the well growth temperature. The cap layer thickness is selected to be sufficient to prevent thermal decomposition of the indium gallium nitride quantum well during the temperature ramp, yet thin enough to avoid introducing excessive strain, optical absorption, or carrier scattering that would degrade device performance. The resulting structure exhibits a marked reduction in compositional inhomogeneity, as evidenced by uniform photoluminescence emission across the wafer surface, and a well-resolved periodicity in X-ray diffraction satellite peaks, indicating abrupt and coherent interfaces between the quantum wells and barriers. The photoluminescence spectra of devices fabricated according to this method demonstrate a redshift in emission wavelength consistent with higher indium incorporation, a narrowing of the full width at half maximum by at least 25% compared to control structures lacking such a cap layer, and an increase in peak intensity by a factor of 1.8 or greater. These improvements are directly attributable to the cap layer’s ability to stabilize the quantum well surface, suppress surface diffusion of indium, and provide a chemically and structurally compatible platform for the subsequent growth of the barrier layer.

The invention further encompasses a method of manufacturing the above-described light-emitting device, comprising the steps of providing a silicon substrate, depositing a buffer layer stack comprising at least one aluminum nitride or aluminum gallium nitride stress-relieving layer and a gallium nitride nucleation layer, growing a plurality of quantum well-barrier periods, wherein each period comprises depositing an indium gallium nitride quantum well layer at a first temperature, depositing a gallium nitride cap layer at the same first temperature with a thickness between 12 and 35 angstroms, and then increasing the reactor temperature by 100°C to 140°C to deposit a gallium nitride barrier layer. The cap layer is grown for a duration sufficient to achieve the specified thickness, typically between 10 and 45 seconds, depending on the growth rate, and is deposited without interruption of precursor flow to ensure a seamless interface. The method further includes the optional step of capping the final barrier layer with a magnesium-doped gallium nitride p-type layer, followed by standard device processing including contact formation, mesa etching, and passivation. The method is compatible with standard metalorganic chemical vapor deposition systems and does not require additional equipment, exotic precursors, or post-growth treatments, making it readily scalable to industrial production. The resulting devices exhibit superior performance characteristics, including higher external quantum efficiency, reduced efficiency droop at high injection currents, and improved thermal stability, all of which are essential for the commercial viability of green LEDs in general lighting and display applications. The invention thus provides a simple, cost-effective, and highly effective solution to one of the most persistent challenges in III-nitride optoelectronics, enabling the fabrication of high-performance green LEDs on low-cost silicon substrates without compromising structural integrity or optical output.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The present invention provides a comprehensive and novel approach to the fabrication of high-efficiency green light-emitting diodes based on gallium nitride and its ternary alloy, indium gallium nitride, grown on silicon substrates. The core of the invention resides in the strategic insertion of a thin, in-situ grown gallium nitride cap layer between each indium gallium nitride quantum well and its subsequent gallium nitride barrier layer during metalorganic chemical vapor deposition. This cap layer, which is grown at the same temperature as the quantum well and with a precisely controlled thickness ranging from 12 to 35 angstroms, serves as a thermally protective interlayer that prevents the decomposition and indium segregation of the quantum well during the temperature ramp required to initiate barrier growth. The invention is grounded in the empirical discovery that conventional growth sequences, in which the temperature is abruptly increased immediately after quantum well formation, induce surface mobility of indium atoms, leading to localized depletion zones, phase separation, and a loss of quantum well uniformity. The cap layer acts as a kinetic barrier that stabilizes the quantum well surface, effectively “freezing” the indium distribution before the thermal excursion occurs, thereby preserving the intended composition and morphology of the quantum well. This mechanism is not merely a function of physical shielding but involves the suppression of surface reactions, the reduction of desorption rates of indium-containing species, and the minimization of interfacial strain accumulation that would otherwise propagate into the barrier layer.

The structural architecture of the device begins with a silicon substrate, preferably oriented in the (111) crystallographic direction, which is chosen for its cost-effectiveness, large-diameter availability, and compatibility with existing semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure. Prior to epitaxial growth, the substrate undergoes a series of surface preparation steps including chemical cleaning with sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide, followed by dilute hydrofluoric acid etching to remove native oxides and contaminants. The substrate is then loaded into a metalorganic chemical vapor deposition reactor, where it is subjected to an in-situ high-temperature hydrogen anneal at approximately 1100°C to ensure surface reconstruction and removal of residual impurities. Following this, a thin aluminum film is deposited via trimethylaluminum precursor at a temperature of about 800°C to serve as a nucleation barrier that prevents silicon diffusion into the growing GaN layer and inhibits the formation of silicon nitride due to reaction with ammonia. This aluminum layer is subsequently converted into a high-quality aluminum nitride or aluminum gallium nitride stress-relieving layer, which is grown to a thickness of approximately 0.3 micrometers to accommodate the lattice and thermal mismatch between silicon and gallium nitride. Over this buffer layer, a thick n-type gallium nitride layer doped with silicon is deposited to a thickness of about 3 micrometers, forming the foundation for the active region and providing a low-resistance current path.

The active region of the device consists of six periods of indium gallium nitride quantum wells separated by gallium nitride barrier layers. Each quantum well is grown to a thickness of approximately 2.5 to 3.5 nanometers at a temperature between 720°C and 780°C, using trimethylindium and trimethylgallium as indium and gallium precursors, respectively, with ammonia as the nitrogen source. The indium mole fraction in the quantum well is carefully controlled to achieve an emission wavelength in the green region of the spectrum, typically between 510 and 540 nanometers. Immediately following the completion of the quantum well growth, without any interruption in precursor flow or change in reactor conditions, a gallium nitride cap layer is deposited at the identical temperature as the quantum well. The cap layer is grown for a duration sufficient to achieve a thickness between 12 and 35 angstroms, corresponding to a growth time of approximately 15 to 40 seconds, depending on the specific growth rate of the MOCVD system. This cap layer is composed exclusively of gallium and nitrogen, with no intentional incorporation of indium, and is grown under the same gas flow ratios and pressure conditions as the quantum well to ensure a seamless, atomically abrupt interface. The purpose of this cap layer is not to act as a functional optical layer but to serve as a protective monolayer that shields the underlying quantum well from thermal degradation during the subsequent temperature increase. After the cap layer is deposited, the reactor temperature is ramped upward by 100°C to 140°C to reach the barrier growth temperature, typically between 820°C and 920°C, at which point the gallium nitride barrier layer is deposited to a thickness of approximately 12 to 18 nanometers. This barrier layer is undoped or lightly doped with silicon to maintain high electron mobility and to ensure effective carrier confinement within the quantum wells.

The entire sequence of quantum well, cap layer, and barrier layer is repeated six times to form the multiple quantum well structure. Following the final barrier layer, a p-type gallium nitride layer doped with magnesium is deposited to a thickness of approximately 130 nanometers to serve as the hole injection layer. This layer is grown at a temperature slightly lower than the barrier growth temperature to facilitate magnesium activation and to minimize the risk of magnesium diffusion into the quantum wells. The resulting epitaxial stack is then processed using standard semiconductor fabrication techniques, including photolithography, mesa etching, dielectric passivation, and metal contact deposition to form the anode and cathode electrodes. The final device is characterized using a suite of analytical techniques, including double crystal X-ray diffraction, photoluminescence spectroscopy, and fluorescence microscopy, all of which confirm the superior structural and optical quality of the quantum well structure compared to control devices lacking the cap layer.

The invention’s efficacy is demonstrated through a comparative analysis of three distinct device structures, each differing only in the thickness of the gallium nitride cap layer: a control sample with an 8-angstrom cap layer, a first experimental sample with a 15-angstrom cap layer, and a second experimental sample with a 30-angstrom cap layer. The 8-angstrom cap layer, while nominally present, is found to be insufficient to prevent thermal decomposition of the quantum well, as evidenced by the absence of satellite peaks in X-ray diffraction, the presence of localized bright spots in fluorescence images, and a broad, low-intensity photoluminescence peak. In contrast, the 15-angstrom and 30-angstrom cap layers both exhibit clear satellite peaks corresponding to the first through fourth orders of the multiple quantum well periodicity, indicating the formation of a coherent and abrupt interface structure. The 30-angstrom cap layer demonstrates the most pronounced improvements, with the highest photoluminescence intensity, the narrowest full width at half maximum, and the most uniform fluorescence emission across the wafer surface. These results confirm that the cap layer thickness must exceed a critical threshold—approximately 12 angstroms—to effectively stabilize the quantum well, and that further increases in thickness up to 35 angstroms continue to yield incremental benefits without introducing detrimental effects such as excessive strain, optical absorption, or carrier scattering. The optimal thickness range of 12 to 35 angstroms represents a balance between sufficient thermal protection and minimal disruption to the electronic and optical properties of the active region.

The invention is distinguished from prior art by its recognition that the cap layer must be grown at the same temperature as the quantum well, rather than at an intermediate or higher temperature, and that its thickness must be precisely controlled within the narrow range of 12 to 35 angstroms. Previous attempts to improve quantum well quality have focused on modifying the growth temperature, pressure, or precursor flow rates, or on employing post-growth annealing or surface treatments, none of which have proven effective in preventing the intrinsic thermal decomposition that occurs during the temperature ramp. The present invention introduces a new paradigm in epitaxial growth strategy, wherein the cap layer is not an afterthought or an auxiliary feature but an essential, integral component of the quantum well formation process. The cap layer’s role is not to contribute to light emission, nor to act as a current spreading layer, nor to serve as a strain-relieving medium, but to function as a transient, atomically thin protective shield that enables the quantum well to retain its intended composition and morphology during the critical transition to barrier growth. This insight, combined with the precise control of thickness and growth conditions, results in a device architecture that is simultaneously simpler, more reliable, and more performant than any previously disclosed method.

The implications of this invention extend far beyond the immediate application in green LEDs. The principles underlying the cap layer strategy are directly transferable to other III-nitride device architectures, including blue and violet LEDs, laser diodes, and high-electron-mobility transistors, where the preservation of indium content and interface abruptness is equally critical. The method is compatible with both silicon and sapphire substrates, and can be readily adapted to other growth techniques such as molecular beam epitaxy or hydride vapor phase epitaxy, provided that the temperature and precursor flow conditions can be precisely controlled. Furthermore, the invention eliminates the need for complex buffer layer engineering, post-growth annealing, or additional processing steps, thereby reducing manufacturing cost, improving yield, and enhancing reproducibility across production batches. The resulting devices exhibit higher brightness, longer operational lifetime, and improved thermal stability, making them ideal candidates for next-generation solid-state lighting, automotive illumination, horticultural lighting, and full-color display applications. The invention thus represents a fundamental advancement in the field of III-nitride optoelectronics, providing a scalable, cost-effective, and high-performance solution to one of the most enduring challenges in the development of efficient green light-emitting devices.

## EXAMPLE

In a specific embodiment of the invention, a gallium nitride-based green light-emitting diode was fabricated on a silicon (111) substrate using a metalorganic chemical vapor deposition system equipped with a close-coupled showerhead reactor and capable of precise temperature and precursor flow control. The substrate, measuring 100 millimeters in diameter, was first cleaned using a 3:1 mixture of sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide for 10 minutes at room temperature to remove organic contaminants, followed by a 5-minute immersion in a 5% aqueous hydrofluoric acid solution to eliminate the native oxide layer. The substrate was then rinsed with deionized water and dried under a nitrogen stream before being loaded into the MOCVD reactor. Upon sealing the reactor, the substrate was heated under a continuous flow of hydrogen gas at a rate of 5 liters per minute to a temperature of 1100°C and held at this temperature for 30 minutes to ensure surface reconstruction and outgassing of residual impurities.

Following the anneal, the temperature was reduced to 800°C, and trimethylaluminum was introduced into the reactor at a flow rate of 15 micromoles per minute for a duration of 2 minutes, resulting in the deposition of a thin aluminum film approximately 1 to 2 nanometers thick on the silicon surface. This aluminum layer was then converted into an aluminum nitride layer by switching the precursor flow to ammonia at a rate of 1.2 liters per minute while maintaining the temperature at 800°C for an additional 5 minutes. Subsequently, the temperature was increased to 1050°C, and trimethylgallium and ammonia were introduced at flow rates of 12 micromoles per minute and 1.8 liters per minute, respectively, to grow a 0.3-micrometer-thick aluminum gallium nitride stress-relieving layer with an aluminum mole fraction of 0.15. This layer was followed by the deposition of a 3-micrometer-thick n-type gallium nitride layer doped with silicon, using silane as the dopant precursor at a flow rate of 15 nanomoles per minute. The n-GaN layer was grown at a temperature of 1060°C to ensure high crystallinity and low dislocation density.

The active region was then formed by the sequential growth of six periods of indium gallium nitride quantum wells and gallium nitride barrier layers. For each period, the temperature was first reduced to 750°C, and trimethylindium and trimethylgallium were introduced at flow rates of 1.8 micromoles per minute and 8.5 micromoles per minute, respectively, to grow a 3.0-nanometer-thick indium gallium nitride quantum well. The indium mole fraction in this layer was estimated to be approximately 0.22 based on the growth conditions and subsequent photoluminescence measurements. Immediately following the completion of the quantum well growth, without any change in temperature or interruption in precursor flow, the trimethylindium flow was terminated while the trimethylgallium and ammonia flows were maintained at the same rates as during quantum well growth. Under these conditions, a gallium nitride cap layer was deposited for a duration of 25 seconds, resulting in a thickness of 15 angstroms. The cap layer was confirmed to be stoichiometric and free of indium incorporation through secondary ion mass spectrometry and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy.

After the cap layer deposition, the reactor temperature was ramped upward at a rate of 10°C per minute until it reached 870°C, at which point the gallium nitride barrier layer was grown for a duration of 45 seconds, yielding a thickness of 15 nanometers. The barrier layer was grown under the same ammonia flow rate but with a reduced trimethylgallium flow of 7.2 micromoles per minute to ensure stoichiometric growth and minimize unintentional doping. This quantum well–cap layer–barrier sequence was repeated six times to form the complete multiple quantum well structure. Following the final barrier layer, the temperature was reduced to 820°C, and trimethylgallium and ammonia were introduced at flow rates of 10 micromoles per minute and 1.6 liters per minute, respectively, while dimethylmagnesium was introduced at a flow rate of 12 nanomoles per minute to deposit a 130-nanometer-thick p-type gallium nitride layer. The magnesium doping concentration in this layer was measured to be approximately 1.2 × 10¹⁸ cm⁻³, sufficient to achieve low-resistivity p-type conduction after post-growth annealing.

The resulting epitaxial structure was characterized using a suite of analytical techniques. Double crystal X-ray diffraction measurements were performed using a Rigaku SmartLab system with a Cu Kα source and a double-crystal monochromator. The rocking curve scans centered on the GaN (0002) reflection revealed the presence of clear satellite peaks on the low-angle side of the main peak for the sample with the 15-angstrom cap layer, corresponding to the first through fourth orders of the multiple quantum well periodicity. The full width at half maximum of the GaN (0002) peak was measured at 280 arcseconds, indicating high crystalline quality, while the separation between the first and second satellite peaks was 1250 arcseconds, consistent with the expected period of the quantum well structure. In contrast, a control sample grown without a cap layer exhibited no discernible satellite peaks, and the GaN peak was broader at 410 arcseconds, indicating higher defect density and poor periodicity. A second control sample with an 8-angstrom cap layer showed only faint, unresolved satellite features, confirming that the cap layer must exceed a minimum thickness of approximately 12 angstroms to be effective.

Fluorescence microscopy imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse Ti microscope equipped with a 40× objective, a 430-nanometer dichroic mirror, and a 450-nanometer barrier filter. Excitation was provided by a 405-nanometer laser diode. The sample with the 15-angstrom cap layer exhibited a highly uniform emission pattern across the entire wafer surface, with no localized bright spots or dark regions. In contrast, the control sample without a cap layer displayed pronounced non-uniformities, with discrete bright spots distributed randomly across the image, indicative of localized indium segregation and quantum well decomposition. The sample with the 8-angstrom cap layer showed intermediate uniformity, with some reduction in bright spots but still noticeable local variations. These observations were corroborated by photoluminescence measurements using a 325-nanometer helium-cadmium laser as the excitation source. The sample with the 15-angstrom cap layer exhibited a photoluminescence peak at 525 nanometers, with a full width at half maximum of 38 nanometers and a peak intensity 2.1 times greater than that of the control sample. The sample with the 30-angstrom cap layer showed a further redshift to 530 nanometers and a narrowing of the full width at half maximum to 34 nanometers, with a peak intensity 2.4 times greater than the control. The sample with the 8-angstrom cap layer exhibited a peak at 505 nanometers, a broad full width at half maximum of 52 nanometers, and a peak intensity only 1.1 times that of the control, confirming the insufficient protective effect of the thinner cap layer.

The optical cavity effects observed in the photoluminescence spectra, manifesting as interference fringes, were consistent across all samples and were attributed to the Fabry-Pérot resonance between the GaN-air and GaN-silicon interfaces. The amplitude of these fringes increased with cap layer thickness, indicating improved optical coherence and reduced scattering losses in the quantum well structure. The redshift in emission wavelength with increasing cap thickness is attributed to the preservation of higher indium content in the quantum wells, as confirmed by compositional analysis via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy in scanning transmission electron microscopy. The narrowing of the photoluminescence linewidth is directly correlated with the reduction in compositional fluctuations and the improvement in interface abruptness, both of which enhance radiative recombination efficiency and reduce non-radiative recombination centers.

The device was subsequently processed into a light-emitting diode by standard photolithographic patterning, mesa etching using chlorine-based chemistry, dielectric passivation with silicon dioxide, and the deposition of titanium/aluminum/nickel/gold contacts for the n- and p-layers. The resulting device exhibited a forward voltage of 3.2 volts at 20 milliamperes, a luminous flux of 18.5 lumens per watt at room temperature, and a peak external quantum efficiency of 42%, significantly exceeding the performance of commercial green LEDs grown on sapphire substrates using conventional growth techniques. The device demonstrated stable operation over 1000 hours of continuous testing at 350 milliamperes, with less than 5% degradation in output power, confirming the enhanced thermal and structural stability imparted by the cap layer. These results conclusively demonstrate that the invention provides a highly effective, scalable, and industrially viable method for fabricating high-efficiency green light-emitting diodes on low-cost silicon substrates, overcoming the longstanding challenges associated with indium segregation and interface degradation in III-nitride quantum well structures.
