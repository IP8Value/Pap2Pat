# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of mass spectrometry has undergone profound transformation over the past several decades, particularly through the development of ionization techniques that enable the analysis of large, non-volatile biomolecules. Among these, matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry has emerged as a cornerstone technology for the rapid, high-throughput characterization of peptides, proteins, and other biomolecular species. The inherent robustness, simplicity of sample preparation, and compatibility with complex biological matrices have rendered MALDI-TOF instruments indispensable in clinical diagnostics, microbial identification, proteomics, and natural product analysis. Modern MALDI-TOF platforms are typically equipped with reflectron optics and delayed extraction systems, which collectively enhance mass resolution and accuracy to levels previously considered unattainable for such instruments. Under optimal conditions, these systems routinely achieve resolution values exceeding 15,000 (full-width at half-maximum) and mass accuracy better than five parts per million when calibrated using internal standards. Such performance metrics are sufficient to support critical applications such as peptide mass fingerprinting, wherein the identification of unknown proteins relies on the precise matching of experimentally derived peptide masses against theoretical databases.

Despite these impressive specifications, a persistent and previously unexplained inconsistency has been observed across multiple commercial MALDI-TOF platforms, regardless of manufacturer or model. When replicate spectra of the same biological sample are acquired under identical experimental conditions—using the same calibration standards, laser energy, spot location, and acquisition parameters—the measured masses of individual peptide ions exhibit significant and unpredictable deviations. These deviations frequently exceed the manufacturer’s stated accuracy limits, with a substantial proportion of individual measurements falling outside the five parts per million threshold, even when internal calibration is applied. In some cases, the observed mass errors range from less than one part per million to over twenty parts per million, with no discernible pattern or correlation between the magnitude of error and the mass-to-charge ratio of the ion. Furthermore, these errors are not consistent across replicate measurements of the same peptide; instead, they appear to vary randomly from spectrum to spectrum, even when the underlying sample composition remains unchanged. This variability cannot be attributed to sample degradation, contamination, or instrumental drift, as repeated analyses of the same aliquot under controlled environmental conditions yield identical patterns of inconsistency. Moreover, increasing the number of laser shots per spectrum, a common strategy employed to improve signal-to-noise ratios, has no measurable effect on the reproducibility of mass measurements. This observation suggests that the source of the discrepancy lies not in the physical ionization or detection process per se, but rather in the digital representation and processing of the acquired data.

The underlying mechanism for this phenomenon was initially hypothesized to stem from variations in the timing of ion flight, fluctuations in the laser pulse energy, or imperfections in the calibration algorithm. However, systematic investigations revealed that the calibration function, when reapplied to the same raw data set multiple times, consistently produces identical mass assignments and calibration curves. This finding effectively eliminates the calibration algorithm as a primary source of variability. Similarly, the use of external calibration versus internal calibration, while influencing signal intensity and charge competition dynamics, does not eliminate the observed dispersion in mass measurements. Further analysis of the raw time-of-flight data revealed a consistent and systematic pattern: the intensity profile of every detected ion peak, regardless of mass, is not continuous but rather composed of discrete, quantized steps. These steps correspond to the binning of the analog signal by the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) system, which samples the detector output at fixed intervals determined by the instrument’s internal clock frequency. The width of these bins, measured in nanoseconds of flight time, translates into a mass resolution limit that varies with the mass-to-charge ratio of the ion. For example, at lower masses, the bin size corresponds to approximately 0.0178 atomic mass units, while at higher masses, the bin size increases proportionally to approximately 0.0272 atomic mass units. These bin widths are not fixed across the entire mass range but scale in a predictable manner consistent with the physics of ion flight in a time-of-flight analyzer. Importantly, the magnitude of the observed mass variability in replicate spectra—typically ranging from 0.010 to 0.014 atomic mass units—closely aligns with the size of these discrete bins, suggesting that the ADC quantization process is not merely a limitation of resolution, but a primary contributor to the observed measurement inconsistency.

The critical insight emerged from the observation that, with each new spectrum acquisition, the ADC system resets its internal timing reference and redefines the boundaries of the bins relative to the detected ion arrival times. This reset occurs within the inherent electronic tolerance of the system, typically less than the width of a single bin, but sufficient to cause a slight, random lateral shift in the position of the digitized signal relative to the true ion arrival time. Consequently, the interpolation algorithms used to determine peak centroids—essential for deriving accurate mass assignments—are applied to slightly displaced data sets with each acquisition. Because the binning is discontinuous and the peak shape is inherently continuous, the centroid position derived from one spectrum may differ from that of another, even when the underlying physical ion arrival time remains unchanged. This effect is analogous to sampling a continuous sine wave with a discrete grid that shifts slightly between measurements; the resulting amplitude and phase estimates will vary, even though the underlying signal is identical. The variability observed in MALDI-TOF mass measurements is therefore not a failure of the instrument’s physical components, but rather an unavoidable consequence of the interaction between a continuous physical phenomenon and a discretized digital detection system. This phenomenon has been historically overlooked in commercial software implementations, which assume that the binning process is stable and that the centroid of a peak can be reliably determined from a single acquisition. The consequence of this assumption is that the reported mass accuracy of MALDI-TOF instruments often reflects theoretical performance under idealized conditions rather than the practical, real-world reproducibility experienced by users.

This limitation has profound implications for applications that demand high confidence in mass measurements, particularly in clinical diagnostics and proteomic discovery workflows. In peptide mass fingerprinting, for instance, a single mass measurement with an error of ten parts per million may lead to the misidentification of a protein, especially when the database contains multiple candidates with similar peptide masses. In microbial identification, where spectral libraries are built from thousands of reference spectra, the presence of unexplained variability in mass assignments can reduce the discriminatory power of the system, leading to false negatives or ambiguous identifications. Moreover, the inability to predict or correct for these errors on a per-spectrum basis renders traditional quality control measures ineffective. There is currently no established method to determine, a priori, whether a given mass measurement is reliable or whether it is an outlier due to binning artifacts. As a result, researchers and clinicians are forced to rely on subjective interpretation, manual validation, or orthogonal confirmation techniques such as tandem mass spectrometry, which are time-consuming, costly, and not always feasible in high-throughput settings. The persistence of this issue across multiple instrument platforms and manufacturers indicates that it is not a design flaw specific to any one model, but rather a fundamental characteristic of the MALDI-TOF architecture as currently implemented. The absence of a practical solution to this problem has hindered the full realization of MALDI-TOF’s theoretical potential and has contributed to the gradual displacement of MALDI-TOF by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry platforms in many research laboratories. However, MALDI-TOF retains distinct advantages in speed, cost, and simplicity, and the development of a method to overcome this intrinsic limitation would not only restore its competitive viability but also enhance its reliability in critical applications.

## SUMMARY

The present invention discloses a novel method for enhancing the accuracy and reproducibility of mass measurements obtained from matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometers by leveraging the statistical properties of the inherent variability in analog-to-digital conversion and calibration processes. This method is based on the discovery that the observed inconsistencies in peptide mass assignments across replicate spectra are not attributable to instrumental malfunction, sample heterogeneity, or calibration instability, but rather arise from the random, sub-bin-level repositioning of digitized signal bins during each independent acquisition cycle. These repositioning events, which occur due to the electronic reset of the analog-to-digital converter’s timing reference with each laser shot sequence, result in minor but statistically significant shifts in the apparent peak centroid of each ion signal. When analyzed individually, these shifts produce mass measurements that deviate unpredictably from the true ion mass, often exceeding the instrument’s specified accuracy limits. However, when multiple replicate spectra are acquired and their mass measurements for each peptide are aggregated into a population, the deviations conform to a normal Gaussian distribution, indicating that the errors are random and independent in nature. The invention exploits this statistical behavior by applying descriptive statistical analysis to the population of mass measurements derived from multiple independent spectra, thereby enabling the calculation of a mean mass value that is significantly more accurate and consistent than any single measurement. The mean mass, when calculated from a sufficient number of spectra, converges toward the true theoretical mass of the ion, effectively canceling out the random binning artifacts that plague individual measurements.

The method further incorporates the use of standard deviation as a diagnostic tool to identify mass measurements that exhibit elevated variability, which are indicative of potential measurement errors or anomalous ion behavior. Peptides with standard deviations exceeding a predefined threshold—derived empirically from control samples such as standard protein digests—are flagged as high-risk measurements, suggesting that their individual mass values are unreliable and should be excluded from downstream analyses such as peptide mass fingerprinting or protein identification. This feature provides a quantitative, objective criterion for data filtering that was previously unavailable in MALDI-TOF workflows. Additionally, the invention enables the identification of systematic errors that manifest as consistent directional shifts across multiple spectra, which cannot be corrected by averaging but can be detected through outlier analysis tools such as the Grubbs test. The method is applicable to any MALDI-TOF instrument equipped with a digital detector and internal calibration capability, regardless of manufacturer or model, and does not require hardware modifications, additional reagents, or changes to sample preparation protocols. It is fully compatible with existing data acquisition software and can be implemented as a software update that operates in the background during routine data collection, automatically aggregating spectra, computing descriptive statistics, and generating corrected mass values without user intervention.

The invention has been validated through extensive experimentation using both standard protein mixtures and complex biological samples derived from immunoprecipitation experiments. In all cases, the application of spectral averaging and statistical analysis resulted in a marked improvement in mass accuracy, with the mean mass values of peptides consistently falling within one to three parts per million of their theoretical masses, even when individual measurements exhibited errors exceeding ten parts per million. The method also significantly enhanced the confidence and success rate of protein identification via peptide mass fingerprinting, enabling the correct identification of target proteins even in the presence of contaminating peptides from unrelated proteins. Furthermore, the technique was shown to be effective across a broad mass range, from low-mass peptides below 1,000 daltons to higher-mass ions approaching 6,000 daltons, demonstrating its scalability and general applicability. The approach is equally valid for both reflectron and linear detection modes, as the underlying source of variability—the analog-to-digital binning process—is common to all modern MALDI-TOF platforms. The invention also extends to other time-of-flight-based mass spectrometry systems, including liquid chromatography-time-of-flight (LC-TOF) and liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time-of-flight (LC-Q-TOF) instruments, provided that they employ discrete sampling of the ion signal and utilize internal calibration. The method does not require increased acquisition time per sample, as it operates by aggregating data from multiple spectra that are already routinely acquired during standard workflows. In fact, the total number of laser shots per sample remains unchanged; the innovation lies solely in the post-acquisition processing of the data. This makes the method highly practical, cost-effective, and immediately implementable in both research and clinical laboratories without requiring capital investment in new instrumentation.

The significance of this invention extends beyond the mere improvement of mass accuracy. It transforms MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry from a technique that relies on subjective interpretation and empirical calibration into one that delivers quantifiable, statistically validated data. By introducing a principled, data-driven framework for assessing measurement reliability, the invention enables the automation of quality control procedures that were previously impossible. It provides a mechanism for distinguishing between true biological variability and instrumental artifact, thereby improving the robustness of downstream analyses such as biomarker discovery, pathogen identification, and clinical diagnostics. The method also enhances the reproducibility of results across different laboratories and instruments, facilitating data sharing and standardization in multi-center studies. Moreover, the ability to flag high-variability measurements allows for the prioritization of tandem mass spectrometry validation only on those peptides that are most uncertain, thereby optimizing resource allocation and reducing unnecessary analysis. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in the interpretation of MALDI-TOF data, replacing ad hoc, qualitative assessment with a rigorous, quantitative, and universally applicable statistical methodology that elevates the performance of existing instruments to their theoretical limits.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention relates to a method for improving the accuracy and reproducibility of mass measurements in matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry by employing statistical aggregation and analysis of multiple replicate spectra. This method is grounded in the discovery that the primary source of variability in mass assignments is not due to instrumental instability, sample degradation, or calibration error, but rather arises from the inherent discretization of the analog ion signal by the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) system during each independent acquisition cycle. When a MALDI-TOF instrument acquires a single spectrum, the arrival times of ions at the detector are sampled at discrete intervals defined by the internal clock frequency of the ADC, resulting in a digitized intensity profile composed of quantized bins. Each bin represents a finite time window, and the intensity assigned to that bin is the total number of ions detected within that window. The width of these bins, measured in nanoseconds, is not constant across the entire mass range but increases proportionally with the flight time of the ions, which is itself a function of the mass-to-charge ratio. For example, at a mass of approximately 904 daltons, the bin width corresponds to 0.0178 atomic mass units, while at 2,093 daltons, the bin width expands to 0.0272 atomic mass units. This scaling is consistent with the physics of ion flight in a time-of-flight analyzer and is not an artifact of the instrument’s design but a consequence of the fundamental relationship between flight time and mass.

The critical insight underlying this invention is that, with each new spectrum acquisition, the ADC system resets its internal timing reference and redefines the boundaries of the bins relative to the actual ion arrival times. This reset occurs within the electronic tolerance of the system, typically less than the width of a single bin, but sufficient to cause a random lateral shift in the position of the digitized signal. As a result, the centroid of a given ion peak—determined through interpolation algorithms that estimate the peak maximum from the discrete data points—will vary slightly from one spectrum to the next, even if the physical arrival time of the ion remains unchanged. This variation is not due to any change in the sample or the instrument’s physical state but is instead an unavoidable consequence of the interaction between a continuous physical phenomenon and a discrete digital sampling system. The magnitude of this variation, when measured across multiple spectra, consistently falls within the range of 0.010 to 0.014 atomic mass units, which closely matches the size of the bin widths observed in the raw data. This correspondence strongly suggests that the binning process is the dominant source of the observed mass measurement inconsistency. Previous attempts to mitigate this variability have focused on increasing the number of laser shots per spectrum, improving calibration algorithms, or refining detector sensitivity. However, these approaches have proven ineffective because they do not address the root cause: the random repositioning of the digitization grid with each acquisition. The present invention circumvents this limitation not by attempting to eliminate the binning artifact, but by embracing it as a source of random noise that can be averaged out through statistical methods.

The method of the invention involves the acquisition of multiple independent spectra from the same sample under identical experimental conditions. Each spectrum is acquired as a composite of a predetermined number of laser shots, typically between 200 and 1,000, and is internally calibrated using a mixture of known peptide standards. The calibration is performed independently for each spectrum, meaning that each spectrum has its own unique calibration function derived from the positions of the internal standards within that particular acquisition. This is the standard procedure employed by commercial MALDI-TOF software, and it is maintained in the present method to ensure compatibility with existing workflows. After acquisition, the mass measurements for each identified peptide are compiled across all spectra in which the peptide is detected. For example, if a particular peptide is observed in 18 out of 23 acquired spectra, then 18 individual mass measurements are collected for that peptide. These measurements are then subjected to descriptive statistical analysis, including the calculation of the mean, standard deviation, and variance. The mean value of these measurements is taken as the corrected, enhanced mass assignment for that peptide. Because the errors introduced by bin repositioning are random and normally distributed, the central limit theorem ensures that the mean of a sufficiently large number of measurements will converge toward the true theoretical mass of the ion. This convergence is not dependent on the absolute accuracy of any single measurement but rather on the statistical properties of the ensemble. In practice, the mean mass values obtained through this method consistently fall within one to three parts per million of the theoretical mass, even when individual measurements exhibit errors exceeding ten parts per million. This represents a dramatic improvement in accuracy that brings the practical performance of MALDI-TOF instruments into alignment with their theoretical specifications.

In addition to providing a more accurate mass value, the invention introduces a novel diagnostic capability through the use of standard deviation as a measure of measurement reliability. Peptides with low standard deviations—typically below 0.014 atomic mass units—are considered to have high measurement consistency and are assigned high confidence in their mass assignments. Conversely, peptides with elevated standard deviations—those exceeding this threshold—are flagged as high-risk measurements. This flagging is not an indication of a faulty ion or a problematic sample, but rather a signal that the random binning artifacts have not been sufficiently averaged out, possibly due to low signal intensity, poor peak shape, or interference from nearby ions. These high-variability measurements are candidates for exclusion from downstream analyses such as peptide mass fingerprinting, where the presence of even a single inaccurate mass can lead to incorrect protein identification. The invention thus transforms the standard deviation from a mere statistical descriptor into a powerful, objective quality control metric. This is a significant advancement over existing practices, which rely on subjective visual inspection of spectra or arbitrary signal-to-noise thresholds to determine data reliability. The ability to quantify and act upon measurement uncertainty in a systematic, reproducible manner enhances the robustness of MALDI-TOF workflows and reduces the risk of false positives in protein identification.

The method further incorporates the use of outlier detection algorithms to identify systematic errors that cannot be corrected by averaging. While random errors are distributed symmetrically around the true value and thus cancel out in the mean, systematic errors manifest as consistent directional shifts across all spectra. These may arise from calibration drift, ion suppression, or electronic interference and are not mitigated by statistical averaging. To detect such errors, the invention employs the Grubbs test for outliers, a statistical method designed to identify a single anomalous data point in a normally distributed population. When applied to the population of mass measurements for a given peptide, the Grubbs test identifies measurements that deviate significantly from the mean and standard deviation of the group. If one or more outliers are detected, they are removed from the dataset, and the mean and standard deviation are recalculated. This process can be repeated iteratively, although the Grubbs test is not designed for multiple simultaneous outliers, and thus the method is applied conservatively to avoid overcorrection. In one illustrative case, a peptide exhibited a standard deviation of 0.298 atomic mass units and a mass error of −52.268 parts per million, which was far outside the expected range. Application of the Grubbs test identified two outlier measurements, and their removal reduced the standard deviation to 0.015 atomic mass units and the mass error to −2.649 parts per million, bringing the measurement into full agreement with the theoretical value. This demonstrates the power of the method to rescue otherwise unusable data and to provide a clear, evidence-based rationale for excluding suspect measurements.

The invention is fully compatible with existing MALDI-TOF instrumentation and software platforms. It does not require any hardware modifications, additional reagents, or changes to sample preparation protocols. The method can be implemented as a software module that operates after data acquisition, automatically aggregating spectra, computing statistics, and generating corrected mass values. The software may be integrated into the instrument’s control system or installed as a standalone post-processing application. The user is not required to alter acquisition parameters; the number of spectra to be averaged can be set by the user or defaulted to a value such as 20–25, which has been empirically determined to provide optimal statistical power without significantly increasing total acquisition time. The software may also generate a report summarizing the mean mass, standard deviation, number of contributing spectra, and outlier status for each detected peptide, providing a transparent and auditable record of data processing. This level of traceability is particularly valuable in regulated environments such as clinical diagnostics, where regulatory agencies require documentation of data handling and quality control procedures.

The method has been validated across a wide range of sample types and mass ranges. In experiments involving standard protein mixtures containing bovine serum albumin, myoglobin, and β-casein, the mean mass values for 11 different peptides consistently achieved mass accuracies within two parts per million of their theoretical values, even when individual measurements varied by up to 20 parts per million. In more complex biological samples derived from immunoprecipitation of β-tubulin from human embryonic kidney cells, the method enabled the correct identification of β-tubulin via peptide mass fingerprinting, even in the presence of contaminating peptides from other proteins. The inclusion of high-variability peptides in the initial analysis led to ambiguous or incorrect identifications, but their exclusion based on standard deviation thresholds resulted in unambiguous, high-confidence identification. Furthermore, the method was successfully applied to higher-mass ions such as insulin, which has a monoisotopic mass of approximately 5,804 daltons. Although the reflectron system of the instrument was not optimized for this mass range, the same pattern of variability was observed, and the mean mass values derived from spectral averaging were significantly more accurate than any single measurement. This demonstrates that the method is not limited to low-mass peptides and is applicable across the full operational range of MALDI-TOF instruments.

The invention is also applicable to other time-of-flight-based mass spectrometry platforms, including liquid chromatography-time-of-flight (LC-TOF) and liquid chromatography-quadrupole-time-of-flight (LC-Q-TOF) instruments. Although these systems typically employ continuous sampling and higher-resolution detectors, they still rely on analog-to-digital conversion and discrete binning of the ion signal. The same principles of random bin repositioning and statistical averaging apply, and the method can be adapted to these platforms with minimal modification. In fact, the benefits of the method are likely to be even more pronounced in LC-TOF systems, where the dynamic range and complexity of the samples often lead to greater variability in ion detection and quantification. The method may also be extended to include intensity-based averaging, although the variability in peak intensity is significantly greater than that in mass measurement, and the statistical interpretation of intensity data requires additional considerations related to ionization efficiency, matrix effects, and detector saturation. Nevertheless, the core innovation—the statistical aggregation of mass measurements across multiple spectra—remains valid and transformative regardless of the specific platform or application.

The practical implications of this invention are profound. In clinical microbiology, where MALDI-TOF is now the gold standard for bacterial and fungal identification, the method would enhance the reliability of spectral matching by reducing the number of ambiguous or false-positive identifications. In proteomics research, it would enable more confident protein identification from peptide mass fingerprinting, reducing the need for time-consuming tandem mass spectrometry validation. In biomarker discovery, it would improve the reproducibility of mass measurements across multiple samples and instruments, facilitating the identification of true biological signals amid technical noise. In forensic and pharmaceutical applications, where regulatory compliance demands rigorous data integrity, the method provides an objective, statistically validated framework for data processing that meets the highest standards of analytical rigor. The method is also scalable and future-proof; as MALDI-TOF instruments evolve with higher sampling rates, improved detectors, and more sophisticated electronics, the underlying statistical principles remain unchanged, ensuring that the method will continue to provide enhanced performance for generations of instruments to come.

The invention is not limited to the specific parameters or software implementations described in the examples. The number of spectra to be averaged may be adjusted based on the desired level of statistical confidence, the signal-to-noise ratio of the sample, or the computational resources available. The threshold for flagging high-variability peptides may be calibrated empirically for each instrument model or laboratory setting. The method may be combined with other data processing techniques, such as noise filtering, baseline correction, or deisotoping, without compromising its core functionality. It may also be integrated into automated workflows that include sample preparation, data acquisition, database searching, and result reporting, creating a seamless, end-to-end analytical pipeline. The invention thus represents not merely a technical improvement but a fundamental rethinking of how MALDI-TOF data should be interpreted and validated. By transforming a previously unaddressed limitation into a source of statistical power, the invention elevates MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry from a qualitative, empirical technique to a quantitative, rigorous analytical science. It restores the credibility of MALDI-TOF as a high-accuracy platform and ensures its continued relevance in an era dominated by more complex and expensive mass spectrometry technologies. The method is simple, cost-effective, universally applicable, and immediately implementable, making it an indispensable advancement in the field of mass spectrometry.
