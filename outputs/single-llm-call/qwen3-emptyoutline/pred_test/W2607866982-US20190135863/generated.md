# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The clinical management of solid tumors remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern oncology, primarily due to the pervasive and often lethal nature of metastatic dissemination. Despite advances in surgical resection, radiation therapy, and systemic pharmacological interventions, the majority of cancer-related deaths are attributable not to the primary tumor mass itself, but to the establishment of secondary lesions in distant organs. This process, known as metastasis, is not a random or passive event but a highly orchestrated, multi-step cascade involving the detachment of malignant cells from the primary site, their invasion through the surrounding extracellular matrix, intravasation into the circulatory or lymphatic systems, survival in transit, extravasation at distant sites, and finally, colonization and outgrowth in a foreign microenvironment. Each of these steps is tightly regulated by a complex interplay of molecular signals, cellular interactions, and biochemical modifications that collectively enable tumor cells to overcome physiological barriers and adapt to hostile niches. The ability of cancer cells to migrate and invade is therefore not merely a consequence of uncontrolled proliferation but a defining hallmark of malignancy, and one that has proven exceptionally difficult to therapeutically target.

Among the molecular systems that govern cell motility and tissue invasion, the urokinase-type plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) has emerged as a central orchestrator of metastatic behavior. uPAR is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored cell surface protein composed of three distinct domains—DI, DII, and DIII—that collectively serve as a molecular scaffold for the integration of proteolytic, adhesive, and signaling functions. While its classical role involves the spatial localization of urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA) to the cell membrane, thereby facilitating the conversion of plasminogen to the broad-spectrum protease plasmin, uPAR’s biological significance extends far beyond its enzymatic cofactor function. It participates in the formation of dynamic, multi-protein complexes with extracellular matrix components such as vitronectin, integrins, and crucially, the formyl-peptide receptors (FPRs), particularly FPR1. These interactions are not incidental but are functionally indispensable for the activation of intracellular signaling cascades that drive cytoskeletal reorganization, directional migration, and tissue penetration. The cleavage of uPAR by proteases releases soluble fragments, including the N-terminal domain DI and the linker region spanning residues 84–95, which retain biological activity and can act as autocrine or paracrine signaling molecules. Elevated levels of soluble uPAR in patient serum have been consistently correlated with advanced disease stage, increased metastatic burden, and poor overall survival across a broad spectrum of malignancies, including sarcomas, melanomas, and epithelial carcinomas, underscoring its value as a prognostic biomarker and a compelling therapeutic target.

Despite the clear biological rationale for targeting uPAR, the development of clinically viable inhibitors has been hampered by several persistent challenges. First, the pleiotropic nature of uPAR’s interactions renders it a difficult target for selective intervention; inhibiting one pathway may inadvertently activate compensatory mechanisms through alternative binding partners. Second, many candidate inhibitors derived from peptide sequences derived from uPAR’s functional domains, such as the chemotactic motif Ser88-Arg-Ser-Arg-Tyr92 (SRSRY), suffer from intrinsic instability in biological fluids. These peptides are rapidly degraded by serum proteases, resulting in short half-lives that preclude sustained pharmacological activity in vivo. Third, some analogs exhibit off-target toxicities, potentially due to unintended interactions with other receptors such as the vitronectin receptor, which compromises their therapeutic index. Fourth, even peptides with demonstrated efficacy in vitro often fail to translate into meaningful anti-metastatic effects in animal models, particularly when administered systemically, due to poor pharmacokinetic properties and rapid clearance. These limitations have collectively impeded the clinical translation of uPAR-targeted strategies, leaving a significant unmet medical need for agents capable of specifically disrupting uPAR-mediated signaling without inducing systemic toxicity or losing potency in physiologically relevant environments.

The formyl-peptide receptor 1 (FPR1) represents a particularly attractive node within the uPAR signaling network. FPR1 is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) originally characterized for its role in innate immune responses, where it detects bacterial formylated peptides and mitochondrial-derived formyl peptides as danger signals, triggering neutrophil chemotaxis, oxidative burst, and degranulation. However, accumulating evidence has revealed that FPR1 is aberrantly expressed in a wide variety of non-myeloid tumor cells, including those of sarcomatous, melanocytic, and epithelial origin. In these contexts, FPR1 does not respond to microbial ligands but instead is activated by endogenous ligands derived from the uPAR system, particularly the SRSRY sequence. The uPAR–FPR1 interaction initiates a signaling cascade involving Gαi proteins, leading to the activation of downstream effectors such as phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), protein kinase B (Akt), extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK), and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (p38 MAPK). These pathways collectively promote actin polymerization, focal adhesion turnover, and matrix metalloproteinase secretion—all essential for invasive migration. Moreover, FPR1 activation induces its own internalization via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, a process that further amplifies intracellular signaling and facilitates the recycling of uPAR to the cell surface, creating a self-sustaining loop that enhances metastatic potential. The fact that FPR1 is not essential for normal physiological homeostasis in most tissues, yet is hijacked by tumors to promote dissemination, makes it an ideal target for anti-metastatic therapy: inhibition of this axis may selectively impair cancer cell motility without causing widespread immunosuppression or tissue damage.

The retro-inverso (RI) peptide modification represents a sophisticated chemical strategy designed to overcome the inherent limitations of natural L-amino acid peptides. In this approach, the entire amino acid sequence of a parent peptide is reversed in direction (C- to N-terminus), and all L-amino acids are substituted with their D-enantiomers. This results in a molecule that retains the spatial orientation of its side chains relative to the parent peptide, thereby preserving the pharmacophore necessary for receptor recognition, while simultaneously rendering the peptide backbone resistant to proteolytic cleavage. The inversion of the amide bond orientation eliminates the susceptibility to endopeptidases and exopeptidases that typically recognize and hydrolyze L-peptide bonds, dramatically extending the in vivo half-life. Although the conformational flexibility of RI peptides may differ from their parent counterparts due to altered backbone torsional angles and the absence of canonical hydrogen-bonding patterns, certain sequences—particularly those whose biological activity is predominantly mediated by side-chain interactions rather than main-chain hydrogen bonding—can adopt stable, bioactive conformations despite the inversion. Previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of this approach in other therapeutic contexts, including antimicrobial peptides and neuropeptide analogs, where RI modifications conferred metabolic stability without compromising potency. However, the application of this strategy to uPAR-derived antagonists had not been systematically explored, and no RI analogs of the uPAR84–95 motif had been previously reported or characterized for their anti-metastatic potential.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing a novel class of retro-inverso peptides that specifically and potently inhibit the uPAR–FPR1 interaction. These peptides are designed to mimic the critical signaling sequence of uPAR while incorporating the structural advantages of the retro-inverso modification. The resulting compounds exhibit exceptional stability in human serum, maintain high affinity for FPR1, and effectively block agonist-induced receptor activation, internalization, and downstream signaling. Crucially, they do so without eliciting any intrinsic cellular responses, thereby minimizing the risk of off-target effects. Furthermore, these peptides demonstrate potent inhibition of multiple steps in the metastatic cascade—including cell migration, matrigel invasion, trans-endothelial migration, and angiogenesis—in a dose-dependent manner, and they exert significant anti-metastatic effects in vivo, reducing tumor growth, vascular infiltration, and circulating tumor cell burden without evidence of systemic toxicity. The invention thus provides a first-in-class therapeutic modality capable of simultaneously targeting the molecular drivers of metastasis with unprecedented specificity, stability, and tolerability, offering a transformative approach to the clinical management of metastatic solid tumors.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention encompasses a novel class of retro-inverso peptides that function as potent and selective antagonists of the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR)–formyl-peptide receptor 1 (FPR1) interaction, thereby inhibiting key steps in the metastatic cascade of solid tumors. These peptides are characterized by a reversed amino acid sequence and an all-D-amino acid composition, which together confer exceptional resistance to proteolytic degradation while preserving the spatial topology of side chains necessary for high-affinity binding to FPR1. The invention specifically provides a group of tetrapeptide and tripeptide analogs derived from the uPAR84–95 signaling motif, with the most potent and pharmacologically favorable member being the retro-inverso peptide designated RI-3, with the sequence Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-Aib-(D)-Arg-NH2. This peptide exhibits a stable β-turn conformation stabilized by an intramolecular hydrogen bond between the carbonyl oxygen of the N-terminal D-tyrosine residue and the amide hydrogen of the C-terminal D-arginine residue, a structural feature that closely mimics the bioactive conformation of previously described antagonists such as RERF and UPARANT, but with markedly enhanced metabolic stability.

The retro-inverso peptides of the invention are synthesized using solid-phase peptide synthesis employing Fmoc/t-Bu chemistry, with all amino acid residues in the D-configuration, and are purified to homogeneity by reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography. The peptides are characterized by mass spectrometry to confirm molecular weight and purity, and their structural integrity is validated through molecular dynamics simulations, which demonstrate that RI-3 adopts a stable turn conformation in aqueous solution, with minimal conformational flexibility compared to its linear counterparts. The peptides are designed to competitively inhibit the binding of endogenous uPAR-derived ligands, such as the SRSRY sequence, to FPR1, thereby preventing receptor activation, internalization, and downstream signaling. Unlike conventional peptide inhibitors, which are rapidly degraded in serum, the retro-inverso configuration renders these compounds resistant to cleavage by a broad spectrum of proteases, including trypsin, chymotrypsin, and serum aminopeptidases, resulting in a half-life in human serum exceeding 18 hours with retention of over 80% of biological activity.

The peptides of the invention are effective at nanomolar and even sub-nanomolar concentrations in inhibiting directional cell migration and invasion in a variety of human tumor cell lines, including fibrosarcoma, osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, melanoma, and epithelial ovarian carcinoma. They do not induce cell migration themselves, nor do they affect cell proliferation or viability at concentrations up to 10 micromolar, indicating a highly specific mechanism of action that targets motility without cytotoxicity. Their inhibitory activity is dose-dependent, with IC50 values for migration inhibition in the low femtomolar range and for invasion inhibition in the low picomolar range, demonstrating exceptional potency. The peptides block FPR1 activation by preventing the binding of formylated ligands such as fMLF, as evidenced by competitive binding assays using fluorescently labeled fMLF, and they inhibit FPR1 internalization, as confirmed by confocal microscopy and three-dimensional z-stack reconstruction. Furthermore, the peptides suppress agonist-induced phosphorylation of key signaling intermediates including Akt, ERK1/2, p38 MAPK, CREB, Fgr, and FAK, without altering basal phosphorylation states, thereby confirming their role as pure antagonists rather than partial agonists or modulators.

The therapeutic utility of the peptides extends beyond direct inhibition of tumor cell motility to include suppression of tumor angiogenesis and trans-endothelial migration. The peptides inhibit endothelial tube formation triggered by both fetal bovine serum and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), reducing the formation of capillary-like structures by up to 75% in vitro. They also prevent the adhesion of tumor cells to endothelial monolayers and significantly reduce the ability of tumor cells to transmigrate across endothelial barriers, as demonstrated by real-time impedance-based assays. These effects are consistent with the inhibition of uPAR–FPR1 signaling in endothelial cells, which is known to promote vascular permeability and neovascularization. In vivo, administration of RI-3 via intraperitoneal injection in immunocompromised mouse models of sarcoma, melanoma, and ovarian carcinoma results in a significant reduction in primary tumor volume, a marked decrease in intra-tumoral microvessel density, and a substantial reduction in the number of circulating tumor cells. Most notably, RI-3 treatment prevents the formation of pulmonary metastases, as confirmed by fluorescent imaging and histological analysis of lung sections, whereas control-treated animals develop multiple metastatic nodules. The peptide is well tolerated, with no observable changes in body weight, behavior, or organ morphology, and no signs of systemic toxicity or immune activation.

The invention further includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising the retro-inverso peptides of the invention in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or delivery systems suitable for parenteral, intravenous, subcutaneous, or intraperitoneal administration. The peptides may be formulated as lyophilized powders for reconstitution, aqueous solutions, liposomal suspensions, or nanoparticle conjugates to enhance bioavailability and tissue penetration. The invention also encompasses methods of treating or preventing metastasis in a subject diagnosed with or at risk for a solid tumor, comprising administering an effective amount of a retro-inverso peptide as described herein. The method is applicable to a broad range of malignancies, including but not limited to sarcomas, melanomas, epithelial ovarian carcinomas, breast carcinomas, colorectal carcinomas, and non-small cell lung carcinomas, all of which have been shown to express uPAR and FPR1 at elevated levels. The invention further includes diagnostic kits for identifying patients who are likely to benefit from treatment with these peptides, comprising reagents for detecting uPAR and FPR1 expression in tumor tissue or circulating tumor cells, thereby enabling patient stratification based on molecular biomarkers.

The retro-inverso peptides of the invention represent a paradigm shift in the development of anti-metastatic therapeutics. Unlike conventional small-molecule inhibitors that target single signaling nodes or monoclonal antibodies that require complex manufacturing and have limited tissue penetration, these peptides combine the target specificity of biologics with the metabolic stability and synthetic accessibility of small molecules. Their ability to simultaneously inhibit multiple steps in the metastatic cascade—migration, invasion, trans-endothelial migration, and angiogenesis—without inducing cytotoxicity or systemic toxicity positions them as ideal candidates for prophylactic or adjuvant therapy in high-risk patients. The invention thus provides a novel, potent, stable, and safe therapeutic modality capable of fundamentally altering the natural history of metastatic disease, offering a transformative approach to improving survival outcomes in patients with solid tumors.

## EXAMPLE 1. SYNTHESIS OF THE RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES RI-1, RI-2, RI-3, RI-4, RI-5

The retro-inverso peptides RI-1, RI-2, RI-3, RI-4, and RI-5 were synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis techniques employing Fmoc/t-Bu chemistry on a Rink amide resin to yield C-terminal amide derivatives. All amino acid residues were incorporated in their D-configuration to ensure complete retro-inverso modification, and the N-terminus was acetylated to enhance stability and reduce charge-mediated interactions. The synthesis was performed on an automated peptide synthesizer under inert atmosphere using dimethylformamide as the primary solvent for coupling and deprotection steps. The Fmoc group was removed using 20% piperidine in dimethylformamide for 20 minutes, followed by thorough washing with dimethylformamide and dichloromethane. Amino acid coupling was achieved using HBTU as the coupling reagent and DIPEA as the base, with each residue added in a 4-fold molar excess relative to the resin loading to ensure complete reaction. The synthesis proceeded sequentially from the C-terminus to the N-terminus, with the final acetylation performed using acetic anhydride in dimethylformamide for 30 minutes. After completion of the chain assembly, the peptides were cleaved from the resin using a trifluoroacetic acid-based cocktail containing water and triisopropylsilane to prevent side-chain modifications, followed by precipitation in cold diethyl ether and centrifugation. The crude peptides were dissolved in water and purified by preparative reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography using a C18 column with a gradient of acetonitrile in water containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. Fractions containing the target peptides were collected, lyophilized, and analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry to confirm molecular weight and purity. The theoretical molecular weights of the peptides were calculated based on their amino acid sequences, and the observed mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) of the protonated molecular ions [M+H]+ were in close agreement with the predicted values, confirming successful synthesis and the absence of major side products. RI-1, with the sequence Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-(D)-Glu-(D)-Arg-NH2, exhibited a theoretical molecular weight of 663.7 Da and a measured [M+H]+ of 664.4, consistent with the expected composition. RI-2, Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-(D)-Ser-(D)-Arg-NH2, had a theoretical molecular weight of 619.7 Da and a measured [M+H]+ of 620.4. RI-3, Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-Aib-(D)-Arg-NH2, displayed a theoretical molecular weight of 621.3 Da and a measured [M+H]+ of 622.3, confirming the incorporation of the α,α-disubstituted amino acid Aib. RI-4, Phenyl-Acetyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH2, had a theoretical molecular weight of 533.1 Da and a measured [M+H]+ of 533.5, while RI-5, 3-Phenyl-Propionyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH2, exhibited a theoretical molecular weight of 547.1 Da and a measured [M+H]+ of 547.5. All peptides demonstrated purity greater than 95% as determined by analytical HPLC, and were stored at -80°C in lyophilized form until use. The synthesis protocol was validated through replicate runs, and no significant batch-to-batch variability was observed in purity or biological activity. The inclusion of Aib in RI-3 and RI-4 was critical for stabilizing the β-turn conformation, as confirmed by subsequent molecular dynamics simulations, and the aromatic capping groups in RI-4 and RI-5 were designed to mimic the hydrophobic interactions of the original C-terminal tyrosine residue while eliminating a labile amide bond, thereby enhancing metabolic stability. The successful synthesis of these retro-inverso analogs provided the foundation for subsequent biological characterization and established a reproducible, scalable method for producing these novel anti-metastatic agents.

## EXAMPLE 2. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES DO NOT PROMOTE CELL MIGRATION

To evaluate the intrinsic migratory activity of the retro-inverso peptides, a series of functional assays were conducted using the highly motile human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cell line, which expresses abundant levels of uPAR and FPR1 on its cell surface. Cell migration was monitored in real time using the xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analysis system, which measures changes in electrical impedance caused by cell adhesion and movement across microelectrode-coated membranes. Cells were seeded in serum-free medium in the upper chamber, and migration was induced by the addition of either 10 nM N-formyl-methionyl-leucyl-phenylalanine (fMLF), a known FPR1 agonist, or 10 nM of each retro-inverso peptide (RI-1 through RI-5) to the lower chamber. As expected, fMLF elicited a robust and sustained increase in cell index, reflecting active directional migration over an 18-hour period. In contrast, none of the retro-inverso peptides—RI-1, RI-2, RI-3, RI-4, or RI-5—induced any measurable increase in cell index above baseline levels, even at concentrations up to 100 nM. The absence of migratory response was consistent across multiple independent experiments, with no statistically significant deviation from the negative control (serum-free medium alone). This finding was corroborated in parallel Boyden chamber assays, in which cells were allowed to migrate toward the peptides for four hours, followed by fixation, staining, and quantification of cells that had traversed the membrane. No significant difference in the number of migrated cells was observed between the peptide-treated groups and the negative control, whereas fMLF induced a more than fivefold increase in migration. Importantly, the peptides did not induce morphological changes such as membrane ruffling, pseudopod formation, or cytoskeletal reorganization that are typically associated with active motility, as confirmed by phase-contrast microscopy and phalloidin staining of F-actin. Furthermore, when tested in non-migratory cell lines lacking FPR1 expression, the peptides failed to elicit any response, confirming that their lack of migratory activity is not due to general cytotoxicity or non-specific membrane effects. The absence of intrinsic migratory activity is a critical feature of these retro-inverso peptides, as it distinguishes them from partial agonists or modulators that may inadvertently promote metastatic behavior under certain conditions. Instead, these peptides behave as pure antagonists, capable of blocking FPR1 activation without triggering it themselves. This property is particularly advantageous for therapeutic applications, as it minimizes the risk of paradoxical stimulation of tumor cell motility, a known concern with some GPCR-targeting agents. The data collectively demonstrate that the retro-inverso modification not only preserves the antagonistic properties of the parent peptides but also eliminates any residual agonist activity that might be present in linear analogs, thereby ensuring a clean, predictable pharmacological profile.

## EXAMPLE 3. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 INTERFERES WITH FPR1 BIOLOGICAL ACTIVITY BY BLOCKING AGONIST/FPR1 INTERACTION AND BY PREVENTING AGONIST-INDUCED FPR1 INTERNALIZATION

To determine the mechanism by which RI-3 inhibits cell migration and invasion, a series of binding and internalization assays were performed using rat basophilic leukemia RBL-2H3 cells stably transfected with human FPR1 (RBL-2H3/ETFR) and their parental counterparts lacking FPR1 expression. Competitive binding assays were conducted using fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled fMLF (FITC-fMLF) as a tracer ligand. Cells were pre-incubated for 60 minutes at 4°C with either vehicle, an excess of unlabeled fMLF (100 nM), the previously characterized antagonist RERF (100 nM), a scrambled control peptide ERFR (100 nM), or RI-3 (100 nM), followed by exposure to 10 nM FITC-fMLF for an additional 60 minutes at 4°C to prevent internalization. Cell-associated fluorescence was quantified using a fluorescence plate reader. RBL-2H3 cells, which lack FPR1, exhibited minimal fluorescence that was unaffected by any of the competing peptides, confirming the specificity of the binding signal. In contrast, RBL-2H3/ETFR cells showed a strong, saturable binding signal that was significantly reduced by both unlabeled fMLF and RERF, as expected. Crucially, RI-3 produced a comparable level of inhibition, reducing FITC-fMLF binding by approximately 85%, with no statistically significant difference from the inhibition achieved by fMLF or RERF. This result demonstrates that RI-3 competes directly with fMLF for binding to FPR1, establishing it as a high-affinity competitive antagonist. To further validate this interaction and assess its functional consequences, internalization assays were performed at 37°C, a temperature at which receptor endocytosis occurs. Cells were pre-incubated with the peptides for 30 minutes, followed by exposure to FITC-fMLF for an additional 30 minutes. Confocal microscopy revealed that in the absence of competing peptides, FPR1 internalized rapidly, with fluorescence appearing as distinct intracellular puncta distributed throughout the cytoplasm. Pre-treatment with unlabeled fMLF or RERF effectively prevented this internalization, with fluorescence remaining predominantly localized to the plasma membrane. Similarly, pre-incubation with RI-3 resulted in a marked reduction in intracellular fluorescence, with the majority of signal retained at the cell surface, comparable to the effect of the known antagonist RERF. Three-dimensional z-stack reconstructions confirmed the spatial distribution of FPR1, showing a near-complete absence of internalized vesicles in RI-3-treated cells. These findings indicate that RI-3 not only blocks ligand binding but also prevents the agonist-induced conformational changes that trigger receptor endocytosis, a key step in sustained signaling and downstream pathway activation. To assess the downstream consequences of this blockade, phosphorylation of Akt, a central node in FPR1-mediated signaling, was evaluated by Western blotting. Exposure of RBL-2H3/ETFR cells to fMLF induced a rapid and transient phosphorylation of Akt at Ser473, peaking at 5 minutes. Pre-treatment with RI-3 completely abolished this phosphorylation, whereas RI-3 alone had no effect on basal Akt activity. Similar results were obtained for ERK1/2 and p38 MAPK, as confirmed by phospho-kinase array analysis, which revealed a broad suppression of agonist-induced phosphorylation events across multiple signaling pathways. These data collectively demonstrate that RI-3 functions as a potent, selective, and full antagonist of FPR1, capable of inhibiting both ligand binding and the subsequent receptor internalization and signaling cascade that drive metastatic behavior. The absence of any intrinsic agonist activity, combined with its ability to block multiple downstream effectors, positions RI-3 as a uniquely effective therapeutic agent for disrupting the uPAR–FPR1 axis without inducing compensatory or paradoxical responses.

## EXAMPLE 4. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES INHIBIT CELL MIGRATION IN A DOSE DEPENDENT MANNER

The inhibitory potency of the retro-inverso peptide RI-3 on directional cell migration was rigorously quantified using a dose-response paradigm in human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cells. Migration was assessed using the Boyden chamber assay, in which cells were seeded in serum-free medium in the upper chamber and allowed to migrate toward 10% fetal bovine serum in the lower chamber in the presence of increasing concentrations of RI-3, ranging from 1 femtomolar to 100 nanomolar. After four hours of incubation, cells that had migrated through the membrane were fixed, stained, and counted. The data revealed a clear, progressive reduction in migration with increasing peptide concentration, beginning at concentrations as low as 10 femtomolar, where a statistically significant 15% inhibition was observed compared to the vehicle control. The inhibition increased in a sigmoidal fashion, reaching 50% inhibition (IC50) at a concentration of 1 × 10−13 M, and plateauing at approximately 70% inhibition at concentrations above 1 nanomolar. The dose-response curve was highly reproducible across three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate, with coefficients of variation less than 10%. To confirm these findings using a real-time, label-free method, the xCELLigence RTCA system was employed to monitor cell migration over an 18-hour period. In this assay, cells were seeded in serum-free medium and allowed to migrate toward 10% FBS in the presence of RI-3 at concentrations ranging from 100 femtomolar to 100 nanomolar. The cell index, which reflects the number of adherent and migrating cells, was recorded continuously. The kinetics of migration were markedly altered in a concentration-dependent manner: at the lowest effective concentration (100 fM), the slope of the migration curve was reduced by approximately 20%, while at 1 pM, the rate of migration was diminished by 50%, and at 10 pM and above, migration was nearly abolished, with the cell index plateauing at levels comparable to the serum-free control. The IC50 for migration inhibition in this real-time assay was determined to be 80 femtomolar, in close agreement with the endpoint Boyden chamber data. Importantly, the inhibition was not due to cytotoxicity or reduced cell adhesion, as cell viability and proliferation remained unaffected at all concentrations tested, as confirmed by MTS and xCELLigence proliferation assays. The steepness of the dose-response curve, extending into the femtomolar range, indicates an exceptionally high affinity of RI-3 for its target, consistent with its ability to compete with endogenous ligands that are present at low picomolar concentrations in the tumor microenvironment. This potency is significantly greater than that of previously described uPAR/FPR1 antagonists, such as RERF or UPARANT, which typically exhibit IC50 values in the low nanomolar range. The dose-dependent nature of the inhibition further supports a specific receptor-mediated mechanism, as non-specific effects would be expected to plateau at lower concentrations or exhibit erratic behavior. These findings establish RI-3 as one of the most potent inhibitors of tumor cell migration ever reported, with a therapeutic window that spans several orders of magnitude between effective and non-toxic concentrations, making it uniquely suited for systemic administration in clinical settings.

## EXAMPLE 5. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES INHIBIT CELL INVASION IN A DOSE DEPENDENT MANNER

The ability of the retro-inverso peptide RI-3 to inhibit tumor cell invasion through extracellular matrix barriers was evaluated using both endpoint and real-time assays in human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cells. Invasion was assessed using matrigel-coated Boyden chambers, in which cells were seeded in serum-free medium and allowed to invade through a 50 μg/filter layer of matrigel toward 10% fetal bovine serum over a six-hour period. Increasing concentrations of RI-3, ranging from 10 femtomolar to 100 nanomolar, were added to the lower chamber. At the conclusion of the assay, cells that had invaded through the matrigel were fixed, stained, and counted. A dose-dependent reduction in invasion was observed, with a statistically significant 20% inhibition evident at 100 femtomolar and a 50% inhibition (IC50) achieved at a concentration of 1 × 10−11 M. The maximum inhibition of approximately 75% was reached at concentrations of 10 nanomolar and above, with no further increase observed at higher doses. To validate these findings and assess the kinetics of invasion inhibition, a real-time invasion assay was performed using the xCELLigence RTCA system with E-well plates coated with matrigel. Cells were seeded in serum-free medium and allowed to invade toward 10% FBS in the presence of RI-3 at concentrations ranging from 100 femtomolar to 100 nanomolar. The cell index, which reflects the number of cells that have traversed the matrigel and adhered to the bottom of the well, was recorded continuously over a 20-hour period. The slope of the invasion curve, representing the rate of invasion, was significantly reduced in a concentration-dependent manner. At 100 femtomolar, the slope was decreased by 18%, while at 1 picomolar, the rate of invasion was reduced by 50%, and at 10 picomolar and above, invasion was nearly completely suppressed, with the cell index plateauing at levels indistinguishable from the serum-free control. The IC50 for invasion inhibition in this real-time model was determined to be 1.2 × 10−11 M, closely matching the endpoint assay. Importantly, the inhibition of invasion was not attributable to reduced cell viability or proliferation, as confirmed by parallel MTS and xCELLigence proliferation assays, which showed no effect on cell metabolism or growth at concentrations up to 10 micromolar. The dose-response relationship was highly reproducible across multiple independent experiments, with inter-assay variability less than 12%. The fact that the IC50 for invasion inhibition is approximately 100-fold higher than that for migration suggests that the physical barrier posed by the matrigel requires a higher concentration of peptide to achieve maximal inhibition, possibly due to diffusion limitations or the involvement of additional proteolytic pathways. Nevertheless, the potency of RI-3 in inhibiting invasion at sub-nanomolar concentrations underscores its potential to block the critical step of tissue penetration that enables metastatic dissemination. The consistency between the endpoint and real-time assays, combined with the absence of cytotoxicity, confirms that RI-3 acts as a highly specific and potent inhibitor of tumor cell invasion, with a therapeutic profile that is both efficacious and safe.

## EXAMPLE 6. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 DOES NOT AFFECT CELL PROLIFERATION

To ensure that the observed inhibition of cell migration and invasion by the retro-inverso peptide RI-3 was not an artifact of cytotoxicity or suppression of cell proliferation, a comprehensive assessment of cell viability and growth kinetics was performed. Two complementary assays were employed: the MTS colorimetric assay, which measures mitochondrial reductase activity as an indicator of metabolically active cells, and the xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analysis system, which monitors cell adhesion and proliferation in real time through impedance changes. Human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cells were seeded in growth medium containing either 10 micromolar RI-3 or an equivalent volume of vehicle (dimethyl sulfoxide in phosphate-buffered saline) and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for up to 96 hours. The culture medium was replaced every 24 hours to maintain peptide concentration and nutrient availability. In the MTS assay, absorbance readings at 490 nm were taken at 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours. No statistically significant difference in absorbance was observed between the RI-3-treated and vehicle-treated groups at any time point, indicating that the metabolic activity of the cells was unaffected by the presence of the peptide. Similarly, in the xCELLigence proliferation assay, cells were seeded at low density in E-well plates and monitored continuously for 85 hours. The cell index, which reflects both cell number and adhesion, increased linearly over time in both groups, with identical doubling times and growth rates. The slope of the proliferation curve, the maximum cell index achieved, and the lag phase prior to exponential growth were all indistinguishable between the two conditions. These findings were corroborated by direct cell counting using a hemocytometer and trypan blue exclusion, which revealed no difference in viable cell number or membrane integrity between the treated and control groups. Furthermore, no morphological changes indicative of apoptosis or necrosis—such as cell rounding, membrane blebbing, or detachment—were observed under phase-contrast microscopy. The absence of any effect on proliferation was also confirmed in other tumor cell lines, including osteosarcoma Saos-2 and chondrosarcoma Sarc, which exhibited identical growth kinetics in the presence of 10 micromolar RI-3. These results demonstrate that RI-3 exerts its anti-metastatic effects through a highly specific mechanism that targets motility and invasion without interfering with fundamental cellular processes such as proliferation or viability. This selectivity is a critical advantage over conventional chemotherapeutic agents, which often cause systemic toxicity by indiscriminately targeting rapidly dividing cells. The ability of RI-3 to inhibit metastatic behavior without suppressing tumor growth directly suggests that its primary mechanism of action is to prevent dissemination rather than to kill tumor cells, making it an ideal candidate for adjuvant or prophylactic therapy in patients at high risk of metastatic relapse.

## EXAMPLE 7. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 IS STABLE IN HUMAN SERUM

The metabolic stability of the retro-inverso peptide RI-3 in human serum was rigorously evaluated to determine its suitability for systemic administration. To assess resistance to proteolytic degradation, RI-3 was incubated at a concentration of 1 millimolar in human serum at 37°C for 18 hours, a time frame sufficient to degrade most natural peptides. Following incubation, the peptide was extracted using solid-phase extraction and analyzed for its ability to inhibit cell migration and invasion in HT1080 cells using the xCELLigence and Boyden chamber assays, respectively. The inhibitory activity of RI-3 pre-incubated in human serum was compared to that of fresh, unexposed RI-3 at the same final concentration of 10 nanomolar. In the migration assay, the cell index curves generated by cells exposed to RI-3 pre-incubated in serum were virtually indistinguishable from those exposed to fresh RI-3, with no statistically significant difference in slope or plateau value. Similarly, in the matrigel invasion assay, the percentage of inhibition achieved by the pre-incubated peptide was 81% of that observed with the untreated control, indicating minimal loss of biological activity. These results were confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis of the serum-incubated samples, which revealed the intact molecular ion of RI-3 with no detectable fragments corresponding to proteolytic cleavage products. In contrast, control peptides lacking the retro-inverso modification, such as the linear sequence Ac-Arg-Glu-Arg-Tyr-NH2, were completely degraded within two hours under identical conditions, with no residual inhibitory activity detected. The stability of RI-3 was further confirmed by incubation in bovine serum, where the peptide retained full activity, demonstrating that its resistance is not species-specific but rather a consequence of its all-D-amino acid composition and inverted amide backbone. The half-life of RI-3 in human serum was estimated to exceed 18 hours, far surpassing the typical half-life of L-peptides, which are often degraded within minutes. This exceptional stability is attributed to the retro-inverso modification, which renders the peptide backbone impervious to cleavage by endopeptidases, exopeptidases, and serum aminopeptidases that recognize L-amino acid sequences. The preservation of biological activity after prolonged exposure to serum is a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy, as it ensures that the peptide remains intact and functional during circulation, allowing it to reach and act upon disseminated tumor cells in distant organs. The data collectively establish RI-3 as a metabolically stable peptide therapeutic with pharmacokinetic properties far superior to those of previously described uPAR/FPR1 antagonists, positioning it as a viable candidate for intravenous or intraperitoneal administration in clinical settings.

## EXAMPLE 8. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 PREVENTS MATRIGEL INVASION BY HUMAN SARCOMA, MELANOMA AND EPITHELIAL OVARIAN CARCINOMA CELLS

The anti-invasive activity of the retro-inverso peptide RI-3 was extended beyond fibrosarcoma to encompass a panel of human tumor cell lines derived from distinct tissue origins, including osteosarcoma (Saos-2), chondrosarcoma (Sarc), melanoma (A375), and epithelial ovarian carcinoma (SKOV-3). These cell lines were selected based on their documented expression of uPAR and FPR1, as confirmed by Western blot analysis of whole-cell lysates. All cell lines exhibited robust expression of uPAR, with Sarc and SKOV-3 showing the highest levels, while FPR1 expression was variable, with Sarc and A375 displaying the most abundant receptor levels. Matrigel invasion assays were performed using Boyden chambers, with cells seeded in serum-free medium and allowed to invade toward 10% fetal bovine serum over a 20-hour period in the presence or absence of 10 nanomolar RI-3. In all cell lines, serum induced significant invasion, with Sarc cells exhibiting the highest invasive capacity, consistent with their elevated uPAR and FPR1 expression. Treatment with RI-3 resulted in a marked, statistically significant reduction in invasion across all lines: 64% inhibition in HT1080 fibrosarcoma, 47% in Sarc chondrosarcoma, 58% in A375 melanoma, and 52% in SKOV-3 ovarian carcinoma. Notably, Saos-2 osteosarcoma cells, which expressed low levels of FPR1 despite high uPAR expression, showed only a 24% reduction in invasion, suggesting that the inhibitory effect of RI-3 is dependent on the co-expression of both uPAR and FPR1. This finding was corroborated by siRNA-mediated knockdown of FPR1 in Sarc cells, which abolished the inhibitory effect of RI-3, confirming that its activity is mediated specifically through FPR1 blockade. The consistency of inhibition across diverse tumor types indicates that the uPAR–FPR1 axis is a common and functionally relevant pathway in multiple solid malignancies. The ability of RI-3 to suppress invasion in melanoma and ovarian carcinoma, two cancers with high metastatic potential and limited therapeutic options, highlights its broad applicability. Real-time invasion assays using the xCELLigence system confirmed these findings, demonstrating a dose-dependent reduction in invasion kinetics across all cell lines, with IC50 values ranging from 0.8 to 1.5 nanomolar. Importantly, RI-3 did not affect cell viability or proliferation in any of these lines, even at concentrations up to 10 micromolar, confirming that its anti-invasive effect is not secondary to cytotoxicity. These results establish RI-3 as a pan-tumor anti-invasive agent capable of targeting the uPAR–FPR1 pathway in a wide range of cancers, thereby offering a unified therapeutic strategy for metastasis prevention across multiple indications.

## EXAMPLE 9. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 PREVENTS IN VITRO TRANS-ENDOTHELIAL MIGRATION BY SARCOMA, MELANOMA AND EPITHELIAL OVARIAN CARCINOMA CELLS AS WELL AS BY HUMAN MONOCYTES

The ability of tumor cells to cross the endothelial barrier, a critical step in intravasation and metastasis, was evaluated using a trans-endothelial migration model based on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) grown to confluence on matrigel-coated transwell inserts. Green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged sarcoma (Sarc), melanoma (A375), and ovarian carcinoma (SKOV-3) cells, as well as human peripheral blood monocytes, were seeded in the upper chamber in serum-free medium, while the lower chamber contained 10% fetal bovine serum as a chemoattractant. Migration across the endothelial monolayer was assessed over a 10-hour period using real-time impedance monitoring via the xCELLigence RTCA system, which detects decreases in electrical resistance as cells disrupt endothelial junctions. In the absence of treatment, all cell types induced a significant drop in cell index, reflecting successful trans-endothelial migration. Pre-treatment with 10 nanomolar RI-3 significantly attenuated this drop, reducing trans-endothelial migration by 24% in Sarc cells, 22% in A375 melanoma cells, 20% in SKOV-3 ovarian carcinoma cells, and 28% in human monocytes. The inhibition was dose-dependent, with maximal effect observed at 10 nanomolar and no further increase at higher concentrations. To confirm these findings, confocal microscopy was employed to visualize the spatial distribution of migrating cells relative to the endothelial monolayer. Z-stack reconstructions revealed that in control conditions, the majority of tumor cells and monocytes were located beneath the endothelial layer, having successfully crossed the barrier. In contrast, in the presence of RI-3, the majority of cells remained confined above the monolayer, with markedly fewer cells penetrating the junctional complexes. This effect was not due to reduced adhesion, as cell binding to the endothelium was only partially reduced, indicating that RI-3 specifically interferes with the transmigration step rather than initial attachment. The inhibition of monocyte migration is particularly significant, as it suggests that RI-3 may also modulate inflammatory processes that contribute to the metastatic niche without inducing immunosuppression. The fact that RI-3 inhibits trans-endothelial migration in both tumor cells and immune cells underscores its ability to target a fundamental biological process shared across cell types, likely through blockade of FPR1-mediated signaling in endothelial cells themselves. This dual action—suppressing both tumor cell extravasation and the recruitment of pro-tumorigenic immune cells—positions RI-3 as a multifunctional agent capable of disrupting multiple components of the metastatic cascade. The consistency of inhibition across diverse cell types further supports the central role of the uPAR–FPR1 axis in trans-endothelial migration, validating it as a high-value therapeutic target.

## EXAMPLE 10. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 INHIBITS BOTH FBS AND VEGF-TRIGGERED ENDOTHELIAL TUBE FORMATION

The anti-angiogenic potential of the retro-inverso peptide RI-3 was evaluated using an in vitro model of endothelial tube formation, a key process in tumor neovascularization. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were seeded on growth factor-reduced matrigel in the presence of either 10% fetal bovine serum or 40 ng/mL vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF165), both of which are potent inducers of capillary-like structure formation. The formation of tubular networks was assessed after six hours by phase-contrast microscopy, and the number of cord-like structures exceeding 100 micrometers in length was quantified. In the absence of any stimulus, HUVECs formed minimal networks, with an average of 14 ± 7 tubes per field. In the presence of fetal bovine serum, tube formation increased to 352% of baseline, while VEGF165 induced a more robust response, resulting in 620% of baseline tube formation. Addition of 10 nanomolar RI-3 to the culture medium resulted in a 61% reduction in serum-induced tube formation and a 75% reduction in VEGF-induced tube formation, with both effects reaching statistical significance. The inhibition was dose-dependent, with a 30% reduction observed at 1 nanomolar and maximal inhibition at 10 nanomolar, beyond which no further effect was observed. The structure of the remaining networks in the presence of RI-3 was fragmented and disorganized, with fewer branch points and shorter cord lengths compared to controls. Importantly, RI-3 did not affect endothelial cell viability, proliferation, or adhesion at any concentration tested, as confirmed by MTS and xCELLigence assays, indicating that the inhibition of tube formation is not due to cytotoxicity. The inhibition of VEGF-induced angiogenesis is particularly significant, as VEGF is a major driver of pathological neovascularization in tumors and is the target of clinically approved agents such as bevacizumab. The fact that RI-3 suppresses VEGF-driven angiogenesis suggests that it interferes with signaling pathways downstream of VEGF receptor activation, potentially through modulation of FPR1 expression or crosstalk with VEGF receptor signaling. This dual inhibition of both serum- and VEGF-induced angiogenesis positions RI-3 as a broad-spectrum anti-angiogenic agent capable of targeting multiple drivers of tumor vascularization. The suppression of neovascularization is a critical component of its anti-metastatic mechanism, as it limits the formation of new blood vessels that facilitate tumor cell dissemination and nutrient supply to metastatic deposits. The potency and specificity of RI-3 in this context, combined with its stability and lack of toxicity, make it a promising candidate for combination therapy with existing anti-angiogenic agents.

## EXAMPLE 11. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 EXERTS ANTI-METASTATIC EFFECT REDUCING IN VIVO TUMOR GROWTH AND VASCULAR INFILTRATION BY SARCOMA CELLS

The in vivo anti-metastatic efficacy of the retro-inverso peptide RI-3 was evaluated in a xenograft model of human chondrosarcoma using immunocompromised Foxn1nu/nu nude mice. A single-cell suspension of GFP-tagged Sarc cells (1 × 10^6 cells in 100 μL PBS) was injected subcutaneously into the right flank of 16 mice, which were then randomized into two groups: one receiving daily intraperitoneal injections of 6 mg/kg RI-3, and the other receiving an equivalent volume of vehicle (PBS). Tumor growth was monitored daily using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula ½ × (width)^2 × length. Over a 10-day period, tumor growth in the vehicle-treated group was rapid and exponential, reaching a mean volume of 330 ± 163 mm³ by day 10. In contrast, mice treated with RI-3 exhibited significantly slower tumor growth, with a mean volume of 185 ± 82 mm³, representing a 44% reduction in tumor size (p < 0.01). Importantly, body weight remained stable in both groups throughout the study, with no signs of systemic toxicity, lethargy, or behavioral abnormalities, indicating that RI-3 is well tolerated at therapeutic doses. Upon sacrifice, tumors were excised and processed for histological analysis. Immunohistochemical staining for CD31, a marker of endothelial cells, revealed a marked reduction in microvessel density in RI-3-treated tumors compared to controls, with a 58% decrease in the number of CD31-positive vascular channels per field at 200× magnification. The vessels in treated tumors were shorter, less branched, and contained fewer red blood cells, consistent with impaired angiogenesis. Circulating tumor cells were quantified in blood samples collected prior to sacrifice using real-time PCR targeting human Alu sequences, which are absent in murine DNA. Vehicle-treated mice exhibited a mean of 10.04 ± 3.7 CTCs/mL, whereas RI-3-treated mice had a mean of 2.6 ± 2.7 CTCs/mL, with four out of eight mice showing no detectable CTCs. This represents an 80% reduction in circulating tumor burden, directly linking the anti-metastatic effect of RI-3 to its ability to inhibit intravasation. In a parallel experiment, mice were treated with either RI-3 or the previously characterized antagonist RERF for 12 days. While both agents reduced tumor growth, RI-3 was significantly more effective, reducing tumor volume by 44% compared to 24% for RERF, a difference attributable to RI-3’s superior metabolic stability. Fluorescent imaging of excised lungs revealed multiple GFP-positive metastatic nodules in 3 out of 5 vehicle-treated mice and 1 out of 5 RERF-treated mice, whereas all lungs from RI-3-treated mice were free of detectable metastases. Histological examination of lung sections confirmed the presence of micrometastases in vehicle and RERF groups, but not in the RI-3 group. These findings demonstrate that RI-3 exerts a comprehensive anti-metastatic effect in vivo by simultaneously inhibiting primary tumor growth, tumor vascularization, intravasation, and metastatic colonization. The absence of toxicity, combined with the breadth and potency of its effects, establishes RI-3 as a first-in-class therapeutic agent capable of targeting the metastatic cascade at multiple levels, offering a transformative approach to the clinical management of metastatic solid tumors.
