# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of machine learning and graph theory, specifically to a novel framework for inductive representation learning on graphs that enables the automated discovery of sparse, interpretable, and transferable relational functions for nodes and edges across arbitrary networks. The invention is particularly applicable to large-scale attributed graphs where traditional embedding methods fail to generalize beyond the training graph, require excessive memory resources, or produce opaque, non-interpretable feature representations. The framework is designed for use in domains including but not limited to social network analysis, biological interaction networks, citation networks, communication systems, recommendation engines, fraud detection, cybersecurity threat modeling, and knowledge graph completion. The invention provides a scalable, parallelizable, and mathematically grounded approach to learning hierarchical graph representations that preserve structural and attributive information while enabling cross-network inference without retraining. Unlike prior art that produces dense, node-specific embeddings tied to fixed graph topologies, the disclosed system generates relational functions—compositional operations over neighborhood features—that can be applied to any graph, regardless of size, node identity, or structural composition, thereby enabling true inductive learning for tasks such as link classification, network alignment, graph similarity, anomaly detection, and role discovery.

## BACKGROUND

The representation of relational data in the form of graphs has become a cornerstone of modern machine learning, underpinning applications ranging from social network analysis to drug discovery and recommendation systems. A fundamental challenge in this domain lies in the transformation of raw graph structures—comprising nodes, edges, and associated attributes—into numerical feature representations that capture meaningful patterns for downstream predictive modeling. Early approaches relied heavily on hand-engineered features such as degree centrality, clustering coefficients, or shortest-path distances, which, while interpretable, are often insufficient to capture complex higher-order topological dependencies. With the rise of deep learning, a new class of methods emerged that sought to automatically learn low-dimensional vector embeddings for nodes by leveraging random walks and neural language models inspired by word2vec. These methods, including DeepWalk, node2vec, and LINE, achieved notable success in transductive learning settings, where the model is trained and evaluated on the same graph, and where node identities are preserved throughout the process.

However, these approaches suffer from critical limitations that severely restrict their practical utility. First, they are fundamentally transductive in nature: the learned embeddings are tied to specific node identifiers within a fixed graph, rendering them incapable of generalizing to unseen nodes or entirely new graphs. This renders them useless for dynamic environments where graphs evolve over time, or for transfer learning scenarios where knowledge from one network must be applied to another—such as predicting interactions in a new social platform using patterns learned from an existing one. Second, the resulting feature vectors are dense, real-valued, and often negative, requiring substantial memory to store, especially at scale. For example, embedding a graph with ten million nodes using 128-dimensional vectors demands over ten gigabytes of memory, a prohibitive cost for many real-world deployments. Third, these embeddings are inherently uninterpretable; there is no clear semantic meaning attached to individual dimensions, making it impossible for domain experts to validate, debug, or trust the model’s predictions. Fourth, these methods are computationally expensive, with training times that scale poorly with graph size, often requiring hours or days for large networks, and they lack efficient parallelization strategies that leverage modern multi-core architectures. Fifth, they are unable to capture higher-order subgraph structures beyond local neighborhoods, limiting their ability to detect complex roles or motifs such as stars, cliques, or cascading patterns that are critical in many domains.

Moreover, existing methods do not provide mechanisms for feature selection or redundancy removal, leading to the accumulation of highly correlated or noisy features that degrade model performance and increase computational overhead. The absence of a principled mechanism for pruning or evaluating feature utility results in feature spaces that are bloated, inefficient, and difficult to manage. Furthermore, no prior method has successfully combined the ability to learn from both structural and attributed information in a unified, scalable, and interpretable framework. While some approaches have attempted to incorporate node attributes, they do so in a passive manner—concatenating attributes to embeddings without learning how to compose them meaningfully with structural features. Other methods, such as GraphSAGE, introduced limited forms of inductive learning by aggregating neighborhood features, but they remain constrained to fixed architectures with predefined aggregation functions and lack the compositional expressivity to discover novel relational patterns autonomously. They also do not produce sparse representations, nor do they provide a mechanism to interpret the learned functions in terms of their constituent operators and base features.

The need for a new paradigm in graph representation learning is therefore acute. There exists a critical gap between the theoretical promise of automatic feature learning and the practical demands of real-world applications: scalability, interpretability, transferability, and efficiency. The prior art fails to deliver on any one of these dimensions simultaneously. This invention addresses this gap by introducing DeepGL, a novel framework that learns hierarchical, sparse, and interpretable relational functions through iterative composition of well-defined operators over base features derived from graph topology and attributes. Unlike embedding-based methods that map nodes to points in a latent space, DeepGL learns functions that map graph elements to feature values through explicit, compositional operations—functions that can be applied to any graph, regardless of its structure or size. This shift from representation to function enables true inductive learning, dramatically reduces memory footprint, accelerates computation, and provides unprecedented transparency into the learned representations. The invention thus represents a fundamental departure from the prevailing paradigm in graph representation learning, offering a robust, scalable, and intelligible solution to the challenges of modern network analysis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention, referred to herein as DeepGL, is a general, inductive framework for learning hierarchical, sparse, and interpretable relational functions over nodes and edges in attributed graphs. The framework operates by iteratively composing simple, well-defined relational operators over base features derived from the graph structure and any available node or edge attributes, thereby constructing increasingly complex and discriminative representations across multiple layers. Each layer in the hierarchy captures features of progressively higher order, moving from local neighborhood statistics to global structural roles and subgraph patterns. Unlike prior methods that output dense, fixed-length embeddings tied to node identities, DeepGL produces a set of relational functions—each representing a composition of operators applied to base features—that can be evaluated on any arbitrary graph, enabling true transfer learning across networks without retraining. The framework is designed to be both space-efficient and computationally scalable, with runtime complexity linear in the number of edges and memory consumption reduced by up to sixfold compared to existing approaches. Furthermore, the learned functions are inherently interpretable, allowing domain experts to trace the meaning of each feature back to its constituent base feature and operator, thereby facilitating model validation, debugging, and trust.

The process begins with the derivation of a set of base graph features, which serve as the foundational building blocks for all subsequent representations. These base features are computed using the graph’s topology and any initial attributes provided as input. If no attributes are given, the base features are derived solely from structural properties such as node degree, edge degree, k-core numbers, and egonet statistics. For each node or edge, the in-degree, out-degree, total degree, and weighted variants thereof are computed. Egonet features are also generated, capturing the local neighborhood structure within a specified hop distance—typically one or two hops for scalability. These include counts of neighbors, neighbor degrees, and aggregated properties of the immediate neighborhood. In addition, graphlet-based features are computed using local decomposition methods to count the occurrences of small induced subgraphs (graphlets) of sizes up to five nodes, partitioned into automorphism orbits. Each orbit corresponds to a unique positional role within a graphlet, such as a leaf node in a star or a bridge node in a path. The counts of these orbits for each node or edge are concatenated into a feature vector, providing a fine-grained characterization of local structural context. All base features—structural, attributed, and graphlet-based—are concatenated into a single initial feature matrix, X, where each row corresponds to a graph element (node or edge) and each column corresponds to a distinct base feature. This matrix is sparse by design, as many base features are zero for most elements, and it serves as the input to the iterative feature composition phase.

The core innovation of DeepGL lies in its iterative, compositional search over a space of relational functions, defined as compositions of relational feature operators applied to previously learned features. A relational feature operator is a function that aggregates feature values across a set of related graph elements, such as the immediate neighbors of a node or edge. Examples of such operators include the mean, sum, product, maximum, minimum, and standard deviation, as well as more complex operators such as the Hadamard product or weighted aggregation. Each operator is applied to a set S of related graph elements, which may be defined as the one-hop in-neighbors, out-neighbors, or total neighbors of a given element. The framework maintains a set F of relational feature operators, which may be predefined or learned via cross-validation. At each iteration, the framework generates candidate features by applying every operator in F to every feature learned in the previous layer. For instance, if a base feature is the in-degree of a node, applying the mean operator over its in-neighbors yields a new feature: “the mean in-degree of individuals who have sent messages to this node.” This new feature is a relational function of order two: one operator applied to a base feature. Subsequent layers may apply additional operators to this result, yielding functions of higher order, such as “the maximum of the mean in-degrees of the in-neighbors,” which captures whether the most influential senders to a node are themselves highly connected. The framework explores this combinatorial space efficiently by generating candidate features in parallel across all graph elements and all existing features, leveraging the independence of computations across nodes and edges.

To prevent the explosion of redundant or noisy features, the framework employs a rigorous feature evaluation and pruning mechanism. After generating candidate features for a new layer, the system constructs a weighted feature dependence graph, where each node represents a candidate feature and each edge represents the statistical dependence between two features. Dependence is measured using a scoring function K, such as agreement score, mutual information, or correlation, which quantifies how similarly two features behave across the graph elements. For example, if two features assign nearly identical values to all nodes, they are deemed redundant and connected by a high-weight edge. The resulting graph is then partitioned into connected components using a clustering algorithm, such as union-find or community detection. From each connected component, only one representative feature is retained—the earliest generated one—while all others are discarded. This ensures that only novel, non-redundant features survive to the next layer, maintaining a minimal, discriminative feature set. The pruning step is critical: it prevents the feature space from becoming bloated and ensures that each retained feature contributes unique information. The retained features are then transformed using a binning technique, such as logarithmic binning, to further enhance sparsity. In this process, feature values are sorted, and the smallest a fraction of values are mapped to zero, followed by successive bins mapped to discrete integer levels. This transformation converts continuous values into sparse, integer-encoded representations, reducing memory usage from eight bytes per value to one or two bytes, and enabling efficient storage as sparse vectors. The transformed feature vectors are then concatenated to the feature matrix, and the process repeats for a fixed number of layers or until no new features are discovered.

The framework supports both supervised and unsupervised learning modes. In the unsupervised setting, feature selection is driven purely by redundancy minimization and feature novelty. In the supervised setting, the evaluation function K is replaced by an objective that maximizes predictive relevance to a target variable—such as a binary label for link classification—while simultaneously minimizing redundancy among selected features. This is formalized as an optimization problem that balances the mutual information between each candidate feature and the target label against its mutual information with already-selected features. The greedy selection process begins with the feature most predictive of the label and iteratively adds the next best feature that provides the highest marginal gain in relevance while contributing the least redundancy. This ensures that the final set of features is both predictive and compact, ideal for downstream classification or regression tasks.

An additional innovation is the optional integration of feature diffusion, a graph regularization technique that smooths feature values across the network to enhance generalization. After each feature layer is computed, the feature matrix may be updated by propagating values through the graph using a diffusion operator such as the normalized Laplacian or simple random walk transition matrix. This process allows features to be influenced by their distant neighbors, effectively capturing global structural context without requiring explicit higher-order computation. The diffusion step is applied after feature generation but before pruning, ensuring that the dependence graph is built on smoothed, stabilized values that better reflect true structural relationships rather than local noise.

The framework is fully parallelizable, with each graph element’s feature computation being independent of others. The generation of candidate features for a given layer can be distributed across multiple processing units, with each unit responsible for a subset of nodes or edges. The evaluation and pruning steps are also amenable to parallelization, as the dependence graph can be constructed in parallel by computing pairwise scores across disjoint subsets of features. The system achieves strong scaling on multi-core architectures, with near-linear speedup observed across a range of graph sizes and operator configurations. This scalability is further enhanced by the sparsity of the feature representations, which reduce memory bandwidth requirements and enable efficient cache utilization.

The output of DeepGL consists of two components: a sparse feature matrix X and a set of relational functions F. The feature matrix contains the final learned representations for each node or edge, encoded as sparse, integer-valued vectors. The set of relational functions F contains the symbolic definitions of each feature, expressed as compositions of base features and operators. For example, a feature may be defined as F_mean(F_sum(d_in)), meaning “the mean of the sum of in-degrees of in-neighbors.” These definitions are stored as executable functions that can be applied to any graph, regardless of its size or node identity. This is the key to inductive transfer learning: to apply the learned features to a new graph, one simply evaluates the same set of functions on the new graph’s structure and attributes, producing feature vectors in the same dimensional space. There is no need for retraining, fine-tuning, or node alignment. This enables applications such as cross-network link classification, where a model trained on a social network from one platform can be deployed on another platform with entirely different users and connections, or network alignment, where nodes in two different graphs are matched based on the similarity of their relational function outputs.

The space complexity of the framework is dramatically lower than that of existing methods. While traditional embeddings require O(N × D) memory for N nodes and D dimensions, DeepGL requires only O(F × (a × N)) memory, where F is the number of learned features, N is the number of nodes, and a is the binning parameter controlling sparsity. For a typical setting with a = 0.3, this reduces memory usage by a factor of three to six compared to dense embeddings. Time complexity is linear in the number of edges, O(M × F × K), where M is the number of edges, F is the number of features, and K is the number of operators. This is due to the fact that each feature computation involves aggregating over the neighbors of each node or edge, and the total number of neighbor accesses is proportional to the number of edges. The framework avoids expensive operations such as matrix factorization, eigen-decomposition, or stochastic gradient descent, which dominate the runtime of competing methods.

The interpretability of DeepGL’s outputs is a defining feature. Each learned feature can be traced back to its base component and the sequence of operators applied. A domain expert can examine a feature definition and understand its semantic meaning in the context of the graph. For instance, in an email network, a feature defined as “the mean in-degree of the in-neighbors” translates to “the average number of emails received by people who have sent emails to this user,” revealing whether a user is surrounded by highly active communicators. This level of transparency is impossible with dense embeddings, where individual dimensions have no interpretable meaning. Moreover, the framework naturally discovers structural roles—such as gatekeepers, peripheral nodes, or hubs—by composing operators that capture hierarchical neighborhood patterns. These roles are not predefined but emerge from the data, and their definitions provide a direct link between statistical patterns and behavioral interpretations.

The framework is also robust to noise and partial observations. Experiments show that DeepGL maintains stable performance even when significant fractions of edges are missing or spurious edges are introduced. The pruning mechanism naturally filters out features that become unstable under perturbation, and the iterative composition allows the system to adapt its feature depth based on graph complexity. Unlike fixed-dimension embedding methods, DeepGL automatically determines the appropriate number of layers and features, eliminating the need for manual hyperparameter tuning in most cases.

In summary, DeepGL represents a paradigm shift in graph representation learning. It replaces the notion of node embeddings with the notion of relational functions, enabling inductive generalization, sparse storage, linear-time computation, and human-interpretable outputs. The framework is not tied to any specific operator, base feature, or evaluation criterion, making it highly flexible and extensible. It can incorporate features from other methods as base inputs, integrate new operators as they are developed, and adapt to diverse graph types and application domains. It is the first system to simultaneously achieve scalability, transferability, interpretability, and efficiency in graph representation learning, and it enables a new class of applications that were previously infeasible due to the limitations of existing methods.
