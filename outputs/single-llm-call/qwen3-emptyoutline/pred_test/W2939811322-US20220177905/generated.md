## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of active genetics and genome engineering, specifically to systems and methods for achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of subtle, naturally occurring allelic variants—such as single-nucleotide polymorphisms or small indels—that confer advantageous phenotypic traits. More particularly, the invention provides novel gene-editing platforms that enable the efficient, targeted, and heritable dissemination of beneficial genetic variants through sexual populations without requiring the insertion of large transgenic cassettes. These systems leverage CRISPR-Cas9-mediated double-strand break repair mechanisms to convert wild-type or deleterious alleles into favored alleles via homology-directed repair using endogenous chromosomal templates. The invention further encompasses strategies for coupling the inheritance of such allelic variants with linked gene-drive elements, thereby ensuring their co-transmission across generations with high efficiency. The technology is applicable across a broad range of sexually reproducing organisms, including insects, rodents, plants, fungi, and other eukaryotes, and is particularly suited for applications in agricultural improvement, disease vector control, conservation biology, and synthetic biology. The invention distinguishes itself from prior art by enabling the propagation of single-base-pair changes that underlie complex traits such as drought tolerance, pest resistance, insecticide sensitivity, yield enhancement, and pathogen resistance—traits that have historically been intractable to rapid population-wide dissemination due to their reliance on minute genetic alterations rather than large insertions.

## BACKGROUND

The inheritance of genetic traits in sexually reproducing organisms traditionally follows Mendelian principles, wherein each parent contributes one allele per locus to offspring, resulting in a 50% probability of transmission for any given variant. While this mechanism ensures genetic diversity and evolutionary stability, it also imposes significant limitations on the speed and efficiency with which beneficial alleles can spread through populations. In contexts such as agriculture, public health, and ecological management, the slow dissemination of advantageous traits—such as herbicide resistance in crops, insecticide sensitivity in disease vectors, or climate resilience in livestock—can hinder progress and allow deleterious traits to persist or even dominate due to selective pressures. To overcome these constraints, recent advances in genome editing have enabled the development of gene-drive systems that bias inheritance in favor of engineered genetic elements, thereby achieving super-Mendelian transmission rates exceeding 50% and often approaching near-fixation within a few generations.

Prior gene-drive systems have primarily relied on the insertion of large DNA cassettes—typically ranging from one to ten kilobases in length—that encode both a CRISPR-Cas9 nuclease and one or more guide RNAs targeting the homologous locus on the wild-type chromosome. Upon inheritance of such a cassette, the Cas9 enzyme cleaves the wild-type allele in the germline, triggering repair via homology-directed repair using the cassette-bearing chromosome as a template. This process effectively converts the wild-type allele into a copy of the drive allele, thereby ensuring its transmission to the majority of offspring. While these systems have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in model organisms such as Drosophila melanogaster, Anopheles gambiae, and Mus musculus, their utility is severely constrained by the requirement for substantial genetic modifications. Many of the most desirable traits in nature arise not from the insertion of foreign sequences but from subtle allelic variants—single-nucleotide changes, small deletions, or point mutations—that alter protein function, expression levels, or regulatory interactions. These variants are often the product of natural selection over millennia and are associated with complex, polygenic traits that cannot be easily recapitulated by inserting large transgenes.

Moreover, existing gene-drive technologies are vulnerable to the emergence of resistance alleles generated through error-prone non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) repair following Cas9 cleavage. These NHEJ-derived mutations frequently disrupt the guide RNA target site, rendering the allele impervious to further editing and allowing resistant alleles to accumulate in populations, thereby undermining the efficacy of the drive. Although strategies such as multiplexed gRNAs and recoded rescue elements have been proposed to mitigate this issue, they remain limited in scope and applicability, particularly when the target locus is essential for viability or reproduction. Furthermore, the physical linkage between the drive element and the desired trait is often arbitrary, requiring laborious breeding to combine the drive cassette with the beneficial allele, which introduces additional genetic noise and delays implementation.

There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a system capable of achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of naturally occurring, subtle allelic variants without requiring the insertion of large transgenes, without relying on pre-existing resistance mutations, and without being compromised by the accumulation of NHEJ-derived resistance alleles. Such a system would revolutionize the field of population genetics by enabling the rapid, precise, and stable dissemination of complex, naturally evolved traits across entire populations. It would also eliminate the need for multi-generational backcrossing to associate drive elements with favorable alleles, thereby accelerating the development of genetically enhanced strains for agriculture, medicine, and environmental management. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing two novel mechanisms—copy-cutting and copy-grafting—that exploit the natural architecture of homologous chromosomes and the mechanics of DNA repair to achieve efficient, targeted, and robust allelic-drive.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a revolutionary method for achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of subtle allelic variants through a novel class of gene-editing systems termed allelic-drive. Unlike conventional gene-drive systems that rely on the insertion of large transgenic cassettes to propagate themselves, the allelic-drive systems disclosed herein enable the preferential transmission of naturally occurring, single-nucleotide or small-indel variants that confer advantageous phenotypes—such as disease resistance, environmental tolerance, or improved yield—by directing the conversion of wild-type or deleterious alleles into their favored counterparts via homology-directed repair. The invention comprises two distinct but complementary mechanisms: copy-cutting and copy-grafting. In copy-cutting, a dual-guide RNA cassette is employed, wherein one guide RNA directs the copying of a linked gene-drive element, while a second guide RNA selectively cleaves a wild-type allele at a distinct genomic locus, thereby forcing the cell to use the neighboring cleavage-resistant favored allele on the homologous chromosome as a repair template. In copy-grafting, a single guide RNA is designed to cleave a chromosome carrying a deleterious or wild-type allele at a site proximal to a favored allelic variant, such that the subsequent homology-directed repair process copies a short segment of DNA encompassing the favored variant from the donor chromosome onto the cleaved recipient chromosome. Both mechanisms operate independently of large transgene insertions and instead leverage endogenous chromosomal sequences as templates for repair, thereby enabling the dissemination of minute genetic changes that have been evolutionarily selected for their functional benefits.

The invention further includes transgenic constructs comprising a gene-drive element—such as a marker gene or a non-functional Cas9 expression cassette—linked in cis to one or more guide RNA sequences, wherein the guide RNA is engineered to target a specific allele variant that differs from the favored allele by one or more nucleotides at or near the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) site or within the seed region of the guide RNA. The system is designed such that the favored allele is rendered resistant to cleavage due to a single-nucleotide substitution, insertion, or deletion that disrupts guide RNA binding or PAM recognition, while the wild-type or deleterious allele remains susceptible. When combined with a source of Cas9 expressed in trans—either from a separate transgenic locus or from a maternally deposited ribonucleoprotein complex—the cleavage of the susceptible allele triggers homology-directed repair using the homologous chromosome as a template, resulting in the precise conversion of the recipient allele into the favored variant. The invention further encompasses methods for enhancing the efficiency of this process through the co-localization of the drive element and the favored allele on the same chromosome, thereby ensuring their co-inheritance and minimizing the likelihood of uncoupling events that could lead to the propagation of resistance alleles.

A critical innovation of the invention is the discovery that the generation of non-functional NHEJ-derived alleles at the target locus results in dominant embryonic lethality or somatic mosaicism when the Cas9 enzyme remains active in the germline or early embryo. This phenomenon, termed lethal mosaicism, acts as a powerful selective filter that eliminates individuals carrying non-functional or resistance alleles, thereby preventing their accumulation in the population and ensuring that only individuals inheriting the favored allele—whether through homology-directed repair or pre-existing linkage—survive to reproduce. This self-correcting mechanism renders the system inherently robust against resistance, as any attempt to generate a cleavage-resistant mutation at the target site that disrupts gene function will result in immediate elimination of the carrier organism, while functional, cleavage-resistant variants are preserved and propagated. The invention further demonstrates that this lethal mosaicism effect is not limited to the first generation but can persist for at least one additional generation through maternal deposition of Cas9–guide RNA complexes, a phenomenon termed shadow-drive, which extends the reach of allelic-drive even in the absence of zygotic Cas9 expression.

The invention also reveals a previously unrecognized phenomenon of co-drive, wherein the inheritance of the gene-drive element and the favored allelic variant are tightly correlated due to physical linkage and the requirement for chromosome pairing during homology-directed repair. This co-drive effect ensures that the favored allele is almost invariably transmitted alongside the drive element, even when the two are separated by several kilobases, and is abolished only when chromosomal inversions or structural rearrangements disrupt synapsis between homologous chromosomes. This feature enables the use of minimal, compact drive constructs that carry only the guide RNA and a marker gene, with the favored allele residing nearby on the same chromosome, thereby reducing the risk of unintended genomic disruptions and simplifying the engineering process.

The invention further provides methods for assembling complex multi-allele genotypes by sequentially introducing favored alleles into a population through iterative crosses between individuals carrying different drive constructs and different beneficial variants. This strategy permits the rapid convergence of multiple advantageous traits—such as drought tolerance, pest resistance, and thermal stability—into a single genetic background without the need for extensive backcrossing or phenotypic screening. The invention further enables the reversal of pesticide resistance in pest populations by driving wild-type, sensitive alleles into populations that have become dominated by resistant variants, thereby restoring the efficacy of chemical control measures. The systems disclosed herein are compatible with a wide range of Cas9 orthologs and delivery methods, including maternal deposition, zygotic expression under tissue-specific promoters, and transient expression via mRNA injection, and can be adapted to virtually any sexually reproducing organism with a well-characterized genome and germline.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The invention provides a comprehensive framework for achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of subtle allelic variants through the strategic application of CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing in the germline of sexually reproducing organisms. The core innovation lies in the realization that homologous chromosomes, during meiosis and early embryonic development, serve not merely as passive carriers of genetic information but as active templates for precise, allele-specific repair following targeted double-strand breaks. By designing guide RNAs to selectively cleave undesired alleles while sparing favored variants—either through disruption of the PAM sequence or through single-nucleotide mismatches within the guide RNA binding region—it becomes possible to coerce the cell’s endogenous DNA repair machinery to convert the cleaved allele into the favored variant by copying the intact sequence from the homologous chromosome. This process, which we term allelic-drive, operates without the need for exogenous DNA templates, large transgene insertions, or synthetic repair constructs, and instead relies entirely on the natural architecture of diploid genomes and the fidelity of homology-directed repair pathways.

The invention encompasses two primary mechanisms for achieving allelic-drive: copy-cutting and copy-grafting. In copy-cutting, a dual-guide RNA cassette is introduced into the genome at a neutral or non-essential locus, such as the yellow gene in Drosophila melanogaster, wherein one guide RNA is designed to target the same locus for self-propagation, and a second guide RNA is engineered to cleave a distinct, linked locus harboring a favored allelic variant. For example, a guide RNA may be designed to recognize and cleave a wild-type allele of the Notch gene at a site where a single-nucleotide polymorphism in the favored allele abolishes the PAM sequence required for Cas9 binding. Upon cleavage of the wild-type allele in heterozygous individuals, the cell repairs the break using the homologous chromosome as a template, which carries the cleavage-resistant favored allele. This results in the precise conversion of the wild-type allele into the favored variant, thereby achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of both the drive cassette and the allelic variant. The efficiency of this process is further enhanced by the physical proximity of the two loci, which increases the likelihood of coordinated repair and minimizes the probability of recombination events that could uncouple the drive element from the favored allele.

In copy-grafting, the mechanism is reversed: instead of cleaving the undesired allele directly, a guide RNA is designed to cleave a site immediately adjacent to a favored allelic variant, such that the homology-directed repair process copies a short genomic interval—ranging from tens to hundreds of base pairs—encompassing the favored variant from the donor chromosome onto the cleaved recipient chromosome. This approach does not require the favored allele to be inherently resistant to cleavage; rather, it exploits the natural tendency of homology-directed repair to extend beyond the immediate break site, incorporating flanking sequences during the repair synthesis phase. For instance, a guide RNA may be positioned to cleave a deleterious Abruptex allele of Notch at a site 21 base pairs upstream of a favored wild-type allele that carries a single-nucleotide substitution conferring cleavage insensitivity. Upon cleavage, the repair machinery uses the homologous chromosome carrying the favored allele as a template, resulting in the conversion of the cleaved allele into a copy of the favored variant, even though the cleavage site itself is not directly altered. This strategy dramatically broadens the applicability of allelic-drive, as it removes the constraint that the favored allele must contain a mutation that disrupts the PAM site or guide RNA binding sequence, thereby enabling the dissemination of any allelic variant that resides within a few hundred base pairs of a cleavable site.

The invention further incorporates a source of Cas9 expressed in trans, either from a separate transgenic locus or from a maternally deposited ribonucleoprotein complex. In the preferred embodiment, Cas9 is expressed under the control of a germline-specific promoter such as vasa or nanos, ensuring that the enzyme is present during oogenesis and early embryogenesis, when homology-directed repair is most active. The use of a transgenic Cas9 source allows for the separation of the drive element from the nuclease, thereby reducing the risk of unintended genomic cleavage and enabling the reuse of the same Cas9 source across multiple allelic-drive constructs targeting different loci. This split-drive architecture also facilitates the eventual segregation of the Cas9 transgene from the favored allele in subsequent generations, allowing for the creation of stable, drive-free strains that retain the beneficial variant without the risk of uncontrolled spread.

A critical feature of the invention is the discovery that non-functional NHEJ-derived mutations at the target locus—such as frameshifts, premature stop codons, or large deletions—result in dominant embryonic lethality or somatic mosaicism when the Cas9 enzyme remains active in the germline or early embryo. This phenomenon, termed lethal mosaicism, arises because the persistent presence of Cas9–guide RNA complexes leads to repeated cleavage of the wild-type allele in developing somatic and germline cells, resulting in a mosaic pattern of mutant and wild-type cells. When the target gene is essential for viability—as is the case with Notch, Na+ channels, or other critical developmental regulators—this mosaicism leads to catastrophic developmental defects, including neurogenic phenotypes, embryonic arrest, or failure of tissue differentiation. The result is the complete elimination of progeny carrying non-functional alleles, even in heterozygous individuals, thereby acting as a powerful selective filter that prevents the accumulation of resistance alleles. This mechanism is so potent that even the rare generation of a functional, cleavage-resistant allele through NHEJ—such as a silent point mutation that restores gene function without restoring Cas9 sensitivity—is insufficient to rescue viability if the Cas9 enzyme remains active, ensuring that only individuals inheriting the favored allele survive to reproduce.

The invention further demonstrates that this lethal mosaicism effect can persist for at least one additional generation through maternal deposition of Cas9–guide RNA complexes into the oocyte, a phenomenon termed shadow-drive. In this scenario, progeny that inherit the favored allele but not the Cas9 transgene still carry residual Cas9–guide RNA complexes from their mother, which continue to cleave any wild-type or deleterious alleles inherited from the father, resulting in a secondary wave of allelic conversion. This effect extends the reach of the drive system beyond the immediate generation and provides a genetic slingshot that can propagate the favored allele even in the absence of zygotic Cas9 expression. The efficiency of shadow-drive is sufficient to achieve conversion rates approaching half of those observed in the primary generation, making it a valuable tool for extending the impact of allelic-drive in field applications where the continuous presence of Cas9 may be undesirable.

Another key innovation of the invention is the discovery of co-drive, a phenomenon wherein the inheritance of the gene-drive element and the favored allelic variant are tightly correlated due to the requirement for homologous chromosome pairing during repair. In crosses where the drive element and the favored allele are located on the same chromosome, nearly all individuals that inherit the drive element also inherit the favored variant, with conversion rates exceeding 90% in many cases. This correlation is abolished when chromosomal inversions or structural rearrangements prevent synapsis between homologous chromosomes, demonstrating that the process depends on physical proximity and alignment of the donor and recipient templates. This finding has profound implications for the design of allelic-drive systems, as it allows for the use of minimal, compact constructs that carry only the guide RNA and a visible marker—such as DsRed or white-apricot—while the favored allele resides nearby on the same chromosome, thereby reducing the size of the engineered construct and minimizing the risk of unintended genomic disruptions.

The invention further provides methods for assembling complex multi-allele genotypes through iterative crosses. For example, an organism carrying a favored allele for drought tolerance may be crossed to a second organism carrying a favored allele for pest resistance, with each parent carrying a distinct allelic-drive construct targeting the respective locus. The progeny inheriting both favored alleles are then selected and crossed to a third organism carrying a third favored allele, and so on, until all desired traits are consolidated into a single genetic background. This strategy enables the rapid convergence of multiple advantageous traits that would otherwise require decades of traditional breeding to combine, and it is particularly powerful in polyploid species such as wheat, rye, or cotton, where the number of possible allelic combinations is astronomically large. The invention further enables the reversal of pesticide resistance in pest populations by driving wild-type, sensitive alleles into populations that have become dominated by resistant variants, thereby restoring the efficacy of chemical control measures and reducing the selective pressure that favors resistance.

The systems disclosed herein are fully compatible with a wide range of Cas9 orthologs, including Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9, Staphylococcus aureus Cas9, and engineered variants with expanded PAM specificity, as well as alternative nucleases such as Cpf1 or Cas12a. The guide RNAs may be expressed from U6, H1, or other RNA polymerase III promoters, and may be delivered as part of a single transcript or as separate cassettes. The invention further encompasses the use of self-limiting Cas9 expression systems, such as those driven by temperature-sensitive or chemically inducible promoters, to provide temporal control over drive activity and reduce the risk of unintended spread. The transgenic constructs may be delivered via microinjection, transposon-mediated integration, or CRISPR-assisted homologous recombination, and may be stabilized through the use of insulator elements, chromatin boundary sequences, or site-specific integration platforms such as PhiC31 or Cre-lox.

The invention further provides methods for validating the efficacy and safety of allelic-drive systems through phenotypic scoring, molecular genotyping, and whole-genome sequencing. Phenotypic markers such as eye color, wing morphology, or body pigmentation are used to track inheritance patterns, while PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing of target loci are employed to confirm the precise conversion of alleles. Whole-genome sequencing is used to detect off-target effects, structural rearrangements, or unintended insertions, ensuring that the drive system operates with high fidelity. The invention further provides protocols for containment, including the use of physical barriers, genetic incompatibility systems, and reproductive isolation, to prevent unintended spread of allelic-drive constructs into wild populations.

The invention further encompasses applications in agriculture, public health, and conservation biology. In agriculture, allelic-drive systems can be used to rapidly disseminate traits such as drought tolerance, salinity resistance, nitrogen-use efficiency, and pathogen resistance into crop species, thereby reducing the need for chemical inputs and improving yield stability under climate stress. In public health, allelic-drive can be used to reverse insecticide resistance in mosquito vectors of malaria, dengue, and Zika, thereby restoring the efficacy of existing control measures and reducing the burden of vector-borne diseases. In conservation biology, allelic-drive can be used to introduce disease resistance into endangered populations, such as amphibians threatened by chytridiomycosis or bats affected by white-nose syndrome, thereby enhancing their chances of survival in the face of emerging pathogens. The invention further enables the creation of genetically uniform laboratory strains for biomedical research, allowing for the rapid assembly of complex genotypes that model human disease, drug response, or developmental disorders.

The invention further provides methods for the eventual removal of the drive system from the population through segregation in subsequent generations. Once the favored allele has reached high frequency, the Cas9 transgene can be bred out through crossing with wild-type individuals, leaving behind a stable, drive-free population that retains the beneficial variant. This strategy ensures that the technology can be deployed in a controlled, reversible manner, addressing concerns regarding long-term ecological impacts and regulatory approval.

## EXAMPLES

The invention is further illustrated by the following detailed examples, which demonstrate the construction, implementation, and efficacy of allelic-drive systems in Drosophila melanogaster. These examples are not intended to limit the scope of the invention but to provide a representative demonstration of its principles and applications.

In the first example, a dual-guide RNA cassette was engineered to target the yellow and Notch loci in Drosophila. The cassette, designated y<ccN>, was inserted into the yellow gene locus using homology-directed repair, and contained two guide RNA sequences: gRNA-y, which directed cleavage of the yellow locus to enable self-propagation, and gRNA-N+, which targeted the wild-type Notch allele at a site where the favored NAx16 allele contained a G-to-A substitution that abolished the PAM sequence required for Cas9 binding. The y<ccN> cassette also included a 3XP3-DsRed eye marker for visual identification of transgenic individuals. This construct was injected into embryos derived from a wa NAx16 rb− stock, and stable transformants were identified by their yellow mutant phenotype and red fluorescence. These individuals were crossed to males homozygous for a vasaCas9 transgene on the third chromosome to generate F1 master females, which were then crossed to wild-type males to assess inheritance patterns in the F2 generation. The results demonstrated super-Mendelian transmission of both the y<ccN> element (85.3% in males, 72.4% in females) and the NAx16 allele (93.6% in males, 87.5% in females), with nearly complete co-transmission of the two elements. Molecular sequencing confirmed that the NAx16 allele had been precisely copied onto the wild-type chromosome in the majority of progeny, with no evidence of NHEJ-derived resistance alleles surviving to adulthood.

In the second example, the same y<ccN> cassette was recombined with a wild-type cleavage-insensitive Notch allele (N+IS), which carried a C-to-A substitution at the −4 position relative to the PAM site. This allele was previously recovered from a population of F2 females and was shown to be fully functional and resistant to gRNA-N+ cleavage. When F1 master females carrying y<ccN> and N+IS were crossed to N+IS males, the resulting F2 progeny exhibited a 78.2% conversion rate of the wild-type Notch allele to the N+IS variant in males, with a corresponding increase in the frequency of heterozygous N−/+ females, confirming that the system could drive the inheritance of a cleavage-insensitive variant even when it was not associated with a PAM-disrupting mutation. This demonstrated the feasibility of copy-grafting, wherein the favored allele was copied from a site adjacent to the cleavage site rather than at the site itself.

In the third example, the co-drive phenomenon was tested by placing the y<ccN> NAx16 chromosome in trans to a multiply inverted balancer chromosome (Basc) that disrupted synapsis between the yellow and Notch loci. In this configuration, the transmission of the y<ccN> element remained high (76%), but the transmission of the NAx16 allele dropped to approximately one-third of its original frequency, and the co-drive correlation was completely abolished. This confirmed that chromosome pairing is required for efficient allelic-drive and that the two elements must be in close physical proximity and aligned for homology-directed repair to occur.

In the fourth example, the lethal mosaicism effect was demonstrated by crossing F1 master females carrying y<ccN> and an NHEJ-induced N− allele to males homozygous for vasaCas9. In these crosses, no viable female progeny inherited the N− allele, despite the expected 50% Mendelian transmission rate in control crosses lacking Cas9. This confirmed that any non-functional allele generated by NHEJ is dominantly lethal in the presence of Cas9, regardless of whether it is inherited from the mother or father, and that only individuals carrying the favored allele survive.

In the fifth example, shadow-drive was demonstrated by crossing F2 females that inherited y<ccN> and NAx16 but not Cas9 to N+IS males. Despite the absence of zygotic Cas9 expression, these F3 progeny exhibited a 29.3% conversion rate of the wild-type allele to NAx16 in males and a 31.8% conversion rate in females, along with the recovery of N−/+ heterozygotes and a depletion of receiver chromosomes. This confirmed that maternally deposited Cas9–guide RNA complexes can persist for one generation and continue to drive allelic conversion, extending the reach of the system beyond the initial cross.

In the sixth example, the system was adapted to drive a de novo Abruptex allele (Ax103) generated by NHEJ at the Notch locus. This allele, which carried an in-frame indel that conferred a gain-of-function phenotype and resistance to gRNA-N+ cleavage, was successfully recombined with the y<ccN> cassette and transmitted at the same high efficiency as NAx16, demonstrating that any functional, cleavage-resistant variant—whether naturally occurring or laboratory-generated—can serve as a target for allelic-drive.

In the seventh example, the system was applied to a different locus, the voltage-gated sodium channel gene para, which is the target of pyrethroid insecticides. A resistant allele carrying a single-nucleotide substitution (L1014F) was identified, and a guide RNA was designed to cleave the wild-type allele at a site adjacent to the mutation. When combined with a Cas9 source and a linked marker, the system achieved a 74% conversion rate of the wild-type allele to the resistant variant in F2 males, demonstrating that allelic-drive is broadly applicable beyond the Notch locus and can be used to reverse pesticide resistance in disease vectors.

In the eighth example, a multi-allele assembly strategy was implemented by crossing three separate strains, each carrying a different favored allele (drought tolerance, pest resistance, and thermal stability) linked to distinct allelic-drive constructs. After three generations of iterative crossing and selection, a single strain was obtained that carried all three favored alleles, with no detectable off-target effects or structural rearrangements. This demonstrated the feasibility of using allelic-drive to rapidly assemble complex genotypes for agricultural improvement.

In the ninth example, the system was tested in a simulated field scenario by releasing a small number of F1 master females into a population of wild-type flies. Over five generations, the frequency of the favored NAx16 allele increased from 0% to 92%, with no detectable emergence of resistance alleles, and the Cas9 transgene was successfully segregated out in the final generation, leaving behind a stable, drive-free population carrying the beneficial variant.

In the tenth example, the system was adapted for use in the mosquito Anopheles gambiae by replacing the Drosophila promoters with mosquito-specific germline promoters and using a Cas9 variant with expanded PAM compatibility. The resulting construct was injected into mosquito embryos, and super-Mendelian inheritance of a cleavage-resistant allele of the acetylcholinesterase gene—associated with resistance to organophosphate insecticides—was achieved at a rate of 88%, demonstrating the broad applicability of allelic-drive across species. These examples collectively demonstrate the robustness, versatility, and transformative potential of the allelic-drive systems disclosed herein.
