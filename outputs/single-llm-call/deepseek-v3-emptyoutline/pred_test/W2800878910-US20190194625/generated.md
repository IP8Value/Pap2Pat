# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is a critical physiological structure that selectively permits the passage of essential molecules while restricting harmful substances from entering the central nervous system. Despite its importance, current in vitro models of the BBB fail to fully replicate the complex cellular interactions and three-dimensional architecture of the human neurovascular unit (NVU). Existing models typically incorporate only two or three cell types, such as endothelial cells, pericytes, and astrocytes, neglecting other essential components like microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons. These simplified models lack the physiological relevance necessary for accurate drug screening and toxicity assessment, contributing to the high failure rate of central nervous system drug candidates in clinical trials.  

Moreover, conventional two-dimensional cultures do not adequately mimic the structural and functional properties of the BBB, leading to inaccurate predictions of drug permeability and neurotoxicity. There is a pressing need for an advanced in vitro model that incorporates all major cell types of the human NVU in a three-dimensional arrangement to better simulate the BBB's selective permeability, transport mechanisms, and response to pathological conditions such as hypoxia.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides a three-dimensional multicellular organoid model of the human neurovascular unit that includes all six major cell types found in the brain cortex: human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs), human brain vascular pericytes (HBVPs), human astrocytes (HAs), human microglia (HMs), human oligodendrocytes (HOs), and human neurons (HNs). The organoid is constructed using a staged assembly method, wherein neural and glial components (HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs) are first aggregated, followed by the addition of HBMECs and HBVPs to form a functional BBB-like structure.  

This model demonstrates key features of the human BBB, including the expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, claudin-5), adherens junction proteins (VE-cadherin, β-catenin), and transport proteins (P-glycoprotein, GLUT1). The organoid exhibits selective permeability, restricting the passage of charged molecules such as mercury ions while allowing lipophilic compounds like MPTP to cross. Additionally, the model responds to physiological perturbations, such as hypoxia, by disrupting junctional protein localization, mimicking ischemic conditions observed in stroke.  

The invention further provides methods for using the organoid in drug screening, neurotoxicity assessment, and disease modeling, offering a physiologically relevant platform for evaluating BBB penetration and neurological effects of therapeutic candidates.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS  

### 1. Blood-Brain Barrier Models and Methods of Making the Same  

The multicellular organoid model of the invention comprises six cell types: HBMECs, HBVPs, HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs, arranged in a three-dimensional structure that mimics the human NVU. The organoid is constructed using a staged assembly process to ensure proper cellular organization and BBB functionality.  

In one embodiment, the organoid is formed by first aggregating HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs in a hanging drop culture system. These cells are mixed in a ratio optimized to replicate the cellular composition of the human brain cortex, typically comprising 15% HAs, 5% HMs, 15% HOs, and 20% HNs. The neural-glial organoids are cultured for 48 hours to allow self-assembly before the addition of HBMECs (30%) and HBVPs (15%). The endothelial cells and pericytes coat the surface of the neural-glial core, forming a functional BBB-like structure.  

The organoids are maintained in a specialized medium comprising 60% neural maintenance medium, 20% endothelial growth medium, and 20% astrocyte growth medium to support the viability and functionality of all cell types. The model exhibits high cell viability for up to 21 days in culture, making it suitable for long-term toxicity and drug efficacy studies.  

Key features of the organoid include the expression of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, claudin-5) and adherens junction proteins (VE-cadherin, β-catenin), which are critical for BBB integrity. The model also expresses transport proteins such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), which mediate efflux and nutrient transport, respectively. Immunofluorescence staining confirms the localization of these proteins at the endothelial cell borders, demonstrating the formation of a functional barrier.  

### 2. Methods of Use  

The multicellular organoid model of the invention has broad applications in drug development, neurotoxicity screening, and disease modeling.  

**Drug Screening:** The organoid can be used to evaluate the permeability of therapeutic candidates across the BBB. Compounds are applied to the organoid, and their penetration is assessed using fluorescence-labeled tracers (e.g., FITC-IgG) or mass spectrometry. The model's selectivity can distinguish between lipophilic (e.g., MPTP) and hydrophilic (e.g., MPP+) compounds, providing insights into drug design optimization.  

**Neurotoxicity Assessment:** The organoid is particularly useful for assessing the neurotoxic effects of chemicals that cross the BBB. For example, mercury chloride toxicity can be evaluated by measuring ATP production or cell viability in BBB+ (six-cell-type) versus BBB− (neuronal-only) organoids. The model can also screen for glial toxicity and neuronal dysfunction, offering a comprehensive evaluation of compound safety.  

**Disease Modeling:** The organoid can replicate pathological conditions such as hypoxia, which disrupts junctional protein localization and mimics ischemic stroke. By culturing the organoid under low oxygen conditions (0.1% O₂), researchers can study BBB breakdown and identify potential therapeutic interventions.  

**Patient-Specific Applications:** The use of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons enables the development of patient-specific models for personalized medicine and disease research.  

## EXAMPLES  

### Example 1  

**Self-Assembly and Barrier Formation in a Four-Cell-Type Organoid**  

A preliminary organoid model was constructed using HBMECs, HBVPs, HAs, and neuronal cells (HCN-2) in a ratio of 1:1:5:6. The cells were labeled with fluorescent dyes and cultured in a hanging drop system for 96 hours. Confocal microscopy confirmed the localization of endothelial cells and pericytes at the organoid periphery, while astrocytes and neurons formed the core. Immunofluorescence staining detected the tight junction protein ZO-1, demonstrating barrier formation. Permeability assays with FITC-IgG showed restricted diffusion in untreated organoids, whereas histamine treatment increased IgG penetration, indicating dynamic barrier functionality.  

### Example 2  

**Human Cortex Model with Spheroid Culture System**  

A six-cell-type organoid was assembled using the staged method, with HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs aggregated first, followed by the addition of HBMECs and HBVPs. The organoids exhibited high viability for 21 days, as confirmed by live-dead staining. Immunohistochemistry identified cell-specific markers: CD31 (endothelial cells), PDGFR-β (pericytes), GFAP (astrocytes), Iba1 (microglia), O4/MBP (oligodendrocytes), and MAP2 (neurons). Tight junction (claudin-5, ZO-1) and transport proteins (P-gp, GLUT1) were expressed, confirming BBB functionality.  

### Example 3  

**Bioprinting Functional Cortical Tissue**  

The organoid model was adapted for bioprinting by encapsulating the six cell types in a bioink composed of collagen I and Matrigel. The bioprinted tissue retained BBB properties, including selective permeability and junctional protein expression. This approach enables scalable production of standardized organoids for high-throughput screening.  

### Example 4  

**Three-Dimensional Bioprinting of the Human Neurovascular Unit**  

A bioprinted NVU model was constructed by layering HBMECs and HBVPs around a pre-formed neural-glial core. The bioprinted tissue replicated the structural and functional features of the in vivo BBB, including hypoxia-induced junctional disruption. This model is particularly useful for studying ischemic stroke and screening neuroprotective drugs.
