# DESCRIPTION

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The present invention provides a microfluidic metering trap system comprising a main rectangular channel having a defined depth, two side bypass channels milled to approximately one-quarter of the depth of the main channel, and a terminal barrier within the main channel extending upward to approximately three-quarters of the channel height. In operation, a droplet shorter than the length of the trap flows into the module and becomes locked due to increased Laplace back-pressure created when attempting to pass through the barrier. The continuous liquid phase bypasses the trapped droplet through the side channels. When a droplet longer than the trap arrives at the barrier, the rear portion of the droplet blocks the bypass channel entrances, causing the continuous liquid to push the droplet forward until the rear aligns with the trap entrance. This alignment triggers a Rayleigh-Plateau instability that breaks the liquid neck at the front barrier, leaving a precisely metered volume locked in the trap. This system enables accurate volume measurement without requiring precise flow rate control, as the geometric parameters of the trap determine the captured droplet volume rather than operational parameters.

### Example 2

The invention further provides a microfluidic merging trap system comprising bypass channels similar to the metering trap but with side intrusions at both termini rather than a complete barrier. These intrusions create obstacles that increase Laplace back pressure while maintaining greater stability against Rayleigh-Plateau instabilities compared to complete barriers. In operation, a droplet longer than the trap passes through unimpeded as it continuously blocks the bypass inlets. A shorter droplet becomes locked when its front approaches the side intrusions, allowing continuous liquid to flow through the bypasses. When a second droplet enters the trap, it flows to the first droplet as the continuous liquid exits between them through the bypasses, enabling droplet merging. The merged droplet either remains trapped if shorter than the trap or is released if longer, as its increased length blocks bypass inlets, building pressure to push it through the front barrier. This system provides bidirectional functionality and enables controlled droplet merging without precise timing or flow rate requirements.

### Example 3

A lock-and-shift trap embodiment of the invention comprises bypass channels connected to the main channel only at the trap entrance and exit, with complete separation along the trap length. This configuration includes either a terminal barrier similar to the metering trap or side intrusions like the merging trap. The system operates by locking short droplets through increased Laplace back-pressure at the terminal obstacle while allowing continuous liquid bypass through the parallel channels. Unlike previous shift registers, this trap maintains separation between consecutive droplets while enabling single droplet trapping and subsequent release upon approach of another droplet. The geometric isolation of the bypass channels along the trap length provides enhanced control over droplet positioning and prevents unintended interactions between sequential droplets in multi-trap arrays.

### Example 4

The invention includes a delay trap configuration featuring bypass channels similar to the metering trap but without terminal barriers. This geometry allows continuous liquid to bypass trapped droplets, reducing the pressure drop along the droplet and consequently slowing its forward motion. Experimental implementations demonstrate up to a two-fold reduction in droplet speed while maintaining the continuous liquid flow rate. The degree of delay is determined by the geometric parameters of the bypass channels and their interface with the main channel, allowing tunable delay effects without requiring adjustment of flow parameters. This passive delay mechanism proves particularly useful in protocols requiring defined temporal spacing between droplet processing events.

### Example 5

An integrated serial dilution system combines metering, delay, and lock-and-shift traps to transform continuous buffer streams and sample inputs into arrays of serially diluted droplets. The system comprises three functional sections: (1) a metering and delay trap array converting continuous buffer into precisely metered droplets, (2) a sample metering trap, and (3) a lock-and-shift trap array for final dilution positioning. Operation proceeds through three phases: first, buffer is segmented into metered aliquots; second, sample is metered and trapped; third, buffer droplets sequentially merge with and dilute the sample, with each dilution step producing an exponentially decreasing concentration series. The geometric arrangement of traps ensures proper protocol execution regardless of flow rate variations, with experimental results showing less than 5% volume variation across a three-fold flow rate range.

### Example 6

The DOMINO (Droplet Operations through Microfluidic Integrated Network Organization) system represents an advanced embodiment combining metering and merging traps in an alternating array. Each segment consists of a metering trap paired with a merging trap, configured to perform serial dilution through directional flow switching rather than rate control. Operation involves three steps: (1) solvent loading into metering traps, (2) transfer to merging traps and sample loading, and (3) directional flow driving sequential dilution. The system produces consistent two-fold dilutions at each stage, with concentration determined solely by the fixed volume ratio designed into the trap geometry. This configuration demonstrates complete protocol execution with only directional flow control, eliminating requirements for precise timing or flow rate regulation while maintaining less than 3% volume variation at fixed flow rates.

### Example 7

A bi-directional merging trap variant incorporates side intrusions at only one terminus, creating directionally asymmetric functionality. When flow proceeds toward the intrusion terminus, the trap operates as a standard merging module. Flow reversal transforms the trap into a delay configuration for short droplets while allowing unimpeded passage of long droplets. This asymmetric design enables complex protocol sequencing through simple flow direction changes, as the same physical structure performs different functions depending on flow orientation. The directional sensitivity provides additional control dimensions without requiring geometric modifications or active components, maintaining the passive operation advantages of the invention.

### Example 8

The invention includes a high-throughput serial dilution system comprising multiple parallel DOMINO arrays fed by a common sample inlet and solvent distribution network. This scaled configuration produces identical dilution series across multiple channels simultaneously, enabled by the flow-rate independence of the individual trap modules. The parallel arrays share common inlet/outlet controls while maintaining independent operation in each channel, demonstrating the scalability of the passive trap approach. Geometric parameters remain consistent across channels, ensuring uniform performance without requiring individual channel calibration or separate flow control systems.

### Example 9

A diagnostic implementation integrates metering, merging, and lock-and-shift traps with assay regions containing dried reagents. Sample introduction and buffer flow through the passive trap network automatically generates required dilutions and delivers them to predefined assay zones. The geometric arrangement encodes both the dilution protocol and the spatial distribution of assay tests, enabling complex diagnostic panels from simple user actions. This embodiment demonstrates the invention's application in point-of-care diagnostics, where passive operation and robustness against user variability are essential. The system maintains analytical performance across a wide range of operation speeds and user skill levels, as critical parameters are determined by device geometry rather than operational technique.
