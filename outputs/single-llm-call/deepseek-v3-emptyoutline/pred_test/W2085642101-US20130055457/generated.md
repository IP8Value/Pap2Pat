## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE  

The present disclosure relates generally to plant biotechnology and agronomy. More specifically, the disclosure concerns transgenic maize plants exhibiting improved yield characteristics through overexpression of the Zea mays ARGOS (ZAR1) gene, methods for producing such plants, and their use in agricultural systems. The disclosure further encompasses specific allelic variants of ZAR1 that demonstrate superior performance in hybrid combinations, as well as methods for identifying and utilizing these variants in breeding programs.  

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE  

Modern agriculture faces increasing challenges to meet global food demands while contending with environmental stresses such as drought and suboptimal growing conditions. Conventional breeding has achieved steady yield improvements in major crops like maize through selection of favorable genetic combinations. However, this process is time-consuming and limited by existing genetic variation within sexually compatible germplasm.  

Biotechnological approaches offer potential to accelerate crop improvement by introducing specific genes that confer desirable traits. While transgenic crops with simple traits like insect resistance and herbicide tolerance have been successfully commercialized, improving complex agronomic traits such as yield and drought tolerance through single gene modifications has proven more challenging. These traits are typically polygenic and highly influenced by environmental interactions.  

The Arabidopsis ARGOS gene has been shown to regulate organ size through auxin-mediated pathways. However, the function of its maize orthologs, particularly in the context of commercial crop production and their interaction with breeding germplasm, remains poorly understood. There exists an unmet need for transgenic approaches that can effectively enhance yield and stress tolerance while maintaining compatibility with modern breeding systems and performing consistently across diverse environments.  

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE  

The present disclosure provides compositions and methods for improving maize yield and stress tolerance through transgenic expression of ZAR1 genes. Key aspects include:  

1. Isolated nucleic acids encoding ZAR1 polypeptides, particularly allelic variants ZAR1-SS1 and ZAR1-NS1 derived from Stiff Stalk and Non-Stiff Stalk heterotic groups respectively.  

2. Recombinant expression cassettes containing ZAR1 coding sequences operably linked to constitutive, tissue-specific, or inducible promoters.  

3. Transgenic maize plants, plant parts, and seeds comprising ZAR1 expression cassettes, demonstrating improved growth characteristics including increased biomass, enhanced drought tolerance, and improved yield under specific environmental conditions.  

4. Methods of producing transgenic maize plants by introducing ZAR1 expression constructs into maize cells and regenerating plants therefrom.  

5. Hybrid maize plants containing heterozygous combinations of ZAR1 alleles showing synergistic improvements in yield stability across environments.  

6. Methods for identifying superior ZAR1 allelic combinations through association analysis with agronomic traits in breeding populations.  

The transgenic plants of this disclosure exhibit several advantageous properties including: enhanced growth rate without altering developmental timing; increased cell number leading to larger organs; improved performance under drought stress and high planting density; and environment-dependent yield improvements that are particularly pronounced in hot, dry growing conditions.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSURE  

The following detailed description sets forth preferred embodiments of the invention and is not intended to limit its scope, applicability, or configuration. Rather, it provides sufficient detail to enable those skilled in the art to practice the invention.  

### Nucleic Acids  

The disclosure provides isolated nucleic acid molecules encoding ZAR1 polypeptides. In preferred embodiments, the nucleic acids comprise sequences selected from:  

a) The coding sequence of ZAR1-SS1 allele (GenBank Accession No. JN252296)  
b) The coding sequence of ZAR1-NS1 allele (GenBank Accession No. JN252297)  
c) Variants of (a) or (b) having at least 90% sequence identity while retaining ZAR1 biological activity  
d) Nucleic acids hybridizing under stringent conditions to the complement of (a) or (b)  

The ZAR1-SS1 and ZAR1-NS1 alleles differ in both their promoter regions and coding sequences. The SS1 promoter contains distinct cis-regulatory elements compared to the NS1 promoter, resulting in differential expression patterns. At the protein level, key differences include:  

i) A leucine/methionine polymorphism at position 42 (SS1/NS1 respectively) within the conserved core domain  
ii) An alanine/glycine substitution at position 56 affecting protein flexibility  
iii) Additional minor amino acid variations  

These structural differences contribute to functional divergence between the alleles, as demonstrated by their distinct performance in transgenic hybrids and native genetic backgrounds.  

### Recombinant Expression Cassettes  

The disclosure provides recombinant DNA constructs for ZAR1 expression in plants. A typical expression cassette includes:  

1. A promoter region selected from constitutive (e.g., maize ubiquitin), tissue-specific, or inducible promoters  
2. A ZAR1 coding sequence (SS1, NS1, or combination)  
3. A 3' untranslated region providing transcriptional termination (e.g., PINII terminator)  

Preferred constructs incorporate:  

- The native SS1 or NS1 promoter for allele-specific expression patterns  
- A molecular stack of both SS1 and NS1 coding sequences for heterozygote simulation  
- Selectable marker genes (e.g., Ds-Red) for transformation tracking  

The cassettes may be incorporated into plant transformation vectors using standard molecular biology techniques. Both Agrobacterium-mediated and direct DNA delivery transformation methods are suitable for introducing these constructs into maize cells.  

### Expression of Proteins in Host Cells  

Transgenic expression of ZAR1 polypeptides in maize cells confers several phenotypic advantages:  

1. Enhanced growth rate during vegetative development without altering flowering time  
2. Increased cell proliferation leading to larger leaves, stems, and ears  
3. Improved tolerance to abiotic stresses, particularly drought and high planting density  
4. Environment-dependent yield improvements, especially under hot, dry conditions  

Protein expression can be monitored using standard techniques including Western blotting, ELISA, or activity assays. Optimal expression levels vary by tissue type and developmental stage, with highest impacts observed during active growth phases.  

## EXAMPLES  

The following examples illustrate specific embodiments of the invention but should not be construed as limiting its scope.  

### Example 1  

**Identification and Characterization of ZAR Gene Family**  

The maize ZAR gene family was identified through homology searches using Arabidopsis ARGOS sequences. Nine putative members (ZAR1-9) were initially found, with subsequent analysis revealing ZAR2 to be an allele of ZAR1. The family members share a conserved core domain but vary in tissue expression patterns:  

- ZAR1: Broad expression with highest levels in roots and pericarp  
- ZAR3: Ubiquitous high expression  
- ZAR4/ZAR7: Root-predominant expression  
- ZAR8: Kernel-specific expression  
- ZAR9: Pollen-preferred expression  

Protein size ranges from 64-152 amino acids. ZAR1 was selected for further study as the closest Arabidopsis ARGOS ortholog based on sequence similarity and functional conservation.  

### Example 2  

**Transgenic Overexpression of ZAR1 in Maize**  

ZAR1-SS1 was constitutively expressed in maize using the ubiquitin promoter. Transgenic plants showed:  

1. 15-20% increase in vegetative biomass (leaf area, stalk dry weight)  
2. 12-18% larger ear size at maturity  
3. Faster growth rate without extended growth duration  
4. Increased cell number in leaves (microscopic analysis)  

These effects were consistent across multiple transformation events and generational advancement, confirming stable inheritance of the phenotype.  

### Example 3  

**Drought Tolerance Phenotypes**  

ZAR1 transgenics exhibited improved drought tolerance traits:  

1. 25-30% reduction in anthesis-silking interval under water-limited conditions  
2. 40-50% decrease in tip kernel abortion at high planting density  
3. Lower incidence of barren plants in stress environments  

These improvements were more pronounced at higher plant densities (40,000 plants/acre vs 10,000), suggesting ZAR1 enhances stress tolerance under intensive cultivation.  

### Example 4  

**Yield Performance Across Environments**  

Multi-location yield trials revealed environment-dependent ZAR1 effects:  

1. Significant yield increases (8-12%) in Temperate Dry environments  
2. Neutral to slightly positive effects in Temperate conditions  
3. Yield reductions (5-8%) in cool, humid High Latitude environments  

This pattern held across three years of testing, demonstrating consistent genotype-by-environment interaction. The ZAR1-NS1 allele showed similar but less negative effects in unfavorable environments compared to ZAR1-SS1.  

### Example 5  

**Natural Allelic Variation in Breeding Germplasm**  

Analysis of 424 elite inbreds revealed:  

1. Two major ZAR1 haplotypes account for 95% of allele diversity  
2. Haplotype 1 (NS1-type) predominates in Non-Stiff Stalk lines (85%)  
3. Haplotype 2 (SS1-type) is most common in Stiff Stalk lines (60%)  
4. 66% of commercial hybrids carry both haplotypes  

This distribution suggests selection for heterozygous ZAR1 combinations in hybrid breeding programs.  

### Example 6  

**Association with Agronomic Traits**  

Genetic analysis linked ZAR1 alleles to multiple traits:  

1. Positive association with kernel weight under drought (P<0.05)  
2. Correlation with reduced barrenness and improved staygreen  
3. Small-to-moderate effect sizes typical of yield QTLs  

The favorable effects were most apparent when alleles were in heterozygous combinations, mirroring the transgenic results.  

### Example 7  

**Functional Analysis of Allelic Differences**  

Comparative analysis revealed:  

1. Promoter differences affecting expression level and pattern  
2. Protein coding changes altering conserved domains  
3. Differential expression in heterozygotes (SS1>NS1)  

These findings provide mechanistic explanations for the observed phenotypic variation between alleles.  

### Example 8  

**Transgenic Allele Stacking**  

Constructs combining both SS1 and NS1 alleles showed:  

1. More consistent yield increases across environments  
2. Higher magnitude of improvement than single alleles  
3. Synergistic effects resembling heterosis  

This demonstrates the value of preserving natural allelic diversity in transgenic approaches.  

### Example 9  

**Molecular Characterization**  

Protein structure modeling predicted:  

1. The Leu/Met polymorphism affects core domain stability  
2. The Ala/Gly substitution influences loop flexibility  
3. Combined changes likely alter protein-protein interactions  

These structural differences may underlie the functional divergence between ZAR1 alleles in both transgenic and natural contexts.
