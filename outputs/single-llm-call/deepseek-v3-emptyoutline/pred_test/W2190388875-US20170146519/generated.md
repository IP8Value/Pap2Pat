## BACKGROUND  

The innate immune system serves as the first line of defense against microbial pathogens through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns. Activation of PRRs triggers signaling cascades leading to interferon (IFN) production and expression of antiviral effector genes. The stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway plays a pivotal role in innate immunity by sensing cytoplasmic DNA and cyclic dinucleotides, ultimately activating interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and inducing type I interferon responses.  

Despite the therapeutic potential of targeting STING for antiviral applications, current small molecule STING agonists exhibit species-specific activity, with known compounds like DMXAA and CMA activating murine but not human STING. This limitation has hindered clinical translation. There remains an unmet need for synthetic small molecules that specifically activate human STING to elicit protective antiviral immunity.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides novel compounds, pharmaceutical compositions, and methods for inducing STING-dependent innate immune responses in human cells. Specifically, the invention discloses the small molecule G10 (4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide) which activates IRF3 phosphorylation and interferon production through a STING-dependent mechanism.  

Key aspects of the invention include:  
- G10 induces IRF3 phosphorylation and interferon-stimulated gene expression without activating NF-κB in human fibroblasts  
- The antiviral effects of G10 require STING, IRF3, and STAT1 proteins  
- G10 demonstrates potent activity against Old World (Chikungunya virus) and New World (Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus) alphaviruses  
- The compound triggers STING-dependent interferon secretion and establishes an antiviral state in primary human cells  
- G10 represents the first synthetic small molecule shown to activate human STING-dependent signaling  

These discoveries enable new therapeutic strategies for treating viral infections and other conditions where STING pathway activation would be beneficial.

## EXAMPLES  

### Example 1 Identification of a Novel IFN/IRF3-Inducing Molecule by High Throughput In Vitro Screening  

A high-throughput screen was performed using human fibroblasts stably expressing an IRF3/IFN-responsive luciferase reporter. Screening of 51,632 compounds identified G10 as a potent inducer of reporter activity, ranking third among all tested molecules. G10 demonstrated dose-dependent activation of the reporter system with minimal cytotoxicity at effective concentrations.  

### Example 2—G10 Induces IFN/IRF3- but not NF-κB-Dependent Transcription in Human Fibroblasts  

G10 treatment of human fibroblasts induced expression of IRF3-dependent genes (ISG54, ISG56, ISG15, Viperin) without activating NF-κB-responsive genes (IL8, IL1β, MIP1α). This selective pathway activation distinguishes G10 from many innate immune stimuli that typically activate both IRF3 and NF-κB pathways.  

### Example 3—G10 Elicits Antiviral Activity Against New and Old World Alphaviruses  

Pretreatment of cells with G10 significantly inhibited replication of Chikungunya virus (IC90 = 8.01μM) and Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (IC90 = 24.57μM), demonstrating broad anti-alphaviral activity. The compound showed lesser effects against West Nile virus and Vaccinia virus, indicating selectivity for alphaviruses.  

### Example 4—IRF3 is Essential to G10-Induced Transcription and Anti-Alphaviral Activity  

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated IRF3 knockout abolished G10-induced reporter activity and antiviral effects against CHIKV, VEEV, and SINV. These results demonstrate IRF3 is required for both the innate immune activation and antiviral activity elicited by G10.  

### Example 5—G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation and Anti-Alphaviral Activity Occur Independently of IPS-1/MAVS-Dependent Signaling  

Deletion of IPS-1/MAVS did not impair G10-induced IRF3 phosphorylation or antiviral activity, indicating the compound acts through an IPS-1-independent pathway. This distinguishes G10 from dsRNA-sensing pathways that require IPS-1/MAVS.  

### Example 6—STING is Required for G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation, Gene Expression, and Anti-Alphaviral Activity  

STING knockout cells failed to exhibit G10-induced IRF3 phosphorylation, gene expression, or antiviral activity. These findings establish STING as essential for G10's biological effects and identify G10 as the first synthetic small molecule human STING agonist.  

### Example 7—G10 Induces STING-Dependent Synthesis and Secretion of Bioactive Interferon  

G10 treatment stimulated production and secretion of type I and III interferons in a STING-dependent manner. Conditioned media from G10-treated cells induced interferon signaling in reporter cells, confirming bioactive interferon secretion.  

### Example 8—STAT1 is Required for G10-Mediated Anti-Alphaviral Activity and ISG Expression  

STAT1 knockout abolished G10's antiviral effects while preserving some interferon-induced activity, indicating STAT1 is required for the full antiviral response. This suggests G10 establishes antiviral states through both IRF3 and STAT1-dependent mechanisms.  

### Example 9—G10 Triggers IRF3-Dependent Activity with Less Potency than 2′3′-cGAMP  

Comparative analysis showed G10 induces IRF3 phosphorylation more rapidly but with lower potency than the natural STING ligand 2′3′-cGAMP. This may reflect differences in binding affinity or signaling kinetics between synthetic and natural STING agonists.
