# DESCRIPTION  

## FIELD OF INVENTION  

The present invention relates generally to the field of medical image processing and more specifically to systems and methods for interactive three-dimensional segmentation of anatomical structures in medical images using contextual deep learning. The invention employs a neural network architecture that utilizes contextual information from manually contoured image slices to propagate segmentations through volumetric medical imaging data such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance (MR) scans. This technical field combines elements of medical imaging, computer vision, and machine learning to create an improved interactive segmentation tool for clinical applications including radiotherapy planning, surgical planning, and diagnostic imaging.  

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION  

### Image Segmentation  

Accurate segmentation of anatomical structures in medical images serves as a critical component in numerous clinical workflows. In radiotherapy treatment planning, for instance, precise delineation of both target volumes and organs-at-risk directly impacts the quality of treatment by enabling optimal radiation dose delivery. Traditional manual contouring methods require clinicians to painstakingly trace anatomical boundaries slice-by-slice through volumetric imaging data, a process that is both time-consuming and subject to significant inter-observer variability. While automated segmentation algorithms have demonstrated promising results for specific anatomical structures, their performance remains inconsistent when applied to structures with high morphological variability or those not explicitly represented in training datasets.  

### The Need for Interactive Segmentation  

The limitations of fully automated segmentation approaches have created a need for semi-automatic methods that combine the efficiency of algorithmic processing with the accuracy and adaptability of human oversight. Interactive segmentation systems allow clinicians to provide strategic inputs that guide and refine algorithmic outputs, creating a collaborative workflow between human expertise and computational efficiency. Such systems must balance several competing requirements: they should minimize necessary user interactions while maximizing segmentation accuracy; they must generalize across diverse anatomical structures without requiring structure-specific training; and they need to maintain clinically acceptable processing speeds for integration into real-world clinical workflows.  

### Application Examples of Segmentation:  

The invention finds particular utility in several medical imaging applications including but not limited to: radiotherapy treatment planning where accurate delineation of tumors and sensitive anatomical structures directly impacts treatment efficacy and patient safety; diagnostic imaging workflows where quantitative analysis of anatomical structures aids disease characterization and monitoring; surgical planning systems where three-dimensional modeling of anatomical relationships informs procedural approaches; and medical research applications requiring large-scale quantitative analysis of anatomical features across patient populations. The system's ability to generalize across unseen anatomical structures makes it particularly valuable for clinical scenarios involving rare pathologies or anatomical variants not well-represented in training datasets.  

## PRIOR ART  

### Fully Automated Segmentation Using ML  

Previous approaches to medical image segmentation have predominantly focused on fully automated solutions employing convolutional neural networks (CNNs) such as U-Net architectures. These methods demonstrate strong performance when applied to anatomical structures well-represented in their training datasets, achieving segmentation accuracy comparable to manual delineation in some cases. However, these systems exhibit significant limitations when encountering anatomical variations or pathologies outside their training distribution. The performance degradation stems from their reliance on learning structure-specific features rather than developing a generalized understanding of anatomical context. Additionally, such systems lack mechanisms for incorporating human feedback to correct errors or adapt to unique cases.  

### Interactive 2D Image Segmentation Approaches  

Interactive segmentation in two-dimensional medical images has been implemented through various approaches including graph-cut methods, random walker algorithms, and machine learning models incorporating user-provided scribbles or bounding boxes. While these methods successfully reduce manual contouring effort for individual slices, they fail to leverage the volumetric nature of medical imaging data. Their slice-by-slice application requires repeated user interactions throughout the image volume, negating much of the potential efficiency gains. Furthermore, these 2D approaches cannot capture the three-dimensional anatomical relationships that often provide critical context for accurate segmentation.  

### Interactive 3D Segmentation in Medical Images  

Three-dimensional interactive segmentation methods have attempted to address the limitations of 2D approaches by employing volumetric processing techniques. Some implementations use user-provided contours on key slices to initialize 3D segmentation algorithms, while others employ sparse annotation strategies that train networks using limited manual inputs. However, these methods typically specialize in specific anatomical structures or require extensive pre-training on the target anatomy. Their performance degrades significantly when applied to unseen structures, as they lack mechanisms to generalize anatomical context beyond their training distribution. The requirement for structure-specific training limits their clinical applicability, particularly for rare pathologies or complex anatomical variations.  

### Interactive Contour Propagation Methods  

Existing contour propagation techniques attempt to reduce manual segmentation effort by automatically propagating user-drawn contours across adjacent image slices. Traditional approaches use image registration or interpolation methods to extend contours through the image volume, while more recent implementations employ machine learning to predict contour propagation. However, these methods often fail when anatomical appearance changes significantly between slices or when propagating contours across large interslice distances. Their performance depends heavily on the similarity between adjacent slices, limiting their effectiveness in regions of rapid anatomical variation. Additionally, most propagation methods lack mechanisms to automatically determine when to terminate contours at anatomical boundaries, often requiring extensive manual correction.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The present invention provides a contextual deep learning system for interactive three-dimensional medical image segmentation that addresses the limitations of existing approaches. At the core of the invention is a neural network architecture that processes three distinct input channels containing: (1) the target image slice to be segmented, (2) a reference image slice with manual contour, and (3) the binary mask of the manual contour. This three-channel input structure enables the network to learn contextual relationships between anatomical appearance and segmentation boundaries without relying on structure-specific features.  

The network architecture builds upon a modified U-Net framework incorporating residual connections, recurrent layers, and attention gates. The residual connections facilitate training of deeper networks by mitigating vanishing gradient problems, while the recurrent layers enable the network to maintain context across sequential processing steps. Attention gates automatically focus network resources on salient image regions, improving computational efficiency and segmentation accuracy. The network outputs a probability map through a final sigmoid activation layer, which can be thresholded to produce a binary segmentation mask.  

A critical innovation of the system lies in its training methodology, which emphasizes exposure to diverse anatomical structures rather than optimization for specific targets. During training, the network receives input triplets sampled from various anatomical structures with varying interslice distances. The interslice distance between reference and target slices follows a carefully designed sampling strategy that balances proximity (to maintain anatomical continuity) with sufficient separation (to force contextual learning rather than simple contour copying). This distance is scaled relative to each structure's size, ensuring consistent learning across differently sized anatomies.  

The training process employs an evolutionary optimization approach where multiple network instances are initialized and progressively pruned based on performance. This methodology helps overcome the variability introduced by random weight initialization and converges on optimal network configurations. Data augmentation during training includes random affine transformations (translation, rotation, and scaling) to improve generalization across anatomical variations and imaging parameters.  

In clinical application, the system supports two distinct contour propagation modes: direct prediction and iterative prediction. In direct prediction mode, a single user-provided contour serves as reference for segmenting all other slices in the volume. In iterative prediction mode, each newly segmented slice becomes the reference for subsequent slices, creating a chain of contextually linked segmentations. The system automatically selects the optimal propagation mode based on analysis of anatomical continuity through the image volume.  

The invention includes specialized preprocessing modules that prepare medical images for network input. For CT data, pixel intensities are clamped to a physiologically relevant Hounsfield Unit range (-1000 to 2000 HU) to eliminate artifacts while preserving tissue contrast. All input images are resampled to a standardized resolution (256×256 pixels in preferred embodiments) to ensure consistent network processing while maintaining sufficient detail for accurate segmentation.  

Performance evaluation modules provide quantitative metrics including Dice similarity coefficient, Hausdorff distance, and relative added path length to assess segmentation quality. These metrics are computed at the original image resolution after upsampling network outputs, ensuring clinical relevance of the evaluations. The system incorporates these quality metrics in an adaptive feedback loop that can trigger requests for additional user input when segmentation confidence falls below predetermined thresholds.  

The system's ability to generalize across unseen anatomical structures stems from its context-based learning paradigm rather than structure-specific feature detection. This is demonstrated by its maintained performance on anatomical structures excluded from training, a capability lacking in prior approaches. The invention thus represents a significant advance in interactive medical image segmentation by combining the efficiency of automated processing with the flexibility and accuracy of human-guided refinement.
