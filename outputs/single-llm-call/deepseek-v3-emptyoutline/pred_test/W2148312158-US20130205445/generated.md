# DESCRIPTION  

## TECHNICAL FIELD  

The present invention relates to the field of plant biotechnology and genetic engineering, specifically to methods and compositions for enhancing sugar transport and storage in plants. More particularly, the invention concerns genetically modified plants with increased expression of tonoplast monosaccharide transporter (TMT) proteins, resulting in improved vacuolar sugar accumulation, altered sugar signaling, and enhanced plant growth characteristics. The invention further encompasses methods for producing such plants and their use in agriculture to improve crop yield and biomass production.  

## BACKGROUND ART  

In plant cells, sugars serve as essential energy sources, signaling molecules, and osmotic regulators. The vacuole plays a crucial role in sugar storage and homeostasis, acting as the primary intracellular compartment for sugar accumulation. Transport of monosaccharides across the vacuolar membrane is mediated by specific transporter proteins, particularly the tonoplast monosaccharide transporters (TMTs).  

Previous studies have identified TMT proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana and demonstrated their involvement in glucose (Glc) accumulation in vacuoles. However, the extent to which TMT-mediated transport limits plant performance and sugar storage capacity has remained unclear. Conventional approaches to modifying sugar metabolism in plants have focused primarily on altering biosynthetic pathways or sink strength, with limited success in significantly improving overall plant productivity.  

A significant problem in the art has been the inability to effectively manipulate intracellular sugar partitioning to enhance both source and sink capacities simultaneously. Current methods for increasing plant biomass and yield often lead to metabolic imbalances or unintended pleiotropic effects. There exists a need in the art for novel genetic modifications that can enhance vacuolar sugar storage capacity while maintaining proper sugar signaling and metabolic homeostasis.  

## DISCLOSURE OF INVENTION  

The present invention provides genetically modified plants with enhanced vacuolar sugar transport capacity through overexpression of TMT proteins. The invention demonstrates that increased TMT1 expression leads to:  

1. Enhanced electrogenic transport of monosaccharides across the vacuolar membrane, as evidenced by patch-clamp measurements showing increased glucose-dependent currents in TMT1-overexpressing vacuoles compared to wild-type.  

2. Altered subcellular sugar partitioning, with significantly increased vacuolar accumulation of monosaccharides and corresponding reduction in cytosolic sugar levels.  

3. Modified sugar signaling responses, as shown by differential expression of sugar-responsive genes (CAB1 and NR1) in TMT1 overexpressors compared to wild-type plants.  

4. Improved plant growth characteristics, including faster early development, increased biomass accumulation under high sugar conditions, and enhanced seed production.  

5. Increased seed quality and yield, manifested by higher seed weight, elevated lipid and protein content in seeds, and greater total seed biomass per plant.  

6. Enhanced source capacity, demonstrated by increased sugar export from leaves and elevated expression of the phloem sucrose transporter SUC2.  

The invention further provides methods for producing such plants by introducing and expressing TMT-coding sequences under the control of constitutive or tissue-specific promoters. The genetic modifications can be introduced into various plant species to improve agricultural productivity.  

## BEST MODE FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION  

The following describes preferred embodiments for practicing the invention, including specific genetic constructs, transformation methods, and analytical techniques used to characterize the modified plants.  

### Examples  

**Example 1: Generation of TMT1 Overexpressor Plants**  

TMT1 overexpressor lines were created by transforming a TMT1 gene construct into Arabidopsis tmt1-2::tDNA mutant plants. The TMT1 coding sequence was modified to include a c-Myc tag after amino acid 375 and placed under the control of the constitutive cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter. The construct was inserted into the pART27 vector and introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 for plant transformation using the floral dip method.  

Transformed plants were selected based on high TMT1 mRNA levels as determined by northern blot analysis. Three independent overexpressor lines (lines 1, 4, and 10) showing the strongest TMT1 expression were chosen for further characterization. Under standard growth conditions (21°C), TMT1 mRNA was undetectable in wild-type plants but highly expressed in the transgenic lines.  

**Example 2: Electrophysiological Characterization of Vacuolar Transport**  

Patch-clamp measurements on isolated mesophyll vacuoles demonstrated the electrogenic nature of TMT1-mediated transport. Experiments were conducted under physiological pH gradients (vacuolar pH 5.5, cytosolic pH 7.5) with varying glucose concentrations.  

Application of a 50 mM glucose gradient induced negative currents corresponding to proton export from the vacuole. Vacuoles from TMT1-overexpressor line 4 showed a 2-fold higher current response compared to wild-type, while tmt1-2::tDNA mutant vacuoles exhibited minimal glucose-induced currents. Symmetrical pH conditions (pH 7.5 both sides) reduced glucose-induced currents by approximately 50%, confirming the proton-coupled nature of TMT1 transport.  

**Example 3: Growth Performance Under High Sugar Conditions**  

Wild-type, tmt1-2::tDNA mutant, and three TMT1 overexpressor lines were grown in liquid culture with or without 5% glucose supplement. Under standard conditions, all genotypes developed similarly, reaching about 185 mg fresh weight in 12 days.  

With glucose supplementation, wild-type plants showed severe growth inhibition (25 mg fresh weight), while tmt1-2::tDNA mutants grew minimally (9 mg). In contrast, all three TMT1 overexpressor lines exhibited significantly better growth (49-78 mg fresh weight), with line 4 showing the strongest effect corresponding to its highest TMT1 expression level.  

**Example 4: Subcellular Sugar Partitioning Analysis**  

Nonaqueous fractionation was used to analyze sugar compartmentation in wild-type and TMT1 overexpressor plants grown in high glucose conditions. Wild-type plants distributed glucose as follows: 40% vacuolar, 43% plastidic, and 17% cytosolic.  

TMT1 overexpressor lines showed dramatically altered partitioning:  
- Line 1: >80% vacuolar, 20% plastidic, <1% cytosolic  
- Line 4: 65% vacuolar, 35% plastidic, 0% cytosolic  

Similar shifts were observed for fructose distribution, with overexpressor lines accumulating >90% in vacuoles compared to 45% in wild-type.  

**Example 5: Seed Characteristics and Yield**  

Analysis of mature seeds revealed significant improvements in TMT1 overexpressor lines:  
- 1000-seed weight increased by 13-28% compared to wild-type  
- Seed lipid content increased by 5-12%  
- Seed protein content increased by 10-14%  
- Total seed yield per plant increased by 25-74%  

These improvements occurred without changes in seed number per silique, indicating enhanced seed filling rather than altered reproductive development.  

**Example 6: Source-Sink Relationships and Phloem Transport**  

TMT1 overexpressor lines exhibited:  
- Increased SUC2 sucrose transporter expression (up to 150% of wild-type levels)  
- Enhanced sugar export capacity from source leaves (16.6-17.9 μmol C6 units/g fresh weight vs. 12.5 in wild-type)  
- Reduced nocturnal respiration rates (indicating improved carbon use efficiency)  

These modifications demonstrate improved source strength and phloem loading capacity in the transgenic plants.  

The examples demonstrate that TMT1 overexpression creates plants with enhanced vacuolar sugar storage capacity, improved growth characteristics, and increased yield potential. The invention provides valuable tools for agricultural improvement through genetic modification of sugar transport systems.
