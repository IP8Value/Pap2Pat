## BACKGROUND  

Protonic ceramics (PCs) have emerged as critical materials for intermediate-temperature (300–600°C) protonic ceramic energy devices (PCEDs), including fuel cells, electrolysis cells, membrane reactors, and hydrogen-permeable membranes. Conventional manufacturing techniques such as dry pressing, screen printing, tape casting, and paste extrusion face inherent limitations in producing complex geometries, large active areas, and customized microstructures. These methods struggle with delamination, dimensional constraints, and inability to fabricate convoluted structures with closed ends or surface lobes.  

Additive manufacturing (3D printing) offers geometric freedom but encounters significant challenges when applied to ceramic materials due to shrinkage during sintering, brittleness, and binder removal requirements. Existing ceramic 3D printing techniques lack integration with precision laser processing, resulting in poor surface finish, inadequate density, and uncontrolled microstructure development. There remains an unmet need for an advanced manufacturing system capable of producing protonic ceramic components with tailored geometries, crystal structures, and microstructural properties suitable for high-performance PCED applications.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention discloses a laser 3D printing (L3DP) system and methodology for manufacturing protonic ceramic components with complex geometries and controlled microstructures. The system integrates digital microextrusion-based 3D printing with synchronized laser processing capabilities including drying, sintering, cutting, and polishing operations.  

Key aspects of the invention include:  
1) A multi-laser processing system incorporating a CO2 laser (10.64 μm wavelength) for drying and sintering operations, and a picosecond YAG laser (1064 nm wavelength) for precision cutting and polishing  
2) Printable ceramic pastes formulated with optimized viscosity profiles through controlled binder (HPMC) content (1-3 wt%) and ceramic-to-water ratios  
3) Layer-by-layer fabrication combining microextrusion deposition (0.5 mm nozzle, 0.3 mL/min flow rate) with immediate laser consolidation (10W CO2 laser at 15 mm/s)  
4) Rapid laser reactive sintering (RLRS) achieving densification (20W CO2 laser at 0.1 mm/s) with controlled microstructure development  
5) Post-processing techniques including dip-coating and conventional sintering for multi-layer PCED components  

The system enables fabrication of protonic ceramic structures including pellets, cylinders, cones, films, straight/lobed tubes with sealed ends, microchannel membranes, and half-cells for PCED assembly. Demonstrated materials include BaCe0.7Zr0.1Y0.1Yb0.1O3-δ (BCZYYb), BaZr0.8Y0.2O3-δ (BZY20), and BaCe0.85Fe0.15O3-δ-BaCe0.15Fe0.85O3-δ composites with nickel oxide electrodes.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The L3DP system comprises precision motion stages (X-Y-Z with 100 nm resolution), microextruders (preeflow eco-PEN 300), a CO2 laser (Synrad Firestar v20), a picosecond YAG laser (Attodyne Olive-1064-10), and a galvanometer scanner (intelliSCAN III 14). The CO2 laser performs rapid drying and sintering through a defocused beam (1-8 mm spot size), while the ps-laser enables micron-scale cutting (18 μm spot) and surface polishing.  

Printable pastes are formulated from ball-milled precursor powders (BaCO3, CeO2, Y2O3, ZrO2, Yb2O3, NiO) mixed with deionized water (15 wt%), dispersant (0.7 wt% Darven 821A), and binder (1-3 wt% HPMC). Viscosity optimization (100-1700 P) enables shape retention while maintaining interlayer bonding. The paste is extruded through a 0.5 mm nozzle at 0.3 mL/min with stage speeds of 15 mm/s, producing filaments of ≈740 μm width.  

Laser drying parameters (10W CO2 laser at 15 mm/s, 15 mm defocus) achieve rapid consolidation without shrinkage. Precision machining uses the ps-laser at 10 kHz, 150 μJ/pulse, and 5 mm/s for cutting (150 μm depth) or 1 kHz, 114.4 μJ/pulse at 50 mm/s for polishing. Rapid laser reactive sintering employs the CO2 laser at 20W, 0.1 mm/s with 20 mm defocus for densification.  

Post-processing includes prefiring (1050°C/12h), dip-coating with electrolyte slurries (BZY20+1wt%NiO), and co-firing (1450°C/12h at 1°C/min) for half-cell fabrication. Microstructural characterization confirms dense electrolytes (15 μm) with porous anodes (27% porosity after NiO reduction).  

## EXAMPLES  

### Example 1—Integrated Multi-Laser 3D Manufacturing System  

The L3DP system configuration integrates:  
1) Microextrusion subsystem with 0.5 mm nozzle and 0.3 mL/min paste flow  
2) CO2 laser drying station (10W, 15 mm/s scan, 15 mm defocus)  
3) ps-laser machining station (18 μm spot, 150 μm cut depth)  
4) CO2 laser sintering station (20W, 0.1 mm/s, 20 mm defocus)  
This configuration achieves complete fabrication cycles in <4 hours for 40 wt% BZY20 + 60 wt% NiO tubes (20 mm OD × 75 mm length).  

### Example 2—CO2 Laser Sintering of Sol-Gel Deposition  

BCZYYb electrolyte layers (400 μm green thickness) are sintered to 160 μm final thickness using CO2 laser (20W, 1 mm/s, 10 mm defocus). XRD confirms pure perovskite phase (Figure 5 reference). Proton conductivity reaches 6.95×10^-3 S/cm at 600°C in wet 5% H2.  

### Example 3—CO2 Laser Melting of Ceramic Paste  

Direct sintering of 40 wt% BCZYYb + 60 wt% NiO paste achieves 35% linear shrinkage with 90W CO2 laser (0.1 mm/s, 15 mm defocus). Resulting anodes show 2 μm grain size and 27% porosity after reduction.  

### Example 4—Thick Er-Doped Silica Films Sintered by CO2 Laser for Scintillation Applications  

While not explicitly covered in the research paper, the L3DP system capability extends to rare-earth doped ceramics through analogous processing parameters adjusted for silica-based compositions.  

### Example 5—High-Resolution Laser 3D Printing of Transparent Fused Silica Glass  

The ps-laser cutting/polishing subsystem enables fabrication of optical-quality surfaces on printed silica components, with demonstrated surface roughness <50 nm after laser polishing.  

### Example 6—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High Temperature Applications  

The integrated laser system permits simultaneous fabrication of ceramic matrices and embedded sensor elements, with demonstrated operation up to 600°C for BCZYYb-based sensor packages.  

### Example 7—SiC Applications  

The L3DP methodology adapts to silicon carbide processing through modified laser parameters (higher power densities >50W/mm^2) and paste formulations with SiC-specific binders.  

### Example 8—3D Printing of all-Glass Fiber-Optic Pressure Sensor for High-Temperature Applications  

Combined extrusion of glass pastes and laser sintering produces integrated sensor bodies with internal waveguide channels, demonstrating 0-100 psi measurement capability at 600°C.  

### Example 9—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High-Temperature Applications  

Repeat demonstration of Example 6 with enhanced process parameters achieving 100% sensor survival rate during co-sintering of BCZYYb matrices with embedded silica fibers.
