# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

### 1. Field of the Invention  

The present invention relates generally to the field of superconducting materials and thin film fabrication techniques. More specifically, the invention pertains to methods for producing wafer-scale magnesium diboride (MgB2) superconducting thin films with high transition temperatures (Tc) and low surface roughness, as well as devices fabricated from such films. The disclosed techniques enable large-scale manufacturing of superconducting microdevices suitable for applications in quantum computing, high-frequency electronics, and photon detection systems.  

### 2. Description of the Related Art  

Conventional approaches to fabricating MgB2 thin films have faced significant technical challenges that have limited their practical implementation in device manufacturing. Previous methods utilizing in-situ magnetron sputtering typically resulted in films with low critical temperatures (Tc) due to oxidation, small grain sizes, and stoichiometric imbalances caused by the high vapor pressure and low sticking coefficient of magnesium at elevated temperatures exceeding 200°C. While post-annealing of these films showed some improvement in Tc, this came at the expense of increased surface roughness exceeding 10 nm root mean square (rms).  

Alternative approaches involving room temperature deposition followed by post-annealing presented their own limitations. Annealing magnesium-boron composite films in vacuum led to magnesium evaporation and surface roughness, with transition temperatures around only 6 K. Previous attempts to prevent magnesium evaporation through capping layers using materials such as tantalum proved problematic, as tantalum cracks at temperatures above 700°C and requires removal for superconducting property measurements.  

The fabrication of superconducting devices from MgB2 films has been further complicated by substrate compatibility issues. While sapphire substrates have been commonly used due to their hexagonal crystal structure matching that of MgB2, oxygen diffusion from sapphire into MgB2 creates detrimental interdiffusion layers, particularly in films thinner than 100 nm. Silicon carbide substrates, while providing good lattice matching, are prohibitively expensive for large-scale production. Silicon substrates present challenges due to the formation of Mg2Si when magnesium directly contacts silicon.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution to the aforementioned challenges through a novel fabrication process for high-quality MgB2 superconducting thin films. The invention encompasses several key innovations: a room-temperature co-deposition technique for magnesium-boron precursor films using optimized magnetron sputtering parameters; the use of a boron capping layer that forms a viscous, crack-free oxide layer during annealing to prevent magnesium evaporation; implementation of a silicon nitride buffer layer on silicon substrates to prevent unwanted reactions; and precise control of post-annealing conditions using rapid thermal processing to achieve high transition temperatures while maintaining film smoothness.  

The resulting MgB2 thin films exhibit exceptional superconducting properties, including transition temperatures up to 37.2 K (the highest reported for sputtered films), surface roughness as low as 0.476 nm rms, and the ability to be produced on wafer scales up to 100 mm diameter. The invention further includes methods for fabricating superconducting microdevices from these films, demonstrating fabrication maturity comparable to conventional superconducting materials like NbTiN. The films enable precise control of critical device parameters including kinetic inductance (ranging from 5-50 pH/□) and sheet resistance through adjustable magnesium content in the precursor films.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

### Technical Description  

The fabrication process begins with the deposition of a magnesium-boron composite precursor film at room temperature using RF magnetron sputtering. This approach overcomes the limitations of high-temperature deposition by enabling uniform magnesium distribution while avoiding the issues of oxidation and stoichiometric imbalance. The sputtering system utilizes separate magnesium and boron targets, with boron sputtering power fixed at 580 W RF and magnesium power adjustable between 50-250 W RF to control film stoichiometry.  

A critical innovation involves the application of a low-power RF substrate bias (15 W) during deposition, which reduces surface roughness from 1.74 nm to 0.34-0.45 nm rms by promoting film densification without compromising stoichiometric control. Excessive bias power is avoided as it leads to undesirable heating or etching effects that disrupt the magnesium-boron ratio. The as-deposited films show indications of initial magnesium-boron reactions, evidenced by the onset of superconducting transitions prior to annealing, albeit at temperatures below liquid helium levels.  

Following deposition, the precursor films are capped with a thin boron layer (tens of nanometers thick) that serves multiple critical functions during subsequent annealing. Unlike alternative capping materials, boron forms a viscous oxide layer at temperatures above 450°C that remains crack-free and effectively contains magnesium within the film. While minor amounts of byproducts (MgB4 and MgB7) may form at the interface, these dielectric materials do not interfere with superconducting property measurements.  

The annealing process employs a rapid thermal processor (RTP) to ensure temperature uniformity across large wafer areas. The annealing temperature is carefully maintained below 650°C (the melting point of magnesium) to prevent surface roughening from magnesium evaporation. Optimized annealing conditions produce films with transition temperatures up to 32 K and surface roughness of 0.476 nm rms. Even higher transition temperatures (up to 37.2 K) can be achieved by alternating magnesium and boron layers in the precursor film at a 1:2 atomic ratio, though this approach increases surface roughness due to enhanced grain growth and void formation.  

Substrate selection and preparation constitute another key aspect of the invention. Silicon wafers with low-stress silicon nitride buffer layers (30-150 nm thick) deposited by LPCVD or PECVD provide an optimal platform, preventing magnesium-silicon reactions while being commercially viable. The silicon nitride thickness is determined by the substrate roughness to ensure complete coverage without pinholes. Comparative studies demonstrate superior performance on silicon nitride buffers compared to sapphire substrates, as magnesium migration into sapphire creates stoichiometric imbalances near the film-substrate interface.  

For device fabrication, the invention includes specialized processes to handle the air-sensitive MgB2 films. The boron capping layer is first removed by argon ion milling, followed by deposition of a titanium-gold bilayer that serves as both a fabrication passivation layer and eventual electrical contact. Patterning is accomplished using maskless lithography with chlorine-based ICP etching for the titanium layer and subsequent ion milling to define the MgB2 structures. Final devices are passivated with dielectric layers (silicon, silicon nitride, or aluminum nitride) to protect the superconducting material.  

The resulting films enable precise control of electrical properties critical for device applications. By adjusting the magnesium content in the precursor films through RF power variation, the sheet resistance and consequently the kinetic inductance can be tuned across a wide range (5-50 pH/□ for ~40 nm thick films). Thinner films can achieve even higher inductance values approaching 1 nH/□. This tunability, combined with the high transition temperatures and low microwave losses (internal quality factors exceeding 10^4 at 4.5 K), makes the films particularly suitable for high-frequency superconducting devices operating at elevated temperatures compared to conventional materials.  

## CONCLUSION  

The present invention represents a significant advancement in superconducting thin film technology by overcoming longstanding barriers to the practical implementation of MgB2 in device fabrication. The developed processes enable wafer-scale production of high-performance superconducting films with transition temperatures exceeding 37 K, surface roughness below 0.5 nm rms, and precise control over critical electrical parameters. These characteristics, combined with demonstrated fabrication maturity supporting feature sizes down to 0.5 µm, establish the technology as immediately implementable for a wide range of applications.  

The invention's unique combination of room-temperature deposition, boron capping, optimized annealing, and silicon-compatible processing provides a manufacturable pathway for MgB2-based devices that was previously unattainable. The resulting films exhibit exceptional figures of merit including high critical current density, low RF losses, and large nonlinear kinetic inductance - properties that are particularly valuable for next-generation quantum computing circuits, parametric amplifiers, frequency multipliers, and kinetic inductance-based photon detectors. By solving the fundamental materials challenges that have hindered MgB2 device development, this invention opens new possibilities for high-temperature superconducting electronics operating beyond the limitations of conventional low-temperature superconductors.
