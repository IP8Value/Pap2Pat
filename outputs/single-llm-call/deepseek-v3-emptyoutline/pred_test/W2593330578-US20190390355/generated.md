# DESCRIPTION  

## TECHNICAL FIELD  

The present invention relates to carbon anodes used in aluminum production, particularly to methods of protecting carbon anodes from oxidative degradation during electrolysis. More specifically, the invention pertains to an improved boron-based impregnation process that significantly reduces air reactivity and dust formation in carbon anodes while maintaining structural integrity during high-temperature operations. The disclosed method provides superior protection compared to conventional coating techniques by creating a distributed boron oxide barrier throughout the anode pore structure.  

## BACKGROUND  

In conventional aluminum production using Hall-Héroult cells, carbon anodes participate in the electrolytic reduction of alumina while simultaneously undergoing undesirable oxidation reactions with atmospheric oxygen. The anode surface exposed to air at temperatures between 400-600°C reacts according to two primary mechanisms: complete combustion to carbon dioxide (CO₂) and partial combustion to carbon monoxide (CO). Both reactions represent significant sources of carbon overconsumption, with air reactivity and CO₂ reactivity accounting for the majority of excess anode consumption.  

Traditional anode compositions comprising calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode material (butts), and coal tar pitch exhibit differential oxidation rates, with the binder matrix (pitch + fine particles) burning faster than larger coke grains. This differential reactivity leads to premature binder removal and subsequent detachment of unburned coke particles, contributing to the problematic dusting phenomenon. Prior protection strategies have focused on either creating physical barriers or modifying the intrinsic carbon reactivity.  

Physical barrier approaches include alumina-based coatings sprayed onto baked anodes and cryolite bath applications that solidify on anode surfaces. However, these methods provide limited protection due to inherent porosity in the deposited layers. Reactivity modification attempts have explored doping carbon with phosphorus and boron, though phosphorus contamination of the electrolytic bath makes boron the only practical dopant. While boron doping can theoretically inhibit oxidation through electron density redistribution, graphitization enhancement, or active site blocking via boron oxide formation, practical implementation faces significant challenges.  

Existing boron treatment methods suffer from several limitations. High boron concentrations (1000 ppm to several atomic percent) required for effective doping are economically impractical for industrial-scale anode production. Furthermore, conventional boron doping processes conducted under inert atmospheres prior to graphitization are incompatible with standard anode manufacturing. Prior art describing boron oxide impregnation techniques has demonstrated laboratory-scale success but failed to translate into industrial adoption, likely due to inadequate understanding of impregnation dynamics and protection mechanisms.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides a solution to these limitations through an optimized boron impregnation process that achieves superior anode protection with practical industrial applicability. The method involves impregnating carbon anodes with aqueous boron solutions under controlled temperature and duration parameters, followed by specific drying protocols.  

Key aspects of the invention include:  
1. An impregnation process where anode samples are immersed in boron solutions (preferably 0.17-2 wt%) at temperatures between 50-100°C for durations ranging from 1-20 minutes  
2. A drying step conducted at approximately 100°C to facilitate boron hydroxide precipitation and partial conversion to boron oxide  
3. Demonstrated reduction of anode oxidation rates from 4%/h to 0.2%/h through optimized impregnation parameters  
4. Preservation of anode structural integrity during oxidation, significantly reducing dust generation  
5. Distributed protection throughout the anode pore network rather than just surface coverage  

The invention further provides characterization data showing that extended impregnation times (15-20 minutes) in warm solutions produce anodes that maintain their external dimensions during oxidation while reacting internally at controlled rates. This represents a significant improvement over untreated anodes that exhibit rapid surface degradation and disintegration. X-ray tomography analysis confirms that the inventive method alters the fundamental reaction mode from surface-attack dominant to controlled internal oxidation.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The present invention discloses a method for protecting carbon anodes through optimized boron impregnation, with particular attention to impregnation parameters that govern protection effectiveness. The detailed process involves several critical steps and parameters that collectively produce superior anode performance.  

**Anode Preparation**  
Industrial-scale baked anodes composed of approximately 65% calcined petroleum coke, 22% recycled anode material, and 13% coal tar pitch serve as the base material. The anodes are typically baked at 1200°C prior to impregnation. Samples may be extracted from regions of the anode most exposed to air during electrolysis, though the method applies equally to entire anode blocks.  

**Boron Solution Preparation**  
The impregnation solution comprises dissolved boron compounds in aqueous medium, with concentrations preferably ranging from 0.17 g/mL to 2 wt%. The solution temperature during impregnation critically affects boron uptake, with warm solutions (50-100°C) demonstrating superior performance compared to room temperature treatments.  

**Impregnation Process**  
The anode samples undergo immersion in the prepared boron solution for precisely controlled durations. Experiments demonstrate that impregnation times between 1-20 minutes provide progressively improved protection, with 15-minute treatments in 2 wt% solutions at 50°C showing particularly effective results. The process may involve single or multiple impregnation cycles, though testing indicates that surface saturation typically occurs during the first treatment.  

**Drying Protocol**  
Following impregnation, samples undergo controlled drying at approximately 100°C to facilitate boron compound precipitation within the anode pore structure. During this stage, boron hydroxide forms and partially dehydrates to boron oxide, creating a distributed protective network throughout the anode microstructure.  

**Protection Mechanism**  
The invention's effectiveness stems from two complementary mechanisms:  
1. Physical blockage of pore networks by precipitated boron compounds, limiting oxygen diffusion  
2. Modification of carbon reactivity at the molecular level through boron oxide interactions  

Microstructural analysis via scanning electron microscopy reveals lamellar boron oxide crystals formed within anode pores, creating a physical barrier against oxygen penetration. Importantly, the protection extends beyond surface coverage, with boron compounds distributed throughout the accessible pore network.  

**Performance Characteristics**  
Treated anodes exhibit dramatically reduced oxidation rates compared to untreated controls. Standard air reactivity tests at 500°C show untreated anodes losing 52-75% mass within 24 hours, while impregnated samples demonstrate only 4.5-11% mass loss under identical conditions. Extended impregnation times (15-20 minutes) produce anodes with reaction rates approximately 20 times slower than untreated specimens.  

Structural integrity preservation represents another key advantage. While untreated anodes completely disintegrate into powder within 40 hours, properly impregnated samples maintain their original shape even after 50% mass loss, significantly reducing dust generation. X-ray tomography confirms that treated anodes oxidize internally rather than through surface attack, maintaining dimensional stability throughout their service life.  

## EXAMPLES  

### Example 1: Anode Preparation  

Industrial baked anodes were sampled using core drilling equipment to obtain cylindrical specimens of varying dimensions. For initial screening tests, small samples (19 mm diameter × 14 mm length, 6-7 g mass) were extracted. Larger scale testing employed standard core samples (50 mm diameter × 30 mm length, ≈100 g mass) representative of industrial anode dimensions. All samples were dried at 140°C for 4 hours to establish baseline mass measurements prior to impregnation.  

### Example 2: Anode Characterization  

Baseline characterization of untreated anode samples included:  
- Bulk density measurement via geometric dimensions and mass  
- Apparent density determination using helium pycnometry  
- Open porosity assessment through water immersion techniques  
- Microstructural analysis via scanning electron microscopy  

These measurements established reference values for subsequent comparison with impregnated samples. Typical untreated anodes exhibited 25-30% open porosity with heterogeneous pore size distribution.  

### Example 3: Air Reactivity Measurements  

Air reactivity testing employed a muffle furnace with controlled airflow (3-5 L/min) at isothermal conditions (400-600°C). Samples were protected by nitrogen during heating/cooling cycles, with air exposure only at target temperatures. Mass loss measurements occurred at regular intervals using precision balances (±0.0001 g).  

Key findings included:  
- Untreated anodes showed consistent 4%/h reaction rates at 500°C  
- 1-minute impregnation reduced reaction rates by 50%  
- 15-20 minute treatments achieved 0.2%/h rates (20× improvement)  
- Treated samples maintained structural integrity despite significant mass loss  

### Example 4: CO₂ Reactivity Measurements  

Complementary CO₂ reactivity tests followed similar protocols to air reactivity assessments, substituting CO₂ for air in the reaction chamber. Results demonstrated that boron impregnation provided comparable protection against CO₂ reactivity, though absolute reaction rates differed due to distinct reaction mechanisms.  

### Example 5: Electrical Resistivity Measurements  

Four-point probe measurements confirmed that boron impregnation did not adversely affect anode electrical conductivity, a critical parameter for electrolysis performance. Resistivity values remained within acceptable industrial ranges (≈50 μΩm) for all treated samples.
