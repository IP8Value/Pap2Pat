# DESCRIPTION  

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates generally to semiconductor nanocrystals and quantum dots, and more particularly to methods for heavily doping indium arsenide (InAs) nanocrystal quantum dots with metal impurities including copper (Cu), silver (Ag), and gold (Au). The invention encompasses novel synthetic methods for incorporating controlled amounts of dopant atoms into InAs nanocrystals, the resulting doped nanocrystal compositions, and their unique electronic and optical properties. The doped nanocrystals exhibit modified band structures and carrier concentrations that make them particularly useful for optoelectronic applications including photodetectors, light-emitting diodes, solar cells, and other semiconductor devices requiring precise control over doping levels and carrier types.  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

Semiconductor nanocrystals, also known as quantum dots, have attracted significant scientific and technological interest due to their size-dependent optical and electronic properties. The ability to dope semiconductor nanocrystals is crucial for controlling their electrical conductivity and optoelectronic characteristics. However, conventional doping approaches developed for bulk semiconductors often fail when applied to nanocrystals due to strong quantum confinement effects, large surface-to-volume ratios, and self-purification mechanisms that expel dopants.  

Prior attempts at doping semiconductor nanocrystals have faced several challenges. First, the small size of nanocrystals makes it difficult to incorporate dopant atoms at controlled concentrations. Second, the position of dopant atoms within the nanocrystal lattice (substitutional vs. interstitial) significantly impacts the resulting electronic properties but has been difficult to control. Third, conventional doping methods often lead to surface segregation of dopants rather than their incorporation into the nanocrystal core. Finally, existing techniques lack the precision to tune doping levels across a wide range while maintaining narrow size distributions and good crystallinity.  

There remains an unmet need for reliable methods to dope semiconductor nanocrystals, particularly III-V materials like InAs, with precise control over dopant type, concentration, and lattice position. The present invention addresses these needs through novel synthetic approaches that enable heavy doping of InAs nanocrystals while maintaining their structural integrity and quantum confinement properties.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides methods for synthesizing heavily doped InAs nanocrystals and the resulting doped nanocrystal compositions. The methods involve reacting indium and arsenic precursors in the presence of dopant metal salts to form doped InAs nanocrystals with controlled dopant concentrations. Three primary embodiments are disclosed covering copper-doped, silver-doped, and gold-doped InAs nanocrystals, each exhibiting distinct electronic and optical properties.  

In one embodiment, copper-doped InAs nanocrystals are prepared by adding a solution containing CuCl2 to pre-formed InAs nanocrystals. The copper atoms incorporate primarily at interstitial sites within the nanocrystal lattice, acting as n-type dopants that shift the Fermi level toward the conduction band. Optical characterization reveals a blue shift in the absorption spectrum while the photoluminescence remains largely unchanged, consistent with n-type doping.  

In another embodiment, silver-doped InAs nanocrystals are prepared using AgNO3 or AgCl as silver sources. Silver atoms substitute for indium in the lattice, acting as p-type dopants that shift the Fermi level toward the valence band. Both absorption and photoluminescence spectra show red shifts, and the larger size of silver atoms causes significant lattice distortion compared to copper doping.  

In a third embodiment, gold-doped InAs nanocrystals are prepared using AuCl3 as the gold source. Gold atoms substitute isovalently for indium in the lattice without introducing additional charge carriers. The absorption and photoluminescence spectra remain largely unchanged compared to undoped nanocrystals, and minimal lattice distortion occurs due to the similar sizes of gold and indium atoms.  

The invention further provides methods for controlling dopant concentration by adjusting the ratio of metal precursor to nanocrystals in the doping reaction. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) measurements confirm the incorporation of dopant atoms at concentrations ranging from single atoms per nanocrystal to several dozen atoms per nanocrystal.  

The doped InAs nanocrystals of the invention exhibit several advantages over prior art. First, the doping methods allow precise control over dopant type and concentration while maintaining narrow size distributions. Second, the dopant atoms incorporate uniformly throughout the nanocrystal volume rather than segregating to the surface. Third, the methods enable both n-type and p-type doping of the same host material (InAs) simply by changing the dopant metal. Fourth, the heavily doped nanocrystals remain stable and maintain their quantum confinement properties.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS  

The following detailed description provides specific embodiments of the invention with reference to the accompanying experimental data. These embodiments are intended to illustrate rather than limit the invention.  

### Copper-Doped InAs Nanocrystals  

Copper doping of InAs nanocrystals is achieved by reacting pre-formed InAs nanocrystals with a solution containing CuCl2. In a typical procedure, a metal solution is prepared by dissolving 10 mg of CuCl2, 80 mg didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB), and 120 mg dodecylamine (DDA) in 10 mL of toluene. This solution is added dropwise to 2 mL of a toluene solution containing InAs nanocrystals under inert atmosphere. After 15 minutes of reaction, the copper-doped nanocrystals are precipitated with methanol and collected by centrifugation.  

The incorporation of copper atoms is confirmed by several characterization techniques. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) measurements show that the number of copper atoms per nanocrystal can be precisely controlled by adjusting the Cu:NC ratio in the reaction mixture. For 3.6 nm diameter nanocrystals, the number of copper atoms per nanocrystal ranges from approximately 1 to 30 as the Cu:NC ratio increases from 0.1 to 10.  

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) reveals the presence of copper in the doped nanocrystals through characteristic Cu 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 peaks at 932.7 eV and 952.5 eV, respectively. The binding energies match reported values for Cu in the 0 or +1 oxidation state. High-resolution XPS of the In 3d region shows broadening of the peaks for copper-doped samples compared to undoped controls, suggesting partial oxidation of some indium atoms to In2O3.  

Optical characterization shows that copper doping causes a blue shift in the absorption spectrum while leaving the photoluminescence spectrum largely unchanged. This asymmetric shift indicates that copper acts primarily as an n-type dopant, donating electrons to the nanocrystal conduction band. Scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy measurements confirm that the conduction and valence band edges shift to lower energies (more negative potentials) upon copper doping.  

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis show that the copper-doped nanocrystals maintain their crystalline structure with only minor lattice distortions. The small size of copper atoms (compared to indium) allows them to occupy interstitial sites without significantly altering the overall lattice parameter.  

### Silver-Doped InAs Nanocrystals  

Silver doping is achieved using similar methods to the copper doping procedure, but with AgNO3 or AgCl as the silver source. The metal solution is prepared by dissolving 10 mg of silver salt, 80 mg DDAB, and 120 mg DDA in 10 mL of toluene. After reaction with InAs nanocrystals, the silver-doped samples are precipitated with acetone rather than methanol to avoid silver reduction.  

ICP-AES measurements demonstrate controlled incorporation of silver atoms, with the number of silver atoms per nanocrystal adjustable from approximately 1 to 20 for 3.6 nm diameter nanocrystals. The relationship between Ag:NC ratio in solution and final silver content shows two distinct regimes, suggesting different incorporation mechanisms at low versus high silver concentrations.  

XPS analysis confirms silver incorporation through characteristic Ag 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 peaks at 368.2 eV and 374.2 eV, respectively. These binding energies are consistent with either Ag0 or Ag1+ oxidation states. Unlike copper doping, silver doping does not lead to significant oxidation of indium or arsenic atoms, as evidenced by the absence of additional peaks in the In 3d and As 3d regions.  

Optical characterization reveals that silver doping causes red shifts in both absorption and photoluminescence spectra. This symmetric shift indicates p-type doping behavior, with silver atoms acting as acceptors that create holes in the valence band. STM/STS measurements confirm that the conduction and valence band edges shift to higher energies (more positive potentials) upon silver doping.  

TEM and XRD analysis show that silver doping causes more significant lattice distortion than copper doping, due to the larger size of silver atoms compared to indium. The lattice parameter increases by up to 1% for heavily doped samples, as calculated from covalent radius considerations. The XRD patterns show broadening of peaks at higher angles, indicating increased disorder in the silver-doped nanocrystals.  

### Gold-Doped InAs Nanocrystals  

Gold doping is achieved using AuCl3 as the gold source, following similar procedures to the copper and silver doping methods. The metal solution is prepared by dissolving 10 mg of AuCl3, 80 mg DDAB, and 120 mg DDA in 10 mL of toluene. After reaction with InAs nanocrystals, the gold-doped samples are precipitated with methanol.  

ICP-AES measurements confirm gold incorporation at controlled levels, with approximately 1 to 15 gold atoms per 3.6 nm diameter nanocrystal achievable by varying the Au:NC ratio. XPS analysis shows characteristic Au 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 peaks at 83.9 eV and 87.5 eV, matching reported values for Au0.  

Unlike copper and silver doping, gold doping produces minimal changes in the optical spectra. Both absorption and photoluminescence spectra remain similar to undoped nanocrystals, with only minor shifts observed. STM/STS measurements confirm that the band edges remain largely unchanged upon gold doping.  

TEM and XRD analysis reveal that gold doping causes minimal lattice distortion due to the similar sizes of gold and indium atoms. The nanocrystals maintain good crystallinity with no significant peak broadening in XRD patterns. This is consistent with gold atoms substituting isovalently for indium without introducing additional charge carriers or significant strain.  

### EXPERIMENTAL  

The following experimental examples further illustrate the invention without limiting its scope:  

**Example 1: Synthesis of Undoped InAs Nanocrystals**  
Undoped InAs nanocrystals were synthesized as a control for doping experiments. A mixture of indium and arsenic precursors was prepared by adding 0.3 g (1 mmol) of tris(trimethylsilyl) arsenide (TMS3As) to 1.7 g of a 1.4 M InCl3 trioctylphosphine (TOP) solution (2 mmol total). 1 mL of this solution was injected into a three-neck flask containing 2 mL of TOP at 300°C under vigorous stirring. The temperature was reduced to 260°C and additional precursor solution was added to achieve particle growth. Growth was monitored by absorption spectroscopy of aliquots extracted from the reaction. Upon reaching the desired size, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature and transferred to a glovebox. Anhydrous toluene was added, and the nanocrystals were precipitated by adding anhydrous methanol. Size selective precipitation with toluene and methanol improved the size distribution to approximately 10%.  

**Example 2: Copper Doping of 3.6 nm InAs Nanocrystals**  
A copper solution was prepared by dissolving 10 mg CuCl2, 80 mg DDAB, and 120 mg DDA in 10 mL toluene. This solution was added dropwise to 2 mL of a toluene solution containing 3.6 nm diameter InAs nanocrystals (optical density ~1 at first exciton peak). After 15 minutes reaction under inert atmosphere, the copper-doped nanocrystals were precipitated with methanol and collected by centrifugation. ICP-AES measurements confirmed incorporation of approximately 12 copper atoms per nanocrystal. Absorption spectroscopy showed a 50 meV blue shift of the first exciton peak compared to undoped nanocrystals.  

**Example 3: Silver Doping of 4.1 nm InAs Nanocrystals**  
A silver solution was prepared by dissolving 10 mg AgNO3, 80 mg DDAB, and 120 mg DDA in 10 mL toluene. This solution was added to 2 mL of a toluene solution containing 4.1 nm diameter InAs nanocrystals. After reaction, the silver-doped nanocrystals were precipitated with acetone. ICP-AES measurements showed approximately 8 silver atoms per nanocrystal. Absorption spectroscopy revealed a 40 meV red shift of the first exciton peak, and photoluminescence showed a 35 meV red shift compared to undoped controls.  

**Example 4: Gold Doping of 3.3 nm InAs Nanocrystals**  
A gold solution was prepared by dissolving 10 mg AuCl3, 80 mg DDAB, and 120 mg DDA in 10 mL toluene. This solution was added to 2 mL of a toluene solution containing 3.3 nm diameter InAs nanocrystals. After reaction, the gold-doped nanocrystals were precipitated with methanol. ICP-AES measurements indicated approximately 5 gold atoms per nanocrystal. Optical spectroscopy showed minimal changes compared to undoped nanocrystals, with less than 10 meV shifts in absorption and photoluminescence spectra.  

**Example 5: Effect of Dopant Concentration on Optical Properties**  
The relationship between dopant concentration and optical properties was investigated by preparing a series of copper-doped InAs nanocrystals (3.6 nm diameter) with varying Cu:NC ratios. ICP-AES measurements showed the number of copper atoms per nanocrystal increased from 2 to 28 as the Cu:NC ratio increased from 0.2 to 5. The blue shift in absorption spectrum increased from 15 meV to 80 meV over this range, demonstrating precise control over doping-induced band structure modifications. Similar trends were observed for silver doping, where increasing Ag:NC ratios from 0.1 to 3 produced nanocrystals with 1 to 18 silver atoms per nanocrystal and red shifts increasing from 10 meV to 60 meV.  

**Example 6: Stability of Doped Nanocrystals**  
The stability of doped nanocrystals was evaluated by storing samples under inert atmosphere and periodically measuring their optical properties. Copper-doped, silver-doped, and gold-doped InAs nanocrystals all maintained their characteristic absorption and photoluminescence spectra for at least 30 days, with less than 5% variation in peak positions and intensities. This demonstrates that the dopant atoms remain incorporated in the nanocrystal lattice and do not segregate to the surface or otherwise become inactive over time.  

The foregoing examples demonstrate that the invention provides methods for reliably doping InAs nanocrystals with controlled amounts of copper, silver, or gold atoms. The resulting doped nanocrystals exhibit modified electronic structures that make them valuable for optoelectronic applications requiring precise control over carrier type and concentration. The methods are scalable and compatible with existing nanocrystal synthesis and processing techniques.
