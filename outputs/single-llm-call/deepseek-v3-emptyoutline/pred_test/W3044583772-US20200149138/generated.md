# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

### Technical Field  

The present invention relates to metallurgical compositions and processes, specifically to low-carbon steel compositions and methods of producing low-carbon steel with a hardened surface layer. More particularly, the invention concerns a novel low-carbon steel product having a surface region comprising a unique combination of microstructures including lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, which together provide exceptional surface hardness and strength while maintaining the ductility and toughness characteristic of low-carbon steels.  

### Description of the Related Art  

Low-carbon steels are widely used in industrial applications due to their excellent ductility and toughness. However, these steels typically exhibit inferior strength and hardness compared to higher carbon steels or alloyed steels. Various surface hardening techniques have been developed to improve the surface properties of low-carbon steels, including laser hardening, induction hardening, carburizing, and nitriding processes. These conventional methods require additional processing steps that increase manufacturing complexity and cost.  

Prior attempts to enhance the surface hardness of low-carbon steels without additional processing have achieved limited success. The microstructure of low-carbon steels typically consists of various phases including bainite, acicular ferrite, polygonal ferrite, and in some cases, lath martensite. Lath martensite, being the hardest microstructure in low-carbon steel, is characterized by ultrafine laths arranged in packets separated by high-angle boundaries. During martensitic transformation, carbon atoms align in one direction, creating a body-centered tetragonal (bct) crystal structure rather than the typical body-centered cubic (bcc) structure of ferrite.  

Existing methods for producing hardened surface layers on low-carbon steels typically require either additional alloying elements or post-processing heat treatments. There remains a need for a low-carbon steel composition and production method that can achieve high surface hardness comparable to martensitic steels while maintaining the core properties of conventional low-carbon steels, without requiring additional processing steps.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides a novel low-carbon steel product and method of production that overcomes the limitations of prior approaches. The steel product features a unique microstructure in its surface region comprising a combination of lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, resulting in exceptional surface hardness without additional processing steps.  

The steel composition contains approximately 0.023 wt% carbon, with controlled amounts of other alloying elements. The production method involves sub-rapid solidification using specific cooling rates that produce distinct microstructural zones in the surface region. In the 0-50 μm depth zone, the microstructure consists primarily of lath martensite and upper bainite with large aspect ratios (designated B1). In the 50-200 μm depth zone, the microstructure transitions to acicular ferrite and upper bainite with smaller aspect ratios (designated B2).  

The lath martensite in the surface region exhibits exceptional hardness (9.87 ± 0.51 GPa) despite the low overall carbon content of the steel, which is attributed to the extremely fine lath structure (average width of 128 nm) and carbon segregation during phase transformation. The upper bainite shows intermediate hardness (4.18 ± 0.39 GPa), while the acicular ferrite exhibits lower hardness (2.93 ± 0.30 GPa). The resulting surface layer provides yield strength up to 2378 MPa in the martensitic regions, giving the steel exceptional abrasion resistance unattainable in conventional low-carbon steels without additional processing.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The present invention relates to a low-carbon steel product and method of manufacture that produces a hardened surface layer through controlled solidification and cooling processes, without requiring additional processing steps. The steel composition contains approximately 0.023 wt% carbon, with specific ranges of other alloying elements to achieve the desired microstructure formation.  

The manufacturing process begins with melting the steel composition in an induction furnace at approximately 1600°C. The melt chemistry is carefully controlled, with free oxygen content adjusted to 35-50 ppm using FeO additions, and titanium content controlled to 0.01-0.02% using Fe-Ti additions. Manganese and silicon contents are adjusted through sequential additions of Fe-Mn and Fe-Si.  

The key innovation lies in the solidification process, where two copper plates with a precisely controlled gap are pressed into the melt and quickly removed. This rapid heat extraction creates a flat steel sheet with controlled thickness (approximately 2.5 × 75 × 100 mm). The solidification rates are carefully controlled to be in the sub-rapid range: approximately 900-1000 K/s at 0-50 μm depth and 400-900 K/s at 50-200 μm depth. Following solidification, the steel undergoes air cooling for approximately 30 seconds before final water quenching.  

The resulting microstructure in the surface region (0-200 μm depth) consists of three distinct phases:  
1. Lath martensite (M) characterized by ultrafine parallel laths with an average width of 128 nm, exhibiting a body-centered tetragonal crystal structure with measurable lattice distortion due to carbon segregation.  
2. Upper bainite (UB) consisting of lath-like ferrite with cementite precipitates between the laths, showing an Isaichev orientation relationship between the ferrite and cementite phases.  
3. Acicular ferrite (AF) characterized by randomly oriented needle-like grains with high-angle misorientations.  

The distribution of these phases varies with depth:  
- In the 0-50 μm zone: A mixture of lath martensite and B1-type upper bainite (large aspect ratio laths, length/width >30)  
- In the 50-200 μm zone: A mixture of acicular ferrite and B2-type upper bainite (smaller aspect ratio laths, length/width <10)  

The exceptional hardness of the surface layer, particularly in the martensitic regions, results from several factors:  
1. The extremely fine scale of the martensite laths (average 128 nm width)  
2. Carbon segregation during phase transformation, leading to locally high carbon content in the martensite (approximately 0.14 wt%) despite the low bulk carbon content  
3. The unique combination and distribution of the three microstructural constituents  

### EXAMPLES  

Example 1: Production of Steel Sheet  
A steel melt with composition 0.023 wt% C, balance Fe and minor alloying elements was prepared in an induction furnace at 1600°C. The free oxygen content was adjusted to 40 ppm using FeO additions, and titanium content was controlled to 0.015% using Fe-Ti. After manganese and silicon adjustments, the melt was cast using the copper plate method described above, producing a steel sheet approximately 2.5 mm thick. The sheet was air cooled for 30 seconds followed by water quenching.  

Example 2: Microstructural Characterization  
Cross-sectional samples from the steel sheet were prepared and examined using SEM and TEM. The surface region (0-50 μm depth) showed a mixture of lath martensite and upper bainite (B1 type), while the subsurface region (50-200 μm depth) showed acicular ferrite mixed with upper bainite (B2 type). TEM analysis confirmed the bct structure of martensite with measurable lattice distortion, and the presence of cementite precipitates in the upper bainite regions.  

Example 3: Mechanical Property Testing  
Nanoindentation testing revealed the following hardness values:  
- Lath martensite: 9.87 ± 0.51 GPa  
- Upper bainite: 4.18 ± 0.39 GPa  
- Acicular ferrite: 2.93 ± 0.30 GPa  

The calculated yield strengths were:  
- Lath martensite: 2378 ± 123 MPa  
- Upper bainite: 1007 ± 94 MPa  
- Acicular ferrite: 706 ± 72 MPa  

These examples demonstrate the unique properties achievable with the invented steel composition and production method, providing exceptional surface hardness in a low-carbon steel without requiring additional processing steps.
