# DESCRIPTION  

## INTRODUCTION  

The present invention relates to the field of protein synthesis and neurodegenerative diseases. More specifically, it concerns the discovery that the non-protein amino acid β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA) can be misincorporated into human proteins during translation, replacing L-serine, and methods for preventing such misincorporation. This misincorporation leads to protein misfolding, aggregation, and subsequent cellular apoptosis—processes strongly associated with neurodegenerative pathologies such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), Parkinson's disease, and Alzheimer's disease.  

Protein translation is a highly regulated process that requires precise discrimination among the twenty canonical amino acids to maintain fidelity. While error rates are typically low (1 in 10³ to 10⁴), structural analogs of proteinogenic amino acids can bypass proofreading mechanisms and become incorporated into polypeptides. Such misincorporation events have been implicated in various neurodegenerative disorders, including ALS/Parkinsonism dementia complex (ALS/PDC) observed in Guam. BMAA, a neurotoxic non-protein amino acid produced by cyanobacteria, has been detected in protein-bound form in post-mortem brain tissues of ALS and Alzheimer's patients, suggesting a potential role in disease pathogenesis.  

The present invention provides experimental evidence demonstrating that BMAA is misincorporated into human proteins via ribosomal translation, specifically replacing L-serine. Furthermore, the invention discloses that co-administration of L-serine competitively inhibits BMAA incorporation, thereby preventing protein misfolding and subsequent cellular toxicity. These findings have significant implications for therapeutic interventions in neurodegenerative diseases linked to environmental BMAA exposure.  

## SUMMARY  

The invention encompasses methods and compositions for preventing misincorporation of β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA) into human proteins during translation. Experimental data demonstrate that BMAA is erroneously incorporated into nascent polypeptides in place of L-serine through a protein synthesis-dependent mechanism. This misincorporation leads to protein misfolding, aggregation, and apoptosis—hallmarks of neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS and Alzheimer's.  

Key aspects of the invention include:  
1. Demonstration that BMAA incorporation into cellular proteins is inhibited by cycloheximide, confirming ribosomal translation as the incorporation mechanism.  
2. Competitive inhibition studies showing L-serine, but not D-serine, prevents BMAA incorporation in a dose-dependent manner.  
3. Analytical validation using LC/MS/MS that confirms BMAA is covalently bound in proteins via peptide linkages.  
4. Cellular consequences of BMAA misincorporation including protein aggregation (detected by autofluorescence) and apoptosis.  
5. Therapeutic applications utilizing L-serine supplementation to prevent BMAA incorporation and associated neurotoxicity.  

The invention provides diagnostic methods for detecting BMAA incorporation in biological samples and therapeutic strategies employing L-serine administration to mitigate BMAA-induced protein misfolding. These approaches are particularly relevant for populations exposed to environmental sources of BMAA, such as cyanobacterial blooms or traditional foods containing BMAA-producing organisms.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

The present invention is based on the discovery that the non-protein amino acid β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA) is misincorporated into human proteins during translation, specifically replacing L-serine residues. This misincorporation occurs through the normal protein synthesis machinery and leads to profound cellular consequences including protein misfolding, aggregation, and apoptosis.  

**Mechanism of BMAA Misincorporation**  
BMAA incorporation into proteins was shown to be protein synthesis-dependent through experiments with cycloheximide (CHX), a known translation inhibitor. In human MRC-5 fibroblasts, SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells, and HUVEC endothelial cells, CHX treatment reduced BMAA incorporation to levels comparable to its inhibition of canonical amino acid incorporation (e.g., L-leucine). This demonstrates that BMAA enters proteins through ribosomal translation rather than post-translational modification.  

The specificity of BMAA for L-serine tRNA synthetase was established through competitive inhibition studies. When individual proteinogenic amino acids were tested for their ability to block BMAA incorporation, only L-serine showed significant inhibition in a dose-dependent manner. The stereospecificity of this inhibition was confirmed by the inability of D-serine to prevent BMAA incorporation. Furthermore, omission of L-serine from a complete mixture of protein amino acids resulted in significantly increased BMAA incorporation, while inclusion of all twenty standard amino acids substantially reduced BMAA uptake.  

**Analytical Confirmation of BMAA Incorporation**  
The covalent incorporation of BMAA into proteins was rigorously confirmed through multiple analytical approaches. Treatment of BMAA-containing proteins with reducing agents (DTT) or detergents (SDS) failed to release BMAA, indicating it was not merely adsorbed to protein surfaces. However, BMAA was liberated by treatments that cleave peptide bonds—acid hydrolysis or enzymatic digestion with pronase.  

Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS) provided definitive identification of BMAA in hydrolyzed cellular proteins. The method employed a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer with heated electrospray ionization, monitoring multiple specific transitions (m/z 459→119, 171, 188, 214, 258, 289) to distinguish BMAA from structural analogs. Retention times and ion ratios matched authenticated BMAA standards, and quantification showed linear recovery across a range of concentrations (r²=0.99).  

**Cellular Consequences of Misincorporation**  
BMAA incorporation induced measurable cellular pathologies characteristic of neurodegenerative processes. Autofluorescence microscopy revealed protein aggregation in cells treated with BMAA, particularly in perinuclear and cytosolic regions. This aggregation was prevented by co-administration of L-serine, linking it directly to BMAA misincorporation rather than other potential toxic effects.  

Flow cytometry with Annexin V staining demonstrated that BMAA treatment induced apoptosis, which was mitigated by either L-serine supplementation or cycloheximide treatment. This indicates that apoptosis resulted from BMAA incorporation into proteins rather than from other mechanisms of BMAA toxicity. Notably, necrosis was not observed, as measured by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release and propidium iodide exclusion.  

**Therapeutic Implications**  
The discovery that L-serine competes with BMAA for incorporation into proteins provides a basis for therapeutic intervention in BMAA-associated neurodegenerative diseases. By maintaining adequate L-serine concentrations, particularly in neural tissues, BMAA misincorporation can be prevented, thereby averting the cascade of protein misfolding, aggregation, and neuronal apoptosis.  

This approach may be particularly beneficial for:  
1. Populations with dietary exposure to BMAA (e.g., through cyanobacterial blooms or traditional foods containing BMAA-producing organisms)  
2. Individuals with genetic susceptibilities to protein misfolding disorders  
3. Patients exhibiting early biomarkers of ALS or related neurodegenerative diseases  

The invention encompasses both prophylactic and therapeutic use of L-serine, as well as diagnostic methods to detect BMAA incorporation in patient samples. The LC/MS/MS methodology described provides a sensitive and specific means to quantify BMAA in biological tissues, enabling both research and clinical applications.  

### EXAMPLES  

**Example 1: Protein Synthesis-Dependent Incorporation of BMAA**  
Human MRC-5 fibroblasts were incubated with ³H-BMAA (31.25 nM) in Hank's buffered salt solution (HBSS) containing 10% fetal bovine serum. Cell lysates showed time-dependent increases in radiolabel incorporation (2-16 hours). Parallel cultures treated with cycloheximide (2 µg/ml) demonstrated 72-85% reduction in BMAA incorporation, comparable to cycloheximide's inhibition of ³H-leucine incorporation (78-87%). Similar results were obtained in SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells and HUVEC endothelial cells, confirming the protein synthesis-dependent mechanism across cell types.  

**Example 2: L-Serine Specifically Inhibits BMAA Incorporation**  
MRC-5 cells were incubated with ³H-BMAA (31.25 nM) in the presence of individual protein amino acids (250 µM). Only L-serine showed significant inhibition of BMAA incorporation (68% reduction). Dose-response studies demonstrated progressive inhibition with increasing L-serine concentrations (50-250 µM). In contrast, D-serine (250 µM) showed no inhibitory effect. When cells were incubated with ³H-BMAA in medium containing all 20 protein amino acids (400 µM each), BMAA incorporation was reduced by 89% compared to amino acid-free conditions. Omission of L-serine from this complete mixture resulted in 4.7-fold increased BMAA incorporation.  

**Example 3: LC/MS/MS Confirmation of BMAA in Proteins**  
MRC-5 cells were incubated with non-labeled BMAA (0-300 µM) for 24 hours. Proteins were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid, hydrolyzed in 6M HCl, and analyzed by LC/MS/MS. BMAA was detected with retention time 4.98 min (±0.26 min) and showed characteristic ion transitions including m/z 459→258. Quantification demonstrated linear recovery (r²=0.99) across the concentration range. The limit of detection was 48 femtomoles, with intra-day and inter-day precision <7% RSD for peak areas and <0.3 min RSD for retention times.  

**Example 4: Prevention of BMAA-Induced Protein Aggregation**  
MRC-5 cells incubated with 300 µM BMAA for 96 hours developed perinuclear autofluorescence indicative of protein aggregation. Co-incubation with 300 µM L-serine completely prevented this aggregation. Flow cytometry revealed that BMAA treatment increased Annexin V staining (indicating apoptosis) by 3.2-fold compared to controls. This apoptotic effect was reduced by 61% with L-serine co-treatment and by 58% with cycloheximide, confirming the dependence on protein incorporation.  

**Example 5: Proteolytic Release of Protein-Bound BMAA**  
SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells were incubated with ³H-BMAA (31.25 nM) for 24 hours. Isolated proteins resisted release of radiolabel by DTT (1 mM) or SDS (2%), but treatment with pronase (2 mg/ml) released 89% of incorporated BMAA after 48 hours. Acid hydrolysis (6M HCl, 110°C, 16 hours) released 94% of incorporated BMAA, confirming its covalent incorporation via peptide bonds.  

These examples demonstrate the protein synthesis-dependent incorporation of BMAA into human proteins, its specific replacement of L-serine, the analytical methods for its detection, and the cellular consequences of its misincorporation. They further establish L-serine as an effective inhibitor of this process with therapeutic potential for BMAA-associated neurodegenerative diseases.
