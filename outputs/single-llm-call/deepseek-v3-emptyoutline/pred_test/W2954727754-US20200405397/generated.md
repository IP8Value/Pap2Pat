## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

The field of interventional cardiology has witnessed significant advancements in recent years, particularly in the area of transcatheter procedures for treating structural heart disease. These minimally invasive techniques have become the preferred treatment approach for conditions including atrial septal defects, valvular disorders, and other cardiac abnormalities. Current standard practice relies heavily on X-ray fluoroscopy as the primary imaging modality during these procedures due to its real-time imaging capabilities and ability to visualize radiopaque markers on medical devices. However, fluoroscopic imaging presents several inherent limitations that complicate interventional procedures. The technology provides only two-dimensional projections of three-dimensional anatomical structures, fundamentally lacking depth perception critical for precise catheter navigation. While contrast agents can temporarily opacify cardiac structures, they provide limited temporal resolution and require repeated administration throughout procedures.

Complementary imaging modalities such as transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) have been employed to mitigate some limitations of fluoroscopy. TEE offers direct visualization of cardiac tissue and blood flow patterns, but requires specialized equipment and highly trained operators not universally available. Furthermore, both fluoroscopy and echocardiography display images on two-dimensional screens, forcing clinicians to mentally reconstruct three-dimensional relationships from multiple viewing angles. This cognitive translation introduces potential for error and increases procedural complexity.

Preoperative imaging techniques including computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging provide detailed three-dimensional anatomical information that could theoretically enhance procedural guidance. Various attempts have been made to overlay these preoperative images onto real-time fluoroscopy or echocardiography displays. However, such hybrid imaging approaches often obscure critical real-time information and fail to provide intuitive three-dimensional spatial relationships. The fundamental challenge lies in accurately registering preoperative volumetric data with real-time two-dimensional imaging while maintaining clinically relevant spatial resolution and temporal fidelity.

Existing image registration techniques face significant technical hurdles when applied to cardiac interventions. Intensity-based methods struggle with inconsistent pixel characteristics across imaging modalities, while gradient-based approaches have limited applicability due to variable tissue contrast. Feature-based registration methods show promise but encounter difficulties in reliably identifying cardiac features across different imaging techniques. These technical limitations have prevented the development of a robust, real-time three-dimensional guidance system for cardiac interventions.

Augmented reality technology has emerged as a potential solution for enhanced medical visualization, with applications demonstrated in orthopedic and neurosurgical procedures. However, existing augmented reality systems lack the real-time quantitative feedback necessary for cardiac interventions, where continuous monitoring of catheter position relative to dynamic cardiac anatomy is essential. The unique challenges of cardiac motion, combined with the need for millimeter-scale precision in catheter positioning, have thus far prevented effective application of augmented reality in interventional cardiology.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure describes an advanced augmented reality image guidance system specifically designed for cardiac interventional procedures. The system integrates preoperative three-dimensional cardiac imaging with real-time catheter tracking to provide holographic visualization of the complete procedural environment. At the core of the invention is a novel registration algorithm that precisely aligns coordinate systems between volumetric CT or MR images and intraoperative fluoroscopy using the spinal column as a stable fiduciary marker.

The system operates through several integrated components. Preoperatively, high-resolution three-dimensional models of patient-specific cardiac anatomy are generated through segmentation of diagnostic CT or MR images. These models include not only cardiac structures but also adjacent vertebral anatomy, which serves as a reference frame for subsequent image registration. The segmentation process employs adaptive thresholding and three-dimensional reconstruction techniques to create accurate digital representations of cardiac chambers, valves, and associated vascular structures.

During the interventional procedure, the system continuously processes fluoroscopic images acquired from at least two distinct angles. A specialized algorithm detects and tracks catheter position in these images through edge detection and centerline calculation techniques. Simultaneously, the system identifies vertebral anatomy in fluoroscopic images and registers these features with corresponding structures in the preoperative three-dimensional model using a Fourier-based transformation method. This registration establishes a common coordinate system that enables precise correlation between real-time catheter position and the preoperative cardiac model.

The registered data is rendered as three-dimensional holograms through augmented reality display technology, such as head-mounted displays. The holographic visualization presents the interventionalist with an intuitive three-dimensional representation of catheter position relative to cardiac anatomy, overcoming the limitations of conventional two-dimensional displays. The system incorporates interactive features including voice-controlled view adjustments, virtual camera perspectives from the catheter tip, and pre-programmed procedural guidance pathways.

Technical innovations of the system include the Fourier-Mellin transformation method for robust image registration across modalities, algorithms for three-dimensional catheter position determination from biplanar fluoroscopy, and specialized rendering techniques for medical augmented reality applications. The system demonstrates superior registration accuracy compared to conventional feature-based methods, with experimental results showing submillimeter alignment precision and 100% registration success rates in controlled testing.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The augmented reality image guidance system for cardiac interventions comprises several interconnected subsystems that work in concert to provide real-time three-dimensional procedural visualization. Each component incorporates specialized technical solutions to address the unique challenges of cardiac intervention guidance.

The preoperative imaging subsystem begins with acquisition of high-resolution volumetric CT or MR scans of the patient's thoracic cavity. These scans must include both cardiac anatomy and adjacent vertebral structures, typically spanning from approximately thoracic vertebrae T4 through T8. The imaging protocol ensures consistent patient positioning in supine orientation to maintain anatomical relationships between preoperative and intraoperative configurations.

Image segmentation employs a multi-step process to extract three-dimensional cardiac models from the volumetric data. Initial processing uses adaptive thresholding to differentiate cardiac tissues based on Hounsfield units or signal intensity values. Subsequent morphological operations remove noise and fill voids to create continuous three-dimensional surfaces. The system identifies and preserves the largest connected components corresponding to cardiac chambers while selectively removing extraneous structures such as ribs or peripheral vasculature. Final smoothing algorithms generate watertight three-dimensional meshes suitable for holographic rendering.

For procedural planning applications, the system allows annotation of target sites and preferred catheter pathways directly onto the three-dimensional model. These annotations appear as virtual landmarks during the actual procedure, providing visual guidance to the interventionalist. The planning interface supports measurement tools for quantifying anatomical dimensions and spatial relationships critical for device selection and approach planning.

The intraoperative imaging subsystem processes real-time fluoroscopic data to track both anatomical references and catheter position. Fluoroscopic images are acquired from at least two distinct angles, typically ranging from 30° to 60° in left or right anterior oblique orientations. The system automatically records the exact angular configuration of the C-arm for each acquisition to enable precise geometric reconstruction.

Spinal detection in fluoroscopic images employs a multi-stage algorithm beginning with noise reduction through Gaussian smoothing. Adaptive thresholding using Otsu's method creates binary images where vertebral structures are clearly delineated. Morphological closing operations eliminate small artifacts while preserving the overall shape of vertebral bodies. The system calculates bounding boxes around the largest connected components to define regions of interest for subsequent processing.

The registration subsystem performs coordinate system alignment between preoperative three-dimensional models and intraoperative fluoroscopic images using a Fourier-based approach. This method begins by generating two-dimensional projection images from the three-dimensional vertebral model that match the viewing angles of acquired fluoroscopic images. The system then applies Fourier-Mellin transformation to these image pairs, converting rotational and scaling differences into translational shifts in a log-polar coordinate space.

Phase correlation analysis in the frequency domain identifies the optimal transformation parameters that align the two imaging modalities. The system calculates a composite transformation matrix incorporating scaling, rotation, and translation components to precisely register the three-dimensional coordinate systems. Experimental validation demonstrates this method achieves mean registration errors below 0.5 mm across a range of imaging angles.

Catheter tracking employs specialized algorithms to identify and localize interventional devices in fluoroscopic images. The system initiates detection at the catheter entry point, typically near the image inferior border, and follows the device using iterative region-of-interest analysis. Within each region, edge detection identifies catheter boundaries through gradient analysis perpendicular to the device axis. The catheter centerline is calculated as the midline between detected edges, with subsequent regions of interest positioned along this trajectory.

Three-dimensional catheter localization derives from analysis of biplanar fluoroscopic images. The system reconstructs the complete spatial position by solving the geometric relationship between two-dimensional catheter coordinates in each view and the known angular separation between imaging planes. This calculation incorporates corrections for image magnification and accounts for the registered coordinate system established through vertebral alignment.

The augmented reality visualization subsystem renders the integrated data as interactive three-dimensional holograms. The system imports the segmented cardiac model and registered catheter positions into a holographic display environment. Real-time rendering maintains accurate spatial relationships while allowing viewpoint adjustment through head movement or voice commands. The interface provides multiple visualization modes including translucent anatomy, cross-sectional views, and virtual camera perspectives from the catheter tip.

Interactive features enhance procedural guidance and user experience. The system supports gesture recognition for manipulating three-dimensional models, allowing clinicians to reposition or rotate anatomical renderings as needed. Voice commands enable rapid access to preset viewing angles or visualization modes without interrupting sterile technique. Procedural waypoints and target sites appear as highlighted regions within the three-dimensional display, providing continuous visual feedback during device navigation.

Technical implementation utilizes distributed processing across specialized hardware components. High-performance workstations handle computationally intensive tasks including image registration and three-dimensional reconstruction, while head-mounted displays focus on real-time holographic rendering. Wireless networking maintains continuous data exchange between components, ensuring seamless updates to the augmented reality visualization as the procedure progresses.

The system architecture incorporates redundancy and fault tolerance mechanisms to maintain reliability during critical procedures. Backup registration algorithms can engage if primary methods encounter challenging image characteristics, while cached position data ensures continuous visualization during temporary imaging interruptions. Quality assurance metrics continuously monitor registration accuracy and catheter tracking precision, alerting operators to any degradation in system performance.

Clinical integration features facilitate adoption into existing workflow patterns. The system interfaces with standard DICOM imaging systems and hospital networks, minimizing requirements for additional infrastructure. User interfaces follow familiar paradigms from conventional imaging systems while introducing augmented reality capabilities gradually. Training modules and simulation modes allow clinicians to develop proficiency with the three-dimensional visualization environment before employing it in actual procedures.
