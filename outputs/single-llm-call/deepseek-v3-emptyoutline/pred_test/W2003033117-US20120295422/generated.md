# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

The present invention relates generally to semiconductor light-emitting devices, and more particularly to gallium nitride (GaN)-based light-emitting diodes (LEDs) grown on silicon substrates. In recent years, III-nitride LEDs have gained significant commercial adoption in applications such as digital displays, liquid crystal display backlighting, traffic signals, vehicle lighting, and general illumination. Despite these advancements, widespread replacement of traditional incandescent and fluorescent lamps with GaN-based LEDs has been hindered by two primary challenges: the high cost of production and the need for improved electricity-to-light conversion efficiency.  

A substantial portion of the production cost for LED chips is attributed to the substrate, accounting for approximately 25 to 35 percent of the total expense. Currently, commercially available GaN LED structures are predominantly grown on either sapphire or silicon carbide (SiC) substrates. While these substrates have been widely utilized, they present notable drawbacks. Sapphire and SiC substrates are relatively expensive, particularly in the case of SiC, and their inherent hardness complicates device fabrication processes, further increasing manufacturing costs. Silicon substrates have emerged as a promising alternative due to their low cost, availability in large sizes, high surface quality, excellent conductivity, and compatibility with well-established processing techniques. Several research groups have successfully demonstrated the growth of GaN-based blue LEDs on silicon substrates, offering a viable low-cost solution for solid-state lighting applications.  

Another critical challenge in the development of high-efficiency LEDs lies in the growth of indium gallium nitride (InGaN)/GaN multiple quantum wells (MQWs), which are essential for light emission. This challenge is particularly pronounced in green LEDs, where high-indium-content InGaN layers with superior crystal quality are required. Achieving homogeneous indium incorporation within the InGaN quantum wells (QWs) and maintaining abrupt interfaces between the wells and barriers are fundamental to optimizing device performance. Prior art has explored various approaches to address these issues, but a comprehensive solution that balances cost-effectiveness with high efficiency remains elusive.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides a novel method for fabricating high-efficiency GaN-based green LEDs on silicon substrates, addressing the aforementioned challenges through an optimized growth process for InGaN/GaN MQWs. The invention introduces a critical improvement by incorporating a GaN cap layer of specific thickness between the InGaN well layer and the GaN barrier layer. This cap layer serves to protect the InGaN well from thermal decomposition during subsequent high-temperature growth steps, thereby preserving the homogeneity of indium distribution and ensuring abrupt interfaces between the quantum wells and barriers.  

In one embodiment, the invention comprises a semiconductor structure grown on a silicon (111) substrate using metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD). The structure includes a stress-releasing layer of aluminum nitride (AlN) and aluminum gallium nitride (AlGaN), followed by an n-type GaN layer. An InGaN strain pre-relief layer is deposited atop the n-GaN layer, and a series of InGaN/GaN MQWs are grown thereon. Each period of the MQW structure consists of an InGaN well layer, a GaN cap layer grown at the same temperature as the well, and a GaN barrier layer grown at a temperature approximately 120°C higher than the well. The thickness of the GaN cap layer is carefully controlled within a range of 8 Å to 30 Å, with optimal performance observed at 15 Å and 30 Å.  

Experimental results demonstrate that the inclusion of the GaN cap layer significantly enhances the structural and optical properties of the MQWs. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis reveals the formation of well-defined satellite peaks, indicative of abrupt interfaces and periodic structures in samples with cap layer thicknesses of 15 Å and 30 Å. Fluorescence microscopy (FLM) images further confirm improved indium homogeneity, while photoluminescence (PL) measurements show increased emission intensity and narrower full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) values, indicative of superior light-emitting performance.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

### Overview  

The invention provides a method for fabricating GaN-based green LEDs on silicon substrates, with a focus on optimizing the growth of InGaN/GaN MQWs to achieve high efficiency and cost-effectiveness. The key innovation lies in the introduction of a GaN cap layer of controlled thickness between the InGaN well and the GaN barrier, which mitigates thermal decomposition of the well during high-temperature barrier growth.  

The semiconductor structure is grown using MOCVD on a silicon (111) substrate. Prior to growth, the substrate undergoes a cleaning process involving degreasing with a sulfuric acid and hydrogen peroxide mixture, followed by etching with diluted hydrofluoric acid to remove surface contaminants and oxide layers. The substrate is then subjected to in-situ hydrogen heating at 1100°C within the reactor. A thin aluminum film is deposited on the silicon surface to prevent reaction with gallium or nitridation by ammonia.  

Subsequent layers include a stress-releasing AlN/AlGaN layer, a 3 μm-thick n-type GaN layer, and a 200 nm-thick InGaN strain pre-relief layer. The MQW structure consists of six periods, each comprising an InGaN well layer, a GaN cap layer grown at the well temperature, and a GaN barrier layer grown at a temperature 120°C higher than the well. The cap layer thickness is varied between 8 Å, 15 Å, and 30 Å to evaluate its impact on MQW quality. Finally, a 130 nm-thick p-type GaN layer is deposited to complete the LED structure.  

### EXAMPLE  

An exemplary embodiment of the invention was implemented as follows:  

1. **Substrate Preparation**: A silicon (111) substrate was cleaned using a 3:1 mixture of H2SO4 and H2O2, followed by etching with 5% diluted HF to remove surface oxides. The substrate was then loaded into a Thomas Swan 6 × 2′′ MOCVD reactor with a close-coupled showerhead design.  

2. **In-Situ Heating**: The substrate was heated at 1100°C under hydrogen flow for 30 minutes to ensure a clean surface.  

3. **Aluminum Film Deposition**: Trimethylaluminum (TMAl) was introduced into the reactor at 800°C for 2 minutes to form a thin aluminum film, preventing undesirable reactions during subsequent growth.  

4. **Stress-Releasing Layer Growth**: A 0.3 μm-thick AlN/AlGaN layer was grown to mitigate lattice mismatch between the silicon substrate and GaN.  

5. **N-Type GaN Layer**: A 3 μm-thick GaN layer doped with silicon was deposited to serve as the n-type contact layer.  

6. **Strain Pre-Relief Layer**: A 200 nm-thick InGaN layer was grown to relieve strain in the subsequent MQW structure.  

7. **MQW Growth**: Six periods of InGaN/GaN MQWs were grown, with each period consisting of:  
   - An InGaN well layer.  
   - A GaN cap layer grown at the same temperature as the well, with thicknesses of 8 Å (Sample A), 15 Å (Sample B), and 30 Å (Sample C).  
   - A GaN barrier layer grown at a temperature 120°C higher than the well.  

8. **P-Type GaN Layer**: A 130 nm-thick Mg-doped GaN layer was deposited to complete the p-type contact.  

**Characterization Results**:  
- **XRD Analysis**: Samples B and C exhibited clear satellite peaks, indicating well-defined periodic structures and abrupt interfaces. Sample A showed no discernible peaks, suggesting poor MQW formation due to insufficient cap layer thickness.  
- **Fluorescence Microscopy**: Sample A displayed uneven fluorescence with bright spots, indicative of InGaN decomposition. Samples B and C showed progressively more uniform fluorescence, with Sample C exhibiting the best homogeneity.  
- **Photoluminescence**: PL intensity increased with cap layer thickness, and the FWHM narrowed, demonstrating improved optical performance. Sample C exhibited the highest intensity and narrowest FWHM, with a peak wavelength of 530 nm.  

The results confirm that the GaN cap layer is a critical parameter for achieving high-quality InGaN/GaN MQWs, leading to enhanced LED performance.
