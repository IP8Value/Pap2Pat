## BACKGROUND  

The study of protein three-dimensional structures in solution has traditionally relied on techniques such as X-ray crystallography and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. While these methods provide high-resolution structural information, they are often limited by requirements for protein crystallization or size constraints. In recent years, protein footprinting methods have emerged as powerful alternatives for studying protein conformations in solution. These techniques involve measuring solvent accessibility of peptide backbones or amino acid side chains through covalent or non-covalent labeling, followed by mass spectrometric analysis.  

Hydrogen deuterium exchange (HDX) is one of the most widely used protein footprinting methods. However, HDX presents several technical challenges, including time-sensitive back-exchange, the need for low-temperature operation, and reliance on acidic conditions that necessitate the use of non-specific proteases. Furthermore, HDX provides information primarily about peptide backbone accessibility rather than amino acid side chains, potentially offering an incomplete picture of protein structure.  

Chemical footprinting methods using hydroxyl radicals have shown promise as complementary approaches. Current hydroxyl radical generation techniques include fast photochemical oxidation of proteins (FPOP) and synchrotron-based radiolysis. While effective, these methods have limitations. FPOP requires hydrogen peroxide and laser exposure within the absorption range of aromatic residues, while synchrotron radiation necessitates access to specialized facilities and operates on millisecond timescales.  

There remains a significant need in the field for protein footprinting methods that combine the advantages of hydroxyl radical labeling with greater accessibility, faster timescales, and reduced potential for protein perturbation. The present invention addresses these needs through a novel plasma-based approach to hydroxyl radical generation for protein footprinting.  

## SUMMARY  

The present invention provides a novel system and method for protein footprinting using plasma-induced modification of biomolecules (PLIMB). The invention encompasses apparatus, methods, and applications for generating microsecond (µs) pulses of hydroxyl radicals from water in solution using plasma discharge. This technology enables protein footprinting without requiring added chemicals or access to specialized facilities.  

Key aspects of the invention include:  
1. A plasma generation system configured to produce µs bursts of hydroxyl radicals in aqueous protein solutions  
2. Methods for controlling plasma exposure to achieve dose-dependent protein modification while maintaining native protein structure  
3. Applications for mapping protein-protein interactions and conformational changes in solution  
4. Specific protocols for analyzing model proteins (e.g., bovine serum albumin) and clinically relevant protein systems (e.g., epidermal growth factor receptor)  

The PLIMB technology offers several advantages over existing protein footprinting methods. The plasma-based system generates hydroxyl radicals directly from water without requiring chemical additives. The µs timescale of radical generation minimizes potential perturbation of protein structure during labeling. The benchtop-compatible design makes the technology widely accessible for both research and clinical applications.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

### Methods  

The invention provides methods for protein footprinting using plasma-generated hydroxyl radicals. A key method involves generating a dielectric-barrier discharge plasma above an aqueous protein solution. The plasma is created by applying a time-varying high voltage signal (1-31 kV) at frequencies ranging from 0 to 15 kHz to a needle electrode positioned approximately 1 mm above the sample surface.  

Protein samples are prepared in lightly buffered aqueous solutions (e.g., phosphate buffered saline or ammonium acetate) at concentrations typically ranging from 50 nM to 50 µM. Samples are maintained at 2-4°C during plasma treatment using a Peltier cooling system. Treatment durations can be varied from microseconds to several minutes depending on the desired degree of modification.  

Following plasma exposure, proteins are processed for mass spectrometric analysis. Processing steps may include:  
- Protein precipitation using trichloroacetic acid  
- Reduction and alkylation of cysteine residues  
- Proteolytic digestion (e.g., with trypsin/Lys-C)  
- Desalting and concentration of peptides  

Mass spectrometric analysis is performed using techniques such as electrospray ionization time-of-flight (ESI-TOF) or liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Data analysis involves identifying and quantifying oxidative modifications to specific amino acid residues, which serve as markers of solvent accessibility.

## EXAMPLE 1  

### Labeling Cytochrome C  

Cytochrome c (10 µM in 50 mM ammonium acetate) was treated with PLIMB for durations ranging from 0 to 60 seconds. Following treatment, samples were processed by reduction, alkylation, and tryptic digestion. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed dose-dependent oxidation of multiple amino acid residues, with surface-exposed residues showing greater modification than buried residues.  

Specific oxidation hotspots were identified at solvent-accessible regions known from the cytochrome c crystal structure. Comparison with denatured controls confirmed that the native protein structure was maintained during plasma treatment. This example demonstrates the utility of PLIMB for mapping solvent accessibility in small, well-characterized proteins.  

## EXAMPLE 2  

### Labeling Bovine Serum Albumin  

Bovine serum albumin (BSA, 50 µM in PBS) was treated with PLIMB for 0-60 seconds in both native and pre-digested (denatured) states. Analysis revealed distinct modification patterns:  

In native BSA:  
- Surface-exposed regions showed rapid, dose-dependent oxidation  
- Buried regions exhibited minimal modification  
- Structural integrity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE  

In pre-digested BSA:  
- Previously buried regions showed increased oxidation  
- Modification patterns differed significantly from native state  
- Dose-response curves demonstrated exposure-dependent labeling  

This example highlights PLIMB's ability to distinguish between solvent-accessible and buried protein regions while maintaining native structure during labeling.  

## EXAMPLE 3  

### Breakdown of DNA in Size-Dependent and Exposure-Dose-Dependent Fashion  

Plasmid DNA (pUC19, 100 ng/µL in TE buffer) was treated with PLIMB for varying durations. Gel electrophoresis revealed:  

- Dose-dependent strand breakage  
- Size-dependent fragmentation patterns  
- Preservation of supercoiled form at low doses  
- Complete linearization at intermediate doses  
- Extensive fragmentation at high doses  

This example demonstrates PLIMB's applicability to nucleic acid footprinting and the controlled generation of oxidative damage for structural studies.  

## EXAMPLE 4  

### Protein Labeling in Intact/Live Cells  

Human HEK293 cells in PBS were treated with PLIMB for 5-30 seconds. Following treatment:  

- Cell viability was maintained (>90% by trypan blue exclusion)  
- Plasma membrane integrity was preserved  
- Intracellular proteins showed dose-dependent oxidation  
- Modification patterns differed from lysate controls  
- Specific oxidative markers of subcellular localization were identified  

This example establishes PLIMB's utility for footprinting in cellular contexts and intact biological systems.
