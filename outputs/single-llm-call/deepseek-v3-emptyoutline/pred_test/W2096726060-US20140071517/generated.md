## FIELD OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention relates to luminescent materials and more particularly to nanocomposite materials capable of converting infrared radiation into visible light through an upconversion process. The invention specifically discloses lanthanide-doped nanocrystals functionalized with infrared-absorbing dye molecules that act as optical antennae, enabling enhanced upconversion efficiency and spectral discrimination of infrared radiation.  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

Optical upconversion represents a multiphoton process wherein sequential absorption of two or more photons leads to emission of radiation at shorter wavelengths. While this phenomenon holds significant potential across applications ranging from solid-state lasers to biomedical imaging, conventional lanthanide-based upconverters suffer from fundamental limitations. The Laporte-forbidden f-f transitions characteristic of lanthanide ions exhibit exceptionally low molar absorptivity coefficients (ε ∼ 0.5-5 M⁻¹cm⁻¹) and narrow absorption bandwidths (<20 nm FWHM). These constraints severely restrict the practical utility of such materials in applications requiring efficient light harvesting.  

Prior attempts to address these limitations have focused primarily on host matrix optimization and dopant concentration tuning. The sodium-yttrium-tetrafluoride (NaYF₄) crystal lattice has emerged as the predominant host material due to its low phonon energy (370 cm⁻¹), which minimizes nonradiative relaxation pathways. However, even state-of-the-art NaYF₄:Yb³⁺,Er³⁺ systems demonstrate monochromatic quantum efficiencies below 0.3%, with overall performance constrained by the fundamental absorption characteristics of the lanthanide ions.  

Recent developments in dye-sensitized approaches have demonstrated the potential for optical antennae to overcome these limitations. Organic dyes typically exhibit absorption cross-sections 4-5 orders of magnitude greater than lanthanide ions, coupled with absorption bandwidths exceeding 100 nm. When properly coupled to the nanocrystal through Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET), these antennae can dramatically enhance upconversion efficiency. However, existing implementations suffer from inadequate dye-nanoparticle coupling, chemical instability of the dye-nanocrystal interface, and inability to discriminate between different infrared wavelengths through distinct visible emissions.  

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a nanocomposite material system comprising lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals covalently functionalized with infrared-absorbing dye molecules. The system overcomes limitations of conventional upconverters through three key innovations:  

First, the invention discloses a robust chemical linkage between the optical antenna dye and the nanoparticle surface. This is achieved through amine-functionalized siloxane encapsulation followed by covalent amide bond formation with dye molecules containing carboxylic acid, isothiocyanate, or N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester functional groups. The linkage maintains optimal 2-6 nm separation for efficient FRET while providing chemical stability absent in physisorbed or ligand-exchanged systems.  

Second, the invention teaches specific pairings of infrared dyes with lanthanide dopant combinations to enable spectral discrimination. Particular embodiments utilize DY-831 dye (absorption peak ∼830 nm) with NaYF₄:Yb³⁺,Ho³⁺ nanoparticles and DyL800 dye (absorption peak ∼785 nm) with NaYF₄:Er³⁺ nanoparticles. These pairings produce visibly distinct emission spectra (green and red, respectively) when excited at different infrared wavelengths, enabling colorimetric identification of infrared bands.  

Third, the invention provides optimized synthetic protocols for hexagonal-phase NaYF₄ nanocrystals with controlled size (15-25 nm) and dopant concentrations (Yb³⁺ 20-25%, Er³⁺ 15%, Ho³⁺ 2%, Tm³⁺ 0.3%). The synthesis employs oleic acid/octadecene solvent systems with precise temperature control (300-324°C) to ensure high crystallinity while preventing Ostwald ripening beyond target dimensions.  

The resulting nanocomposites demonstrate greater than 10-fold enhancement in both spectral bandwidth (∼100 nm vs <20 nm) and monochromatic upconversion efficiency compared to direct atomic excitation. The visible emission remains stable under continuous illumination at power densities of 1-60 W/cm², with characteristic two-photon power dependence confirming the upconversion mechanism.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

### X=—NH₂, —CO₂M′, —SH, —OH,  

The surface functional groups denoted by X represent the reactive moieties on the infrared-absorbing dye molecules that enable covalent attachment to the amine-functionalized nanoparticles. Preferred embodiments utilize dyes containing carboxylic acid (—CO₂H), NHS ester (—CO₂NHS), or isothiocyanate (—N=C=S) groups, which form stable amide bonds with surface amines under mild reaction conditions. Alternative embodiments may employ thiol (—SH) or hydroxyl (—OH) groups for attachment through thioether or ether linkages, respectively. The selection of X is coordinated with the nanoparticle functionalization strategy to maintain optimal 2-6 nm separation for efficient FRET.  

### M=H₂, Cu, Co, CoR₃, Ni, SiR₃R₄, AlR₃, Zn, Fe, FeR₃, Mn  

The metal centers denoted by M represent potential lanthanide dopants and charge-balancing species in the NaYF₄ host lattice. Primary embodiments utilize Yb³⁺ as sensitizer paired with Er³⁺, Ho³⁺, or Tm³⁺ as activators. The invention also encompasses systems where Er³⁺ serves as both sensitizer and activator. Charge compensation may be achieved through incorporation of divalent cations (Cu²⁺, Co²⁺, Ni²⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Mn²⁺) or through formation of complex anions with organic ligands (CoR₃, FeR₃). Silicon- and aluminum-based moieties (SiR₃R₄, AlR₃) may be incorporated as surface modifiers during silica encapsulation.  

### R₄=—R₃, —Cl, —CH₃, —H, -Ph, —OCH₃, —OCH₂CH₃  

The substituents denoted by R₄ represent organic ligands that may be attached to metal centers or silicon atoms during functionalization. Methyl (—CH₃), phenyl (-Ph), and methoxy (—OCH₃) groups are particularly useful for tuning solubility and preventing nanoparticle aggregation. Ethoxy (—OCH₂CH₃) groups may be employed in siloxane precursors for controlled hydrolysis rates during silica encapsulation. Chloride (—Cl) serves as a labile ligand in certain metal complexes, facilitating subsequent substitution reactions.  

### M′=K, Li, Na, H  

The counterions denoted by M′ balance the charge on dye molecules containing carboxylate groups (—CO₂M′). Sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺) salts are preferred for aqueous processing, while lithium (Li⁺) salts may be used in nonpolar solvents. Protonated carboxylic acids (—CO₂H) are employed when covalent amide bond formation with surface amines is desired.  

## Synthesis of Lanthanide-Doped NaYF₄ Nanocrystals  

The synthesis of lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals proceeds through a modified hot oil method employing Schlenk-line techniques. In a representative embodiment, stoichiometric quantities of acetate salts (Na⁺, Y³⁺, and lanthanide dopants) are converted to oleate complexes by heating at 120°C under vacuum in octadecene/oleic acid solvent. Ammonium fluoride (NH₄F) is added as fluorine source, generating amorphous NaYF₄ that subsequently crystallizes into the hexagonal phase upon heating to 300-324°C for 20-100 minutes.  

Critical parameters include:  
- Oleic acid to lanthanide molar ratio of 1:1.2 for optimal shape control  
- Reaction temperature between 315-324°C to ensure complete hexagonal phase formation  
- Precise dopant concentrations (Yb³⁺ 20-25%, Er³⁺ 15%, Ho³⁺ 2%, Tm³⁺ 0.3%) to balance energy transfer and minimize self-absorption  

The resulting nanocrystals exhibit well-defined hexagonal morphology with dimensions of 25±5 nm (long axis) and 15±5 nm (short axis), as confirmed by high-resolution TEM. X-ray diffraction confirms pure hexagonal phase formation, with lattice parameters matching JCPDS reference patterns.  

## Encapsulation of Attached Dye  

Surface functionalization proceeds through a modified reverse microemulsion technique. Oleic acid-capped nanoparticles are dispersed in cyclohexane with Igepal CO-520 surfactant, followed by addition of ammonium hydroxide to form a reverse microemulsion. Amine-functionalized siloxane monomers (3-aminopropyltrimethoxysilane) are added to create a thin (∼2 nm) silica shell with surface amines.  

Key innovations include:  
- Microemulsion size tailored to encapsulate single nanoparticles  
- Use of secondary/tertiary amine silanes (e.g., 3-(methylamino)propyltrimethoxysilane) for dye compatibility  
- Controlled hydrolysis conditions preventing dye degradation  

FTIR spectroscopy confirms successful silica encapsulation through disappearance of oleic acid C=O stretch (1710 cm⁻¹) and appearance of Si-O-Si stretches (1100 cm⁻¹). TEM shows uniform encapsulation without particle aggregation.  

## Attachment of Dye to Encapsulated Nanoparticle  

Dye attachment is achieved by reacting amine-functionalized nanoparticles with infrared dyes containing NHS ester, isothiocyanate, or carboxylic acid groups. Representative conditions employ:  
- 100 mg nanoparticles dispersed in 10 mL ethanol  
- 300 μL of 5 mM dye solution (∼200:1 dye:nanoparticle ratio)  
- Reaction at 25-35°C for 20 min (NHS esters) to 2 hr (carboxylic acids)  

Excess dye is removed by ethanol washing until eluent is colorless. Optical absorption measurements indicate saturation at ∼60 dye molecules per 25 nm nanoparticle. Dyes exhibiting particularly effective performance include:  
- DY-831 NHS ester (absorption 700-900 nm, emission to 975 nm)  
- DyL800 NHS ester (absorption 700-850 nm)  
- NIR-797 isothiocyanate (absorption 700-850 nm)  

## Fabrication of Films  

The dye-nanoparticle composites may be incorporated into polymer films through solution processing. A representative protocol involves:  
1. Dispersing functionalized nanoparticles in ethanol or toluene  
2. Mixing with polymer solution (e.g., PMMA in toluene)  
3. Casting onto substrates using spin-coating or doctor-blading  
4. Drying at 60-80°C to form uniform films  

Alternative embodiments employ layer-by-layer assembly with polyelectrolytes or incorporation into sol-gel matrices. The films maintain upconversion efficiency while providing mechanical stability and environmental protection. Potential applications include:  
- Infrared visualization coatings for security/surveillance  
- Spectral-converting layers for solar cells  
- Laser threat warning sensors
