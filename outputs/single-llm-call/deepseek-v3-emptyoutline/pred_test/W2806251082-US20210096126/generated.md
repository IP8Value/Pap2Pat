# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND  

Radio frequency identification (RFID) technology has become increasingly prevalent in various industries due to its ability to provide wireless identification and tracking capabilities. While RFID has been widely adopted in sectors such as transportation and logistics, its application in biomedical research and healthcare remains limited. Existing methods for tracking biological specimens, such as barcoding or manual labeling, are inefficient and prone to errors, particularly when handling large numbers of samples in high-throughput screening environments.  

In the field of organoid research, three-dimensional (3D) tissue cultures derived from stem cells have emerged as powerful tools for disease modeling, drug discovery, and personalized medicine. However, current organoid tracking methods rely on external labeling or separate documentation, which can lead to sample misidentification and data inconsistencies. There is a critical need for an integrated system that enables reliable, automated identification of organoids while preserving their native structure and function.  

Prior attempts to incorporate RFID technology into biological systems have faced challenges related to biocompatibility, tissue integration, and functional interference. Traditional RFID implantation methods often disrupt tissue integrity or impair cellular functions, limiting their utility in sensitive biological applications. Furthermore, existing RFID solutions lack the miniaturization required for seamless integration into microscale organoid structures without compromising their viability or performance.  

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a novel system and method for integrating ultracompact RFID microchips, referred to herein as "O-Chips," into organoid structures to enable digital tracking and identification without impairing the organoids' native biological functions. The invention leverages the self-assembling properties of organoids to naturally encapsulate the O-Chip during tissue formation, ensuring minimal disruption to cellular architecture and physiological processes.  

The O-Chip is a miniature RFID device with dimensions suitable for incorporation into organoids, typically ranging between 10 and 600 micrometers. Each O-Chip contains a memory storage area capable of storing identification data, such as an Electronic Product Code (EPC), which can be wirelessly read by an external RFID reader. The system operates without an internal power source, instead harvesting energy from the reader's radiofrequency signals to transmit stored information over short distances (e.g., 1–2 mm).  

A key innovation of this invention is the method for integrating the O-Chip into organoids during their formation. By introducing the O-Chip during the aggregation phase of organoid development, the self-assembling tissue naturally surrounds and encapsulates the microchip, resulting in RFID-integrated organoids (RiOs). These RiOs maintain full functionality, including protein secretion, metabolic activity, and response to pharmacological stimuli, while enabling automated identification and tracking.  

The invention further includes a specialized detection system for simultaneously reading RFID data and capturing fluorescence imaging of RiOs in flow-based assays. This integrated platform allows for high-throughput phenotypic screening of pooled organoid samples from multiple donors, with automated identification of individual specimens based on their unique RFID signatures.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION  

### Definitions  

For purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings set forth below:  

- **Organoid**: A three-dimensional tissue structure derived from stem cells that self-organizes to recapitulate key aspects of organ architecture and function in vitro.  
- **O-Chip**: An ultracompact radio frequency identification (RFID) microchip specifically designed for integration into organoid structures, typically measuring between 10 and 600 micrometers in size.  
- **RiO (RFID-integrated organoid)**: An organoid that has incorporated an O-Chip during its formation process, enabling wireless identification while maintaining native biological functions.  
- **Self-assembly**: The process by which dissociated cells spontaneously reorganize into three-dimensional structures with proper tissue architecture and polarity.  
- **Forward cellomics**: An analytical approach wherein phenotypic variations are first identified in pooled organoid samples, followed by determination of their genetic or cellular basis through RFID-based identification.  

### Examples  

**Example 1: Production of RFID-Integrated Organoids (RiOs)**  

The process for generating RiOs begins with the differentiation of human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) into posterior foregut organoids through sequential exposure to Activin and FGF4/CHIR 99021. At day 6 of differentiation, the foregut organoids are dissociated into single cells and seeded into multi-well plates. O-Chips are then introduced into the cell suspension at a density optimized for single-chip incorporation per organoid.  

As the cells re-aggregate, they naturally encapsulate the O-Chips during tissue formation. The developing organoids are treated with a laminin-rich basement membrane matrix to induce proper polarization and cavitation. After 3 days in culture, mature RiOs are formed, with each organoid completely encompassing one O-Chip. This method achieves incorporation efficiencies exceeding 95%, with no observable morphological differences between RiOs and conventional organoids.  

**Example 2: Functional Validation of RiOs**  

Comprehensive functional analyses demonstrate that RiOs maintain full biological activity comparable to non-chipped control organoids. Gene expression profiling reveals equivalent levels of liver-specific markers (ALB, AFP, AAT) between RiOs and controls. Protein secretion assays show identical albumin production rates (152.9 ng/mL/24h in RiOs vs. control organoids).  

Functional capacity is further confirmed through:  
1) Bile acid transport assays using fluorescent substrates (CLF and rhodamine 123), showing normal uptake and lumenal accumulation in RiOs.  
2) Lipid metabolism studies demonstrating equivalent fat accumulation patterns in response to fatty acid treatment.  
3) Iron handling tests revealing normal metal ion processing capabilities.  

**Example 3: Durability and Cryopreservation**  

The O-Chips demonstrate remarkable durability under various laboratory conditions:  
- Temperature tolerance from 4°C to -196°C (liquid nitrogen)  
- Resistance to pH variations typical of cell culture media  
- Compatibility with standard sterilization methods (autoclaving)  
- Stability in organic solvents used for tissue processing  

RiOs maintain viability and function after cryopreservation using slow-freezing methods with DMSO-based cryoprotectants. Post-thaw recovery rates exceed 90%, with preserved:  
1) Morphological integrity  
2) Protein secretion capacity  
3) Metabolic functions (lipid accumulation, bile acid transport)  

**Example 4: High-Throughput Screening System**  

A specialized flow-based detection system enables simultaneous RFID reading and fluorescence imaging of RiOs. The device comprises:  
- A microliter-scale syringe pump for controlled sample flow  
- An optical path with integrated fluorescence microscopy  
- An RFID reader antenna positioned adjacent to the flow channel  

In operation, RiOs are pumped through the detection chamber where:  
1) Each O-Chip's EPC is wirelessly recorded  
2) Fluorescence images are captured for phenotypic analysis  
3) Data is automatically correlated for each identified organoid  

This system facilitates pooled screening of multiple donor-derived RiOs, as demonstrated in a steatosis assay where Wolman disease organoids were automatically identified by their distinctive lipid accumulation patterns.  

**Example 5: Forward Cellomics Application**  

The invention enables a novel "forward cellomics" approach for population-scale organoid studies:  
1) Generate RiOs from multiple donor iPSC lines (including disease models)  
2) Pool RiOs in culture and subject to phenotypic assays  
3) Automatically identify outliers via combined RFID/imaging  
4) Retrieve genomic data for phenotype-genotype correlation  

This method was successfully applied to identify Wolman disease organoids in a mixed population based on their pathological lipid accumulation, demonstrating the system's potential for efficient, large-scale phenotypic screening.
