# DESCRIPTION  

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION  

Malaria remains one of the most devastating infectious diseases worldwide, with Plasmodium falciparum causing an estimated 216 million clinical cases and 655,000 deaths annually. The development of an effective malaria vaccine has been challenging despite evidence that durable sterile immunity can be induced in humans through exposure to irradiated or drug-attenuated sporozoites. These whole parasite vaccination approaches appear to rely primarily on cell-mediated immunity (CMI), particularly cytotoxic CD8+ T cells that target infected hepatocytes. However, logistical challenges associated with whole parasite vaccines have driven efforts to develop subunit vaccines that can elicit similar protective immune responses.  

The most advanced subunit vaccine candidate, RTS,S, contains the circumsporozoite protein (CSP) fused to hepatitis B surface antigen and provides approximately 50% protection by inducing antibody responses against sporozoites. However, RTS,S does not effectively induce CD8+ T cell responses needed to target the intracellular liver stages of malaria. Gene-based vaccine technologies offer a promising alternative for inducing protective CD8+ T cell responses against intracellular pathogens. While widely used in veterinary medicine, gene-based vaccines have seen limited application in humans, with only one adenovirus-vectored vaccine currently licensed.  

There exists an unmet need for a malaria vaccine that can effectively induce both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses, particularly CD8+ T cell responses capable of targeting the liver stages of Plasmodium infection. The present invention addresses this need through a novel heterologous prime-boost vaccination strategy combining DNA plasmid priming with adenoviral vector boosting, designed to maximize induction of protective cell-mediated immunity while maintaining safety and tolerability.  

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a novel heterologous prime-boost malaria vaccine regimen comprising: (a) a priming component consisting of two DNA plasmids encoding Plasmodium falciparum circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1), and (b) a boosting component consisting of two non-replicating recombinant human serotype 5 adenovirus vectors encoding the same antigens.  

The DNA plasmid component contains codon-optimized genes for P. falciparum 3D7 strain CSP and AMA1 inserted into plasmid VR1020 under control of the human cytomegalovirus immediate early (CMV IE) gene promoter/enhancer. The CSP gene is modified by deletion of 16 central repeat sequences and addition of a human tissue plasminogen activator (TPA) signal sequence at the N-terminus and a bovine growth hormone transcriptional terminator segment at the C-terminus. The AMA1 gene expresses the ectodomain with replacement of the native signal sequence by a TPA signal sequence.  

The adenovirus vector component contains the same codon-optimized CSP and AMA1 genes inserted into the E1 region of adenovirus serotype 5 under control of a modified human CMV promoter. The CSP construct lacks the TPA signal sequence while the AMA1 construct includes full-length protein with native signal sequence, transmembrane domain, and cytoplasmic domain. The adenovirus vectors are replication-deficient due to deletion of E1 and E4 regions and part of E3 region.  

The vaccine regimen is administered as three priming intramuscular injections of the DNA plasmid mixture at weeks 0, 4, and 8 followed by a single boosting intramuscular injection of the adenovirus vector mixture at week 24. This immunization schedule induces potent cell-mediated immune responses, particularly CD8+ T cell responses against both CSP and AMA1, providing sterile protection against P. falciparum malaria in a significant proportion of vaccine recipients.  

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION  

The present invention provides a novel heterologous prime-boost vaccination strategy for inducing protective immunity against Plasmodium falciparum malaria. The vaccine regimen combines the advantages of DNA plasmid immunization for priming immune responses with adenoviral vector immunization for robust boosting of cell-mediated immunity, particularly CD8+ T cell responses against liver-stage parasites.  

The DNA plasmid priming component comprises two separate plasmids, each containing one malaria antigen gene optimized for expression in mammalian cells. The CSP gene is derived from P. falciparum 3D7 strain (GenBank X15363) and modified to enhance expression and immunogenicity. Specifically, 16 of the central repeat sequences (64 amino acids) are deleted to focus immune responses on conserved protective epitopes. The native signal sequence is supplemented with a human tissue plasminogen activator (TPA) signal sequence at the N-terminus to enhance secretion and processing in mammalian cells. A 23 amino acid segment from the transcriptional terminator of bovine growth hormone is added at the C-terminus to stabilize mRNA and increase protein expression.  

The AMA1 gene is derived from P. falciparum 3D7 strain (GenBank XM1347979) and expresses the ectodomain, which contains the major protective epitopes. The native signal sequence is replaced with a TPA signal sequence to enhance expression. Both genes are codon-optimized for high expression in human cells while maintaining the authentic amino acid sequence of the parasite proteins.  

The adenovirus vector boosting component uses the same antigen genes but in different configurations optimized for viral vector expression. The CSP construct maintains the codon-optimized sequence and central repeat deletion but lacks the TPA signal sequence, relying instead on the native signal sequence for proper processing. The AMA1 construct includes the full-length protein with native signal sequence, transmembrane domain, and cytoplasmic domain to ensure proper localization and presentation of protective epitopes.  

The adenovirus vectors are based on human serotype 5 (Ad5) with deletions in the E1 and E4 regions required for replication, making them replication-deficient and safe for human use. The vectors also have partial deletion of the E3 region to accommodate the malaria antigen genes. Expression is driven by a modified human cytomegalovirus immediate early (CMV IE) promoter that provides strong transgene expression in human cells.  

The vaccination regimen consists of three priming doses of the DNA plasmid mixture administered intramuscularly at weeks 0, 4, and 8, followed by a single boosting dose of the adenovirus vector mixture at week 24. Each DNA dose contains 2 mg total plasmid DNA (1 mg each construct) delivered by needle-free jet injection to enhance cellular uptake. The adenovirus boost contains 2×10^10 particle units (1×10^10 pu each construct) delivered by standard intramuscular injection.  

This prime-boost strategy takes advantage of the unique immunological properties of each platform. The DNA priming induces antigen-specific T cell responses that are expanded and maintained by the adenovirus boost. The adenovirus vectors are particularly effective at inducing strong CD8+ T cell responses due to their ability to infect antigen-presenting cells and mediate endogenous antigen processing and presentation via MHC class I.  

The combination of CSP and AMA1 provides complementary targeting of different parasite stages and multiple epitopes for immune recognition. CSP is expressed on sporozoites and early liver stages, while AMA1 is expressed during hepatocyte invasion and liver stage development. This multi-antigen approach increases the likelihood of immune recognition across diverse HLA types and provides multiple targets for protective immune responses.  

Immunological monitoring demonstrates that the regimen induces both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses, with protection correlating most strongly with CD8+ T cell responses to AMA1. These immune responses are characterized by production of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), a key cytokine for anti-malarial immunity. The vaccine shows an excellent safety profile, with primarily mild local reactions and no vaccine-related serious adverse events.  

### Example 1  

A Phase 1 clinical trial was conducted to evaluate the safety and efficacy of the heterologous prime-boost malaria vaccine regimen. Twenty healthy, malaria-naïve adults received three doses of the DNA plasmid mixture at weeks 0, 4, and 8 followed by one dose of the adenovirus vector mixture at week 24. The vaccines were administered by intramuscular injection, with DNA delivered by needle-free jet injection and adenovirus vector by standard needle injection.  

Fifteen fully immunized volunteers and six unimmunized controls underwent controlled human malaria infection at week 28 via bites from five P. falciparum-infected mosquitoes. Four of 15 vaccinated volunteers (27%) showed sterile protection with no detectable parasitemia by quantitative PCR or blood smear through 28 days post-challenge. In contrast, all six control volunteers developed parasitemia detected by day 7-8 by PCR and day 11-16 by blood smear.  

Immunological analysis revealed that protection correlated strongly with IFN-γ ELISpot responses to AMA1 (p=0.019) and CD8+ T cell responses to AMA1 measured by intracellular cytokine staining (p=0.0492). Protected volunteers showed high frequencies of AMA1-specific CD8+ T cells producing IFN-γ (0.18-0.38% of CD8+ T cells). CSP-specific responses also contributed to protection in some volunteers, though the association was not statistically significant at the group level.  

Antibody responses to both antigens were low and showed no association with protection, confirming that the vaccine's protective mechanism is primarily cell-mediated. The regimen was well tolerated, with mostly mild local reactions and no vaccine-related serious adverse events.  

### Example 2  

Analysis of T cell responses in protected volunteers revealed distinct patterns of antigen recognition. One protected volunteer developed CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses to both CSP and AMA1, while another showed CD8+ T cell responses to both antigens. A third protected volunteer developed CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses specific for AMA1 only, and the fourth showed CD4+ T cell responses to AMA1 with high IL-2 production.  

These findings demonstrate that the vaccine can induce protection through multiple mechanisms of cell-mediated immunity, with CD8+ T cell responses playing a predominant role. The diversity of protective immune responses across volunteers suggests that the multi-antigen approach provides redundancy that increases the likelihood of protection across individuals with different HLA types.  

Pre-existing immunity to adenovirus serotype 5 did not prevent protection in volunteers with low neutralizing antibody titers (<500), though higher titers may reduce vaccine efficacy. This suggests that the vaccine would be most effective in populations with low pre-existing adenovirus immunity, such as young children in malaria-endemic areas.  

### Example 3  

Further characterization of immune responses revealed that protected volunteers typically developed multifunctional T cell responses, with some showing IFN-γ+IL-2+TNF-α+ CD4+ T cells and others showing predominantly IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells. The CD8+ T cell responses in protected volunteers were largely single cytokine producers (IFN-γ+ only), which may represent terminally differentiated effector cells capable of immediate parasite control.  

The vaccine induced memory T cell responses that persisted through the challenge period, as evidenced by strong recall responses post-challenge in protected volunteers. Geometric mean ELISpot responses to AMA1 increased from 154 spot forming cells/million PBMC at baseline to 348 at the time of challenge, with the highest responses in protected volunteers (810-1270 sfc/m).  

These results demonstrate that the heterologous prime-boost regimen induces durable, high-quality T cell responses capable of providing sterile protection against P. falciparum malaria. The combination of DNA priming and adenovirus boosting proves particularly effective for generating the cell-mediated immunity needed to target liver-stage parasites.
