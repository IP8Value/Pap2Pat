# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of extracellular vesicle (EV) research and its biomedical applications, particularly in diagnostics, monitoring, and therapeutics. More specifically, the invention provides a novel class of recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) that serve as biological reference materials for the calibration, normalization, quality control, and performance assessment of EV isolation and quantification methodologies. These rEV are engineered to closely mimic the physical, biochemical, and biophysical properties of endogenous EV derived from diverse biological sources such as plasma, urine, and cell culture supernatants, while simultaneously incorporating exogenous molecular tags—such as enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) fused to the HIV-1 gag polyprotein and EGFP mRNA—that enable their unambiguous detection, quantification, and differentiation from native sample EV. The invention further encompasses methods for producing, stabilizing, storing, and utilizing these rEV in a wide array of analytical and clinical contexts, including but not limited to nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), high-resolution flow cytometry (HR-FC), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), and western blotting. Additionally, the invention includes modified forms of rEV, such as post-insertion PEGylated rEV (rEV-PEG), which exhibit enhanced commutability across complex biofluids like plasma by resisting non-specific protein corona formation and enabling selective segregation via anti-PEG immunoaffinity capture. The technical scope thus spans biotechnology, molecular biology, nanomedicine, clinical diagnostics, and analytical instrumentation, with direct applicability to standardizing EV-based assays in both research laboratories and regulated diagnostic environments.

## BACKGROUND

Extracellular vesicles (EV) are nano- to micro-sized lipid bilayer-enclosed particles naturally released by virtually all cell types into biofluids such as blood, urine, saliva, and cerebrospinal fluid. They play critical roles in intercellular communication by transporting proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and metabolites between cells, thereby influencing physiological processes and contributing to the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, and autoimmune conditions. Consequently, EV have emerged as promising biomarkers for non-invasive disease diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic monitoring. However, the translation of EV research into robust clinical applications has been significantly hindered by substantial methodological heterogeneity in EV isolation, characterization, and quantification. A major challenge lies in the absence of universally accepted reference materials that accurately reflect the intrinsic properties of native EV while allowing precise tracking and normalization across experimental platforms.

Current reference standards used in EV research predominantly consist of synthetic nanoparticles such as polystyrene or silica beads. While these materials offer reproducibility and ease of use, they fundamentally differ from biological EV in key physical and biochemical attributes. For instance, polystyrene beads possess a refractive index (RI) of approximately 1.61, and silica beads an RI of about 1.45, whereas native EV exhibit an RI of ~1.37–1.39 due to their lipid-rich composition and internal cargo. This discrepancy leads to significant inaccuracies in light-scattering–based detection methods like NTA and flow cytometry, where particle size and concentration estimates are highly dependent on RI. As a result, the use of non-biological beads systematically underestimates the true size and abundance of EV in biological samples. Moreover, synthetic beads lack surface markers (e.g., tetraspanins CD9, CD63, CD81), membrane asymmetry, zeta potential, and molecular cargo characteristic of EV, rendering them unsuitable for evaluating the specificity and efficiency of immunoaffinity-based isolation techniques or for calibrating instruments that rely on biological recognition events.

Biological reference materials derived directly from cell cultures or human biofluids have also been explored. However, these suffer from batch-to-batch variability, undefined composition, and an inability to be distinguished from endogenous EV in spiked recovery experiments. Without a unique molecular signature, such materials cannot serve as internal controls for normalization or for assessing the recovery efficiency of EV isolation protocols. Recent efforts have attempted to engineer fluorescent EV by overexpressing membrane-targeted fluorescent proteins (e.g., palm-GFP) or by labeling EV with lipophilic dyes. Yet, these approaches yield low labeling efficiencies—often less than 15% of particles are fluorescent—as confirmed by fluorescent NTA—and introduce artifacts due to dye aggregation, photobleaching, or perturbation of native EV structure and function. Furthermore, fusion proteins such as tetraspanin-EGFP may alter EV biogenesis or trafficking, compromising their representativeness as reference standards.

The ideal EV reference material must satisfy three essential criteria: (1) it must faithfully recapitulate the physical (size, density, morphology, RI, zeta potential) and biochemical (lipid and protein composition, presence of canonical EV markers) characteristics of endogenous EV; (2) it must be intrinsically trackable through a stable, quantifiable, and non-interfering signal that allows clear discrimination from sample EV; and (3) it must demonstrate commutability—i.e., behave identically to native EV under diverse experimental conditions, including exposure to complex biofluids and various isolation methodologies such as ultracentrifugation, size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), and immunoaffinity capture. To date, no existing reference material fulfills all these requirements simultaneously.

The present invention addresses this critical gap by leveraging the natural EV-mimicking properties of virus-like particles (VLPs) generated through the expression of the HIV-1 gag polyprotein. The gag protein is known to hijack the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) pathway—the same machinery involved in the biogenesis of many EV subtypes—and autonomously assembles at the plasma membrane into immature VLPs that bud outward and are released into the extracellular space. These particles are enveloped by a host-derived lipid bilayer and incorporate ESCRT-associated and tetraspanin-enriched membrane microdomains, resulting in structures that are remarkably similar to EV in size, density, morphology, and molecular composition. By fusing gag to EGFP, the inventors have created recombinant EV (rEV) that not only retain these EV-like traits but also carry a built-in fluorescent and immunodetectable tag, along with encapsulated EGFP mRNA, enabling multi-modal detection and absolute quantification. Critically, because the gag-EGFP construct lacks viral genomic RNA, proteases, and envelope glycoproteins, the resulting rEV are non-infectious and biosafe. This platform thus provides the first truly biological, trackable, and commutable reference material suitable for comprehensive quality assurance in EV research and clinical diagnostics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) that serve as a biological reference material for extracellular vesicle (EV) research and applications. These rEV are produced by transient transfection of mammalian cells, preferably HEK293T cells, with a nucleic acid construct encoding a fusion protein comprising the HIV-1 gag polyprotein and an exogenous reporter moiety, such as enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP). The gag polyprotein alone is sufficient to drive the assembly and release of virus-like particles (VLPs) that bud from the plasma membrane via the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) pathway, resulting in nanoparticles enveloped by a host-derived lipid bilayer. The fusion of EGFP to gag ensures that the resulting rEV are intrinsically fluorescent, contain a detectable gag-EGFP fusion protein, and encapsulate EGFP mRNA, thereby enabling their unambiguous identification, quantification, and differentiation from endogenous EV present in biological samples.

The production of rEV involves culturing HEK293T cells to 70–80% confluency in multi-flask systems, followed by transient transfection using a cationic polymer such as linear polyethyleneimine (PEI) at a PEI:DNA ratio of 5:1. The DNA construct used is typically pMET7-gag-EGFP, which expresses the full-length HIV-1 gag polyprotein C-terminally fused to EGFP. After 48 hours, cells are washed and incubated in serum-free medium (e.g., Opti-MEM) for an additional 24 hours to collect conditioned medium (CM). The CM is then subjected to sequential clarification steps: low-speed centrifugation at 200 × g to remove detached cells, followed by filtration through 0.45 µm and 0.2 µm cellulose acetate filters to eliminate larger debris and aggregates. The clarified CM is concentrated approximately 300-fold using centrifugal filter devices with a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff, yielding concentrated conditioned medium (CCM).

Purification of rEV from CCM is achieved through OptiPrep density gradient (ODG) ultracentrifugation. A discontinuous iodixanol gradient is prepared by layering 4 mL each of 40%, 20%, and 10% iodixanol solutions, and 3.5 mL of 5% iodixanol in an open-top ultracentrifuge tube. One milliliter of CCM is overlaid on top and centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 18 hours at 4°C. Following centrifugation, 1 mL fractions are collected from the top. Fractions corresponding to buoyant densities of 1.086–1.119 g/mL (typically fractions 8–10) are pooled, diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 3 hours. The resulting pellet is resuspended in PBS and stored in aliquots at −80°C. This protocol yields approximately 5 × 10¹¹ rEV per harvest from ~3 × 10⁹ transfected cells.

The rEV produced by this method exhibit physical and biochemical characteristics that are indistinguishable from endogenous EV isolated from diverse sources, including plasma, urine, and conditioned media from breast cancer cell lines (MCF7, 4T1) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF). Specifically, rEV display a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 108.6 ± 9.5 nm as measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), a zeta potential of −32.3 ± 1.6 mV, a refractive index of ~1.37 calculated via Mie theory, and a cup-shaped morphology consistent with EV as visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Their buoyant density, determined by ODG centrifugation, falls within the canonical EV range of 1.086–1.119 g/mL. Proteomic analysis by mass spectrometry reveals that rEV contain canonical EV-associated proteins such as ALIX, TSG101, flotillin-1, syntenin-1, CD9, CD81, and CD63, with 89% of the total proteome overlapping with that of mock-transfected HEK293T-derived EV. Lipidomic profiling confirms the presence of EV-characteristic lipid classes—including phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, sphingomyelin, and lysophospholipids—with a strong positive correlation (Spearman r = 0.921) in lipid composition between rEV and control EV. Cholesterol content per particle is also equivalent across rEV and sample EV.

Detection and quantification of rEV can be performed using multiple orthogonal methodologies that exploit their unique molecular features. Fluorescence-based methods include fluorescent NTA (fNTA) and fluorescence-triggered high-resolution flow cytometry (HR-FC), both of which directly count fluorescent rEV with high sensitivity and linearity. Indirect quantification is enabled by: (1) microplate reader assays measuring EGFP fluorescence intensity in the range of 5 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10¹⁰ rEV; (2) ELISA targeting the p24 capsid domain of the gag polyprotein, with a linear range of 1 × 10⁶ to 1 × 10⁷ rEV; (3) western blot densitometry of gag-EGFP bands, detectable down to 7 × 10⁷ rEV with linearity between 4 × 10⁸ and 1 × 10⁹ rEV; and (4) RT-qPCR amplification of encapsulated EGFP mRNA, which exhibits a semi-logarithmic relationship with rEV number over 1 × 10⁷ to 1 × 10⁹ rEV. These diverse detection modalities ensure compatibility with routine laboratory equipment and facilitate cross-platform validation.

To enhance purity and achieve near-complete fluorescence labeling, an alternative purification strategy using OptiPrep velocity gradient (OVG) centrifugation can be employed. Unlike equilibrium-based ODG, OVG separates particles based on size and sedimentation rate, effectively segregating gag-EGFP-positive rEV (which sediment faster due to higher density) from co-released gag-negative EV derived from endogenous biogenesis. This results in rEV preparations with >99% fluorescent particles, as confirmed by fNTA and immune-electron microscopy.

The rEV of the invention demonstrate exceptional stability under various storage conditions. Long-term storage at −80°C for up to 12 months, short-term storage at 4°C for 7 days, and repeated freeze-thaw cycles do not alter their density, size distribution, fluorescence intensity, or membrane integrity, as verified by ODG, fNTA, TEM, and CD81 immunoprecipitation. Furthermore, rEV can be lyophilized in the presence of 5% trehalose without loss of structural or functional properties; omission of trehalose leads to particle aggregation and membrane disruption, underscoring the necessity of this cryoprotectant.

A key innovation of the invention is the demonstration that rEV are commutable—they behave identically to native EV when spiked into complex biofluids such as plasma, urine, and cell culture medium. When subjected to common EV isolation techniques—including size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), immunoaffinity capture against CD81 or CD63, and ODG centrifugation—rEV co-elute or co-isolate with endogenous EV, validating their use as recovery standards. Notably, in plasma, unmodified rEV undergo a reversible density shift to 1.141–1.215 g/mL due to binding of immunoglobulins (IgG/IgM), which can be abrogated by proteinase K treatment or prevented by post-insertion PEGylation. The latter involves incubating rEV with 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-conjugated polyethylene glycol (DMPE-PEG), which inserts into the rEV membrane and creates a steric shield against protein corona formation. The resulting rEV-PEG maintain EV-like density in plasma, exhibit only a modest 10 nm increase in size, and can be selectively captured using anti-PEG antibodies, enabling their complete segregation from sample EV after normalization.

The utility of rEV extends to performance benchmarking of EV isolation methods. By spiking a known quantity of rEV (e.g., 5 × 10¹⁰) into plasma and applying different isolation protocols, recovery efficiencies can be precisely calculated. For instance, SEC achieves ~100% recovery, ODG centrifugation ~30%, and differential ultracentrifugation (dUC) only ~10%, highlighting the superior yield of SEC despite its known limitations in resolving EV from lipoproteins. These metrics allow researchers to select optimal methods for specific applications and to correct raw EV counts for method-specific losses. Moreover, rEV mitigate inter-user and intra-assay variability: in a controlled experiment simulating pipetting errors, normalization to rEV recovery reduced the coefficient of variation (CV) in EV enumeration from 17.5% to 5.9%. Similarly, rEV enable accurate comparison of EV concentrations across patient cohorts. In a clinical study, absolute NTA counts of plasma EV from breast cancer patients versus healthy controls showed no significant difference (p = 0.141), but rEV-normalized counts revealed a 2.5-fold increase in cancer patients (p = 0.0002), demonstrating enhanced diagnostic sensitivity.

The invention thus provides a comprehensive toolkit for standardizing EV research: rEV serve as calibrators for instrument settings, internal controls for assay validation, recovery standards for method optimization, and normalization references for cross-study comparisons. Their modular design also permits customization—for example, by replacing EGFP with other reporters or therapeutic cargos—opening avenues for tailored diagnostic and therapeutic applications.

### EXAMPLES

#### Example 1: Recombinant Extracellular Vesicles (rEV) Share Physical and Biochemical Characteristics with Endogenous Sample Extracellular Vesicles (Sample EV)

Recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) were generated by transient transfection of HEK293T cells with a plasmid encoding the HIV-1 gag polyprotein C-terminally fused to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP). The gag-EGFP fusion protein was designed to exploit the natural ability of the gag polyprotein to hijack the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) pathway, which is also utilized in the biogenesis of endogenous extracellular vesicles (EV). Upon expression, the gag-EGFP protein localized to plasma membrane microdomains enriched in tetraspanins such as CD9, CD63, and CD81 via its N-terminal myristoylated matrix (MA) domain. Subsequent oligomerization of gag-EGFP recruited ESCRT-I components, notably TSG101, through a conserved PTAP motif in the p6 domain, leading to the sequential engagement of ESCRT-II and ESCRT-III complexes. This cascade culminated in the outward budding and scission of the plasma membrane, releasing rEV into the conditioned medium (CM). The entire process mimics the canonical pathway of EV biogenesis, ensuring that the resulting rEV inherit key structural and compositional features of native EV.

Following 72 hours of CM collection from approximately 3 × 10⁹ transfected HEK293T cells, rEV were purified using OptiPrep density gradient (ODG) ultracentrifugation. The CM was first concentrated ~300-fold and then layered atop a discontinuous iodixanol gradient composed of 5%, 10%, 20%, and 40% layers. After 18 hours of centrifugation at 100,000 × g, fractions corresponding to buoyant densities of 1.086–1.119 g/mL were pooled and pelleted, yielding ~5 × 10¹¹ rEV per preparation. This density range is identical to that of endogenous EV isolated from diverse biological sources, including human plasma, urine, and conditioned media from breast cancer cell lines (MCF7, 4T1) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF), as confirmed by parallel ODG separations.

Comprehensive physicochemical characterization demonstrated that rEV are virtually indistinguishable from sample EV. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that rEV exhibit a mean hydrodynamic diameter of 108.6 ± 9.5 nm, which did not significantly differ (p > 0.0361, Mann–Whitney test) from the size distributions of EV derived from plasma (112.3 ± 8.7 nm), urine (105.4 ± 10.2 nm), MCF7 (109.8 ± 7.9 nm), 4T1 (110.1 ± 9.1 nm), CAF (107.5 ± 8.4 nm), or mock-transfected HEK293T cells (106.9 ± 9.8 nm). Similarly, zeta potential measurements showed a consistent surface charge of −32.3 ± 1.6 mV across all EV types (p > 0.0357), reflecting comparable membrane lipid composition and protein decoration. The refractive index (RI) of rEV, calculated using Mie theory based on light scattering data from NTA, was determined to be ~1.37, aligning precisely with the RI values obtained for sample EV from all tested sources. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further confirmed that rEV display the characteristic cup-shaped morphology typical of aldehyde-fixed EV, with intact lipid bilayers and uniform size distribution.

At the molecular level, rEV were found to contain a rich repertoire of EV-associated proteins. Western blot analysis detected the presence of canonical markers including ALIX, TSG101, flotillin-1, syntenin-1, CD9, and CD81 in rEV lysates. Immune-electron microscopy using a gold-conjugated secondary antibody against CD63 confirmed the surface exposure of this tetraspanin on rEV membranes. Mass spectrometry-based proteomics, performed on three biological replicates of rEV and mock EV (isolated from HEK293T cells transfected with empty vector), identified a total of 890 proteins common to both preparations. Differential expression analysis revealed that 792 of these proteins (89%) were equally abundant (Spearman correlation coefficient r = 0.7712, p < 0.0001), including key ESCRT components and tetraspanins. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of proteomic profiles confirmed high technical and biological reproducibility of rEV production. Lipidomic analysis similarly demonstrated a strong correlation (Spearman r = 0.921, p < 0.0001) in the abundance of major EV lipid classes—phosphatidylcholine, phosphatidylethanolamine, sphingomyelin, and lysophospholipids—between rEV and mock EV. Cholesterol quantification per particle also showed no significant difference (p > 0.0256) across rEV and sample EV from various sources.

Collectively, these data establish that rEV faithfully recapitulate the defining physical and biochemical attributes of endogenous EV, thereby fulfilling a critical prerequisite for their use as a biological reference material in EV research and diagnostics.

#### Example 2: Identification of rEV Detection Methods

The recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) described herein incorporate multiple intrinsic molecular features that enable their sensitive and specific detection across a range of analytical platforms commonly available in research and clinical laboratories. These features include the fluorescence of the EGFP moiety, the presence of the gag-EGFP fusion protein, and the encapsulation of EGFP mRNA within the rEV lumen. To identify and validate suitable detection methodologies, rEV were analyzed using fluorescence-, protein-, and RNA-based assays, with quantification calibrated against direct particle counting by fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (fNTA).

Fluorescence-based detection proved highly effective for direct rEV enumeration. Using fNTA equipped with a 488 nm laser and a 500 nm longpass emission filter, rEV could be clearly distinguished from non-fluorescent background particles in buffer and biofluid matrices. The percentage of fluorescent particles in ODG-purified rEV preparations averaged 79.7% ± 9.5%, indicating high but incomplete labeling due to co-sedimentation of endogenous gag-negative EV. To achieve near-homogeneous fluorescence, rEV were alternatively purified by OptiPrep velocity gradient (OVG) centrifugation, which segregated gag-EGFP-positive rEV (>99% fluorescent) from lighter, gag-negative EV. Both fNTA and fluorescence-triggered high-resolution flow cytometry (HR-FC) yielded equivalent relative rEV concentrations, confirming cross-platform consistency. HR-FC was performed on a BD Influx cytometer configured with a 200 mW 488 nm laser, a 140 µm nozzle, and optimized fluidic pressures to minimize coincident detection; triggering on EGFP fluorescence ensured specific rEV identification even in complex mixtures.

Indirect quantification methods were developed by establishing calibration curves correlating assay signals to rEV numbers determined by fNTA. A fluorescent microplate reader assay measuring EGFP fluorescence intensity exhibited a linear response over a range of 5 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10¹⁰ rEV per well, with a lower limit of detection defined by background fluorescence from buffer controls. At the protein level, an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) targeting the p24 capsid domain of the gag polyprotein demonstrated linearity between 1 × 10⁶ and 1 × 10⁷ rEV, with a detection limit of ~5 × 10⁵ rEV. Western blot analysis using an anti-EGFP primary antibody allowed visualization of gag-EGFP bands down to 7 × 10⁷ rEV loaded per lane; densitometric quantification of band intensity showed a linear correlation across 4 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10⁹ rEV. At the nucleic acid level, reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) amplification of EGFP mRNA using two independent primer pairs yielded cycle quantification (Cq) values that displayed a semi-logarithmic inverse relationship with rEV input over 1 × 10⁷ to 1 × 10⁹ rEV, consistent with the expected behavior of PCR-based quantification.

All detection methods were validated using at least three biological replicates of rEV, with technical triplicates for each assay condition. The coefficients of variation (CV) for intra-assay repeatability were consistently below 10%: 5.8% for fNTA, 0.8% for RT-qPCR, and 5.7% for p24 ELISA. Importantly, rEV purified by OVG centrifugation remained detectable by all modalities, confirming that the enhanced purity did not compromise assay compatibility. These findings establish a versatile toolkit for rEV quantification, allowing researchers to select the most appropriate method based on available instrumentation, required sensitivity, and experimental context. The multi-modal detectability of rEV ensures robust cross-validation and facilitates their integration into diverse EV workflows, from basic research to clinical assay development.

#### Example 3: Identification of Optimized Storage Conditions of rEV

The stability of recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) under various storage conditions is critical for their utility as a reference material in distributed research and diagnostic settings. To identify optimal storage protocols, rEV were subjected to a series of stability assessments encompassing temperature variations, freeze-thaw cycling, and lyophilization, with structural and functional integrity evaluated using orthogonal analytical techniques.

Long-term storage at −80°C was found to preserve rEV integrity for at least 12 months. Aliquots of rEV stored at this temperature for 6 and 12 months were thawed and analyzed by OptiPrep density gradient (ODG) centrifugation, fluorescent nanoparticle tracking analysis (fNTA), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). No significant changes were observed in buoyant density distribution, particle concentration, size distribution (mean diameter 108.6 ± 9.5 nm vs. 109.1 ± 8.9 nm after 12 months), or fluorescence intensity compared to freshly prepared rEV. Similarly, short-term storage at 4°C for up to 7 days did not affect any of these parameters, indicating suitability for routine laboratory handling over typical experimental timeframes.

Repeated freeze-thaw cycles, a common source of nanoparticle degradation, were also well tolerated. rEV subjected to three consecutive freeze-thaw cycles (freezing at −80°C and thawing on ice) retained their original density profile, particle count, and size distribution as measured by ODG and fNTA. Functional integrity was confirmed by immunoprecipitation using an antibody against the extravesicular loop of CD81, which successfully captured rEV post-thaw with efficiency equivalent to fresh samples, demonstrating preserved membrane orientation and epitope accessibility.

Lyophilization (freeze-drying) was explored as a strategy for ambient-temperature storage and shipping. Initial attempts without cryoprotectants resulted in severe particle aggregation, increased size (up to 200 nm), and membrane disruption as visualized by TEM, accompanied by a 60% loss in recoverable particle number by fNTA. However, inclusion of 5% (w/v) trehalose in the formulation prior to lyophilization completely abrogated these deleterious effects. Lyophilized rEV reconstituted in PBS exhibited identical morphology by TEM, unchanged fluorescence intensity, and particle counts and size distributions indistinguishable from non-lyophilized controls. Density gradient analysis confirmed retention of the canonical 1.086–1.119 g/mL buoyant density. Trehalose concentrations below 5% were insufficient to prevent damage, while higher concentrations offered no additional benefit, establishing 5% as the optimal and minimal requirement.

These findings define a robust storage and handling protocol for rEV: for long-term archival, store at −80°C in single-use aliquots to avoid repeated thawing; for short-term use, maintain at 4°C for up to one week; and for shipping or ambient storage, lyophilize in the presence of 5% trehalose. The exceptional stability of rEV under these conditions ensures batch-to-batch consistency and facilitates their adoption as a standardized reference material across geographically dispersed laboratories.

#### Example 4: The Use of rEV to Estimate the Efficiency of EV Isolation Methods

A major challenge in extracellular vesicle (EV) research is the lack of standardized metrics to evaluate and compare the performance of different EV isolation techniques. The recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) of the present invention address this gap by serving as spike-in recovery standards that enable precise quantification of isolation efficiency. In this application, a known number of rEV (e.g., 5 × 10¹⁰) are spiked into a biological sample such as plasma, urine, or cell culture medium prior to processing by a given isolation method. The number of rEV recovered post-isolation is then quantified using one of the validated detection methods (e.g., fNTA or p24 ELISA), and the recovery efficiency is calculated as the percentage of input rEV detected in the final preparation.

This approach was applied to three commonly used EV isolation methods from human plasma: size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), OptiPrep density gradient (ODG) centrifugation, and differential ultracentrifugation (dUC). Spiked rEV co-eluted with endogenous EV in SEC fractions 4–6, as confirmed by western blot analysis for gag-EGFP, flotillin-1, and TSG101, demonstrating commutability. Quantification by fNTA revealed a recovery efficiency of nearly 100% for SEC, indicating minimal loss during the separation process. In contrast, ODG centrifugation yielded a recovery of approximately 30%, attributed to incomplete pelleting of the density-fractionated rEV. When the pelleting step was replaced by a secondary SEC purification, recovery improved to match primary SEC performance. Differential ultracentrifugation, involving sequential spins at 2,000 × g, 12,000 × g, and 110,000 × g, resulted in only ~10% rEV recovery, consistent with known inefficiencies and co-pelleting of contaminants in this method.

Importantly, the recovery efficiency was independent of the rEV quantification modality used: fNTA and p24 ELISA yielded concordant results across all methods. Furthermore, the use of post-insertion PEGylated rEV (rEV-PEG) did not alter recovery calculations, confirming that the PEG modification does not interfere with isolation physics. The spike-in concentration was also non-critical; recovery percentages remained constant across a 1,000-fold range of input rEV (1 × 10⁸ to 1 × 10¹¹), demonstrating assay robustness.

These quantitative benchmarks provide actionable insights for method selection: SEC offers the highest yield but may co-isolate non-EV particles like lipoproteins; ODG provides high purity at the cost of yield; and dUC is inefficient and prone to artifacts. By enabling objective performance assessment, rEV empower researchers to optimize protocols, troubleshoot losses, and report recovery-corrected EV concentrations, thereby enhancing data reproducibility and comparability across studies.

#### Example 5: rEV Allows for an Accurate Comparison of EV Numbers in Plasma from Cancer Patients Versus Healthy Individuals

The clinical utility of extracellular vesicles (EV) as disease biomarkers is often confounded by technical variability in sample processing and analysis, which can obscure true biological differences. The recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) of the present invention overcome this limitation by enabling normalization of EV counts to account for method-specific and user-dependent losses. This capability was demonstrated in a comparative study of plasma EV from breast cancer patients and healthy individuals.

Plasma samples from 26 early-stage breast cancer patients and 11 sex-matched healthy volunteers were spiked with a fixed number of rEV (2 × 10¹⁰) immediately after thawing. EV and rEV were co-isolated using OptiPrep density gradient (ODG) centrifugation, and total EV were quantified by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), while rEV were quantified by fluorescent NTA (fNTA). Absolute EV counts (particles/mL plasma) showed no significant difference between groups (median 3.91 × 10¹⁰ vs. 2.47 × 10¹⁰; p = 0.1410, Mann–Whitney test). However, when EV counts were normalized to rEV recovery—i.e., divided by the fraction of spiked rEV recovered—the normalized EV concentration was 2.5-fold higher in cancer patients (1.46 × 10¹¹ vs. 5.83 × 10¹⁰; p = 0.0002). This dramatic improvement in statistical significance underscores the ability of rEV to correct for inter-sample variability in isolation efficiency, which can arise from differences in plasma composition, operator technique, or reagent batches.

The normalization approach was validated using an orthogonal rEV quantification method: a p24 ELISA targeting the gag polyprotein. Normalized EV counts derived from ELISA-based rEV recovery yielded equivalent results (1.59 × 10¹¹ vs. 7.55 × 10¹⁰; p = 0.0009), confirming the robustness of the normalization strategy across detection platforms. Recovery efficiencies of rEV varied between individual plasma samples (range: 22–41%), highlighting the necessity of sample-specific correction rather than assuming a fixed recovery rate.

This example illustrates how rEV transform EV enumeration from a semi-quantitative to a truly quantitative metric, enhancing the sensitivity and reliability of EV-based diagnostics. By accounting for pre-analytical and analytical variability, rEV normalization reveals biologically relevant differences that would otherwise remain undetected, thereby advancing the clinical translation of EV biomarkers.

#### Example 6: Implementing the PSI Sequence to Load EGFP mRNA in rEV

While the gag-EGFP fusion protein ensures incorporation of EGFP into recombinant extracellular vesicles (rEV) via protein-protein interactions during particle assembly, the encapsulation of EGFP mRNA occurs passively through the inherent RNA-binding capacity of the gag polyprotein. The HIV-1 gag protein contains zinc-finger motifs in its nucleocapsid (NC) domain that bind RNA with high affinity, and in the absence of viral genomic RNA, gag packages non-specific cellular RNAs to facilitate VLP assembly. To enhance the specificity and efficiency of EGFP mRNA loading into rEV, the present invention contemplates the use of RNA packaging signals, such as the psi (Ψ) sequence from HIV-1.

The psi sequence is a cis-acting RNA element located in the 5' untranslated region of the HIV-1 genome that is recognized by the gag polyprotein, directing selective packaging of viral RNA into nascent virions. By engineering the EGFP mRNA transcript to include the psi sequence in its 5' UTR, the inventors hypothesized that EGFP mRNA would be preferentially incorporated into rEV during biogenesis. To test this, a modified pMET7-gag-EGFP plasmid was constructed wherein the EGFP coding sequence was placed downstream of the HIV-1 psi sequence, ensuring that transcribed EGFP mRNA contained the packaging signal.

HEK293T cells were transfected with either the standard gag-EGFP plasmid or the psi-EGFP variant, and rEV were purified by ODG centrifugation. RT-qPCR analysis of rEV RNA using EGFP-specific primers revealed a 3.2-fold increase in EGFP mRNA copies per rEV in the psi-EGFP group compared to the control (p < 0.001, t-test). This enhancement was specific to EGFP mRNA, as total RNA yield per rEV remained unchanged, and no increase was observed for housekeeping genes like GAPDH. The improved loading efficiency translated into greater sensitivity in RT-qPCR-based rEV quantification, lowering the limit of detection by one order of magnitude.

This example demonstrates a rational engineering strategy to optimize cargo loading in rEV, which can be extended to other nucleic acid payloads such as siRNAs, miRNAs, or mRNA therapeutics. By leveraging natural viral packaging mechanisms, the psi sequence enables high-efficiency, sequence-specific encapsulation, expanding the utility of rEV beyond reference materials to include applications in targeted delivery and gene therapy.
