## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The operation of hydroelectric power generation facilities, particularly those employing hydroturbines such as Kaplan or Francis turbines, presents significant challenges with respect to the safe passage of aquatic organisms, especially migratory fish species such as salmonids. As water is directed through turbine runners and associated hydraulic structures to generate electricity, fish that inadvertently enter these flow paths may be subjected to a range of potentially injurious physical stressors. These stressors include, but are not limited to, rapid and extreme fluctuations in hydrostatic pressure, high levels of fluid shear stress and turbulence, direct mechanical impact from rotating turbine blades, and exposure to cavitation phenomena. Such conditions can result in physiological trauma, barotrauma, tissue damage, or mortality, thereby posing ecological and regulatory concerns for dam operators and resource managers tasked with balancing energy production and environmental stewardship.

In response to these concerns, substantial efforts have been undertaken to evaluate and mitigate the biological impacts of turbine passage on fish populations, particularly in large river systems such as the Columbia and Snake Rivers in the Pacific Northwest of the United States. A critical component of these efforts involves the characterization of the hydraulic environment experienced by fish during downstream passage through turbines. However, conventional field studies that rely solely on live fish releases—while essential for assessing overall survival and injury rates—are inherently limited in their ability to provide precise, quantitative data regarding the specific physical conditions encountered by individual fish within the complex internal geometry of a turbine. These limitations arise because live fish cannot be instrumented to directly measure the dynamic forces acting upon them without altering their natural behavior or compromising their structural integrity, and post-passage necropsies often fail to definitively link observed injuries to specific hydraulic mechanisms or spatial locations within the turbine.

To address this gap in measurement capability, an autonomous sensor device known as the Gen 1 Sensor Fish was previously developed. This device was engineered to mimic the size, shape, and density of juvenile salmon smolts and was equipped with sensors capable of recording pressure and acceleration profiles during simulated turbine passage. The Gen 1 Sensor Fish proved instrumental in numerous biological performance evaluations conducted by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers at mainstem Columbia River dams, providing valuable empirical data that informed turbine rehabilitation strategies aimed at improving fish survival. Despite its utility, the Gen 1 Sensor Fish exhibited several limitations that restricted its broader applicability. These included a relatively narrow pressure measurement range insufficient for high-head hydropower applications, limited robustness under extreme mechanical stresses such as blade strike events, constrained data storage and sampling capabilities, cumbersome deployment and recovery logistics, high unit cost, and a form factor that was optimized specifically for large Kaplan turbines rather than other turbine types such as Francis turbines or pump-turbines commonly found in international hydropower systems.

As global demand for renewable energy continues to grow, there is an increasing need for advanced diagnostic tools that can support the design, evaluation, and optimization of fish-friendly hydropower technologies across diverse operational contexts—including high-head dams, pumped-storage facilities, and emerging small-scale or low-head installations. To meet this need, a next-generation autonomous sensor platform was required—one that overcomes the shortcomings of prior devices while offering enhanced measurement fidelity, durability, versatility, and cost-effectiveness. The present invention fulfills this requirement by introducing a novel, miniaturized, neutrally buoyant sensor device capable of withstanding the most severe hydraulic environments encountered in modern hydropower infrastructure and capturing high-resolution, multi-axis kinematic and environmental data throughout the entire duration of a simulated fish passage event.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a second-generation autonomous sensor device, referred to herein as the Gen 2 Sensor Fish, designed to accurately replicate and record the physical stresses experienced by fish during downstream passage through hydroturbines and other hydraulic structures. The Gen 2 Sensor Fish is configured to closely emulate the morphological and hydrodynamic characteristics of a yearling salmon smolt, with a cylindrical body measuring approximately 24.5 millimeters in diameter and 89.9 millimeters in length, and a total mass of about 42.1 grams, resulting in neutral buoyancy in fresh water at the time of deployment. This biomimetic design ensures that the device follows flow trajectories similar to those of actual fish, thereby enhancing the ecological relevance of the collected data.

Internally, the Gen 2 Sensor Fish integrates a comprehensive suite of high-performance microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) sensors, including a three-axis linear accelerometer capable of measuring accelerations up to ±200 g per axis with a shock survival rating of 10,000 g, a three-axis gyroscope with a full-scale range of ±2000 degrees per second per axis, a three-axis magnetometer combined with a secondary low-range accelerometer in an eCompass module for orientation tracking, a high-range pressure sensor with a full-scale measurement capacity of 12 bars (174 psi absolute) and an overpressure tolerance of 30 bars, and a precision analog temperature sensor with a linear response over a range of −40°C to +125°C. All sensors are strategically positioned near the geometric and mass center of the device to minimize measurement artifacts due to rotational coupling or off-axis loading.

Data acquisition is managed by a low-power microcontroller that samples all sensor channels simultaneously at a high frequency of 2048 Hz, enabling the capture of transient events such as blade strikes or rapid decompression with exceptional temporal resolution. Up to five minutes of continuous sensor data are stored in non-volatile flash memory, ensuring complete coverage of even the longest turbine passage scenarios. Power is supplied by a rechargeable battery that supports multiple deployment cycles.

A key innovation of the Gen 2 Sensor Fish lies in its integrated recovery system. Following a user-programmable delay after data acquisition, a dual-end release mechanism is activated: nichrome heating elements sever fishing-line tethers holding spring-loaded internal weights, causing the device to become positively buoyant and ascend to the water surface. Once surfaced, the device periodically activates a radio-frequency (RF) transmitter operating at 146 MHz—compatible with standard wildlife tracking receivers—and four high-intensity orange light-emitting diodes (LEDs) oriented radially outward to facilitate visual and electronic location in varied lighting and turbidity conditions. The RF and LED activation continues until the device is retrieved and deactivated via a magnetic switch.

The Gen 2 Sensor Fish is activated prior to deployment by bringing a handheld magnet into proximity with an internal reed switch, initiating a self-diagnostic sequence that verifies battery charge level and memory availability. Upon successful validation, a yellow status LED flashes to indicate readiness, followed by a configurable pre-deployment delay before data logging commences. Post-recovery, the device interfaces with a custom docking station that enables simultaneous battery recharging and high-speed serial data download (at 921.6 kbps) via a two-pin connector. Accompanying software, developed specifically for this platform, converts raw binary sensor outputs into calibrated physical units using pre-determined coefficients, exports data in comma-separated value (CSV) format, and provides visualization tools for rapid analysis.

The Gen 2 Sensor Fish has been rigorously calibrated and validated through a series of laboratory and field tests. Pressure accuracy was confirmed to within ±2% using a hyperbaric chamber simulating turbine passage pressure transients. Acceleration measurements were benchmarked against a reference Brüel & Kjaer accelerometer on a linear impact test track, yielding relative errors below 5% (typically under 2%). Rotational velocity was validated using high-speed videography and motion-tracking software in a precision rotation fixture, achieving less than 5% error across the full ±2000°/s range. Orientation accuracy was verified to within ±4° using controlled magnetic alignment tests, and temperature measurements were shown to agree with a calibrated thermocouple within ±2°C. Durability testing demonstrated full functionality after 100 high-g impact events (up to 600 g), confirming resilience to mechanical abuse. Field trials at Ice Harbor Dam on the Snake River successfully recovered all deployed units and yielded complete, high-fidelity datasets from spillway passage, validating real-world operability.

By integrating high-fidelity multi-axis sensing, robust mechanical design, intelligent recovery logic, and streamlined data handling, the Gen 2 Sensor Fish represents a significant advancement over prior art. It enables unprecedented insight into the spatiotemporal distribution of injurious hydraulic conditions within turbines, supports evidence-based turbine design and operational modifications, reduces reliance on live fish testing, and facilitates international deployment across a wide spectrum of hydropower facilities—from low-head run-of-river plants to high-head pumped-storage systems—thereby accelerating the development of ecologically sustainable hydropower worldwide.

## DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to an autonomous, biomimetic sensor device configured to simulate the passage of fish through hydroturbines and other hydraulic structures while recording high-resolution, multi-parameter physical data representative of the stresses encountered during such passage. The device, herein referred to as the Gen 2 Sensor Fish, is engineered to overcome the limitations of earlier sensor platforms by incorporating enhanced measurement capabilities, improved mechanical robustness, extended operational range, simplified deployment and recovery protocols, and reduced manufacturing cost, thereby enabling broader application in both domestic and international hydropower contexts.

The physical embodiment of the Gen 2 Sensor Fish comprises an elongated, cylindrical housing constructed from durable, corrosion-resistant materials suitable for prolonged submersion in freshwater environments. The external dimensions are precisely calibrated to match the average size and density of a yearling salmon smolt—a key migratory species of concern in many regulated river systems—measuring 24.5 millimeters in outer diameter and 89.9 millimeters in total length. The overall mass is maintained at approximately 42.1 grams, resulting in neutral buoyancy at the time of deployment in typical riverine conditions (approximately 1000 kg/m³). This neutral buoyancy is critical to ensuring that the device’s trajectory through complex flow fields closely approximates that of a live fish, thereby enhancing the ecological validity of the recorded data. The housing is hermetically sealed to protect internal electronics from water ingress under high-pressure conditions, with all penetrations and joints engineered to withstand hydrostatic pressures corresponding to depths exceeding 100 meters.

Internally, the Gen 2 Sensor Fish houses a densely integrated array of microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) sensors, each selected for its performance characteristics relevant to the anticipated hydraulic stressors. The primary linear acceleration sensing is performed by a high-g, three-axis analog accelerometer (e.g., Analog Devices ADXL377), which offers a full-scale measurement range of ±200 g per axis and a remarkable shock survival rating of 10,000 g. This wide dynamic range allows the device to accurately capture both the sustained accelerations associated with turbulent eddies and the impulsive, high-magnitude shocks resulting from collisions with turbine blades or structural components. The accelerometer features user-selectable bandwidths—up to 1300 Hz for the x- and y-axes and 1000 Hz for the z-axis—enabling optimal signal fidelity across diverse flow regimes. Critically, the accelerometer is mounted at the geometric center of the main printed circuit board (PCB), which coincides with the device’s center of mass, thereby minimizing cross-axis sensitivity and rotational inertia effects that could otherwise distort acceleration measurements.

Complementing the linear acceleration data, a three-axis digital-output gyroscope (e.g., InvenSense ITG-3200) provides continuous measurement of angular velocity about all three principal axes. With a full-scale range of ±2000 degrees per second per axis and 16-bit analog-to-digital conversion, the gyroscope captures the complex rotational dynamics experienced during tumbling, spinning, or vortex-induced motions within the turbine. The gyroscope includes an embedded temperature sensor for thermal compensation and communicates with the central microcontroller via an inter-integrated circuit (I²C) serial bus. Like the accelerometer, it is positioned near the device’s center of mass to ensure accurate representation of the body’s rotational state.

For orientation tracking and heading determination, the Gen 2 Sensor Fish incorporates an eCompass module (e.g., STMicroelectronics LSM303DLHC) that combines a three-axis digital magnetometer with a secondary three-axis digital accelerometer in a single package. While the secondary accelerometer (±16 g range) is not intended for high-impact events, it enhances the precision of orientation calculations during lower-stress phases of passage by providing tilt compensation for the magnetometer. The magnetometer itself offers a selectable full-scale range up to ±8.1 gauss, sufficient to resolve Earth’s magnetic field vector under most operational conditions. This module enables the reconstruction of the device’s absolute orientation in three-dimensional space relative to magnetic north, which is invaluable for correlating sensor data with specific turbine geometries or flow features.

Hydrostatic pressure is measured by a micromachined silicon piezoresistive sensor (e.g., Measurement Specialties MS5412-BM) mounted on a flexible PCB segment adjacent to the main electronics stack. This sensor provides a full-scale measurement range of 12 bars (174 psi absolute), corresponding to a water depth of approximately 110 meters, with an overpressure tolerance of 30 bars (435 psi absolute)—a substantial improvement over the Gen 1 Sensor Fish and sufficient for high-head applications including pumped-storage facilities. The sensor output, derived from a Wheatstone bridge configuration, is conditioned by an instrumentation amplifier with a fixed gain of 13.0 and an offset of 40 mV, translating the raw millivolt signal into a voltage range compatible with the microcontroller’s analog input stage. This conditioning ensures optimal utilization of the analog-to-digital converter’s dynamic range while maintaining linearity across the entire pressure span.

Temperature is monitored by a precision analog sensor (e.g., Microchip TC1046) with a linear output slope of 6.25 mV/°C over a range of −40°C to +125°C. To ensure accurate thermal coupling with the surrounding water, the temperature sensor is mounted in close proximity to the metal enclosure of the pressure sensor, with the intervening gap filled with thermally conductive epoxy (e.g., Omega Bond 200). This arrangement minimizes thermal lag and ensures that the recorded temperature reflects the actual fluid temperature rather than internal heat generated by electronics.

Power for the entire system is supplied by a rechargeable lithium-polymer or equivalent battery, selected for its high energy density, stable discharge characteristics, and compatibility with the device’s low-power operational profile. The battery is capable of supporting multiple deployment cycles, with each cycle encompassing up to five minutes of continuous high-rate data acquisition (2048 Hz across all channels) followed by extended surface signaling via RF and LEDs. Charging is accomplished through a dedicated contact pad on the download board, accessible when the device is docked in the recovery station.

Data acquisition and system control are managed by a low-power microcontroller featuring sufficient processing speed, memory bandwidth, and peripheral interfaces to handle concurrent sensor sampling, data buffering, and real-time decision-making. Upon activation via a magnetic reed switch, the microcontroller executes a startup sequence that includes battery voltage verification and flash memory integrity check. If both checks pass, a yellow status LED flashes to indicate readiness, and a user-configurable pre-deployment delay begins—allowing time for the device to be released into the flow before logging commences. Data are written continuously to non-volatile flash memory at 2048 Hz, ensuring no loss of transient events. After the acquisition window closes, a green LED flashes to signal the start of a configurable resurface delay. Upon expiration of this delay, the recovery sequence initiates: current is applied to nichrome wires at both ends of the device, heating them sufficiently to melt through fishing-line tethers that secure internal spring-loaded weights. Releasing the first weight shifts the center of buoyancy, causing the device to rise; the second release ensures positive buoyancy regardless of orientation. Once surfaced, the RF transmitter pulses at 146 MHz at regular intervals, synchronized with bursts from the four radial orange LEDs, creating a multimodal beacon detectable by both radio telemetry and visual search teams. This signaling persists until the magnetic switch is toggled again upon recovery, conserving battery life.

Post-recovery, the Gen 2 Sensor Fish is placed into a custom docking station that mates with electrical contacts on the download board. The station provides regulated charging current to the battery while simultaneously establishing a high-speed serial communication link (921.6 kbps TTL-to-USB) for data extraction. Proprietary software running on a connected computer issues commands to read the raw binary data stream, apply factory-calibrated conversion coefficients to translate digital counts into physical units (e.g., g, °/s, psi, °C), and export the results as a human-readable CSV file. The software also includes plotting functions for immediate visualization of pressure transients, acceleration spikes, rotational profiles, and orientation changes, enabling rapid assessment of passage severity.

The Gen 2 Sensor Fish has undergone comprehensive calibration and validation to ensure measurement accuracy and reliability. Pressure calibration was performed in a hyperbaric chamber programmed to replicate the rapid decompression profiles typical of turbine draft tubes, with results showing less than 2% deviation from a NIST-traceable reference transducer. Acceleration was validated on a linear impact test track using a certified Brüel & Kjaer reference accelerometer, yielding relative errors consistently below 5%, often under 2%. Rotational velocity was benchmarked against high-speed video motion analysis (up to 16,000 fps) in a precision rotation fixture, confirming gyroscope accuracy within 5% across the full operational range. Orientation accuracy was verified by aligning the device with magnetic north on a leveled surface, achieving heading resolution within ±4°. Temperature agreement with a calibrated thermocouple was within ±2°C in both ice-water and ambient conditions. Durability was confirmed through 100 repeated 600 g impacts with no degradation in sensor function or recovery mechanism performance. Finally, field trials at Ice Harbor Dam demonstrated successful deployment, data collection, surfacing, and recovery in a real-world spillway environment, with all three released units returning complete, high-quality datasets.

Collectively, these features establish the Gen 2 Sensor Fish as a transformative tool for hydropower bioengineering. It enables precise, repeatable, and ethically sound evaluation of turbine hydraulic conditions without the logistical and ethical constraints of live fish testing. By delivering actionable data on the location, magnitude, and duration of injurious stressors, it empowers engineers to optimize runner blade geometry, guide vane settings, and operational protocols to maximize both energy efficiency and fish survival—advancing the global mission of sustainable hydropower development.
