# DESCRIPTION

## DESCRIPTION

### STATE OF THE ART

Amadoriases, also known as Fructosyl Amino Acid Oxidases (FAOX), constitute a class of flavin-dependent enzymes predominantly found in fungi and certain bacteria. These enzymes catalyze the oxidative deglycation of low molecular weight Amadori products—specifically, glycated amino acids—yielding free amine, glucosone, and hydrogen peroxide as reaction products. The enzymatic mechanism involves oxidation at the C–N bond linking the nitrogen atom of the amino acid moiety to the C1 carbon of the fructosyl group, followed by hydrolysis of the resulting Schiff base intermediate. Concurrently, the reduced flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor is reoxidized by molecular oxygen, generating hydrogen peroxide as a stoichiometric byproduct. This well-characterized biochemical activity has rendered Amadoriases particularly valuable in clinical diagnostics, especially in the context of diabetes monitoring.

In current diagnostic practice, Amadoriases—often referred to in this application as Fructosyl Peptide Oxidases (FPOX)—are integral components of biosensors designed to quantify glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), a critical long-term glycemic marker with a physiological half-life of approximately three months. The detection methodology typically involves an initial proteolytic digestion of HbA1c to liberate N-terminal glycated valine residues, which are then specifically recognized and oxidized by FPOX. The concomitant production of hydrogen peroxide is subsequently measured via a colorimetric assay employing horseradish peroxidase and a chromogenic substrate, enabling indirect quantification of HbA1c levels. A similar enzymatic strategy has been proposed for the detection of glycated albumin, a shorter-term glycemic indicator with a half-life of about three weeks, thereby expanding the utility of Amadoriases in diabetes management.

Despite their diagnostic promise, the practical deployment of Amadoriase-based biosensors is frequently hampered by the inherent instability of the enzyme under ambient storage and operational conditions. Enzymes, as biological macromolecules, are susceptible to denaturation, aggregation, and loss of catalytic function over time, particularly when exposed to temperature fluctuations, oxidative stress, or prolonged storage. This instability directly compromises the shelf life, reliability, and reproducibility of biosensor devices, necessitating stringent cold-chain logistics and limiting their use in resource-limited settings. Consequently, the development of thermostable variants of Amadoriase represents a significant technological advancement with immediate implications for point-of-care diagnostics.

Beyond diagnostics, Amadoriases hold therapeutic potential as enzymatic agents capable of mitigating non-enzymatic protein glycation—a pathological process implicated in the progression of numerous diabetic complications and age-related diseases. Glycation occurs spontaneously between reducing sugars and free amino groups on proteins, forming covalent adducts that alter protein structure, impair function, and contribute to tissue damage. Advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), derived from Amadori intermediates, are associated with arterial stiffening, atherosclerosis, nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy. Although Amadoriases can cleave early glycation adducts on free amino acids, their inability to act on glycated intact proteins—due to steric occlusion of the active site and a narrow substrate access tunnel—has thus far precluded their direct therapeutic application. However, a stabilized enzyme scaffold could serve as a foundational platform for subsequent protein engineering efforts aimed at broadening substrate specificity to include glycated polypeptides or full-length proteins.

Additionally, thermostable Amadoriases present opportunities in the food industry, particularly in the mitigation of acrylamide formation during thermal processing. Acrylamide, a probable human carcinogen, arises during high-temperature cooking (e.g., frying, baking, roasting) via the Maillard reaction between reducing sugars and the amino acid asparagine. Since Amadori products are key intermediates in this pathway, enzymatic interception of fructosyl-asparagine by Amadoriase could theoretically suppress acrylamide generation without compromising desirable sensory attributes such as flavor and browning. However, the efficacy of such an intervention hinges on the enzyme’s ability to retain activity under the elevated temperatures typical of food manufacturing processes, which often exceed 150°C. Current wild-type Amadoriases rapidly denature under these conditions, rendering them unsuitable for industrial application. Thus, the creation of a thermostable variant is not merely advantageous but essential for this use case.

Efforts to enhance protein thermostability have historically employed a range of strategies, including rational design based on structural insights, directed evolution through iterative mutagenesis and screening, and computational prediction of stabilizing mutations. Common rational approaches involve optimizing electrostatic interactions (e.g., salt bridges), introducing hydrogen bonds, enhancing hydrophobic packing, or engineering disulfide bonds to restrict conformational flexibility. Disulfide bonds, in particular, are potent stabilizers that covalently link distant regions of a polypeptide chain, thereby reducing entropy in the unfolded state and increasing the activation energy required for denaturation. However, the successful introduction of disulfide bonds requires precise geometric compatibility between cysteine side chains, typically defined by interatomic distances and dihedral angles within energetically favorable ranges. Traditional methods for identifying suitable sites rely on manual inspection of crystal structures or homology modeling, which is both time-consuming and prone to error.

Computational tools such as SSBOND have been developed to automate the identification of potential disulfide bond sites based on backbone geometry and side-chain rotamer libraries. Nevertheless, even with such aids, the functional impact of introduced disulfides remains uncertain without experimental validation. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations offer a more nuanced approach by modeling protein flexibility and thermal response at atomic resolution, allowing for the assessment of mutation effects on global and local stability. However, conventional MD-based screening is computationally expensive, especially when evaluating large mutant libraries, and often requires millisecond-scale simulations to capture large-scale unfolding events—far beyond routine feasibility. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for efficient, high-throughput computational methodologies that can reliably prioritize a small subset of promising mutants for experimental testing, thereby reducing the cost, time, and labor associated with protein engineering campaigns.

Prior attempts to stabilize Amadoriase enzymes have been limited. One reported study utilized directed evolution to generate a variant with a modest ~5°C increase in T50 (the temperature at which 50% activity is lost), achieved through the accumulation of five point mutations. While effective, this approach required extensive library construction, high-throughput screening infrastructure, and multiple rounds of selection—resources not universally accessible. Moreover, the mutations identified were not necessarily interpretable in structural terms, limiting generalizability. In contrast, a rational, structure-guided strategy that leverages computational pre-screening could offer a more targeted, efficient, and insightful path to thermostabilization. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel computational screening protocol based on short-timescale MD simulations and a quantitative metric—derived from temperature-dependent root mean square fluctuation (RMSF)—to predict thermostability enhancements conferred by engineered disulfide bonds. This method enables the rapid identification of stabilizing mutations with minimal experimental overhead, representing a significant advance over existing paradigms in enzyme engineering.

### SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides thermostable variants of Amadoriase I, a fructosyl amino acid oxidase derived from *Aspergillus fumigatus*, engineered through the strategic introduction of disulfide bonds to enhance thermal stability without substantially compromising catalytic activity. Specifically, the invention discloses two single-point double-cysteine mutants—designated herein as SS03 and SS17—and a double-mutant variant SSDM, each exhibiting significantly improved resistance to thermal denaturation compared to the wild-type enzyme. The SS03 mutant comprises cysteine substitutions at positions corresponding to serine 67 and proline 121 of the wild-type sequence (S67C/P121C), thereby forming a disulfide bond between residues 67 and 121. The SS17 mutant features cysteine substitutions at aspartate 295 and lysine 303 (D295C/K303C), resulting in a disulfide linkage between residues 295 and 303. The SSDM variant combines both sets of mutations (S67C/P121C/D295C/K303C), although experimental evidence suggests that only the SS03 disulfide bond may be fully formed in this construct.

These engineered enzymes retain the core catalytic function of wild-type Amadoriase I, efficiently oxidizing fructosyl-lysine to produce glucosone and hydrogen peroxide, while demonstrating markedly enhanced thermal resilience. The SS03 mutant exhibits a T50 value approximately 3°C higher than wild-type, whereas the SS17 mutant shows a remarkable 8°C increase in T50 and retains measurable enzymatic activity even after exposure to temperatures as high as 95°C—a feat unattainable by the native enzyme, which is completely inactivated above 60°C. Critically, the improved thermostability is reversible upon chemical reduction with dithiothreitol (DTT), confirming that the observed stabilization is directly attributable to the engineered disulfide bonds.

The invention further encompasses a novel computational methodology for the high-throughput screening of disulfide bond candidates to identify thermostabilizing mutations. This method involves: (i) generating a library of potential disulfide bond sites using structure-based prediction software such as SSBOND; (ii) constructing molecular models of each candidate mutant; (iii) performing short-timescale (e.g., 5 nanosecond) molecular dynamics simulations at multiple temperatures (e.g., 273 K, 300 K, and 340 K); (iv) calculating the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of Cα atoms across the protein backbone at each temperature; (v) determining the average RMSF (avg-RMSF) per temperature; and (vi) computing the slope (λ) of the linear regression of avg-RMSF versus temperature. Mutants exhibiting a λ value lower than that of the wild-type enzyme are predicted to possess enhanced thermostability and are selected for experimental validation. This screening protocol dramatically reduces the number of variants requiring laboratory synthesis and characterization, thereby accelerating the enzyme engineering pipeline.

The thermostable Amadoriase variants of the present invention find utility in multiple domains. In clinical diagnostics, they enable the development of more robust and longer-lasting biosensors for the detection of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and glycated albumin, improving the reliability of diabetes monitoring devices during transport, storage, and use. In the food industry, the enhanced thermal tolerance of these enzymes makes them suitable for incorporation into high-temperature food processing protocols aimed at reducing acrylamide formation—particularly in applications such as milk pasteurization (e.g., HTST at 72°C or UHT at 140°C), where the mutant enzymes remain active. Furthermore, the stabilized enzyme scaffolds provide a foundation for future protein engineering efforts directed toward expanding substrate specificity to include glycated peptides or full-length proteins, thereby unlocking potential therapeutic applications in the prevention or reversal of pathological protein glycation in vivo.

The invention also includes pharmaceutical compositions, diagnostic kits, and food processing formulations comprising the disclosed thermostable Amadoriase variants. Additionally, it covers nucleic acid molecules encoding these variants, expression vectors containing such nucleic acids, and host cells engineered to produce the stabilized enzymes. The crystal structures of the SS03 and SS17 mutants, resolved at 2.15 Å and 2.85 Å resolution respectively, confirm the formation of the designed disulfide bonds and demonstrate preservation of the overall wild-type fold, with minimal perturbation to the active site architecture—thereby explaining the retention of catalytic efficiency in these variants.

### DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to engineered variants of Amadoriase I, a flavin-dependent oxidase originally isolated from *Aspergillus fumigatus*, which exhibit enhanced thermal stability due to the introduction of one or more disulfide bonds. The wild-type Amadoriase I enzyme (UniProt or PDB reference 4WCT) consists of a single polypeptide chain of 447 amino acids and non-covalently binds a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor essential for its catalytic activity. The enzyme catalyzes the oxidation of fructosyl-amino acids—early glycation adducts formed between glucose and protein amino groups—yielding the corresponding free amino acid, 3-deoxyglucosone (glucosone), and hydrogen peroxide. This reaction is central to several biotechnological applications, but the utility of the wild-type enzyme is constrained by its moderate thermal stability, with a T50 of approximately 52.4°C under standard assay conditions.

To overcome this limitation, the inventors employed a rational design strategy centered on the introduction of disulfide bonds, which are known to restrict conformational entropy in the unfolded state and thereby increase the free energy barrier to denaturation. Using the high-resolution crystal structure of wild-type Amadoriase I (PDB ID: 4WCT) as a template, potential disulfide bond sites were identified using the SSBOND software, which evaluates geometric and energetic feasibility based on backbone dihedral angles, side-chain rotamers, and distance constraints derived from known disulfide-containing proteins in the Protein Data Bank. To avoid disrupting catalytic function, residue pairs located within 10 Å of the FAD cofactor or the substrate-binding pocket were excluded from consideration. This filtering yielded a preliminary list of 19 candidate disulfide bond sites, designated SS01 through SS19.

Each candidate was modeled in silico by substituting the native residues with cysteines and energy-minimizing the resulting structure. These models, along with the wild-type enzyme, were subjected to molecular dynamics (MD) simulations under explicit solvent conditions using the AMBER99SBildn force field for the protein and TIP3P water model, with FAD parameterized via the General Amber Force Field (GAFF). Simulations were conducted at three temperatures—273 K, 300 K, and 340 K—for a duration of 5 nanoseconds per condition, following standard equilibration protocols involving position restraints on Cα atoms. From each trajectory, the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of Cα atoms (excluding the highly mobile N- and C-termini) was computed as a measure of local flexibility. The average RMSF (avg-RMSF) across the entire protein was then plotted against temperature, and the slope (λ) of the best-fit linear regression was calculated. A lower λ value indicates reduced sensitivity of protein flexibility to thermal stress and is thus interpreted as a proxy for enhanced thermostability.

Based on this criterion, four mutants—SS03 (S67C/P121C), SS07 (H106C/G150C), SS11 (K233C/P404C), and SS17 (D295C/K303C)—were selected for experimental production due to their λ values being equal to or lower than that of the wild-type enzyme (λWT = 0.00151). These variants were generated via site-directed mutagenesis of the pET-3a-AmadI expression vector and expressed in *E. coli* BL21(DE3)pLysS cells. Following purification by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) and size-exclusion chromatography, the proteins were characterized biochemically and structurally.

UV-visible absorption spectroscopy confirmed that SS03, SS07, and SS17 bound FAD, as evidenced by characteristic absorbance peaks at 368 nm and 454 nm. In contrast, SS11 failed to exhibit these spectral features, displayed extremely low soluble expression (<1 mg/L), and showed no detectable enzymatic activity, indicating severe folding defects likely due to the proximity of the mutated residues (K233 and P404) to the FAD-binding site. Consequently, SS11 was discarded from further analysis.

Steady-state kinetic assays using fructosyl-lysine as substrate revealed that SS03 and SS17 retained catalytic efficiencies (kcat/Km) comparable to wild-type, with Km values of 0.34 mM and 0.68 mM, respectively, versus 0.51 mM for wild-type. The SS07 mutant, however, exhibited a two-fold increase in Km (0.99 mM) and a four-fold reduction in kcat/Km, suggesting impaired substrate binding or access. Computational analysis of the substrate access tunnel using CAVER 3.0.2 indicated a narrowing of the bottleneck radius in SS07 (1.57 Å vs. 1.83 Å in wild-type), consistent with the location of the H106C/G150C disulfide near the tunnel entrance.

Thermal stability assays demonstrated that SS03 and SS17 possessed T50 values of 55.25°C and 60.62°C, respectively—representing increases of +2.85°C and +8.22°C over wild-type. Notably, SS17 retained 50% residual activity after incubation at 60°C and detectable activity up to 95°C, whereas all other variants, including wild-type, were fully inactivated above 60°C. The destabilizing effect of the SS07 mutation was confirmed by its reduced T50 of 40.76°C. Crucially, treatment with 100 mM DTT abolished the thermal stabilization in SS03 and SS17, reverting their T50 values to near-wild-type levels, thereby proving that disulfide bond formation is responsible for the observed thermostability.

The double-mutant SSDM (S67C/P121C/D295C/K303C) exhibited a T50 of 55.79°C—similar to SS03 but significantly lower than SS17—suggesting that the SS17 disulfide may not form efficiently in the presence of the SS03 mutation, possibly due to long-range structural incompatibilities. This hypothesis is supported by crystallographic data showing that the SS17 mutation induces a conformational shift in a distal loop (residues 63–69), altering the spatial relationship between residues 67 and 121.

High-resolution X-ray crystal structures of SS03 (2.15 Å) and SS17 (2.85 Å) confirmed the formation of the designed disulfide bonds, as evidenced by clear electron density connecting the sulfur atoms of the engineered cysteines. Both mutants maintained the overall fold of wild-type Amadoriase I, with main-chain RMSD values of 0.268 Å and 0.804 Å, respectively. No significant distortions were observed in the active site, explaining the preservation of catalytic function. However, SS17 exhibited localized structural rearrangements near the mutation site (residues 290–308) and in the aforementioned distal loop, highlighting the potential for long-range effects even with conservative modifications.

The pH-activity profiles of SS03, SS17, and SSDM mirrored that of wild-type, with optimal activity above pH 8.0 and sharp declines below pH 7.0, indicating that disulfide engineering did not broaden pH tolerance. This is consistent with the targeted nature of the modifications, which were designed solely to enhance thermal rigidity without altering surface charge distribution.

In summary, the invention provides thermostable Amadoriase I variants with demonstrable improvements in thermal resilience, validated by biochemical, biophysical, and structural analyses. The SS17 mutant, in particular, represents a significant advance, offering sufficient stability for applications in moderate-temperature food processing (e.g., milk pasteurization) and enhanced durability in diagnostic biosensors. The accompanying computational screening method offers a generalizable framework for rapid thermostabilization of other enzymes, reducing reliance on labor-intensive directed evolution. The invention thus enables new applications in healthcare, food safety, and biocatalysis, while providing robust molecular tools for future engineering endeavors aimed at expanding substrate scope or enhancing catalytic properties.
