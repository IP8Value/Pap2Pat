# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNOLOGICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of ophthalmic medical devices and methods, and more particularly to a noninvasive system and method for the acoustic manipulation of particulate matter within the anterior chamber of the eye. The invention provides a novel approach for controlling the spatial distribution and movement of intraocular particles—whether pathological or therapeutic in nature—through the application of controlled acoustic radiation forces generated by external transducers. This technology operates without direct contact with ocular tissues and avoids surgical intervention, thereby offering a safe, reversible, and targeted means of managing conditions associated with intraocular particulate dispersion, such as pigment dispersion syndrome, pseudoexfoliation, hyphema, inflammatory cells, or injected pharmacologic agents like corticosteroid suspensions. The invention leverages principles of acoustophoresis, specifically the use of standing acoustic waves to induce particle migration toward nodal regions of minimal acoustic pressure, enabling precise spatial organization of particles within the aqueous humor. The disclosed system is designed to be compatible with clinical ophthalmic practice, utilizing biocompatible materials, low-intensity ultrasound parameters within established safety thresholds, and configurations that allow integration with standard diagnostic or therapeutic workflows. By enabling the controlled repositioning of intraocular particles away from critical optical or drainage structures—such as the visual axis or the trabecular meshwork—the invention addresses an unmet clinical need for preventive and interventional strategies in ocular disorders where particulate matter contributes to vision loss, elevated intraocular pressure, or secondary glaucoma. Furthermore, the invention opens avenues for enhancing drug delivery precision, improving diagnostic clarity, and supporting regenerative therapies through acoustic guidance of cellular or microscale therapeutic agents.

## BACKGROUND

Intraocular particulate matter represents a significant yet often inadequately managed challenge in clinical ophthalmology. Aqueous humor, the transparent fluid filling the anterior and posterior chambers of the eye, normally maintains optical clarity and supports homeostasis of avascular ocular tissues such as the corneal endothelium, lens, and trabecular meshwork. However, various pathological conditions—including trauma, inflammation, hemorrhage, pigment dispersion, pseudoexfoliation syndrome, and postoperative complications—can introduce or disperse solid particles into this fluid. These particles range widely in composition, size (from sub-micron to tens of microns), and origin: red blood cells in hyphema, iris pigment granules in pigmentary glaucoma, protein aggregates in uveitis, or exogenous therapeutic particles such as triamcinolone acetonide crystals used in intravitreal or anterior chamber injections. While some particles may clear spontaneously over time, many persist and pose risks including obstruction of the trabecular meshwork leading to secondary open-angle glaucoma, deposition on the corneal endothelium causing Krukenberg’s spindle or endothelial dysfunction, and scattering of light that reduces visual acuity. Current clinical management remains largely reactive: clinicians monitor for complications such as elevated intraocular pressure or corneal decompensation and intervene only after damage has occurred, typically with medications (e.g., IOP-lowering drops) or surgery (e.g., trabeculectomy, corneal transplant). No existing therapy actively removes, relocates, or neutralizes intraocular particles in a noninvasive manner prior to the onset of irreversible tissue injury.

Ultrasound has long been employed in ophthalmology, primarily for diagnostic imaging (A-scan and B-scan ultrasonography) and, more recently, for therapeutic ablation. High-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) systems, for instance, are used to selectively destroy ciliary body tissue to reduce aqueous production in refractory glaucoma. Other applications include ultrasound-mediated phacoemulsification during cataract surgery and experimental uses in retinal reattachment. However, these modalities rely on mechanical displacement, thermal effects, or cavitation—mechanisms that inherently carry risks of tissue damage and are unsuitable for delicate manipulations within the anterior chamber. In contrast, low-intensity ultrasound can generate non-destructive acoustic radiation forces capable of exerting piconewton-scale forces on microscopic particles suspended in fluid—a phenomenon known as acoustophoresis. First observed by Michael Faraday in 1831 and later formalized through the theory of acoustic radiation pressure, this effect arises when a particle interacts with a sound field, scattering or absorbing acoustic energy and thereby experiencing a net force that drives it toward regions of minimal acoustic potential energy, typically the nodes of a standing wave. Acoustic manipulation has found utility in microfluidics, cell sorting, and lab-on-a-chip technologies, where it enables label-free, contactless handling of cells and microparticles based solely on differences in density and compressibility relative to the surrounding medium—an “acoustic contrast factor.” Crucially, the power densities required for such manipulation are orders of magnitude lower than those used in HIFU or even diagnostic imaging, minimizing thermal and mechanical bioeffects.

Despite its promise in other biomedical domains, acoustic manipulation has not been previously adapted for intraocular use. The eye presents unique challenges: its small dimensions (the anterior chamber depth is typically 2–4 mm), optical sensitivity, and the presence of delicate tissues such as the corneal endothelium, which is non-regenerative in humans. Moreover, the aqueous humor is a low-viscosity, low-density fluid, and intraocular particles are often heterogeneous in size and composition, complicating predictable response to acoustic fields. Prior art lacks any teaching or suggestion of applying standing acoustic waves to control particle distribution within the confined space of the anterior chamber without inciting inflammation, thermal injury, or mechanical disruption. Existing ultrasound devices for the eye are either imaging probes that do not generate standing waves or high-energy ablation systems incompatible with particle manipulation. There remains a critical gap in the art for a method that can safely, noninvasively, and reversibly organize intraocular particles to prevent their pathologic interactions while preserving ocular integrity. The present invention directly addresses this gap by introducing a purpose-built acoustic resonator system configured to generate controlled standing wave patterns within the anterior chamber, validated through both in vitro and ex vivo models to demonstrate efficacy and safety at intensities well below regulatory thresholds for ophthalmic ultrasound exposure.

## GENERAL DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a system and method for the noninvasive acoustic manipulation of particulate matter within the anterior chamber of the eye using externally applied standing acoustic waves. The core principle relies on the generation of a stable acoustic field via one or more piezoelectric transducers positioned adjacent to the ocular surface, typically over the cornea, with an intervening coupling medium such as saline or a biocompatible gel. When activated at or near their resonant frequency—preferably in the range of 0.5 to 5 MHz, and more specifically around 1.1 MHz as demonstrated—the transducers produce standing waves within the aqueous humor due to reflections at the boundaries of the anterior chamber (e.g., cornea-anterior chamber-iris interface). Particles suspended in the aqueous humor that possess a positive acoustic contrast factor (i.e., higher density and/or lower compressibility than the surrounding fluid) experience an acoustic radiation force that drives them toward the nodal planes of the standing wave, where the acoustic pressure amplitude is minimal. Over time, these particles accumulate into organized patterns—typically concentric rings in a cylindrical geometry—corresponding to the spatial distribution of the acoustic nodes. This aggregation is stable even after deactivation of the acoustic field, persisting for at least one hour under static conditions, and can be dynamically repositioned by translating the transducer or modulating the acoustic frequency.

The invention encompasses both the device and the therapeutic method. The device comprises a radial-mode piezoelectric resonator, preferably fabricated from lead zirconate titanate (PZT), with dimensions tailored to match the curvature and size of the human cornea (e.g., inner diameter of 22 mm, outer diameter of 26 mm, length of 20 mm). The resonator is driven by a signal generator and power amplifier capable of delivering continuous sine waves at amplitudes between 1 and 20 volts peak-to-peak (Vpp), corresponding to effective transducer intensities of 20 to 80 mW/cm²—well within the U.S. Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) safety limit of 50 mW/cm² for diagnostic ophthalmic ultrasound, and significantly below the intensities used in HIFU procedures (which exceed several watts per cm²). To ensure biocompatibility and prevent direct contact with the eye, the transducer is coated with a thin layer (~70 µm) of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), a medical-grade silicone elastomer known for its optical clarity, flexibility, and inertness. The system may further include real-time monitoring capabilities such as thermal imaging to track temperature changes in the coupling fluid and optical coherence tomography (OCT) integration for concurrent visualization of particle movement and tissue response.

The method involves placing the coated transducer in gentle contact with the cornea via a reservoir of coupling fluid, activating the acoustic field for a predetermined duration (e.g., 1 to 5 minutes), and allowing particles to migrate to nodal regions. Clinically, this enables several key applications: (1) clearing the central visual axis by aggregating particles peripherally, thereby improving visual acuity in conditions like hyphema or severe pigment dispersion; (2) protecting the trabecular meshwork by directing particles away from the iridocorneal angle or concentrating them in non-critical sectors, thus preserving aqueous outflow and preventing secondary glaucoma; (3) enhancing the efficacy of intraocular drug delivery by acoustically concentrating therapeutic particles (e.g., corticosteroid crystals) at target sites such as the ciliary body or corneal endothelium; and (4) aiding diagnosis by organizing unknown particles into characteristic patterns that may reveal size, density, or composition. The technique is reversible, repeatable, and does not require incisions, implants, or pharmacologic agents, thereby minimizing infection risk and patient discomfort. Experimental validation using porcine iris pigment particles and triamcinolone acetonide in enucleated porcine eyes confirmed successful particle manipulation into stable ring patterns without detectable thermal, mechanical, or histological damage to corneal or anterior segment tissues, even at the highest tested intensity (20 Vpp, 80 mW/cm²). Temperature rise in the coupling fluid was limited to less than 2.5°C over 60 seconds, and no signs of cavitation, endothelial disruption, or stromal edema were observed via OCT or histopathology. The invention thus establishes a new paradigm in ophthalmic therapeutics—one that treats the eye not just as a passive recipient of drugs or surgery, but as a dynamic environment where physical forces can be harnessed for precise, noninvasive intervention.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, illustrating how the system and method may be constructed, operated, and integrated into clinical practice. It should be understood that these embodiments are provided for illustrative purposes and do not limit the scope of the invention, which encompasses all equivalents and variations falling within the claims.

In one embodiment, the acoustic manipulation system comprises a radial-mode piezoelectric resonator constructed from type-II lead zirconate titanate (PZT), selected for its high electromechanical coupling coefficient and stability at megahertz frequencies. The resonator is cylindrical in geometry, with an inner diameter of 22 millimeters to accommodate the typical human corneal diameter (approximately 11–12 mm), an outer diameter of 26 millimeters to provide structural rigidity, and a length of 20 millimeters to ensure sufficient active surface area. The resonator is tuned to a center resonance frequency of 1.1 ± 0.1 MHz, a frequency chosen to balance penetration depth, spatial resolution of nodal patterns, and avoidance of cavitation thresholds in aqueous media. The resonator is connected to a signal generator capable of producing continuous sine waves with adjustable amplitude (1–10 Vpp) and frequency fine-tuning (±100 kHz around resonance), which is then amplified by a linear power amplifier with a bandwidth exceeding 3 MHz to preserve waveform fidelity. The total output amplitude delivered to the transducer ranges from 10 to 20 Vpp during operation, translating to an effective acoustic intensity of 20 to 80 mW/cm² when normalized to the vibrating surface area. This intensity range is deliberately maintained below 100 mW/cm² to comply with international safety standards for ophthalmic ultrasound and to minimize any risk of thermal or mechanical bioeffects.

To ensure patient safety and comfort, the transducer is coated with a biocompatible layer of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). The coating is formed by dipping the resonator first into a Sylgard™ 184 silicone elastomer base and then into a curing agent mixed in a 10:1 weight ratio, followed by thermal curing at 80°C for two hours. The resulting PDMS layer is approximately 70 micrometers thick, optically transparent, flexible enough to conform to minor corneal irregularities, and chemically inert, preventing leaching of PZT components or irritation of the ocular surface. During use, the coated transducer is placed in gentle contact with the cornea, with a reservoir of sterile normal saline solution filling the space between the transducer and the eye to serve as an acoustic coupling medium. This fluid layer ensures efficient transmission of acoustic energy into the anterior chamber while also acting as a thermal buffer.

The method of use begins with patient preparation: topical anesthetic drops are administered to minimize discomfort, and the eyelids are gently retracted using a speculum. The transducer, pre-filled with saline, is positioned over the cornea without applying excessive pressure. The acoustic field is then activated for a duration ranging from 1 to 5 minutes, depending on the particle type, concentration, and desired degree of aggregation. Real-time monitoring may be performed using slit-lamp biomicroscopy or integrated OCT to visualize particle movement. In experimental models, triamcinolone acetonide particles (average diameter 11.5 µm, density 1.3 g/cm³) aggregated into concentric rings within 30–60 seconds at 10 Vpp, while smaller iris pigment particles (<2 µm) required up to 2–3 minutes due to their lower volume and thus reduced acoustic force response (since acoustic force scales with particle volume). The spacing between adjacent rings was measured at approximately 0.75 ± 0.1 mm, consistent with the half-wavelength of a 1.1 MHz wave in aqueous humor (speed of sound ~1530 m/s). Once the desired pattern is achieved, the acoustic field is deactivated, and the particle aggregates remain stable due to gravitational settling and interparticle adhesion, resisting dispersion for extended periods.

Safety is ensured through multiple design and operational features. Thermal effects are mitigated by the low intensity and short duration of exposure: thermal imaging showed a maximum temperature rise of 2.5°C in the coupling fluid after 60 seconds at 20 Vpp, well below the 5–10°C threshold known to cause protein denaturation or endothelial damage. Mechanical effects such as cavitation are avoided by operating at high frequency (>1 MHz), where the rarefaction phase of the wave is too brief to nucleate stable bubbles. Histological and OCT evaluations of ex vivo porcine eyes exposed to the full protocol revealed no evidence of corneal edema, endothelial cell loss, stromal disruption, or lens opacification. Control eyes (unexposed) showed identical tissue architecture, confirming that observed effects were attributable to the procedure rather than postmortem changes.

Clinically, the system may be deployed in an outpatient setting for conditions such as acute hyphema following trauma, where red blood cells obscure vision and risk angle closure; pigment dispersion syndrome, where liberated iris pigment clogs the trabecular meshwork; or following intracameral injection of corticosteroids, where uncontrolled dispersion can cause IOP spikes. By aggregating particles into peripheral rings, the central visual axis is cleared within minutes, accelerating functional recovery. Moreover, by steering aggregates away from the superior or inferior angles—regions most susceptible to outflow obstruction—the method preserves aqueous drainage capacity. In advanced implementations, the transducer may be mounted on a motorized stage allowing controlled translation or rotation, enabling dynamic redirection of particle clusters to predefined safe zones. Alternatively, frequency sweeping could be used to shift nodal positions without moving the device. Future iterations may incorporate feedback from OCT or ultrasound biomicroscopy to auto-adjust parameters based on real-time particle response.

The invention also extends to therapeutic enhancement. For example, when triamcinolone acetonide is injected for uveitis or macular edema, acoustic manipulation could concentrate the drug near the ciliary body or posterior iris, increasing local bioavailability while reducing systemic exposure. Similarly, in corneal endothelial cell therapy—where cultured cells are injected into the anterior chamber—acoustic forces could organize cells into a monolayer over the host endothelium, improving engraftment efficiency. Diagnostic applications include differentiating particle types based on their migration speed and final pattern geometry, which correlate with size and acoustic contrast.

In summary, the disclosed embodiments represent a robust, safe, and versatile platform for noninvasive intraocular particle management. By harnessing fundamental acoustic principles in a clinically feasible configuration, the invention fills a critical therapeutic void and establishes a foundation for next-generation ophthalmic interventions based on physical field manipulation rather than chemical or surgical means.
