# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of quantum computing and, more specifically, to hybrid quantum-classical variational algorithms for solving combinatorial optimization problems on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) computers. The invention provides an improved methodology for optimizing parameterized quantum circuits used in Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) frameworks by replacing the conventional expectation value objective function with a Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) aggregation function. This approach is particularly advantageous for combinatorial optimization problems that can be mapped to diagonal Hamiltonians, where the primary goal is to increase the probability of sampling optimal or near-optimal solutions rather than minimizing the average energy of the quantum state. The invention addresses fundamental limitations of existing variational quantum algorithms when applied to classical optimization problems and provides a robust framework that demonstrates superior convergence properties and solution quality both in classical simulation and on actual quantum hardware.

## BACKGROUND

Combinatorial optimization problems represent a fundamental class of computational challenges that arise across numerous domains including logistics, finance, manufacturing, telecommunications, and artificial intelligence. These problems typically involve finding the best configuration from a finite but exponentially large set of possible solutions, subject to various constraints. Many combinatorial optimization problems are classified as NP-hard, meaning that no known classical algorithm can solve them efficiently for all instances as the problem size grows. Despite their computational complexity, such problems are routinely addressed in industrial applications using heuristic and approximation methods that seek good-quality solutions within practical time limits, even if global optimality cannot be guaranteed.

The advent of quantum computing has sparked significant interest in developing quantum algorithms that could potentially provide advantages over classical approaches for solving combinatorial optimization problems. While it is widely believed that quantum computers cannot solve NP-hard problems in polynomial time (i.e., NP ⊆ BQP), there remains considerable hope that quantum algorithms might find high-quality approximate solutions more efficiently than classical counterparts, particularly for practically relevant problem instances. Among the most promising candidates for implementation on current and near-term quantum hardware are hybrid quantum-classical variational algorithms, which combine the computational power of quantum processors with classical optimization techniques.

Two prominent examples of such hybrid algorithms are the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA). Both approaches utilize a parameterized quantum circuit, often referred to as a variational form or ansatz, to prepare a trial quantum state |ψ(θ)⟩, where θ represents a vector of tunable parameters. The quantum processor is used to prepare this state and perform measurements, while a classical optimizer adjusts the parameters θ to minimize a specific objective function derived from the measurement outcomes. In the standard formulation, this objective function corresponds to the expectation value ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩ of a problem Hamiltonian H, which encodes the combinatorial optimization problem of interest.

The mapping of combinatorial optimization problems to quantum Hamiltonians typically involves transforming binary decision variables into quantum spin operators. For instance, quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problems can be converted to Ising spin glass models by substituting binary variables x_i ∈ {0,1} with spin variables z_i ∈ {-1,+1} via the transformation x_i = (1 - z_i)/2. The resulting Ising Hamiltonian is then constructed by replacing each spin variable z_i with the Pauli Z operator σ_i^Z acting on the i-th qubit, and each product term z_i z_j with the tensor product σ_i^Z ⊗ σ_j^Z. This yields a diagonal Hamiltonian whose eigenvalues correspond to the objective function values of the original combinatorial optimization problem, and whose ground state represents an optimal solution.

In the VQE framework, the variational form is typically constructed using layers of single-qubit rotations interleaved with entangling gates such as controlled-Z operations. A common choice involves applying Y-rotations to all qubits followed by multiple layers of controlled-Z gates between qubit pairs and additional Y-rotations. This construction provides sufficient expressivity to represent any basis state, including the ground state of diagonal Hamiltonians, and the number of parameters scales linearly with both the number of qubits and the circuit depth. The classical optimization process estimates the expectation value by preparing the trial state multiple times, measuring the qubits to obtain bitstrings, computing the corresponding Hamiltonian values, and averaging these samples to approximate ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩.

QAOA, specifically designed for combinatorial optimization, employs a problem-dependent variational form constructed from alternating applications of two unitary operators: U_B(β) = e^{-iβ∑_i σ_i^X} and U_C(γ) = e^{-iγH}, where σ_i^X denotes the Pauli X operator on qubit i. Starting from a uniform superposition state created by Hadamard gates, the algorithm applies p layers of these unitaries with parameters β_k and γ_k for k = 1,...,p. The number of variational parameters in QAOA is 2p, independent of the number of qubits, which represents a significant difference from VQE. While QAOA possesses certain theoretical guarantees regarding approximation ratios for specific problems and converges to the optimal solution as p → ∞ under adiabatic evolution, its performance with small, fixed depth p has shown mixed results in practice.

A critical limitation of both VQE and QAOA in their standard formulations is their reliance on the expectation value as the optimization objective. While this choice is well-justified for quantum mechanical observables such as molecular energies in quantum chemistry applications, it may be suboptimal for combinatorial optimization problems with diagonal Hamiltonians. In such cases, the primary practical goal is not to minimize the average energy but rather to maximize the probability of sampling the ground state (or other high-quality solutions) upon measurement. The expectation value objective can be misleading because it averages over all possible measurement outcomes, potentially obscuring the presence of high-probability optimal solutions if they are accompanied by many poor-quality samples with slightly better average performance.

This fundamental mismatch between the optimization objective and the practical goal has been recognized as a significant weakness in the application of variational quantum algorithms to combinatorial optimization. Empirical studies have shown that VQE's performance suffers from this limitation, and while QAOA offers certain theoretical advantages, its practical performance on current hardware remains questionable. Furthermore, the limited number of variational parameters in QAOA, particularly for small depth p, can lead to "flat" quantum states where amplitudes are distributed relatively uniformly across basis states, resulting in low probability of sampling optimal solutions despite potentially good average objective values.

The need for improved optimization strategies that better align with the practical objectives of combinatorial optimization has motivated the development of alternative aggregation functions for processing measurement samples. Previous attempts to address this issue have included various heuristic modifications, but none have provided a systematic, theoretically grounded approach that consistently improves performance across different problem classes and hardware platforms. The present invention addresses this critical gap by introducing a novel optimization framework based on Conditional Value-at-Risk, which focuses optimization efforts on the tail of the probability distribution corresponding to the best measurement outcomes, thereby directly targeting the practical goal of increasing the likelihood of obtaining high-quality solutions.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides an improved method for solving combinatorial optimization problems using hybrid quantum-classical variational algorithms on noisy intermediate-scale quantum computers. The core innovation lies in replacing the conventional expectation value objective function with a Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) aggregation function during the classical optimization phase of variational quantum algorithms such as Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA).

In accordance with the invention, a combinatorial optimization problem is first mapped to a diagonal Hamiltonian H through established transformations such as converting quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problems to Ising spin glass models. A parameterized quantum circuit (variational form) is then employed to prepare a trial quantum state |ψ(θ)⟩, where θ represents a vector of tunable parameters. Instead of minimizing the expectation value ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩ as in conventional approaches, the present invention minimizes the CVaR_α of the random variable X(θ) representing the Hamiltonian values obtained from measurements of the trial state, where α ∈ (0,1] is a confidence level parameter.

The CVaR_α is defined as the expected value of the lower α-tail of the probability distribution of X(θ), effectively focusing optimization on the best α fraction of measurement outcomes rather than the overall average. This approach directly addresses the practical goal of combinatorial optimization, which is to maximize the probability of sampling optimal or near-optimal solutions, rather than minimizing the average energy of the quantum state. The invention provides a systematic framework that bridges the gap between theoretical quantum algorithm design and practical optimization objectives.

The method according to the invention comprises the following steps: (a) encoding a combinatorial optimization problem as a diagonal Hamiltonian H acting on n qubits; (b) selecting a variational form U(θ) capable of generating trial quantum states |ψ(θ)⟩ = U(θ)|0⟩^⊗n; (c) for each iteration of the classical optimization algorithm, preparing the trial state |ψ(θ)⟩ on a quantum processor, performing K measurements to obtain samples {H_k(θ)}_{k=1}^K of the Hamiltonian values; (d) computing the CVaR_α of these samples by sorting them in non-decreasing order and calculating the average of the first ⌈αK⌉ samples; (e) using this CVaR_α value as the objective function for the classical optimizer to update the parameters θ; and (f) repeating steps (c)-(e) until convergence criteria are met, then returning the best measurement outcome observed across all iterations as the solution to the combinatorial optimization problem.

The invention demonstrates several key advantages over conventional approaches. First, it provides faster convergence to better solutions across multiple classes of combinatorial optimization problems, including maximum stable set, maximum 3-satisfiability, number partitioning, maximum cut, market split, and portfolio optimization. Second, it exhibits improved robustness on actual quantum hardware, where noise and errors can significantly impact algorithm performance. Third, it offers a tunable parameter α that allows practitioners to balance between exploration (higher α values approaching the expectation value) and exploitation (lower α values focusing on the best outcomes), with empirical evidence suggesting that α ∈ [0.01, 0.25] provides optimal performance for most problem instances.

Furthermore, the invention provides analytical insights into the behavior of variational quantum algorithms, particularly regarding the relationship between circuit expressivity and solution quality. The analysis reveals that QAOA with small, fixed depth p may produce relatively "flat" quantum states due to its limited number of variational parameters (2p independent of problem size), leading to low probability of sampling optimal solutions despite potentially good average objective values. This explains the observed performance differences between VQE and QAOA in practical implementations and suggests that increasing circuit depth or parameter count may be necessary to achieve meaningful solution probabilities.

The invention is applicable to both simulated and physical quantum computing platforms and has been validated through extensive numerical experiments on classical simulators as well as demonstrations on IBM's quantum hardware. The results consistently show that CVaR optimization leads to marked improvements in both convergence speed and solution quality compared to conventional expectation value minimization, making it particularly well-suited for implementation on current noisy quantum devices where circuit depth and coherence times are limited.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for improving the performance of hybrid quantum-classical variational algorithms in solving combinatorial optimization problems on noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) computers. The detailed description that follows outlines the complete methodology, including problem encoding, quantum circuit construction, classical optimization procedures, and practical implementation considerations.

The foundation of the invention begins with the proper encoding of combinatorial optimization problems into quantum Hamiltonians. Consider a general quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problem defined as:

minimize x^T A x + b^T x

subject to x ∈ {0,1}^n

where A ∈ ℝ^{n×n} is a symmetric matrix and b ∈ ℝ^n is a vector. This formulation encompasses a wide range of combinatorial optimization problems and can be systematically transformed into an equivalent Ising spin glass model through the variable substitution x_i = (1 - z_i)/2, where z_i ∈ {-1,+1}. This transformation yields:

minimize c + ∑_{i=1}^n Q_{ii} z_i + ∑_{i<j} Q_{ij} z_i z_j

where the constants c and coefficients Q_{ij} are computed directly from the original QUBO parameters A and b. The resulting Ising Hamiltonian H is then constructed by replacing each spin variable z_i with the Pauli Z operator σ_i^Z acting on the i-th qubit, and each product term z_i z_j with the tensor product σ_i^Z ⊗ σ_j^Z. This produces a diagonal Hamiltonian whose eigenvalues correspond exactly to the objective function values of the original combinatorial optimization problem, and whose ground state represents an optimal solution.

For the variational quantum algorithm implementation, two primary approaches are supported: Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) and Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA). In the VQE framework according to the invention, the variational form U(θ) is constructed as follows. Starting with n qubits initialized in the |0⟩ state, the circuit begins with a layer of single-qubit Y-rotations R_Y(θ_{0,i}) applied to each qubit i, where θ_{0,i} represents the rotation angle for qubit i. This initial layer is followed by p repetitions of the following sequence: application of controlled-Z gates between all pairs of qubits (i,j) with i < j, followed by another layer of single-qubit Y-rotations R_Y(θ_{k,i}) for each qubit i and repetition k ∈ {1,...,p}. The total number of variational parameters is n(1+p), providing sufficient expressivity to represent any basis state, including the ground state of diagonal Hamiltonians. When implementing on actual quantum hardware with limited connectivity, the controlled-Z gates are restricted to nearest-neighbor qubit pairs according to the device topology, which reduces the number of two-qubit gates while maintaining the essential structure of the variational form.

In the QAOA framework according to the invention, the variational form is constructed problem-dependently from the Hamiltonian H. The circuit begins with a layer of Hadamard gates applied to all qubits to create a uniform superposition state. This is followed by p layers of alternating unitary operations U_B(β_k) and U_C(γ_k), where:

U_B(β_k) = e^{-iβ_k ∑_{i=1}^n σ_i^X}
U_C(γ_k) = e^{-iγ_k H}

Here, σ_i^X denotes the Pauli X operator on qubit i, and β_k, γ_k ∈ ℝ are the variational parameters for layer k. The implementation of U_B(β_k) requires single-qubit X-rotations R_X(-2β_k) applied to each qubit. The implementation of U_C(γ_k) depends on the structure of H: for each non-zero diagonal coefficient in H, appropriate single-qubit Z-rotations and two-qubit controlled operations are applied. Specifically, terms of the form e^{-iγ σ_i^Z ⊗ σ_j^Z} can be implemented using two CNOT gates and one single-qubit Z-rotation R_Z(-2γ), up to a global phase. The total number of variational parameters in QAOA is 2p, independent of the number of qubits, which represents a fundamental difference from the VQE approach.

The classical optimization procedure according to the invention replaces the conventional expectation value objective with the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) aggregation function. For a given set of parameters θ, the quantum processor prepares the trial state |ψ(θ)⟩ and performs K measurements, yielding K bitstrings that are converted to Hamiltonian values {H_k(θ)}_{k=1}^K. These samples are sorted in non-decreasing order: H_{(1)}(θ) ≤ H_{(2)}(θ) ≤ ... ≤ H_{(K)}(θ). The CVaR_α objective function is then computed as:

CVaR_α({H_k(θ)}) = (1/⌈αK⌉) ∑_{k=1}^{⌈αK⌉} H_{(k)}(θ)

where α ∈ (0,1] is the confidence level parameter. This formulation effectively focuses the optimization on the best α fraction of measurement outcomes, directly targeting the practical goal of increasing the probability of sampling high-quality solutions.

The classical optimizer uses this CVaR_α value as the objective function to update the parameters θ. Various optimization algorithms can be employed, including derivative-free methods such as COBYLA (Constrained Optimization BY Linear Approximations), which has demonstrated good performance in the context of variational quantum algorithms. The optimization process continues until convergence criteria are met, which may include reaching a maximum number of iterations, achieving a sufficiently small change in the objective function between successive iterations, or observing that the probability of sampling optimal solutions has plateaued.

Upon completion of the optimization process, the solution to the combinatorial optimization problem is determined by examining all measurement outcomes obtained throughout the optimization procedure and selecting the bitstring corresponding to the minimum Hamiltonian value observed. This approach ensures that even if the final optimized state does not yield the best solution, previously observed high-quality samples are not discarded.

The invention provides specific guidance for selecting the confidence level parameter α. Theoretical analysis indicates that the variance of the empirical CVaR_α estimator scales as O(1/(Kα²)), suggesting that for fixed accuracy, the number of samples should be increased by a factor of 1/α compared to expectation value estimation. However, empirical evidence from extensive numerical experiments across multiple problem classes demonstrates that choosing α ∈ [0.01, 0.25] provides optimal performance trade-offs between convergence speed and solution quality. This range of α values focuses sufficiently on the best outcomes while maintaining enough samples to provide a smooth, well-behaved objective function for the classical optimizer.

The practical implementation of the invention on actual quantum hardware requires careful consideration of device-specific constraints and error characteristics. When deploying on NISQ devices, the number of samples K should be chosen to balance between statistical accuracy and available quantum resources. Typical values range from 8,192 to 32,768 samples per parameter evaluation, depending on the problem size and hardware capabilities. The initial parameters θ can be set to zero or randomized within appropriate bounds to avoid local minima, though fixing initial parameters to zero has demonstrated consistent performance in experimental validation.

The invention has been validated through comprehensive numerical experiments on six distinct classes of combinatorial optimization problems: maximum stable set, maximum 3-satisfiability, number partitioning, maximum cut, market split, and portfolio optimization. For each problem class, multiple random instances were generated across different problem sizes (6-16 qubits), and the performance of CVaR-VQE and CVaR-QAOA was compared against conventional expectation value minimization across various circuit depths and α values.

The experimental results consistently demonstrate that CVaR optimization provides significant improvements in both convergence speed and solution quality. For CVaR-VQE with depth p=2 and α=1%, nearly all test instances achieved at least 1% probability of sampling an optimal solution within 50 normalized iterations (iterations divided by number of qubits), compared to only 60% of instances with conventional expectation value minimization (α=100%). Similarly, CVaR-QAOA showed improved performance with decreasing α and increasing circuit depth, though its performance remained generally inferior to VQE for equivalent effective depth due to its limited parameter count.

Hardware demonstrations on IBM's quantum processors confirmed that the benefits of CVaR optimization persist in the presence of realistic noise and errors. Experiments on a 6-qubit portfolio optimization problem implemented on the IBM Q Poughkeepsie 20-qubit device showed that CVaR-VQE with α=10% and α=25% achieved substantially higher probabilities of sampling optimal solutions compared to conventional approaches, with the smaller α values demonstrating earlier and more consistent convergence. The CVaR objective function's ability to focus on the best measurement outcomes appears to provide inherent robustness against quantum noise, as it effectively ignores low-quality samples that may result from hardware imperfections.

The analytical framework developed as part of the invention provides important insights into the behavior of variational quantum algorithms, particularly regarding the relationship between circuit expressivity and solution quality. The analysis reveals that QAOA with small, fixed depth p may produce "flat" quantum states where amplitudes are distributed relatively uniformly across basis states, leading to exponentially small overlap with optimal solutions despite potentially good average objective values. This phenomenon occurs when the Hamiltonian's diagonal elements are insufficiently diverse to enable effective amplitude amplification through the limited number of QAOA parameters. The mathematical characterization shows that for Hamiltonians where a large fraction δ of basis states share the same eigenvalue, QAOA with small depth p necessarily produces states with amplitudes that are exponentially small in the number of qubits.

This analytical understanding explains the observed performance differences between VQE and QAOA in practical implementations and suggests that increasing circuit depth or parameter count may be necessary to achieve meaningful solution probabilities. The invention's CVaR optimization framework directly addresses this challenge by focusing optimization efforts on improving the tail of the probability distribution, thereby increasing the likelihood of sampling high-quality solutions even when the overall state remains relatively flat.

The invention also provides practical guidance for implementation across different problem scales and hardware platforms. For small problem instances (≤10 qubits), the benefits of CVaR optimization may be less pronounced due to ceiling effects where conventional methods already perform well. However, as problem size increases, the advantages of CVaR become increasingly evident, with the performance gap between CVaR optimization and conventional approaches widening significantly. This scaling behavior suggests that CVaR optimization will be particularly valuable for larger, more challenging problem instances that are likely to be encountered in practical applications.

The software implementation of the invention has been made available through open-source libraries, facilitating widespread adoption and further research. The methodology integrates seamlessly with existing quantum computing frameworks such as Qiskit, requiring only minimal modifications to replace the expectation value objective with the CVaR aggregation function. This compatibility ensures that the invention can be readily applied to existing variational quantum algorithm implementations without requiring substantial changes to the underlying quantum circuit structures or classical optimization infrastructure.

In summary, the detailed description of the invention provides a complete, practical framework for implementing improved variational quantum algorithms for combinatorial optimization. The methodology addresses fundamental limitations of conventional approaches by aligning the optimization objective with practical goals, demonstrates consistent performance improvements across diverse problem classes and hardware platforms, and provides both theoretical understanding and practical guidance for successful implementation. The invention represents a significant advancement in the application of quantum computing to real-world optimization challenges, particularly in the context of current and near-term quantum hardware limitations.
