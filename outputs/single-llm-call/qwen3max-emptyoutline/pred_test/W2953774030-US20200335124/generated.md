# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The proliferation of conversational artificial intelligence systems, commonly referred to as chatbots or bots, has significantly transformed the landscape of customer service, technical support, sales engagement, and internal enterprise operations. These systems are increasingly deployed across a wide range of industries—including banking, healthcare, human resources, information technology, travel, and retail—where they interact with users in natural language to resolve inquiries, execute transactions, or guide users through complex workflows. As reliance on such systems grows, so too does the need for robust mechanisms to evaluate, monitor, and improve their performance. Unlike traditional software applications, conversational systems exhibit behavior that is highly contextual, dynamic, and dependent on both their underlying design and the patterns of user interaction. This complexity necessitates novel analytical approaches that go beyond conventional metrics such as uptime or response latency, and instead focus on the semantic and structural fidelity of the conversations themselves.

In commercial practice, most bot development platforms represent the conversational logic of a bot as a directed graph. In this paradigm, each node in the graph corresponds to a specific intent or decision point, often associated with a user goal (e.g., “report a technical issue” or “check account balance”). The edges emanating from a node encode the flow of control based on whether the user’s utterance matches the expected intent. Typically, a positive edge leads to a follow-up node that refines the interaction (e.g., asking for more details), while a negative edge routes the conversation to alternative intents or fallback mechanisms. Special nodes, such as root nodes (which initiate the conversation) and sink nodes (which handle unrecognized inputs), further structure the dialogue flow. This graph-based representation enables developers to visually design and debug conversational paths, making it intuitive for non-programmers to build and maintain bots. However, while this abstraction is effective for development, it presents challenges when attempting to analyze bot behavior at scale—especially across thousands of independently developed bots hosted on a single platform.

A critical challenge in bot analytics is the lack of standardized, machine-readable representations that capture the essence of a bot’s behavior in a way that is both generalizable and task-agnostic. Traditional approaches to bot evaluation often rely on handcrafted features derived from conversation logs, such as the number of turns per conversation, average response length, or frequency of fallback responses. While these metrics offer some insight, they fail to encode the structural richness of the bot’s decision graph or the semantic patterns of successful versus failed interactions. Moreover, such features are typically computed per bot in isolation, ignoring the potential for cross-bot learning—i.e., leveraging patterns observed across multiple bots to infer higher-level behavioral traits. This limitation is particularly acute in large-scale bot platforms where hundreds or thousands of bots coexist, each serving different domains but sharing common architectural and interactional principles.

Recent advances in representation learning have demonstrated the power of embedding techniques to transform complex, structured data into dense, low-dimensional vectors that preserve semantic or functional relationships. For example, Word2Vec and related models have shown that words appearing in similar contexts can be mapped to nearby points in a continuous vector space, enabling analogical reasoning and transfer learning. Similarly, graph embedding methods such as DeepWalk and Node2Vec have enabled the representation of nodes and entire graphs based on their topological neighborhoods. However, these approaches are largely static: they treat graphs as fixed structures and do not account for the dynamic, path-dependent nature of real-world interactions. In the context of conversational bots, the true behavior of a system is not fully captured by its static graph topology alone; rather, it emerges from the actual sequences of nodes traversed during live conversations. Two bots with identical graph structures may exhibit vastly different behaviors if one is frequently used in production while the other remains in testing, or if users interact with them in divergent ways.

This gap between static structure and dynamic usage motivates the need for a new class of bot representations that integrate both the architectural blueprint of the bot and the empirical traces of its real-world operation. Such representations would enable a range of downstream applications, including the identification of bots that are actively serving real users (as opposed to those in development or testing), the detection of problematic or “egregious” conversations that require immediate attention, and the clustering of bots by domain or functionality for benchmarking and best-practice dissemination. Critically, these tasks cannot be reliably solved using surface-level features or content-only analyses, as they depend on understanding how the bot’s internal logic is exercised over time. For instance, a bot may generate grammatically correct responses yet consistently misroute users due to poorly designed intent classifiers—a flaw that is only apparent when analyzing the sequence of node visits during conversations.

Prior work in conversational AI has largely focused on improving the quality of individual bot responses, often through end-to-end neural models trained on dialogue history. While effective for response generation, these models do not yield interpretable or reusable representations of the bot as a whole. Other studies have proposed quality metrics for chatbot evaluation, such as engagement duration or coherence scores, but these are typically applied post-hoc to conversation transcripts and do not incorporate the bot’s internal structure. Furthermore, no existing method provides a unified framework for representing bots in a way that supports both bot-level and conversation-level classification tasks across diverse domains. The absence of such a framework hinders the development of intelligent platform-level tools that could automatically diagnose issues, recommend improvements, or prioritize maintenance efforts across a large bot ecosystem.

In light of these limitations, there is a pressing need for a novel, scalable, and generalizable approach to bot representation that captures both the structural design and the dynamic usage patterns of conversational systems. This approach must be capable of learning from raw conversation logs and bot graph definitions without requiring manual feature engineering, and it must produce embeddings that are meaningful across different analytical tasks. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a dual-path representation learning framework—referred to herein as BOT2VEC—that generates vector embeddings for bots based on either the textual content of their conversations or the structural paths traversed during those conversations. By training a neural network to predict the identity of a bot from a representation of one of its conversations, the model learns to compress the essential behavioral characteristics of each bot into a fixed-length vector. These embeddings, once learned, can be used as input features for a variety of classification and clustering tasks, enabling more effective bot management, quality assurance, and performance optimization at scale.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for generating bot embeddings—compact, numerical representations of conversational agents—that capture the behavioral characteristics of bots based on either their structural design or the content of their interactions with users. These embeddings, collectively referred to as BOT2VEC representations, are produced by a neural network trained to predict the identity of a bot given a representation of one of its conversations. The invention introduces two distinct embedding models: a content-based model (BOT2VEC-C) and a structure-based model (BOT2VEC-S). The content-based model derives bot representations from the natural language content of conversations, specifically by encoding the term frequencies of user utterances and bot responses into a concatenated vector. The structure-based model, in contrast, represents conversations as sequences of paths traversed through the bot’s underlying graph structure, which are then aggregated into a standardized bin vector that encodes the frequency and type of node visits (e.g., success, failure, or regular traversal). Both models are trained using a supervised classification objective in which the network learns to map conversation representations to the correct bot identifier, thereby inducing embeddings that reflect the distinguishing behavioral patterns of each bot.

The resulting BOT2VEC embeddings are designed to serve as general-purpose features for a variety of bot analytics tasks. In particular, the invention demonstrates their utility in two key classification problems: (1) detecting whether a bot is in active production use with real users, as opposed to being in a testing or debugging phase; and (2) identifying egregious conversations—those in which the bot’s behavior is so deficient that human intervention would be necessary to salvage the interaction. Experimental results show that the structure-based BOT2VEC-S model achieves a relative improvement of 18.6% over a strong baseline in the production bot detection task and a 16.4% improvement in the egregious conversation detection task, significantly outperforming both the content-based model and conventional handcrafted features. These gains underscore the value of incorporating dynamic structural information—i.e., how the bot’s graph is actually traversed during real conversations—into the representation learning process.

A key innovation of the invention lies in its ability to standardize structural representations across bots with heterogeneous graph topologies. Because different bots may have vastly different numbers of nodes, depths, and branching factors, a direct comparison of their raw graph structures is infeasible. To overcome this, the invention introduces a bin vector abstraction that maps nodes from any bot graph into a fixed-dimensional vector space based on their depth and position within the graph. This mapping is informed by aggregate statistics across all bots in the dataset—such as average graph depth and average width per level—ensuring that the resulting representation is both compact and comparable. Each bin in the vector maintains counters for different types of node interactions (success, failure, regular, and uninvolved), allowing the model to distinguish between productive and problematic conversation paths.

Furthermore, the invention enables cross-bot learning by treating the entire population of bots on a platform as a shared resource for representation learning. Rather than modeling each bot in isolation, the neural network is trained on conversations from all bots simultaneously, forcing it to learn a common embedding space in which similar bots—whether by domain, functionality, or usage pattern—are mapped to nearby points. This global perspective facilitates the discovery of latent behavioral similarities that may not be evident from individual bot analysis. For example, bots in the same domain (e.g., IT support or banking) are shown to have more similar structure-based embeddings than bots from different domains, as measured by cosine distance, validating the semantic coherence of the learned representations.

The invention is implemented as a scalable, end-to-end pipeline that integrates seamlessly with existing bot development platforms. It requires only two inputs: (1) the graph definition of each bot, encoded in a standard format that includes node intents, replies, and edge connections; and (2) historical conversation logs that record the sequence of user utterances and bot responses, along with metadata indicating which bot handled each conversation. From these inputs, the system automatically constructs either content-based or structure-based conversation representations, trains the embedding model, and outputs a fixed-length vector for each bot. These vectors can then be exported and used as features in any downstream machine learning task, including but not limited to bot classification, anomaly detection, clustering, and performance benchmarking.

In summary, the present invention provides a novel, data-driven framework for bot representation that bridges the gap between static design and dynamic usage. By learning embeddings that reflect how bots are actually used in real-world interactions, the invention enables more accurate, efficient, and insightful analysis of conversational systems at scale. This capability is particularly valuable for bot platform providers, enterprise developers, and quality assurance teams seeking to optimize the performance and reliability of their conversational AI deployments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for generating bot embeddings that capture the behavioral characteristics of conversational agents through representation learning. The system operates by training a neural network to predict the identity of a bot based on a representation of one of its conversations, thereby inducing a vector embedding for each bot that reflects its unique interaction patterns. The invention supports two complementary approaches to conversation representation: a content-based approach that leverages the natural language text of user-bot interactions, and a structure-based approach that encodes the dynamic traversal of the bot’s underlying graph architecture during conversations. Both approaches are described in detail below, along with the architecture of the embedding model, the training procedure, and the application of the resulting embeddings to downstream classification tasks.

The foundation of the invention lies in the observation that a bot’s behavior is jointly determined by its structural design and its empirical usage. Most commercial bot development platforms represent a bot as a directed graph, wherein each node corresponds to a specific intent or decision point, and edges define the flow of control based on intent classification outcomes. A conversation with the bot is thus equivalent to a sequence of paths traversed through this graph, where each path corresponds to the processing of a single user utterance. The root node initiates the conversation, and subsequent nodes are evaluated in sequence—typically along vertical negative edges for alternative intents and horizontal positive edges for intent refinement—until a match is found or a sink node is reached. This graph-based paradigm is both expressive and intuitive, enabling developers to construct complex dialogue flows without writing code. However, the static graph alone does not reveal how the bot is actually used; for that, one must examine the logs of real conversations.

To bridge this gap, the invention introduces a structure-based representation that transforms each conversation into a standardized vector capturing the frequency and type of node visits across the bot’s graph. This process begins with the construction of a common bin vector—a fixed-dimensional array that serves as a universal coordinate system for mapping nodes from any bot graph. The dimensions of the bin vector are determined by aggregate statistics computed across all bots in the training set. Specifically, the number of sections in the bin vector corresponds to the average depth of the bot graphs (e.g., seven sections for an average depth of seven levels), and the number of bins within each section is set to the average width of the corresponding graph level (e.g., 108 bins for level one, 10 for level two, and so on). This ensures that the representation is both compact and representative of the typical bot structure on the platform.

Each node in a bot’s graph is assigned coordinates based on its position in the graph hierarchy. For example, a node at depth one (i.e., directly reachable from the root) is assigned a single coordinate, such as (4), indicating it is the fourth node at that level. A node at depth two is assigned a pair of coordinates, such as (4,1), indicating it is the first child of the fourth node at depth one. These coordinates are then mapped to specific bins within the bin vector using a deterministic algorithm that groups nodes by depth and position. Once the mapping is established, each conversation is processed turn by turn. For each user utterance, the system reconstructs the path taken through the bot’s graph and classifies each visited node as one of four types: (1) a success node, if it is the final node in the path and is not a sink; (2) a failure node, if it is the final node and is a sink; (3) a regular node, if it is an intermediate node in the path; or (4) an uninvolved node, if it was not visited during the traversal. For each bin in the vector, four counters are maintained—one for each node type—and these counters are incremented accordingly as the path is processed. After all utterances in the conversation have been processed, the individual turn vectors are aggregated by summing the counters across all turns, yielding a single conversation-level bin vector that encapsulates the structural footprint of the interaction.

In parallel, the invention provides a content-based representation that encodes the linguistic content of the conversation. This representation begins with the construction of a global vocabulary comprising the most frequent terms across all conversations in the dataset, excluding tokens that may reveal bot identity (e.g., bot names, URLs, or HTML tags). For each conversation, two term frequency (TF) vectors are created: one from the concatenation of all user utterances, and another from the concatenation of all bot responses. These vectors are then concatenated to form a single content-based representation of the conversation. To enhance the interaction signal between user and bot, the system also computes the element-wise product and squared difference of the two TF vectors, which are then concatenated with the original vectors and passed through a series of fully connected layers to produce a refined content embedding.

The core of the invention is a neural network architecture that learns bot embeddings by predicting bot identity from conversation representations. The network consists of multiple fully connected layers with ReLU activations and dropout regularization (e.g., a dropout rate of 0.5) to prevent overfitting. For the content-based model (BOT2VEC-C), the input is the concatenated TF vector (augmented with interaction features), which is passed through layers of decreasing dimensionality (e.g., 5000, 1000, and 100 units) before reaching the output layer. For the structure-based model (BOT2VEC-S), the input is the 616-dimensional bin vector (154 bins × 4 counters), which is passed through two hidden layers (e.g., 100 and 20 units). The output layer has a size equal to the number of bots in the training set and uses a softmax activation to produce a probability distribution over bot identities. The network is trained using cross-entropy loss and optimized with the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.001).

Once training is complete, the bot embedding for each bot is extracted as the column of the output weight matrix corresponding to that bot. This embedding resides in the space of the last hidden layer and captures the features that the network has learned to associate with the bot’s identity. Because the training objective encourages the network to distinguish between bots based on their conversation patterns, the resulting embeddings naturally encode the behavioral differences between bots. Bots that handle similar conversations—whether in terms of content or structure—will have similar embeddings, while dissimilar bots will be farther apart in the embedding space.

The utility of these embeddings is demonstrated through two classification tasks. In the first task, the goal is to classify bots as either “production” or “non-production” based on their conversation logs. Production bots are those actively serving real users, while non-production bots are used for testing or debugging. Ground truth labels are obtained by having expert annotators review sampled conversations and reach consensus on the bot’s status. The BOT2VEC embeddings are used as input features to a support vector machine (SVM) classifier, which is evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation. The structure-based model achieves an F1-score that is 18.6% higher than a baseline model using 17 handcrafted features (e.g., number of unique utterances, conversation length statistics), demonstrating the superiority of learned structural representations.

In the second task, the goal is to detect egregious conversations—those in which the bot’s behavior is so poor that human intervention would be required. Ground truth is obtained by annotating conversations from production bots using a standardized protocol. The BOT2VEC embeddings are concatenated with the feature vector of an existing state-of-the-art model (EGR) to enrich its input. Again, the structure-based model yields the best performance, with a 16.4% relative improvement in F1-score over the EGR baseline, confirming that structural information about the bot enhances conversation-level quality assessment.

Finally, the invention validates the semantic coherence of the structure-based embeddings by analyzing cosine distances between bots in the same domain versus different domains. Bots from domains such as IT, HR, and banking exhibit significantly smaller average pairwise distances (0.614) than cross-domain pairs (0.694), indicating that the embeddings capture domain-specific behavioral patterns. This property makes the embeddings suitable for applications such as bot clustering, benchmarking, and knowledge transfer across similar systems.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a comprehensive, scalable, and empirically validated framework for bot representation that leverages both content and structure to generate meaningful embeddings. These embeddings enable more effective analysis, monitoring, and improvement of conversational systems across a wide range of practical applications.
