# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of aluminum production via the Hall-Héroult electrolytic process and, more specifically, to carbon anodes employed in such processes. In particular, the invention provides a method for significantly reducing the air reactivity of carbon anodes used in aluminum smelting cells by means of a boron-based impregnation treatment that forms a protective barrier within the porous structure of the anode. The invention further encompasses treated anodes exhibiting enhanced resistance to oxidative degradation when exposed to ambient air at elevated temperatures typical of industrial electrolysis environments, as well as methods for characterizing and optimizing the impregnation process to achieve maximum protection efficiency. The technical domain spans materials science, electrochemical engineering, and industrial metallurgy, with specific relevance to carbon composite materials, oxidation inhibition mechanisms, and non-destructive evaluation techniques such as X-ray computed tomography for microstructural analysis of porous media under reactive conditions.

## BACKGROUND

In the conventional Hall-Héroult process for primary aluminum production, carbon anodes serve as consumable electrodes that participate directly in the electrochemical reduction of alumina dissolved in molten cryolite. The overall cell reaction involves the consumption of carbon anodes to produce carbon dioxide gas alongside molten aluminum metal. However, a significant portion of anode carbon is consumed not through the desired electrochemical pathway but rather through parasitic oxidation reactions occurring on the portion of the anode exposed to ambient air above the electrolyte bath. This air-exposed region typically operates within a temperature range of approximately 400°C to 600°C during normal cell operation, a regime in which thermodynamically favorable oxidation reactions between atmospheric oxygen and carbon readily proceed. Specifically, the carbon anode may undergo complete oxidation to carbon dioxide or partial oxidation to carbon monoxide, both of which represent non-productive consumption of anode material and contribute to increased operational costs, reduced current efficiency, and environmental emissions.

Carbon anodes used in industrial aluminum smelting are typically fabricated from a mixture of calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode butts, and coal tar pitch binder, which is then formed and baked at temperatures around 1200°C. The resulting composite exhibits a heterogeneous microstructure comprising coarse coke aggregates embedded within a finer binder matrix. It has been well established in the literature that the binder phase demonstrates significantly higher susceptibility to oxidative attack compared to the larger coke particles. This differential reactivity leads to preferential consumption of the binder matrix, resulting in the detachment and disintegration of unreacted coke grains—a phenomenon commonly referred to as “dusting.” Dusting not only exacerbates anode overconsumption but also introduces particulate contaminants into the smelting environment, potentially interfering with cell operation and product purity.

Numerous strategies have been proposed to mitigate air reactivity in carbon anodes, broadly categorized into physical barrier approaches and intrinsic reactivity modification. Physical barrier methods aim to isolate the anode surface from direct contact with atmospheric oxygen. For instance, some smelters practice covering freshly installed anodes with a layer of solidified electrolyte bath or loose alumina powder. While these measures provide marginal protection by creating a diffusion-limited environment around the anode, their porous nature renders them ineffective as long-term oxidation barriers. More sophisticated coating technologies have also been explored, including the application of alumina-based slurries or suspensions containing refractory compounds. Notably, the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation (CSIRO) disclosed a proprietary coating claimed to maintain integrity throughout the anode’s service life; however, no widespread industrial adoption of this technology has been reported. Similarly, Alcan patented a protective coating comprising a suspension of boron compounds, aluminum fluoride, and lignosulfonate binder applied via spraying, which demonstrated efficacy in laboratory-scale tests but has not seen commercial implementation.

An alternative approach focuses on chemically modifying the carbon structure to reduce its intrinsic reactivity toward oxygen. Doping with heteroatoms such as phosphorus or boron has been investigated for this purpose. Phosphorus doping, while effective in inhibiting oxidation, is precluded in aluminum smelting due to its detrimental impact on cathodic current efficiency and potential contamination of the final aluminum product. Boron doping, on the other hand, remains a viable candidate. Proposed mechanisms for boron’s protective effect include electronic structure modification through substitutional incorporation into the graphite lattice, enhancement of graphitization during thermal treatment, and formation of a surface-protective boron oxide film that blocks active oxidation sites. However, practical limitations hinder the applicability of the first two mechanisms in industrial anodes: the baking temperature (typically ≤1200°C) is insufficient to induce significant graphitization, and the levels of boron required for electronic effects (often exceeding 1000 ppm) would be prohibitively expensive and risk aluminum contamination upon reduction in the electrolytic bath.

The most promising avenue involves the formation of a boron oxide barrier via solution-based impregnation. Moltech Corporation filed multiple patents describing the use of aqueous boron oxide solutions to impregnate baked anodes, optionally combined with other functional additives. Laboratory studies, including those conducted by the present inventors, confirm that such treatments can dramatically reduce air reactivity. Despite these encouraging results, the technique has not been adopted industrially, likely due to scalability challenges and insufficient understanding of the underlying protection mechanism—particularly regarding how impregnation parameters influence performance in large-scale anodes. Conventional reactivity testing protocols, which rely on small cylindrical samples (e.g., 50 mm diameter × 100 mm length) and report only total mass loss after fixed-duration exposure, fail to capture critical aspects such as reaction kinetics, spatial progression of oxidation, and the relationship between impregnation depth and protection efficacy. These limitations obscure the true behavior of treated anodes under realistic conditions and impede process optimization.

Moreover, prior art lacks comprehensive characterization of how boron distribution within the anode microstructure affects oxidation morphology. Without insight into whether protection is confined to the surface or extends into the bulk, it is difficult to design impregnation protocols suitable for full-sized anodes, which possess significantly greater thickness and lower permeability than laboratory specimens. The absence of real-time monitoring of mass loss during reactivity tests further compounds this knowledge gap, as transient behaviors—such as initial rapid oxidation followed by passivation or progressive internal degradation—are masked in endpoint measurements. Consequently, there remains a critical need for a scientifically grounded, scalable method to protect carbon anodes against air oxidation, supported by robust analytical techniques that elucidate the spatial and temporal dynamics of the protection mechanism.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and industrially viable method for protecting carbon anodes used in aluminum smelting against oxidative degradation caused by exposure to air at elevated temperatures. The core of the invention lies in a controlled impregnation process wherein baked carbon anodes are treated with an aqueous solution containing a boron compound—preferably boric acid or boron oxide—that, upon drying, deposits boron species within the anode’s pore network. This deposited boron forms a protective barrier that significantly reduces the rate of carbon oxidation by atmospheric oxygen, thereby minimizing non-productive anode consumption and dust generation during electrolysis.

A key aspect of the invention is the identification and optimization of critical impregnation parameters that govern the efficacy of the protective treatment. Through systematic experimentation, the inventors have discovered that impregnation duration is a dominant factor influencing protection performance. Specifically, immersion times of at least 10 minutes, and preferably 15 to 20 minutes, in a warm (e.g., 50°C to boiling) aqueous boron solution of concentration ranging from 0.1 to 0.3 g/mL (equivalent to 1–3 wt%) yield substantial reductions in air reactivity. Under these conditions, the oxidation rate of treated anodes at 500°C can be reduced from approximately 4% mass loss per hour in untreated anodes to as low as 0.2% per hour—a twenty-fold improvement. Importantly, this level of protection is achieved without requiring prohibitively high boron loadings or complex post-treatment steps, making the process economically feasible for industrial deployment.

Another significant feature of the invention is the revelation, enabled by advanced characterization techniques, that effective boron impregnation alters not only the rate but also the mode of anode oxidation. Using X-ray computed tomography (CT), the inventors observed that untreated anodes undergo rapid surface recession, leading to structural disintegration and dusting within 24–40 hours of exposure at 500°C. In contrast, anodes treated according to the invention maintain their external geometry even after prolonged exposure (e.g., 160 hours), with oxidation occurring predominantly within the interior pore structure. This shift from surface-limited to volume-distributed reaction is attributed to the uniform deposition of boron species throughout the accessible pore network, which suppresses the intrinsic reactivity of carbon surfaces while still permitting oxygen diffusion. As a result, the reaction does not become diffusion-limited in the early stages, allowing gradual and homogeneous consumption without catastrophic structural failure.

The invention further includes a refined methodology for evaluating anode air reactivity that overcomes the limitations of standard tests. Rather than measuring only total mass loss after a fixed period, the improved protocol involves periodic withdrawal, cooling, weighing, and reinsertion of samples during continuous exposure. This enables real-time tracking of gasification kinetics and reveals critical transient behaviors, such as acceleration after a threshold conversion or stabilization due to protective layer formation. When combined with CT imaging before and after testing, this approach provides unprecedented insight into the spatial evolution of oxidation, facilitating rational design of impregnation strategies tailored to anode geometry and porosity.

Additionally, the invention addresses variability in treatment outcomes by establishing a reliable method for quantifying boron loading based on solution uptake rather than post-drying weight gain, which is confounded by hydration/dehydration equilibria of boron species. This allows correlation of protection efficacy with actual boron dosage, revealing that even modest loadings (e.g., 0.1–0.15 g of boron oxide per 100 g anode sample) suffice for dramatic reactivity suppression when uniformly distributed.

In summary, the invention offers a scientifically validated, scalable solution to a long-standing problem in aluminum production. By optimizing impregnation time and temperature to ensure deep and uniform boron deposition, the method achieves exceptional air oxidation resistance while preserving anode integrity and minimizing dust—key advantages for industrial operability. The accompanying analytical framework enables precise process control and performance prediction, paving the way for successful scale-up to full-size anodes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for reducing the air reactivity of carbon anodes used in the Hall-Héroult process for aluminum production, wherein said method comprises impregnating a baked carbon anode with an aqueous solution containing a boron compound, followed by drying to deposit boron species within the anode’s porous structure. The invention further encompasses the resulting treated anodes, which exhibit markedly reduced rates of oxidative mass loss when exposed to air at temperatures between 400°C and 600°C, as well as methods for characterizing and optimizing the impregnation process to maximize protection efficiency.

In accordance with the invention, the carbon anode to be treated is a conventional baked anode composed of calcined petroleum coke, recycled anode butts, and coal tar pitch binder, typically baked at temperatures of 1100°C to 1250°C. The anode possesses an open porosity ranging from 15% to 25%, with a pore size distribution that includes micropores (<2 nm), mesopores (2–50 nm), and macropores (>50 nm), the latter providing primary pathways for fluid transport during impregnation. The anode may be in the form of a full-size block used in industrial cells or smaller representative samples for laboratory evaluation, provided that the sample geometry and microstructure are representative of the industrial article.

The boron compound employed in the impregnation solution is selected from compounds that are soluble in water and capable of forming protective boron oxide or borate species upon drying and heating. Preferred compounds include boric acid (H₃BO₃), boron oxide (B₂O₃), and alkali metal borates such as sodium tetraborate (borax). Boric acid is particularly advantageous due to its high solubility in hot water (up to ~0.5 g/mL at 100°C), low cost, and ease of handling. The concentration of the boron compound in the aqueous solution is typically in the range of 0.05 g/mL to 0.5 g/mL, more preferably 0.1 g/mL to 0.3 g/mL (equivalent to 1–3 wt%), which balances solubility, impregnation efficiency, and economic feasibility. Higher concentrations may lead to premature crystallization during impregnation, while lower concentrations may require excessively long treatment times to achieve adequate boron loading.

The impregnation process is carried out by immersing the anode or anode sample fully in the aqueous boron solution under controlled conditions of temperature and duration. The solution temperature is maintained between 50°C and the boiling point of water (100°C), with temperatures of 70°C to 100°C being preferred to enhance solution penetration into the anode’s pore structure via reduced viscosity and increased capillary action. The immersion duration is a critical parameter and must be sufficient to allow the solution to penetrate deeply into the anode body. Experimental results demonstrate that durations of less than 5 minutes provide only superficial protection, whereas durations of 10 minutes or more yield significantly improved and more uniform protection. Optimal results are achieved with immersion times of 15 to 20 minutes, during which the solution permeates the majority of the accessible pore volume in samples up to 50 mm in diameter. For larger anodes, extended times or pressurized impregnation may be necessary, though the invention establishes that even partial-depth treatment can confer substantial benefits if the surface layer is adequately protected.

Following impregnation, the anode is removed from the solution and subjected to a drying step to remove free water and precipitate the boron species within the pores. Drying is performed in air or inert atmosphere at temperatures ranging from 80°C to 150°C, preferably 100°C to 140°C, for a period of 4 to 24 hours. During drying, boric acid dehydrates to form metaboric acid and eventually boron oxide, while borax forms hydrated borates that may partially dehydrate. The exact chemical form of the deposited boron species depends on the drying temperature and atmosphere, but all such forms are effective in inhibiting carbon oxidation, likely through a combination of site blocking and catalytic suppression of the oxidation reaction.

The mechanism of protection is believed to involve the deposition of boron-containing compounds on the internal surfaces of the anode’s pore walls, where they physically block active carbon sites and/or alter the electronic properties of adjacent carbon atoms, rendering them less susceptible to oxygen attack. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals lamellar crystalline deposits of boron oxide within macropores, confirming pore filling. However, the dramatic reduction in reactivity—far exceeding what would be expected from mere pore blockage—suggests that boron also exerts a chemical inhibitory effect, possibly by stabilizing intermediate surface oxides or suppressing the formation of reactive edge sites.

A distinctive feature of the invention is the transformation in oxidation morphology induced by effective impregnation. Untreated anodes exhibit rapid surface recession, leading to disintegration into fine dust within 24–40 hours at 500°C. In contrast, treated anodes retain their macroscopic shape even after extensive mass loss (e.g., >50%), with oxidation proceeding inward from the surface through the pore network. This behavior, confirmed by X-ray computed tomography, indicates that the protective boron layer is sufficiently robust to prevent surface spallation and that oxygen diffusion through the pores remains the rate-limiting step rather than intrinsic carbon reactivity. Consequently, the anode burns more uniformly and generates substantially less airborne particulate matter—a critical advantage for industrial hygiene and process stability.

To evaluate and optimize the treatment, the invention employs an enhanced air reactivity test protocol. Instead of a single endpoint measurement, samples are periodically removed from the furnace at defined intervals (e.g., every 2 hours initially, then every 12 hours), cooled under inert atmosphere to prevent post-test oxidation, weighed, and returned to the furnace. This yields a continuous gasification curve that reveals kinetic details such as induction periods, rate transitions, and long-term stability. When coupled with pre- and post-test CT scanning, this method provides three-dimensional maps of density changes, enabling correlation of local boron distribution with local oxidation resistance.

Boron loading is quantified not by post-drying weight gain—which is unreliable due to variable hydration states—but by measuring the mass of solution absorbed during impregnation. Given the known concentration of the impregnation solution, the absolute mass of boron compound delivered to the anode can be calculated precisely. This approach accounts for natural variations in anode porosity and ensures accurate dosing control.

While the invention is particularly suited for new anodes prior to cell installation, it may also be applied to partially consumed anodes during routine maintenance. Furthermore, the impregnation solution may be modified with surfactants to improve wetting or with co-additives such as aluminum fluoride to enhance high-temperature stability, provided that such modifications do not compromise the core protective function of boron.

In industrial practice, the method may be integrated into existing anode handling workflows. For example, freshly baked anodes could be conveyed through a heated boron solution bath for a controlled dwell time, followed by a drying tunnel before storage or cell installation. Automation and real-time monitoring of solution concentration and temperature would ensure consistent treatment quality.

Thus, the invention provides a practical, effective, and economically viable solution to the persistent problem of anode air reactivity, with demonstrable benefits in terms of material savings, operational stability, and environmental performance.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Anode Preparation

Carbon anode samples were prepared from industrial baked anodes produced from a blend consisting of 65% calcined petroleum coke, 22% recycled anode butts, and 13% coal tar pitch, baked at 1200°C. Cylindrical cores were extracted using a diamond-tipped coring drill to obtain specimens of two sizes: small samples with a diameter of 19 mm and length of 14 mm (mass approximately 6–7 g), and large samples with a diameter of 50 mm and length of 30 mm (mass approximately 60–100 g). All samples were taken from regions of the anode corresponding to areas normally exposed to air in an operating Hall-Héroult cell. Prior to treatment, samples were dried in an oven at 140°C for 4 hours to establish a consistent baseline moisture content and recorded initial dry mass. Aqueous impregnation solutions were prepared by dissolving boric acid (H₃BO₃) in deionized water to achieve concentrations of either 0.17 g/mL (17 wt%) or 0.02 g/mL (2 wt%), depending on the experimental series. The solution was heated to the desired temperature—either 50°C or boiling (~100°C)—and maintained at that temperature using a thermostatically controlled water bath. Anode samples were fully submerged in the solution for predetermined durations ranging from 1 minute to 20 minutes. After removal, excess surface solution was gently blotted with absorbent paper, and samples were placed in a drying oven at 100°C overnight (approximately 16 hours) to evaporate water and deposit boron species within the pore structure. For certain samples in the small-sample series (designated Samples 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6), a second identical impregnation-drying cycle was performed to assess the effect of repeated treatment. Control samples (labeled A, B, and C) underwent the same drying procedure but were not exposed to boron solution. All treated and untreated samples were stored in desiccators until reactivity testing.

### Example 2: Anode Characterization

Following impregnation and drying, selected anode samples were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray computed tomography (CT) to assess boron distribution and microstructural features. SEM analysis was performed on cross-sections of impregnated samples using a JEOL JSM-840A scanning electron microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. Samples were mounted on aluminum stubs and sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity. Micrographs revealed the presence of lamellar crystalline structures within the macropores of the anode, identified as boron oxide based on morphology and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) confirmation of boron signal. These deposits were observed lining the pore walls and occasionally bridging pore throats, indicating effective infiltration of the impregnation solution into the anode’s internal structure. However, the coverage appeared discontinuous at the micron scale, suggesting that additional sub-resolution boron species—undetectable by SEM—may contribute significantly to the observed protection.

X-ray CT scans were acquired using a Siemens Somatom Sensation 64 medical CT scanner adapted for materials imaging. Samples were positioned vertically within the scanner gantry, and a series of 2D projection images were collected over a 360° rotation. Reconstruction algorithms generated 3D volumetric datasets with voxel dimensions of 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.6 mm³ for 50-mm-diameter samples, approaching the system’s maximum resolution of 0.09 × 0.09 × 0.4 mm³. Grayscale intensity in the reconstructed images correlated with local X-ray attenuation, which is proportional to material density. Pores appeared as dark regions due to lower density, while carbon matrix and boron deposits appeared brighter. Comparative analysis of pre- and post-reactivity CT scans enabled visualization of oxidation progression in three dimensions. For instance, untreated samples exhibited pronounced surface recession and overall shrinkage after 6 hours of air exposure at 500°C, whereas impregnated samples retained their original dimensions despite internal porosity increases. Image registration techniques were used to superimpose pre- and post-test slices, clearly highlighting the spatial extent of material loss. Quantitative analysis of porosity evolution confirmed that oxidation in treated anodes occurred homogeneously throughout the volume, in contrast to the surface-localized attack in controls.

### Example 3: Air Reactivity Measurements

Air reactivity tests were conducted in a programmable muffle furnace equipped with mass flow controllers for gas delivery. Two experimental series were performed using different sample sizes and temperature profiles. In the first series, small samples (6–7 g) were tested under a dynamic temperature ramp: samples were heated under nitrogen purge to 525°C, held for 2 hours, cooled to 500°C over 1 hour, held for 2 hours, then cooled to 450°C and maintained until test completion (total duration ~20 hours). Air was introduced at a flow rate of 5 L/min once the target temperature was reached, and nitrogen was restored during cooling to prevent post-test oxidation. Final mass was measured after cooling to room temperature, and residue percentage was calculated as (M_f / M_i) × 100, where M_f and M_i are final and initial masses, respectively. Untreated controls exhibited mass losses of 52–75%, while all boron-treated samples showed losses of only 4.5–11%, with best performance observed for samples impregnated in hot solution (7 and 9).

In the second series, large samples (~100 g) were tested under isothermal conditions at 500°C with continuous air flow of 4 L/min. To capture reaction kinetics, samples were removed every 2 hours for the first 24 hours and every 12 hours thereafter, cooled under nitrogen, weighed, and returned to the furnace. Gasification percentage was calculated as [(M_0 – M_t) / M_0] × 100, where M_0 is initial mass and M_t is mass at time t. Untreated anodes showed a linear mass loss rate of ~4%/h and disintegrated completely by 40 hours. In contrast, samples impregnated for 10 or 20 minutes exhibited rates of ~0.2%/h and retained structural integrity beyond 160 hours, with only 60–70% mass loss. Visual inspection confirmed that untreated samples turned to powder within 24 hours, while treated samples maintained their shape despite significant internal consumption.

### Example 4: CO2 Reactivity Measurements

Although the primary focus of the invention is air reactivity, CO₂ reactivity—the reaction between anode carbon and CO₂ gas evolved at the anode-electrolyte interface—is another major contributor to anode overconsumption. To assess whether the boron impregnation treatment also mitigates CO₂ reactivity, additional tests were conducted using pure CO₂ gas instead of air. Large anode samples (50 mm diameter, 30 mm height) were impregnated for 15 minutes in a 2 wt% boric acid solution at 50°C, dried at 100°C overnight, and tested in a muffle furnace at 970°C—the typical temperature of the anode bottom in a Hall-Héroult cell. CO₂ was flowed at 3 L/min, and mass loss was monitored periodically as in the air reactivity protocol. Preliminary results indicated a modest reduction in CO₂ reactivity (approximately 15–20% lower mass loss compared to untreated controls after 10 hours), though the effect was less pronounced than in air reactivity tests. This suggests that the boron-based protection mechanism is more effective against oxygen than CO₂, possibly due to differences in reaction pathways or the stability of boron species under reducing versus oxidizing conditions. Nevertheless, any reduction in CO₂ reactivity represents an added benefit, and optimization of boron formulation (e.g., inclusion of metal fluorides) may enhance performance in CO₂ environments.

### Example 5: Electrical Resistivity Measurements

To ensure that the boron impregnation treatment does not adversely affect the electrical performance of the anode—a critical requirement for efficient electrolysis—electrical resistivity measurements were performed on treated and untreated samples. Four-point probe DC resistivity tests were conducted at room temperature on cylindrical samples (19 mm diameter, 14 mm height) in accordance with ASTM D4217. Samples were impregnated under various conditions (1 min, 10 min, 20 min in boiling 0.17 g/mL boric acid solution) and dried as described. Results showed that electrical resistivity increased slightly but insignificantly upon treatment: untreated anodes averaged 65 μΩ·m, while treated samples ranged from 67 to 70 μΩ·m, well within acceptable industrial specifications (typically <80 μΩ·m). The minor increase is attributed to partial pore filling by insulating boron oxide, but since the deposit is discontinuous and confined to pore surfaces, bulk electronic conduction through the carbon matrix remains largely unaffected. These findings confirm that the protective treatment preserves the essential electrical functionality of the anode, making it suitable for direct integration into aluminum smelting operations without compromising cell voltage or energy efficiency.
