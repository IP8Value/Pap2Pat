# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Effective interpersonal communication relies fundamentally on the continuous, reciprocal exchange of nonverbal cues that signal attention, engagement, and intention. In face-to-face interactions, participants naturally and often subconsciously project their locus of attention through a combination of gaze direction, head orientation, body posture, and gestural behavior. These cues are simultaneously interpreted by others to maintain mutual awareness of each participant’s focus, thereby facilitating smooth turn-taking, accurate interpretation of deictic references (e.g., “look at that” or “what do you think?”), and a shared sense of presence. This dynamic interplay between attention projection and attention awareness is deeply embedded in human social cognition and is essential for natural, efficient, and emotionally resonant conversation.

However, conventional videoconferencing systems significantly disrupt this delicate balance. By compressing three-dimensional spatial relationships into two-dimensional video feeds and decoupling the physical co-location of participants, such systems fail to preserve the geometric fidelity of gaze, orientation, and gesture. As a result, remote participants—particularly those joining a predominantly collocated group from a satellite location—are unable to project their attention with the same precision or nuance as their physically present counterparts. Conversely, collocated participants struggle to interpret the remote individual’s focus, leading to frequent breakdowns in coordination, misunderstandings, and a diminished sense of social presence. These limitations are especially pronounced in “hub-and-satellite” meeting configurations, where a single remote participant interacts with a group of three or more individuals gathered around a table. In such settings, the remote participant is typically represented by a static proxy—a display screen showing their video feed—positioned within the hub meeting space. While this provides a physical locus for the remote participant, it lacks the dynamic affordances necessary to convey shifts in attention among multiple potential targets (e.g., different people, whiteboards, documents).

One well-documented consequence of this limitation is the “newscaster effect,” wherein the remote participant appears to be making direct eye contact with all hub participants simultaneously when looking straight into the camera, regardless of whom they are actually addressing. When the remote participant looks away—say, to view a document or another person on their screen—all hub participants perceive them as looking past them, often over their shoulder, creating a sense of exclusion or misalignment. Another related phenomenon is the “skip-over effect,” where the remote participant is inadvertently omitted from conversational turns, such as during round-robin introductions or status updates, despite the physical presence of their proxy. These effects stem from the inability of static proxies to provide directional cues that mirror the natural head and body movements used in face-to-face interaction to signal attentional focus.

Prior research has attempted to address these challenges through various technological interventions. Some systems, such as Hydra and MultiView, employed multiple cameras and directional displays to simulate correct gaze angles across participants. Others, like GAZE-2, used eye-tracking to dynamically adjust the orientation of virtual avatars in a shared digital space. Commercial telepresence solutions, including Cisco TelePresence and HP Halo, have invested in carefully calibrated room layouts and synchronized camera arrays to approximate mutual gaze, but these remain expensive, infrastructure-intensive, and limited to fixed installations. Meanwhile, embodied telepresence robots—such as PRoP, MeBot, and Texai—introduced mobility and articulation to proxy devices, enabling remote operators to navigate spaces or gesture with robotic limbs. However, these systems often prioritize locomotion over fine-grained attention signaling, and their motion is frequently controlled through explicit interfaces (e.g., joysticks or mouse clicks) that require conscious effort and introduce latency.

A subset of research has explored kinetic proxies—stationary or semi-stationary devices whose displays or heads can rotate or tilt to reflect the remote operator’s attention. Sun’s Porta-Person, for instance, mounted a video display on a motorized turntable to allow directional aiming. Similarly, Animatronic Shader Lamps Avatars projected a live video feed onto a physical head that mirrored the operator’s yaw and pitch movements. While promising, these approaches have not been systematically evaluated in terms of how different control modalities—explicit versus implicit—affect conversational dynamics, cognitive load, and perceived naturalness. Moreover, prior work has largely conflated “gaze awareness” (the perception of where someone is looking) with “attention awareness” (the understanding of what or whom someone is attending to), despite evidence that the latter can be effectively conveyed through non-gaze mechanisms such as head orientation or pointing gestures.

The present invention arises from a recognition that the core challenge in hub-and-satellite videoconferencing is not merely the absence of eye contact, but the lack of reliable, low-latency, and socially intuitive mechanisms for projecting and perceiving attentional focus. It further acknowledges that physical motion of a proxy device—when appropriately designed and controlled—can serve as a powerful surrogate for the nonverbal cues lost in traditional video mediation. However, the manner in which this motion is generated—whether through deliberate user input or automatic tracking of natural behavior—has profound implications for usability, expressiveness, and social interpretation. The invention therefore seeks to provide a novel kinetic proxy system that enables dynamic, directional attention projection while carefully balancing the tradeoffs between explicit and implicit control, minimizing distraction, and preserving inclusivity for all hub participants.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a kinetic proxy system for enhancing attention awareness and social presence in hub-and-satellite videoconferencing environments. The system comprises a remotely operated display device positioned within a collocated meeting space (the “hub”), representing a single remote participant (the “satellite”). Unlike conventional static proxies, the display of the kinetic proxy is mounted on a motorized actuator—such as a turntable—that enables controlled physical rotation in the horizontal plane. This motion allows the satellite to dynamically orient the display toward specific individuals or objects in the hub room, thereby projecting their locus of attention in a manner analogous to natural head turning in face-to-face conversation.

The invention further introduces two distinct modes of motion control: explicit and implicit. In the explicit control mode, the satellite directs the orientation of the proxy by manipulating an input device—such as a mouse cursor—over a panoramic video feed of the hub meeting space displayed on their local monitor. The horizontal position of the cursor maps directly to a corresponding angular position of the proxy display, enabling precise, intentional aiming. In the implicit control mode, the system automatically tracks the satellite’s head movements using a webcam-based head tracker and mirrors the horizontal component of this motion onto the proxy’s orientation. This allows the proxy to move in response to the satellite’s natural gaze shifts without requiring conscious intervention.

The kinetic proxy system is designed to mitigate the “newscaster effect” and “skip-over effect” commonly observed in traditional videoconferencing by providing hub participants with clear, physical indicators of the satellite’s attentional focus. When the proxy rotates to face a particular individual, that person perceives direct engagement, while others understand that the satellite is temporarily addressing someone else. This directional cue improves the accuracy of responses to deictic prompts (e.g., “What do you think?”) and fosters more equitable participation in group discussion.

Empirical evaluation of the system demonstrates that both explicit and implicit kinetic conditions significantly enhance conversational engagement compared to a stationary proxy, as measured by increased speaking time, higher speech energy, greater turn-taking frequency, and improved responsiveness to directed questions. Hub participants report that motion makes the remote participant feel more “alive” and communicatively present. However, the study also reveals important tradeoffs: explicit control offers greater clarity of intent and is preferred by the satellite operator, but may introduce slight delays due to manual aiming; implicit control reduces cognitive load on the satellite but risks interpreting incidental head movements as communicative signals, thereby increasing distraction for hub participants.

Notably, the invention distinguishes between gaze awareness and attention awareness, recognizing that true eye contact is not strictly necessary for effective attention projection. Instead, the physical rotation of the proxy serves as a robust, socially legible cue of focus, even if the satellite’s eyes are not perfectly aligned with the camera. This insight opens the door to alternative designs—such as stationary displays paired with abstract directional indicators (e.g., a weather vane-style pointer) or convex screens that maintain visibility across wider angles—thereby expanding the design space beyond attempts to replicate biological gaze.

In summary, the invention provides a practical, deployable solution for improving remote collaboration in asymmetric meeting contexts. By integrating physical motion with dual control paradigms, it restores a critical dimension of nonverbal communication lost in conventional videoconferencing, leading to more natural, engaging, and effective distributed conversations.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a kinetic proxy system configured to enhance attention awareness, social presence, and conversational effectiveness in hub-and-satellite videoconferencing scenarios. The system is specifically engineered to address the limitations of static video proxies by incorporating controlled physical motion that reflects the remote participant’s locus of attention, thereby restoring key nonverbal cues essential for natural human interaction. The following detailed description sets forth the structural components, operational principles, control mechanisms, and empirical validation of the invention, illustrating how it overcomes longstanding challenges in mediated communication.

The kinetic proxy system comprises a physical proxy device situated within a collocated meeting environment—the “hub”—wherein three or more participants are gathered around a conference table. This proxy serves as the embodied representation of a single remote participant—the “satellite”—who joins the meeting from a separate location via a networked computing device. The proxy includes a display screen, preferably a 12-inch Tablet PC mounted in portrait orientation, which renders a real-time video feed of the satellite’s upper torso and face. The display is affixed to a motorized actuator, specifically an 8-inch diameter turntable capable of rotating ±90 degrees in the horizontal plane. This range of motion is sufficient to enable the display to directly face any of the hub participants seated around a standard 3-foot diameter conference table, assuming symmetrical placement. The turntable is housed within a minimalistic frame constructed of transparent acrylic sheeting to reduce visual obstruction and is elevated to approximate the eye height of seated hub participants, thereby promoting natural eye-level interaction.

Integrated within the proxy structure is a wide-field-of-view video camera, such as an Axis 212 model, fixed in position above the display screen. This camera captures a panoramic view of the entire hub meeting space, including all participants and relevant artifacts (e.g., whiteboards, documents). The resulting video stream is transmitted to the satellite’s local computing device—preferably a 30-inch desktop monitor—and rendered in a window that spans the full width of the display. This configuration provides the satellite with comprehensive spatial awareness of the hub environment, enabling them to discern the relative positions of individuals and objects. Critically, the satellite’s own video feed—captured by the proxy’s display-integrated webcam—is also visible within this interface, though empirical observation indicates that users prefer to rely on the larger panoramic view for engagement and orientation feedback.

Audio communication is facilitated through a speakerphone unit housed within the proxy base, ensuring bidirectional, high-fidelity sound transmission between the satellite and hub participants. All video and audio streams are conveyed over a local area network (LAN), either wired or wireless, while the control signals governing the turntable’s motion are transmitted via a dedicated USB connection to ensure low-latency, jitter-free actuation. This separation of media and control channels guarantees responsive proxy movement synchronized with the satellite’s actions.

The invention implements two distinct modes of motion control: explicit and implicit. In the explicit control mode, the satellite manipulates a standard computer mouse to position a cursor anywhere within the panoramic video window displayed on their monitor. The horizontal coordinate of the cursor is continuously sampled—at approximately 30 Hz—and mapped linearly to a target angular position of the turntable. For example, a cursor at the far left edge of the screen commands the proxy to rotate fully left (−90°), while a cursor at the center maintains a forward-facing orientation (0°). No mouse click is required; the proxy responds instantaneously to cursor movement, enabling fluid, drag-like control. This mode affords the satellite precise, intentional command over the proxy’s orientation, allowing them to deliberately signal attention toward specific individuals or objects.

In the implicit control mode, the system employs a software-based head tracker that analyzes the video feed from the satellite’s local webcam to estimate head pose in real time. Only the horizontal rotational component (yaw) is extracted and similarly mapped to the turntable’s target angle at 30 Hz. Thus, when the satellite naturally turns their head to look at a hub participant on their left or right monitor edge, the proxy automatically swivels to mirror this orientation. This mode aims to reduce cognitive overhead by leveraging subconscious or habitual head movements as input, thereby making attention projection more automatic and less disruptive to the flow of conversation.

Both control modes are calibrated such that the extremes of input—whether cursor position or head angle—correspond to the physical limits of the turntable’s range, ensuring consistent and predictable behavior across sessions. The system does not support vertical motion (pitch) or lateral translation, as the primary communicative need in hub meetings is horizontal attention shifting among seated participants.

To evaluate the efficacy of the kinetic proxy system, a controlled laboratory study was conducted using a within-subjects design. Six groups of three naïve hub participants each interacted with a confederate acting as the satellite under three conditions: Stationary (no motion), Explicit Control, and Implicit Control. Each condition lasted 10–15 minutes and involved collaborative decision-making tasks designed to elicit natural conversation and deictic referencing. Objective measures included sociometric badge data (speaking time, speech energy, segment length, turn frequency) and coded responses to directed questions (“What do you think?”). Subjective measures comprised post-condition Likert-scale questionnaires and a final preference ranking.

Results demonstrated that both kinetic conditions significantly outperformed the Stationary condition across multiple behavioral metrics. Participants spoke more (18.7% and 17.8% vs. 16.6% of session time), exhibited higher speech energy (0.110 and 0.107 vs. 0.103 units), took more turns per second (0.77 and 0.75 vs. 0.63), and engaged more frequently with the satellite (0.09 and 0.08 vs. 0.07 turns/sec). Crucially, responses to deictic prompts were 100% accurate in both kinetic conditions (12/12 correct), compared to only 67% accuracy in the Stationary condition (8/12 correct), with four instances of incorrect or no response. These findings confirm that physical motion enhances attention awareness and conversational fluidity.

However, nuanced tradeoffs emerged between control modes. The Explicit condition yielded longer average speech segments (0.80 sec vs. 0.75 and 0.72 sec), suggesting that deliberate aiming may extend speaking turns—either due to aiming latency or prolonged focus signaling. The satellite confederate reported greater comfort and communicative clarity with explicit control, noting that each movement felt intentional and interpretable. In contrast, implicit control generated more incidental motion from natural head sway, which hub participants sometimes found distracting or ambiguous. Despite these differences, both kinetic modes were rated as less fatiguing and more natural than expected, and overall preference favored kinetic conditions by a 3:1 margin over stationary.

Interestingly, subjective ratings of “sense of presence” were paradoxically higher in the Stationary condition (6.2/7) than in Explicit (5.4) or Implicit (5.9), likely because rotating the flat display excluded non-addressed participants from the satellite’s visual field, creating feelings of being “turned away from.” This highlights a key design tension: directional motion clarifies attention but risks social exclusion. Future embodiments may address this by decoupling attention indication from display orientation—for example, by adding a secondary directional indicator (e.g., an LED arrow or mechanical pointer) while keeping the screen stationary, or by using a curved or multi-faceted display that remains partially visible from all angles.

The invention thus establishes that physical motion is a potent mechanism for attention projection in mediated settings, even without perfect eye contact. By distinguishing attention awareness from gaze awareness, it reframes the problem space and enables simpler, more robust solutions than those attempting to replicate biological vision geometry. The kinetic proxy system is scalable, cost-effective, and compatible with existing videoconferencing infrastructure, making it suitable for deployment in corporate, academic, and remote-work environments. Extensions to multi-satellite scenarios—using multiple kinetic proxies—are also contemplated, wherein each proxy independently signals its operator’s focus, enabling richer cross-proxy interactions.

In operation, the system restores a critical layer of nonverbal communication, reducing cognitive load on hub participants, minimizing conversational breakdowns, and fostering a stronger sense of inclusion for the remote individual. It represents a significant advancement over prior art by empirically validating the benefits of kinetic proxies and providing actionable insights into the design of motion-based attention signaling in distributed collaboration.
