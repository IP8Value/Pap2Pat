# DESCRIPTION

## STATEMENT REGARDING RESEARCH & DEVELOPMENT

This invention was developed in the course of research conducted at the University of Washington Medical Center, a tertiary academic medical center located in an urban setting. The development involved interdisciplinary collaboration between researchers in electrical engineering, computer science, and clinical cardiology, with the objective of creating a novel contactless system for monitoring cardiac rhythm using commercially available smart speaker hardware. The research was supported by institutional resources and conducted under the oversight of the University of Washington Institutional Review Board, which approved the study protocol and ensured compliance with all applicable ethical regulations governing human subjects research. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study. The inventors declare no competing financial interests that would influence the design, execution, or reporting of the research. The technology described herein represents a proof-of-concept demonstration that builds upon prior work in active sonar-based physiological monitoring while introducing novel algorithmic approaches specifically designed to address the challenges of extracting irregular cardiac rhythms from acoustic signals in real-world conditions using commodity hardware components.

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of non-invasive physiological monitoring systems and methods, and more particularly to contactless cardiac rhythm monitoring using active sonar technology implemented on smart speaker platforms. The invention specifically addresses the technical challenge of extracting individual heartbeats and beat-to-beat intervals (R–R intervals) from acoustic reflections captured by microphone arrays in smart speakers, enabling the assessment of both regular and irregular heart rhythms without requiring physical contact with the subject. The invention operates within the domain of biomedical signal processing, combining principles from acoustics, signal processing, adaptive beamforming, and machine learning to overcome the limitations of conventional frequency-domain analysis methods when applied to irregular cardiac rhythms. The technical field encompasses the transformation of consumer-grade smart speaker devices into short-range active sonar systems capable of detecting sub-millimeter chest wall displacements caused by cardiac activity, while simultaneously suppressing interference from respiratory motion and ambient noise. This invention represents a significant advancement in the field of telemedicine and remote patient monitoring, providing a scalable solution that leverages the widespread adoption of smart speaker technology in both residential and clinical settings.

## BACKGROUND

Clinical assessment of heart rhythm has traditionally relied on electrocardiography (ECG), which requires direct skin contact through electrodes to capture the electrical activity of the heart. While ECG provides comprehensive information about cardiac function, including P waves, QRS complexes, and T waves, it presents several limitations in modern healthcare delivery. Contact-based monitoring can be problematic for patients with skin allergies or sensitivities to adhesives, creates logistical challenges in infectious disease settings where device sterilization is required between uses, and may be impractical for continuous monitoring in home environments. Furthermore, the requirement for physical contact limits the accessibility of cardiac monitoring for patients in remote or rural areas who may benefit from telemedicine applications.

Alternative contactless monitoring approaches have been explored, including Doppler radar systems, optical vibrocardiography, and photoplethysmographic techniques using facial imaging. However, these technologies face significant barriers to widespread adoption. Doppler radar systems typically require specialized hardware with large bandwidth capabilities that are not commonly available in consumer devices, limiting their scalability and cost-effectiveness. Optical vibrocardiography methods, while promising, often suffer from limited range and sensitivity to environmental lighting conditions, and may be affected by excessive adipose tissue in obese individuals. Photoplethysmographic approaches using cameras raise substantial privacy concerns, as they require visual monitoring of the patient's face and may inadvertently capture sensitive personal information beyond the intended physiological measurements.

The emergence of smart speaker technology presents a unique opportunity to address these limitations. Modern smart speakers, such as those produced by Amazon, Google, and Apple, are equipped with multiple microphones arranged in arrays and capable of sophisticated acoustic processing. These devices are already widely deployed in homes and increasingly in healthcare settings, providing a ubiquitous platform for contactless monitoring. Previous research has demonstrated the feasibility of using smart speakers for breathing monitoring through active sonar techniques, but extending this capability to cardiac rhythm monitoring presents significant technical challenges. The displacement caused by cardiac activity is approximately 0.3–0.8 millimeters, which is an order of magnitude smaller than the wavelength of sound at typical operational frequencies. Additionally, commodity smart speakers have limited bandwidth in the inaudible frequency range (typically 18–22 kHz) and sampling rates around 48 kHz, resulting in low signal-to-noise ratios that make precise timing measurements difficult.

A critical limitation of existing approaches is their inability to handle irregular heart rhythms effectively. Frequency domain analysis methods, which work well for regular rhythms, fail when applied to irregular conditions such as atrial fibrillation because the energy becomes spread across a range of frequencies rather than concentrated at a well-defined peak. This problem is compounded by the fact that respiratory motion, which is much larger in amplitude than cardiac motion, creates high-frequency components that interfere with the minute heartbeat signals. Traditional filtering techniques are insufficient to separate these overlapping frequency components, particularly at the low signal-to-noise ratios characteristic of commodity smart speaker hardware.

Prior attempts to address these challenges have either relied on custom ultrasonic hardware with higher power output and better frequency response, or have assumed regular cardiac rhythms that can be analyzed using frequency domain techniques. These approaches do not translate effectively to the constraints of commercial smart speaker platforms, which must balance performance with cost, power consumption, and user experience considerations. The need remains for a contactless cardiac monitoring system that can operate effectively on widely available smart speaker hardware, handle both regular and irregular heart rhythms, and provide clinically relevant measurements of R–R intervals and heart rate variability without compromising user privacy or requiring specialized equipment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for contactless cardiac rhythm monitoring using smart speaker technology operating as a short-range active sonar system. The invention transforms standard smart speaker hardware into a sophisticated physiological monitoring platform capable of detecting sub-millimeter chest wall displacements caused by cardiac activity, while simultaneously suppressing interference from respiratory motion and ambient noise. The system operates by emitting inaudible acoustic signals in the range of 18–22 kHz and analyzing the reflected signals captured by the microphone array to extract individual heartbeats and compute R–R intervals for both regular and irregular heart rhythms.

The core innovation of the invention lies in its multi-stage signal processing pipeline, which addresses the fundamental challenges of low signal-to-noise ratio, interference from respiratory motion, and the need to handle irregular cardiac rhythms without assuming periodic structure. The system begins with the generation of frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) signals, specifically linear chirps that sweep from 18 to 22 kHz over a duration of 50 milliseconds. These signals are played continuously through the smart speaker's audio output, creating a controlled acoustic environment for reflection analysis.

The first stage of processing involves preprocessing the received signals from each microphone in the array to extract the impulse response of the acoustic channel. This is accomplished by performing discrete Fourier transforms (DFTs) over signal blocks of 50 milliseconds duration with a sliding window of 10 milliseconds, resulting in an effective sampling rate of 100 Hz for the output cardiac signal. The frequency domain representation of each received signal block is then equalized by canceling out the phase characteristics of the transmitted FMCW chirp, accounting for timing synchronization offsets introduced by the sliding window approach. The inverse DFT of this equalized signal yields the time-domain impulse response, which represents the times of arrival of various reflections from the speaker to each microphone.

To address the challenge of distant reflections overwhelming the minute cardiac signals, the system implements echo suppression by eliminating reflections arriving from distances greater than one meter. This is achieved through pointwise multiplication of the impulse response with a raised-cosine window starting at time zero, with a roll-off factor of one and length corresponding to the round-trip time of arrival for one meter distance. This approach avoids the abrupt changes and spectral leakage that would result from simple zeroing of the impulse response beyond the desired distance threshold.

The second critical stage of the invention is the implementation of an adaptive maximum signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR) beamformer that combines signals across different microphones and frequencies to maximize the cardiac signal while minimizing interference from respiratory motion and noise. This beamformer is particularly innovative because it does not assume a priori periodic structure to the heart rhythm, making it suitable for both regular and irregular cardiac conditions. The beamformer operates by solving an optimization problem that maximizes the ratio of cardiac signal power to the sum of respiratory interference and noise power.

The optimization function is designed to leverage the frequency domain characteristics of cardiac and respiratory signals. While respiratory motion primarily occupies frequencies below 40 cycles per minute (CPM) and cardiac motion falls within the 60–150 CPM range, there is non-negligible energy leakage between these bands due to the non-sinusoidal nature of respiratory motion. The beamformer uses this frequency separation as a proxy for signal and interference components, applying low-pass, band-pass, and high-pass filters to approximate the respective contributions. The objective function incorporates terms for both the real (in-phase) and imaginary (quadrature) components of the complex cardiac signal, along with a coherence constraint that accounts for their correlation as linear projections of the same underlying cardiac motion.

The optimization is performed using gradient ascent with several innovative regularization techniques to prevent convergence to local maxima that might represent spurious signals rather than true cardiac activity. A dropout mechanism is implemented during the backward pass of gradient ascent, where weights are updated with a probability of 0.6, ensuring that the solution relies on consistent signals across multiple frequency-microphone pairs rather than being dominated by random noise in any single channel. Additionally, a regularization penalty term based on the maximum amplitude of the extracted cardiac signal prevents the algorithm from amplifying impulse-like artifacts that could result from abrupt participant movements.

The third stage of the invention addresses the challenge of segmenting the beamformed signal into individual heartbeats, particularly in the presence of residual respiratory interference that causes rotation between the in-phase and quadrature components of the cardiac signal. Traditional peak detection methods fail in this context because the cardiac signal may appear predominantly in either the real or imaginary component depending on the instantaneous phase relationship with residual respiratory motion. The segmentation algorithm simultaneously identifies both the segment boundaries and the rotation transformation for each segment by comparing adjacent segments after accounting for temporal scaling due to varying R–R intervals.

The algorithm computes the optimal rotation between consecutive segments by minimizing the mean square error between them, which can be analytically solved in the complex domain. The distance metric between segments incorporates both the normalized difference and the sum of the rotated segments, providing a robust measure of similarity that accounts for amplitude variations. Once the segment boundaries are identified, the midpoint of each segment is used as the timing reference for the corresponding heartbeat, enabling the computation of R–R intervals and heart rate.

A key advantage of the invention is its self-supervised nature, which eliminates the need for ground truth training data or pre-trained models. The optimization process adapts to each individual's unique body shape, position, and environmental conditions by maximizing the intrinsic properties of the cardiac signal itself, rather than relying on learned patterns from other subjects. This makes the system highly adaptable to diverse populations and real-world conditions without requiring extensive calibration or training procedures.

The system also incorporates robust synchronization mechanisms to align the extracted cardiac timings with ground truth measurements from reference devices. This includes both coarse synchronization based on known timing offsets and fine alignment through manual adjustment of the first heartbeat timing. For R–R interval comparison, a mutual matching algorithm ensures that only properly synchronized intervals are compared, preventing error propagation from missed beats in either data stream.

The invention is specifically designed to operate within the constraints of commercial smart speaker hardware, including limited bandwidth in the inaudible frequency range, sampling rates of approximately 48 kHz, and non-ideal frequency response characteristics. Despite these limitations, the system achieves clinically relevant accuracy, with median absolute errors in R–R intervals of approximately 28 milliseconds for healthy participants and 30 milliseconds for hospitalized cardiac patients. The system demonstrates particular effectiveness in handling irregular rhythms such as atrial fibrillation, where traditional frequency-domain approaches fail completely.

The contactless nature of the invention provides significant advantages in terms of patient comfort, infection control, and accessibility. The short-range operation (within one meter) ensures user privacy by requiring explicit engagement and proximity to the device, while the use of inaudible frequencies minimizes disturbance to users and avoids capturing audible environmental sounds. The system's compatibility with standard smart speaker hardware enables rapid deployment and scalability across diverse healthcare and home monitoring scenarios.

### IMPLEMENTED EXAMPLES

The invention was implemented and tested using a prototype smart speaker system that closely mimics the hardware characteristics of commercial devices. The prototype consisted of a seven-microphone circular array with 4.3 centimeter separation between microphones, similar to the Amazon Echo Dot, combined with a commercial speaker and housed in a 3D-printed case. The system was connected to a computer via USB and operated at a sampling rate of 48 kHz with a sound pressure level of approximately 75 dB at a distance of 50 centimeters. This configuration was specifically chosen to replicate the constraints and capabilities of actual consumer smart speakers, ensuring that the results would be directly applicable to real-world deployment scenarios.

In the first implementation example, the system was tested with a cohort of 26 healthy participants with no prior history of cardiac conditions. The participants had a median age of 31 years (interquartile range of 8.5 years) and a median body mass index (BMI) of 22 (interquartile range of 3). The female-to-male ratio was 0.6, providing a diverse sample for initial validation. Each participant was fitted with a Polar H10 Sensor System that provided ECG-based ground truth measurements of heart rate and R–R intervals. Testing was conducted in a controlled environment with participants sitting upright on a chair, wearing their normal indoor clothing including blouses, tops, T-shirts, and button-down shirts made from various fabric materials.

The testing protocol included seven distinct 60-second measurement sessions designed to evaluate system performance under various conditions. The first three sessions assessed distance sensitivity by placing the smart speaker at 40, 50, and 60 centimeters from the participant's chest at nipple level. The fourth session evaluated vertical misalignment by positioning the speaker 10 centimeters above the chest at 50 centimeters distance. The fifth session tested angular tolerance by pointing the speaker at a 20-degree angle from the chest at 50 centimeters distance. The sixth session introduced background interference by playing jazz music at approximately 75 dB(A) from a distance of 5 meters. The final session assessed performance during elevated heart rates by having participants jog in place to achieve heart rates above 110 beats per minute before measurement.

The results demonstrated excellent agreement between the smart speaker system and the ECG ground truth. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) for average heart rate were both 0.983, with a median absolute error (MAE) of 1 beat per minute (BPM) and a 90th percentile error of less than 4 BPM. For R–R intervals, the ICC and CCC were 0.929 and 0.927 respectively, with an MAE of 28 milliseconds and a 90th percentile error of 75 milliseconds. The mean absolute error as a percentage of the ground truth R–R interval was 3.6% with a standard deviation of 4.3%.

Distance sensitivity analysis showed that increasing the distance from 40 to 60 centimeters increased the median R–R interval error from 25 to 33 milliseconds, demonstrating the expected signal attenuation with distance. However, the system showed remarkable tolerance to misalignment, with only modest increases in error when the speaker was positioned 10 centimeters above the chest (26 milliseconds error) or at a 20-degree angle (31 milliseconds error). Background music increased the median error to 32 milliseconds, likely due to residual high-frequency components from the music source, while post-exercise measurements showed a similar error increase to 32 milliseconds, attributed to more pronounced respiratory motion. Gender differences were minimal, with female participants showing a median error of 30 milliseconds compared to 27 milliseconds for male participants, and a slight increase in error was observed with higher BMI values.

The second implementation example focused on hospitalized cardiac patients, including both regular and irregular rhythm cases. Twenty-four patients were enrolled from the acute care general cardiology unit at the University of Washington Medical Center. Patients were categorized into regular rhythm (sinus rhythm, atrial flutter with regular conduction, ventricular paced, or atrioventricular paced) and irregular rhythm (atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter with variable conduction) groups based on physician review of their existing telemetry. The irregular rhythm cohort was more likely to have a history of atrial fibrillation and included a higher proportion of female patients, while other demographic and clinical characteristics were well-balanced between groups.

Ground truth measurements were obtained using either the Polar H10 ECG sensor or the CorSense PPG monitor, depending on patient comfort and clinical circumstances. The smart speaker was positioned 50–60 centimeters from the patient's chest at nipple level, with ambient noise sources turned off and visitors positioned at least 2 meters away during measurements. Five 60-second sessions were conducted for each patient, with all patients able to tolerate the procedure except for one case where data acquisition was terminated early due to nausea unrelated to the monitoring system.

System performance in the cardiac patient population showed strong agreement with ground truth measurements, with an MAE of 2 BPM for heart rate and a 90th percentile error of less than 3 BPM. For R–R intervals, the ICC and CCC were 0.901 and 0.898 respectively, with an MAE of 30 milliseconds and a 90th percentile error of less than 93 milliseconds. The mean absolute error as a percentage of ground truth R–R interval was 4.0% with a standard deviation of 7.6%. Notably, performance was comparable between regular and irregular rhythm patients, with atrial fibrillation cases showing an MAE of 35 milliseconds and ICC/CCC values of 0.891 and 0.890 respectively. This demonstrates the system's ability to handle irregular rhythms effectively, which is a critical advantage over traditional frequency-domain approaches.

Time series analysis of R–R intervals revealed the system's capability to capture various cardiac conditions accurately. Examples included atrial fibrillation cases showing the characteristic beat-to-beat variability, respiratory sinus arrhythmia demonstrating the expected relationship between respiration and heart rate, paced rhythms from implanted pacemakers, and cases with minimal heart rate variability due to underlying cardiac conditions and medications. The system successfully captured these diverse patterns, confirming its clinical relevance across different cardiac conditions.

The third implementation example specifically addressed the challenge of extreme obesity, which was excluded from the main cardiac patient study. Five hospitalized cardiac patients with BMIs between 36 and 40.4 (median BMI of 38.6) were evaluated. The system was able to extract cardiac rhythm signals for only one of these five participants, consistent with prior literature indicating that excessive adipose tissue dampens the transmission of cardiac motion to the body surface. This finding aligns with observations from healthy participants where slightly higher errors were noted for female participants, potentially related to differences in body composition.

Additional implementation considerations included the system's operational constraints and safety profile. The 18–22 kHz frequency range used by the prototype is generally inaudible to adults but may be perceptible to younger individuals. Commercial smart speakers supporting higher frequencies (25–30 kHz) would eliminate this concern entirely. The sound pressure level of 75 dB at 50 centimeters corresponds to approximately 66 dB(A) at 20 kHz, well below the World Health Organization's recommended noise limit of 85 dB(A) for 8-hour exposure. The system's short-range operation (within 1 meter) and requirement for participant stillness provide natural privacy protection, as the system cannot operate without explicit user engagement and proximity.

The invention's compatibility with existing smart speaker hardware enables immediate deployment potential. Newer smart speaker models with rotatable directional tweeters and improved frequency response characteristics at target frequencies would further enhance performance. The current implementation supports single-participant monitoring, but future enhancements could enable multi-participant tracking using FMCW algorithms that leverage breathing motion to distinguish between different individuals based on their distance from the speaker.

These implementation examples demonstrate the invention's robustness across diverse populations, environmental conditions, and clinical scenarios. The system achieves clinically relevant accuracy while operating within the constraints of commercial smart speaker hardware, providing a practical solution for contactless cardiac rhythm monitoring that can be deployed at scale in both home and clinical settings.
