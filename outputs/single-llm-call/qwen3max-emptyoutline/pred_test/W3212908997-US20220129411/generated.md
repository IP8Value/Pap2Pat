# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of quantum computation has witnessed rapid theoretical and experimental progress in recent years, driven by the promise of harnessing quantum mechanical principles to solve problems intractable for classical computers. Central to this endeavor is the ability to efficiently implement arbitrary unitary transformations on a set of qubits using physically realizable quantum gates. However, a fundamental challenge arises in the synthesis and optimization of quantum circuits: given a target unitary operation, how can one construct a short, physically implementable sequence of quantum gates that realizes it? This problem, known as quantum circuit synthesis or optimization, becomes particularly critical when considering the constraints of near-term quantum hardware, where gate fidelity, coherence times, and connectivity limitations impose stringent requirements on circuit depth and gate count.

Among the broad landscape of quantum circuits, those composed exclusively of Clifford gates—namely the Hadamard (H), Phase (S), and controlled-NOT (CNOT) gates, along with Pauli X, Y, and Z gates—occupy a special and practically significant position. The set of all such circuits acting on n qubits generates the n-qubit Clifford group, a finite subgroup of the full unitary group. While not universal for quantum computation on its own, the Clifford group underpins numerous essential quantum information processing protocols. For instance, Clifford circuits are foundational to quantum error correction, where they are used to encode logical qubits into entangled states of physical qubits and to perform syndrome measurements. They also play a pivotal role in quantum state distillation, randomized benchmarking for characterizing gate fidelities, and the study of multipartite entanglement. More recently, Clifford circuits have become indispensable in the emerging technique of classical shadow tomography, which provides an efficient method for predicting many properties of a quantum state without performing full state tomography.

A key mathematical property that makes the Clifford group so useful is that it forms a unitary 2-design. This means that the average of any polynomial function of degree at most two over the Clifford group is equal to the average over the entire unitary group with respect to the Haar measure. In practical terms, this allows random Clifford operators to effectively substitute for much more complex and resource-intensive Haar-random unitaries in any protocol that depends only on the second-order statistical moments of the unitary ensemble. Crucially, while sampling from the Haar measure is computationally infeasible for large systems, any Clifford operator can be implemented by a relatively short quantum circuit, making them an ideal practical tool. For example, in randomized benchmarking, sequences of random Clifford gates are applied to a system, and the decay of the survival probability is measured. The 2-design property ensures that the effective noise channel, after averaging over the Clifford group, is a simple depolarizing channel characterized by a single parameter, thereby providing a robust and scalable metric for gate fidelity that is insensitive to state preparation and measurement errors.

Beyond their role as random ensembles, specific Clifford circuits are also used as deterministic basis-change transformations in quantum simulation algorithms. In quantum chemistry simulations, for instance, a Hamiltonian is often expressed as a sum of Pauli operators. If a set of these Pauli terms commute, they can be simultaneously diagonalized by a Clifford transformation, which can significantly reduce the circuit depth required for their implementation. Similarly, techniques like qubit tapering exploit the symmetries of a molecular Hamiltonian, which are often represented by Pauli operators, to remove redundant qubits from the simulation. This is achieved by applying a Clifford circuit that maps the symmetry operator to a single-qubit Z operator, allowing the corresponding qubit to be fixed to a known eigenvalue and thus eliminated from the problem.

Given the ubiquity and importance of Clifford circuits, the problem of synthesizing them with minimal resources is of paramount practical interest. A natural and widely adopted objective is to minimize the number of two-qubit entangling gates, such as CNOTs, because these gates are typically the most expensive in terms of execution time and error rates on leading physical platforms like superconducting circuits and trapped ions. Single-qubit gates, by contrast, are usually much faster and more accurate. Consequently, a significant body of research has been dedicated to finding the shortest possible CNOT-based implementations of a given Clifford unitary.

Early foundational work by Aaronson and Gottesman provided a canonical form for representing any n-qubit Clifford operator, which leads to a synthesis algorithm that uses Θ(n² / log n) gates. This is asymptotically optimal, meaning that no algorithm can do better than this up to a constant factor for the worst-case Clifford operator. However, while theoretically elegant, this approach is not designed for practical optimality on specific, non-worst-case instances. It often produces circuits with a far higher CNOT count than necessary for a given unitary.

In parallel, exhaustive search methods have been developed to find the truly optimal CNOT count for small numbers of qubits. These methods, typically based on breadth-first search through the Clifford group, have successfully cataloged the optimal circuits for all Clifford operators on up to six qubits. However, the size of the Clifford group grows as 2^(O(n²)), making this approach computationally intractable beyond six qubits. This leaves a significant gap: for circuits with more than six qubits, one must rely on heuristic methods that are fast and scalable but may not produce the absolute best circuit.

Existing heuristic approaches for larger Clifford circuits have generally been suboptimal, either producing circuits that are far from the theoretical minimum or being too slow for practical use. Furthermore, general-purpose quantum circuit optimization techniques, such as template matching and peephole optimization, have not been fully adapted to exploit the rich algebraic structure of the Clifford group. Template matching, for instance, involves replacing a subcircuit that matches a part of a known identity circuit (a "template") with the complementary part of the template to reduce the total gate count. While powerful, its application to Clifford circuits can be made more efficient by leveraging the fact that Pauli gates can be commuted through the circuit and collected at the end without affecting the core Clifford structure. Similarly, peephole optimization, which locally optimizes small subcircuits by looking them up in a precomputed database of optimal implementations, is limited by the need for the subcircuit to be completely decoupled from the rest of the system. This is a severe restriction in a highly entangled Clifford circuit.

Therefore, there exists a clear and unmet need in the art for a practical, scalable, and high-quality method for synthesizing and optimizing Clifford circuits that specifically targets the minimization of two-qubit gate count. Such a method should bridge the gap between the non-scalable exact optimizers and the suboptimal asymptotic synthesizers, delivering near-optimal results for circuits of practical size (e.g., tens of qubits) in a reasonable amount of time. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a novel suite of algorithms that are custom-tailored to the unique properties of the Clifford group, thereby achieving a significant advance in the state of the art for quantum circuit compilation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for the synthesis and optimization of quantum circuits that implement a target Clifford unitary operation on a set of qubits, with a primary focus on minimizing the number of two-qubit entangling gates, such as controlled-NOT (CNOT) or controlled-Z (CZ) gates, in the final circuit. The invention comprises a multi-stage process that begins with a novel bidirectional greedy synthesis algorithm to generate a high-quality initial circuit, followed by a series of powerful, Clifford-specific optimization passes that dramatically refine the circuit to approach or achieve the optimal two-qubit gate count.

In a first aspect, the invention provides a method for synthesizing a quantum circuit that implements a target n-qubit Clifford unitary. The method includes a bidirectional greedy compiler that recursively disentangles qubits from the target unitary by constructing a sequence of left and right Clifford circuits whose combined action simplifies the target unitary. At each step of the recursion, the compiler selects a pair of anti-commuting Pauli operators on the remaining qubits that minimizes the combined cost of the left and right disentangling circuits. This bidirectional approach, which considers transformations on both the input and output sides of the target unitary, yields a significantly better initial circuit than a unidirectional approach, which only acts on one side. The synthesized initial circuit is composed of a "compute" stage containing H, S, CNOT, and CZ gates, a "swap" stage containing SWAP gates, and a "Pauli" stage containing only single-qubit Pauli gates, a structure that is deliberately engineered to facilitate subsequent optimization.

In a second aspect, the invention provides a Clifford-specific template matching optimization technique. This technique leverages the algebraic structure of the Clifford group to partition an input circuit into the three aforementioned stages. The Pauli and SWAP gates are first pushed to the end of the circuit using the well-known commutation relations of the stabilizer formalism. The core "compute" stage, now free of Pauli and SWAP gates, is then optimized using a library of Clifford-specific templates. These templates are identity circuits that are carefully designed to exploit the relationships between H, S, CNOT, and CZ gates. The template matching is performed in a way that prioritizes the reduction of two-qubit gate count. After the primary template optimization, a secondary pass of template matching is applied to reduce the single-qubit gate count, further refining the circuit. Additionally, the SWAP stage is optimized by recognizing that a SWAP gate can be implemented at the effective cost of a single two-qubit gate if it can be merged with a nearby CNOT or CZ gate in the compute stage, a process that is explicitly managed by the three-stage circuit representation.

In a third aspect, the invention provides a novel symbolic peephole optimization technique. This technique overcomes the primary limitation of standard peephole optimization—the requirement that the subcircuit to be optimized be completely decoupled from the rest of the system. The symbolic peephole optimizer works by selecting a small subset of qubits and analyzing the subcircuit that acts on them. Any two-qubit gate that entangles a qubit in this subset with a qubit outside the subset is replaced with a Symbolic Pauli Gate (SPG). An SPG is a single-qubit Pauli gate (X, Y, or Z) whose application is conditioned on a symbolic binary variable that represents the state of the external control qubit. This transformation effectively isolates the subcircuit on the chosen qubits, converting it into a symbolic Clifford+SPG circuit. This symbolic circuit is then optimized using a dynamic programming algorithm that minimizes a cost function defined as the sum of the two-qubit gate count and the SPG count. The dynamic programming algorithm efficiently finds the global minimum by leveraging the fact that the cost function is a sum of terms that depend on at most two consecutive variables, and by exploiting the symmetries of the Clifford group to reduce the search space. The optimized symbolic subcircuit is then seamlessly reintegrated into the full circuit by replacing the SPGs with their original entangling gates. This process is repeated iteratively over randomly selected subsets of two and three qubits until no further improvement is observed.

In a fourth aspect, the invention provides a complete, integrated circuit synthesis and optimization workflow. The workflow begins by taking a target Clifford unitary, specified for example by its action on the Pauli group or by its symplectic representation (tableau). The bidirectional greedy compiler is first applied to synthesize an initial circuit. This circuit is then subjected to a first pass of the three-stage template matching optimization to reduce the two-qubit gate count. Next, the symbolic peephole optimization is applied in multiple passes over pairs and triples of qubits to further drive down the two-qubit gate count. Finally, a last pass of template matching is performed to clean up and reduce any residual single-qubit gate overhead introduced during the optimization process. The result is a highly optimized quantum circuit that implements the target Clifford unitary with a minimal number of costly two-qubit gates.

The invention is demonstrated to be highly effective. On a benchmark set of 1,003 six-qubit Clifford unitaries with known optimal CNOT counts, the method of the present invention recovers the optimal CNOT count for 97.9% of the circuits and never produces a circuit more than one CNOT away from the optimum. On a separate benchmark of 2,264 "large" Clifford circuits derived from a toy model of Hamiltonian evolution on graphs with up to 64 qubits, the method reduces the average CNOT count by 64.7% compared to the canonical form of Aaronson and Gottesman, and by an average of 6.58% compared to the state-of-the-art CliffordSimp method from the tket framework. The invention thus provides a practical, scalable, and high-performance solution for a critical problem in the compilation of quantum algorithms, enabling more efficient and reliable execution of quantum protocols on near-term and future hardware.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention will now be described in detail with reference to its various components and operational steps. The following description is provided for the purpose of enabling a person of ordinary skill in the art to make and use the invention and is not intended to limit the scope of the claims in any way.

The invention is a computer-implemented method for generating an optimized quantum circuit that implements a target n-qubit Clifford unitary operation, U, where n is a positive integer. The primary objective of the optimization is to minimize the number of two-qubit entangling gates, such as CNOT or CZ gates, in the final circuit, as these gates are the dominant source of error and latency in contemporary quantum hardware. The method is executed by a classical computer system comprising a processor, a memory, and a non-transitory computer-readable storage medium storing instructions that, when executed by the processor, cause the system to perform the steps of the method. The target Clifford unitary U can be provided as input to the system in any suitable representation, such as a list of its action on the 2n generators of the n-qubit Pauli group, or more compactly, as a 2n x 2n binary symplectic matrix known as a tableau, which is a standard representation in the stabilizer formalism.

The method of the present invention comprises a sequence of four main stages: (1) an initial circuit synthesis stage using a bidirectional greedy compiler, (2) a first optimization stage using a three-stage template matching technique, (3) a second optimization stage using a symbolic peephole optimization technique, and (4) a final cleanup stage using a single-qubit-focused template matching pass. These stages are applied in a specific order to maximize the overall optimization gain, and their combination is a key inventive aspect of the present disclosure.

In the first stage, the bidirectional greedy compiler is employed to synthesize an initial circuit for the target unitary U. The compiler operates on the principle of recursive disentanglement. It seeks to construct two sequences of Clifford circuits, {L_j} and {R_j}, for j from 1 to n, such that the transformed unitary C_j = L_j⁻¹ ... L₁⁻¹ U R₁ ... R_j acts trivially on the first j qubits. The base case is C_0 = U. At each step j, the compiler must find a pair of anti-commuting n-qubit Pauli operators, P and P', such that the cost of a disentangler L for the pair (C_{j-1} x_j C_{j-1}⁻¹, C_{j-1} z_j C_{j-1}⁻¹) and the cost of a disentangler R⁻¹ for the pair (P, P') are minimized, subject to the constraint that the resulting C_j = L⁻¹ C_{j-1} R⁻¹ acts trivially on the j-th qubit. The disentangler for a pair of anti-commuting Pauli operators is a Clifford circuit that maps the first operator to a single-qubit X and the second to a single-qubit Z on a designated qubit, thereby disentangling that qubit from the rest of the system. The cost of a disentangler is defined as its CNOT count. To make the search for the optimal (P, P') pair tractable, the compiler restricts its search to Pauli operators of weight at most two, i.e., operators that act non-trivially on at most two qubits. This restriction reduces the search space from exponential to O(n³) pairs, making the compiler's runtime O(n⁵), which is feasible for practical values of n (e.g., n ≤ 100). The output of the bidirectional greedy compiler is a circuit that is a product of the L_j and R_j circuits, which is then converted into a standard gate sequence. During this conversion, any SWAP gates that were implicitly used to manage qubit ordering are explicitly factored out and placed into a dedicated "swap" stage. The resulting circuit is structured as a concatenation of three distinct stages: a "compute" stage, a "swap" stage, and a "Pauli" stage. The "compute" stage contains only H, S, CNOT, and CZ gates. The "swap" stage contains only SWAP gates. The "Pauli" stage contains only single-qubit X, Y, and Z gates. This three-stage structure is a deliberate and crucial feature of the invention, as it prepares the circuit for the specialized optimizations that follow.

The second stage of the method is the first optimization pass, which employs a Clifford-specific extension of the template matching technique. The input to this stage is the three-stage circuit from the synthesis stage. The first step of this optimization is to ensure that the circuit is in the canonical three-stage form. This is done by pushing all Pauli gates to the end of the circuit and all SWAP gates to just before the Pauli stage. This "pushing" is performed using the well-known commutation rules of the stabilizer formalism: for example, a CNOT gate conjugates a Pauli X on its control to a Pauli X on both control and target, and a Pauli Z on its target to a Pauli Z on both control and target. By systematically applying these rules, all Pauli gates can be moved past the H, S, CNOT, and CZ gates of the compute stage and collected in the Pauli stage. Similarly, SWAP gates can be commuted through the circuit, and their effect can be accounted for by a simple relabeling of the qubit wires. The net effect is that the compute stage is now a pure Clifford circuit without any Pauli or SWAP gates, which is the ideal substrate for template matching.

The compute stage is then optimized using a library of predefined templates. A template is an identity circuit, i.e., a sequence of gates that implements the identity operation. The library used in the present invention consists of a set of small, hand-crafted or algorithmically generated identity circuits that are particularly relevant for Clifford circuits. For example, a common template might be the identity that a CNOT gate followed by a CNOT gate on the same qubits is equivalent to the identity, or more complex identities involving H, S, and CNOT gates that can be used to replace a longer sequence with a shorter one. The template matching algorithm scans the compute stage from left to right. For each position in the circuit, it attempts to match a subsequence of gates with a prefix of a template. If a match of length p is found, and p is greater than half the total length m of the template, then the matched subsequence can be replaced with the inverse of the remaining (m-p) gates of the template, thereby reducing the total gate count. The algorithm is configured to prioritize the reduction of two-qubit gates, so a replacement is only accepted if it results in a net decrease in the CNOT/CZ count, even if it increases the single-qubit gate count. This process is repeated iteratively over the entire compute stage until a full pass yields no further improvements. After the primary template matching pass, the SWAP stage is optimized. This is done by recognizing that a SWAP gate can be decomposed into three CNOT gates, but if a SWAP gate is adjacent to a CNOT or CZ gate that acts on the same pair of qubits, they can be merged to effectively implement the SWAP at the cost of only one additional two-qubit gate. The optimizer analyzes the interface between the compute and swap stages to identify and perform such merges, thereby reducing the total two-qubit gate count.

The third stage of the method is the symbolic peephole optimization, which is a novel and central inventive feature of the present disclosure. This technique is designed to optimize subcircuits that are entangled with the rest of the system, a scenario where standard peephole optimization fails. The optimizer begins by selecting a small subset A of qubits, typically of size two or three. It then identifies all "entangling" CNOT gates in the current circuit that have one qubit in A and the other in its complement, B. Without loss of generality, the control of each such CNOT is assumed to be in B and the target in A; if not, the control and target are swapped by adding H gates on both qubits, which is a local transformation. The entangling CNOTs are then grouped by their control qubit in B. For each group, all CNOTs sharing the same control qubit v_i in B are replaced with a Symbolic Pauli Gate (SPG) on their respective target qubits in A. The SPG is a Pauli X, Y, or Z gate whose application is conditioned on the value of a symbolic binary variable v_i, which represents the state of the control qubit. This transformation effectively decouples the subcircuit on A from the rest of the system, converting it into a symbolic Clifford+SPG circuit, denoted U_A(v).

The symbolic circuit U_A(v) is then optimized. Its cost is defined as the number of two-qubit gates within U_A plus the number of SPGs. The optimization must respect the temporal order of the SPG groups (i.e., all SPGs from group i must come before those from group j if i < j) and must preserve the overall phase of the circuit modulo simple phase factors that can be corrected by single-qubit S or Z gates on the control qubits in B. The optimization problem is to find the sequence of Clifford operations on A that minimizes this cost. The present invention solves this problem using a dynamic programming algorithm. The key insight is that any Clifford+SPG circuit can be parameterized as a sequence of Clifford operators U_j interleaved with layers of SPGs. The total cost is a sum of the CNOT costs of the Clifford segments (U_j U_{j-1}⁻¹) and the Hamming weights of the SPG layers. Because this cost function is a sum of terms that each depend on at most two consecutive variables (U_{j-1} and U_j), it can be minimized efficiently with dynamic programming. The algorithm builds a lookup table of intermediate costs, f_j(U_j), which is the minimum cost to reach the Clifford operator U_j after processing the first j SPG groups. The table is built iteratively from j=1 to k (the number of groups). To make the algorithm practical, the search space is reduced by exploiting the symmetry that the cost is invariant under multiplication by any local single-qubit Clifford gate (H or S) on the qubits in A. This allows the algorithm to work with a set of coset representatives of the local Clifford subgroup, which is vastly smaller than the full Clifford group on A (e.g., 20 for 2 qubits, 6720 for 3 qubits). The dynamic programming algorithm computes the globally optimal symbolic circuit U_A(v)* in time O(k), where k is the number of SPG groups. The optimized symbolic circuit is then converted back into a standard quantum circuit by replacing each SPG with its original entangling CNOT gate, and any necessary phase corrections are applied to the control qubits in B. This entire process is repeated for many randomly selected subsets A of size two and three, in multiple passes, until no further reduction in the two-qubit gate count is observed.

The fourth and final stage of the method is a cleanup pass. After the aggressive two-qubit gate minimization of the previous stages, the circuit may contain an excess of single-qubit H and S gates. A final, single pass of template matching is applied, but this time the template library and matching criteria are configured to reduce the single-qubit gate count. Templates that span a single qubit (e.g., H-H = I, S-S-S-S = I) or that involve an even number of two-qubit gates (which would not change the two-qubit count) are used to simplify the circuit. This pass ensures that the final output is not only optimal in two-qubit gates but also as simple as possible in its single-qubit gate structure.

The output of the entire method is a highly optimized quantum circuit that implements the target Clifford unitary U. This circuit is ready to be executed on a quantum processor or used as a subroutine in a larger quantum algorithm. The method of the present invention has been empirically validated to produce circuits that are very close to the theoretical optimum for small instances and significantly outperform all known prior methods for large instances, thereby providing a substantial and practical advance in the field of quantum circuit compilation.
