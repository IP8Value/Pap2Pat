# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of semiconductor nanomaterials, and more specifically to doped semiconductor nanocrystals exhibiting controlled and tunable optoelectronic properties. The invention provides a method for the synthesis and post-synthetic modification of indium arsenide (InAs) nanocrystals through the incorporation of selected transition metal or noble metal impurities—specifically copper (Cu), silver (Ag), and gold (Au)—in a manner that enables precise modulation of their electronic band structure, absorption characteristics, and photoluminescence behavior. These doped InAs nanocrystals are particularly suited for applications in next-generation infrared optoelectronics, quantum information processing, photodetectors, light-emitting diodes, and solar energy conversion devices where tailored carrier concentration, doping type (n-type or p-type), and minimal lattice disruption are critical performance parameters. The invention further encompasses compositions of matter comprising such doped nanocrystals, as well as methods for characterizing and integrating them into functional devices.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Semiconductor nanocrystals, also known as quantum dots, represent a class of nanoscale materials whose physical properties are governed by quantum confinement effects. When the physical dimensions of a semiconductor particle fall below the excitonic Bohr radius of the bulk material, discrete energy levels emerge, leading to size-tunable optical absorption and emission spectra. This unique feature has enabled widespread use of quantum dots in displays, biological imaging, photovoltaics, and sensing technologies. However, while size control provides a powerful handle for tuning the bandgap energy, it does not inherently allow for independent control over the type and concentration of charge carriers—electrons or holes—which is essential for creating functional electronic and optoelectronic devices such as diodes, transistors, and lasers.

Doping—intentional introduction of impurity atoms into a semiconductor lattice—is the cornerstone of modern electronics, enabling the creation of n-type (electron-rich) and p-type (hole-rich) regions necessary for device functionality. In bulk semiconductors like silicon or gallium arsenide, doping is a mature and highly controlled process. However, extending this concept to colloidal semiconductor nanocrystals has proven exceptionally challenging due to several fundamental obstacles. First, the high surface-to-volume ratio of nanocrystals makes them thermodynamically unfavorable hosts for dopants, as impurity atoms tend to segregate to the surface rather than incorporate into the core lattice. Second, the strong quantum confinement alters the formation energies and ionization potentials of dopants, often rendering traditional dopants ineffective or causing self-purification, wherein the nanocrystal expels impurities to maintain its intrinsic electronic structure. Third, many conventional doping techniques used in bulk materials—such as ion implantation or high-temperature diffusion—are incompatible with the organic ligand shells that stabilize colloidal nanocrystals and prevent aggregation.

Prior attempts to dope III–V semiconductor nanocrystals, including InAs, have yielded inconsistent results. Some studies reported successful incorporation of manganese or other transition metals into InP or InAs, but these typically resulted in deep-level traps that quenched luminescence without providing useful free carriers. Others observed only surface adsorption rather than true lattice substitution or interstitial incorporation. Moreover, even when doping was achieved, the resulting changes in optical properties were often attributed ambiguously to either electronic doping effects or structural distortions (e.g., strain-induced bandgap shifts), making it difficult to establish a clear structure–property relationship.

In the specific context of InAs—a narrow-bandgap semiconductor with a bulk bandgap of approximately 0.35 eV at room temperature and high electron mobility—doping is particularly desirable for infrared applications. Yet, controlled n-type or p-type doping of InAs nanocrystals has remained elusive. Copper has been suggested as a potential donor in bulk InAs, occupying interstitial sites and contributing electrons, while silver and gold are known to act as acceptors when substituting for indium due to their lower valence. However, no systematic study had previously demonstrated how these elements could be controllably introduced into colloidal InAs nanocrystals in a way that produces predictable and reproducible shifts in absorption and emission energies, nor had the underlying mechanisms—whether electronic doping, lattice strain, or surface effects—been definitively elucidated.

Furthermore, existing theoretical models for doped quantum dots were largely limited to single-impurity scenarios or idealized symmetric configurations that do not reflect the stochastic, multi-impurity reality of experimental syntheses. Models based on hydrogenic impurities fail at the nanoscale due to incomplete dielectric screening, while atomistic simulations are computationally prohibitive for ensemble-averaged experimental data. Consequently, there existed a significant gap between theory and experiment in understanding how multiple, randomly distributed dopants influence the density of states, Fermi level position, and optical transitions in heavily doped nanocrystals.

Thus, despite decades of research, a reliable, scalable, and mechanistically understood method for producing doped InAs nanocrystals with tailored electronic properties remained an unmet need in the art. The present invention addresses this gap by providing a robust post-synthetic doping protocol that enables controlled incorporation of Cu, Ag, or Au into pre-formed InAs nanocrystals, coupled with comprehensive experimental and theoretical validation that distinguishes true electronic doping effects from artifacts caused by structural disorder or surface chemistry.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for the controlled doping of indium arsenide (InAs) semiconductor nanocrystals with selected metal impurities—copper (Cu), silver (Ag), and gold (Au)—to produce nanocrystals with tunable and predictable shifts in their optical absorption and photoluminescence spectra, indicative of n-type or p-type doping behavior. The method involves a post-synthetic treatment wherein a solution of metal salts (e.g., CuCl₂, AgNO₃, AgCl, or AuCl₃) in combination with specific surfactants—didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB) and dodecylamine (DDA)—is added dropwise to a dispersion of pre-synthesized, monodisperse InAs nanocrystals in an inert organic solvent such as toluene. This procedure is conducted under strictly anhydrous and oxygen-free conditions to prevent oxidation and ensure reproducibility.

The invention demonstrates that Cu doping induces a blue shift in the first excitonic absorption peak while leaving the photoluminescence energy substantially unchanged, consistent with n-type doping wherein additional electrons occupy states near the conduction band minimum, thereby increasing the effective bandgap for optical transitions due to Pauli blocking or Fermi level movement. In contrast, Ag doping causes a red shift in both absorption and emission energies, characteristic of p-type doping where acceptor states introduce holes near the valence band maximum, reducing the effective bandgap. Gold doping, by virtue of its isovalent nature with indium and similar ionic radius, produces minimal spectral shifts, confirming its role as a neutral impurity that does not contribute free carriers but may serve as a structural probe.

Critically, the invention establishes that these spectral shifts arise primarily from electronic doping effects rather than lattice strain or surface defects. This conclusion is supported by multiple lines of evidence: (1) X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis shows preservation of the zinc blende crystal structure with no detectable peak shifts beyond instrumental resolution, indicating that lattice parameter changes due to dopant incorporation are less than 1%; (2) transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals no secondary metal phases or surface clusters, confirming atomic-level dispersion of dopants; (3) X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) verifies the presence of Cu, Ag, or Au within the nanocrystal films and provides oxidation state information consistent with expected doping behavior (e.g., Cu⁺/Cu⁰, Ag⁺, Au⁰); and (4) inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) quantifies the number of metal atoms per nanocrystal, enabling correlation between dopant concentration and optical response.

The invention further discloses a calibration methodology whereby the metal-to-nanocrystal molar ratio in solution is correlated with the actual number of incorporated dopant atoms per nanocrystal via ICP-AES, allowing for precise control over doping levels. For Cu and Ag, this relationship exhibits distinct linear regimes at low and high concentrations, reflecting saturation effects or changes in incorporation mechanism, which are empirically modeled to enable predictive synthesis.

Additionally, the invention includes scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS) data acquired at cryogenic temperatures (4.2 K) on individual doped nanocrystals, which directly measures shifts in the local density of states. These measurements confirm that Cu-doped nanocrystals exhibit a negative shift in the center of the transport gap (consistent with upward movement of the Fermi level toward the conduction band), whereas Ag-doped nanocrystals show a positive shift (Fermi level moving toward the valence band), and Au-doped samples remain nearly identical to undoped controls. This single-particle evidence rules out ensemble averaging artifacts and conclusively links dopant identity to electronic structure modification.

Theoretical modeling developed as part of the invention introduces a “pseudo-crystal” framework that treats randomly distributed deep impurities as forming a narrow impurity band through short-range wavefunction overlap. This model successfully reproduces the experimentally observed non-monotonic and sign-dependent spectral shifts by accounting for both the number of dopants and their spatial distribution, bridging the gap between single-impurity hydrogenic models and bulk doping theory. Complementary calculations of band tailing due to atomic displacements confirm that structural disorder alone cannot account for the magnitude or directionality of the observed shifts, particularly the blue shift induced by Cu.

Accordingly, the invention provides not only a practical synthetic route to doped InAs nanocrystals but also a comprehensive mechanistic understanding that resolves long-standing ambiguities in the field. The resulting materials offer unprecedented control over carrier type and concentration in colloidal quantum dots, enabling the design of advanced optoelectronic devices that leverage both quantum confinement and intentional doping.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### EXPERIMENTAL

The invention is exemplified through a series of detailed experimental procedures and characterizations that collectively demonstrate the synthesis, doping, and property modulation of InAs nanocrystals. All operations involving air- or moisture-sensitive reagents were performed under an inert atmosphere (nitrogen or argon) using standard Schlenk line or glovebox techniques to ensure reproducibility and prevent oxidation of precursors or products.

**Synthesis of InAs Nanocrystals**: High-quality InAs nanocrystals were synthesized according to a modified literature procedure. In a representative synthesis, tris(trimethylsilyl)arsine (TMS₃As, 0.3 g, 1 mmol) was added to a solution of indium(III) chloride (InCl₃, 1.7 g of a 1.4 M solution in trioctylphosphine (TOP), containing 2 mmol In) in a sealed reaction vessel under nitrogen. This precursor mixture was then rapidly injected into a three-neck flask containing 2 mL of degassed TOP preheated to 300°C under vigorous magnetic stirring. Immediately after injection, the reaction temperature was lowered to 260°C to moderate growth kinetics. Additional aliquots of the precursor solution were introduced incrementally to achieve desired particle sizes, with growth progress monitored in real time by extracting small samples, diluting in toluene, and recording UV-Vis-NIR absorption spectra. Upon reaching the target size—typically yielding nanocrystals with diameters between 2.7 nm and 4.1 nm and a size distribution of approximately 10% full width at half maximum (FWHM)—the reaction was cooled to room temperature and transferred to an argon-filled glovebox. The crude product was purified by precipitation with anhydrous methanol, followed by redispersion in anhydrous toluene. Size-selective precipitation was employed to narrow the size distribution further, using toluene as the good solvent and methanol as the anti-solvent in iterative cycles.

**Metal-Atom Doping Procedure**: Doping was achieved via a post-synthetic treatment designed to facilitate controlled impurity incorporation. A doping solution was prepared by dissolving 10 mg of a metal salt—selected from CuCl₂, AgNO₃, AgCl, or AuCl₃—in 10 mL of anhydrous toluene containing 80 mg of didodecyldimethylammonium bromide (DDAB) and 120 mg of dodecylamine (DDA). DDAB acts as a phase-transfer agent and stabilizer, while DDA serves as a coordinating ligand that may assist in metal ion delivery to the nanocrystal surface. The resulting solutions exhibited characteristic colors: blue for Cu, colorless for AgNO₃/AgCl, and yellow for Au, consistent with known coordination chemistry. This doping solution was then added dropwise via syringe to a stirred 2 mL dispersion of purified InAs nanocrystals in toluene (concentration adjusted to yield an optical density of ~0.5 at the first exciton peak). The mixture was allowed to react for 15 minutes under inert atmosphere, during which time aliquots were periodically withdrawn for optical characterization. After reaction completion, the doped nanocrystals were precipitated by addition of methanol (for Cu and Au samples) or acetone (for Ag samples, to avoid co-precipitation issues), washed, and redispersed in toluene for subsequent analysis. The entire process was conducted without exposure to air or moisture.

**Optical Characterization**: Absorption spectra were recorded on a JASCO V-570 UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer using quartz cuvettes with 1 cm path length. Photoluminescence (PL) measurements were performed using an Ocean Optics NIR-512 spectrometer equipped with a 633 nm HeNe laser as the excitation source. Undoped InAs nanocrystals exhibited a sharp first exciton absorption peak and weak, broad PL due to inherent surface traps. Upon Cu doping, the absorption onset shifted to higher energies (blue shift of up to 80 meV depending on doping level), while the PL peak position remained essentially unchanged, though intensity was partially quenched. Ag doping produced a red shift in both absorption and PL (up to 60 meV), accompanied by stronger quenching. Au doping resulted in negligible spectral shifts, confirming its electrically neutral character. These trends were consistent across multiple nanocrystal sizes, indicating a doping mechanism independent of quantum confinement scale.

**Structural and Compositional Analysis**: Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was carried out on a Tecnai T12 G2 Spirit microscope operating at 120 keV. Samples were prepared by depositing a drop of nanocrystal solution onto carbon-coated copper grids, followed by washing with methanol or acetone to remove excess ligands. TEM images confirmed uniform particle morphology with no evidence of metal nanoparticle formation or surface aggregates, supporting atomic-level dopant incorporation. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected on a Philips PW1830/40 diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA. Samples were deposited as thin films on low-background quartz substrates. All doped samples retained the characteristic zinc blende reflections of InAs (e.g., (111), (220), (311)), with no new peaks attributable to metal oxides or elemental metals. Minor peak broadening at higher angles was observed, particularly for Cu-doped samples, suggestive of localized strain or defect scattering, but no systematic peak shifts were detected, implying lattice parameter changes below 1%.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on a Kratos Analytical Axis Ultra instrument using Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). Survey scans confirmed the presence of In, As, and the respective dopant (Cu, Ag, or Au) in treated samples, with no signals in control samples. High-resolution scans revealed binding energies consistent with expected oxidation states: Cu 2p₃/₂ at 932.7 eV (indicative of Cu⁺ or Cu⁰), Ag 3d₅/₂ at 368.2 eV (Ag⁺), and Au 4f₇/₂ at 83.9 eV (Au⁰). Notably, only Cu-doped samples showed minor In 3d and As 3d shoulder peaks assignable to In₂O₃ and As₂O₃, suggesting Cu may catalyze surface oxidation during brief air exposure, though this did not affect core doping conclusions.

Quantitative dopant loading was determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) using a PerkinElmer Optima 3000 instrument. Doped nanocrystals were digested in concentrated nitric acid, diluted with triple-distilled water, and analyzed for In (230.6, 325.6 nm), As (189.0, 193.7 nm), Cu (324.8 nm), Ag (328.1 nm), and Au (208.2 nm) emissions. By comparing metal-to-In/As ratios and estimating total atoms per nanocrystal from size (using bulk InAs density and lattice constant), the average number of dopant atoms per nanocrystal was calculated. Calibration curves (e.g., Figure S3 for Cu) showed linear dependence at low metal:NC ratios, saturating at higher concentrations, enabling precise dosing control.

**Single-Particle Spectroscopy**: Scanning tunneling microscopy and spectroscopy (STM/STS) provided direct evidence of doping-induced electronic structure changes. Nanocrystals were deposited on atomically flat Au(111) films by drop-casting and loaded into a cryogenic STM within 15 minutes of air exposure. At 4.2 K, spatially resolved dI/dV spectra were acquired on individual nanocrystals. Undoped InAs quantum dots exhibited a symmetric transport gap centered at 0 V bias. Cu-doped dots showed a clear negative shift (~50–100 meV) in the gap center, indicating Fermi level movement toward the conduction band. Ag-doped dots displayed a positive shift of similar magnitude, consistent with p-type behavior. Au-doped dots remained centered near 0 V. These shifts far exceeded sample-to-sample variations, confirming dopant-specific effects.

**Theoretical Validation**: To interpret the experimental data, three theoretical models were developed. The single hydrogenic impurity model predicted only red shifts, inconsistent with Cu-induced blue shifts. The Z-model (central Coulomb potential) predicted blue shifts at high doping but required unrealistic symmetry. The pseudo-crystal model—treating randomly placed deep impurities with short-range coupling—successfully reproduced the observed trends: Cu (donor) led to blue-shifted absorption due to impurity band formation below the conduction band, while Ag (acceptor) caused red shifts via states above the valence band. Band tailing simulations from atomic displacements showed red shifts only, ruling out strain as the cause of Cu’s blue shift.

In summary, the experimental embodiments demonstrate a robust, controllable method for doping InAs nanocrystals with Cu, Ag, or Au, yielding materials with predictable n-type, p-type, or neutral behavior, validated by multimodal characterization and theoretical modeling.
