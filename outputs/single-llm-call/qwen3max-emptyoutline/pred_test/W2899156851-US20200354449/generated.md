# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of targeted cancer therapeutics, specifically to novel fully human monoclonal antibodies that bind with high specificity and affinity to the human receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2 (ROR2). More particularly, the invention provides compositions comprising these anti-ROR2 monoclonal antibodies conjugated to cytotoxic agents via site-specific linkers to form antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) with potent and selective tumor cell-killing activity. The invention further encompasses methods for producing such ADCs, pharmaceutical formulations containing them, and their use in the treatment of ROR2-expressing cancers in human patients. The antibodies of the invention are characterized by their ability to recognize distinct epitopes on the extracellular domain of human ROR2, induce efficient internalization of the antibody-antigen complex upon binding to ROR2-expressing tumor cells, and facilitate the intracellular delivery of conjugated cytotoxic payloads leading to selective tumor cell death while sparing normal tissues that lack ROR2 expression. The invention also includes methods for identifying and selecting internalizing anti-ROR2 antibodies through a functional screening platform that enables direct assessment of ADC-like activity during the early stages of antibody discovery without requiring prior sequence recovery or reformatting of candidate molecules.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide despite significant advances in therapeutic approaches over recent decades. While the development of targeted therapies, including monoclonal antibody-based treatments and cellular immunotherapies, has improved outcomes for certain cancer types, broadly effective and curative treatment options remain limited for many malignancies. Conventional chemotherapy employing cytotoxic or cytostatic small molecules continues to serve as the standard of care for numerous cancer indications, yet these approaches are frequently hampered by dose-limiting toxicities and insufficient selectivity for malignant cells over healthy tissues, resulting in only partial clinical efficacy and significant adverse effects. The longstanding goal in oncology has therefore been to develop therapeutic strategies that can selectively deliver highly potent cytotoxic agents specifically to tumor cells while minimizing exposure and damage to normal tissues.

The advent of monoclonal antibody technology provided a promising avenue toward achieving this selectivity through the specific recognition of tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) that are either uniquely expressed or significantly overexpressed on cancer cells compared to normal tissues. Naked monoclonal antibodies can exert anti-tumor effects through various mechanisms, including direct interference with growth signaling pathways, induction of apoptosis, or recruitment of immune effector functions such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) or complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC). However, these mechanisms often prove insufficient for complete eradication of established tumors, particularly in cases where the target antigen does not play a critical role in tumor cell survival or proliferation.

To overcome these limitations, the concept of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) was developed, combining the target specificity of monoclonal antibodies with the potent cell-killing capacity of cytotoxic agents. ADCs typically consist of three components: a monoclonal antibody that specifically binds to a TAA, a cytotoxic payload (often orders of magnitude more potent than conventional chemotherapeutic agents), and a chemical linker that connects the antibody to the payload while maintaining stability in circulation but allowing for efficient release of the cytotoxic agent within the target tumor cell. This approach theoretically enables the delivery of otherwise intolerably toxic compounds directly to cancer cells, thereby expanding the therapeutic window and improving the safety profile compared to systemic administration of the same cytotoxic agents.

Despite the conceptual elegance of ADC technology, the successful development of clinically effective ADCs has proven challenging due to the complex interplay of multiple factors that influence both efficacy and safety. These include the selection of an appropriate TAA with sufficient tumor selectivity and expression levels, the identification of antibodies that not only bind with high affinity but also efficiently internalize upon antigen engagement, the design of linkers that maintain stability in plasma while enabling efficient intracellular payload release, and the optimization of drug-to-antibody ratios (DAR) to balance potency with pharmacokinetic properties. Consequently, despite decades of research and development efforts, only a limited number of ADCs have received regulatory approval for clinical use, while numerous candidates have failed in clinical trials due to insufficient efficacy or unacceptable toxicity profiles.

Receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2 (ROR2) has emerged as a particularly attractive target for ADC development due to its distinctive expression pattern and biological functions in cancer. ROR2, together with its homolog ROR1, belongs to the ROR family of type I transmembrane proteins that share structural similarities with receptor tyrosine kinases. The extracellular domain of ROR2 contains three distinct structural motifs: an N-terminal immunoglobulin-like domain, a Frizzled domain (also known as a cysteine-rich repeat domain), and a membrane-proximal Kringle domain, followed by a transmembrane region and intracellular domains with homology to receptor tyrosine kinases. During embryonic development, ROR2 is widely expressed across various tissues and plays crucial roles in regulating fundamental cellular processes including cell proliferation, polarity, differentiation, migration, metabolism, and survival through modulation of Wnt signaling pathways.

Critically, ROR2 expression is largely absent in most adult normal tissues, making it an ideal candidate for tumor-selective targeting. In contrast, ROR2 is frequently overexpressed in a diverse array of human malignancies, including renal cell carcinoma, osteosarcoma, melanoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumors, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, oral squamous cell carcinoma, and pancreatic cancer. Moreover, elevated ROR2 expression in these tumor types has been consistently associated with more aggressive disease phenotypes and poorer patient prognosis, suggesting a functional role in tumor progression and metastasis. Recent studies have demonstrated that ROR2 can interact with Wnt ligands such as Wnt5a and Wnt3a to activate both non-canonical and canonical Wnt signaling pathways, respectively, with upregulation of ROR2 promoting pro-tumorigenic activities including polarized cell migration, invasion, and tumor growth primarily through non-canonical Wnt signaling mechanisms.

Interestingly, ROR2 appears to exhibit context-dependent functions in carcinogenesis, as it can also act as a tumor suppressor in certain cancer types driven by canonical Wnt signaling, such as colon cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma, where ROR2 expression is typically lost. This dual functionality underscores the importance of careful patient selection based on ROR2 expression status when considering ROR2-targeted therapies. Additionally, ROR2 expression is generally mutually exclusive with ROR1 expression in most tumor types, with only rare instances of co-expression reported, which may have implications for combination therapeutic strategies targeting both receptors.

The development of effective ADCs against ROR2 presents several unique challenges that must be addressed to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. Unlike some TAAs that are expressed at very high levels on tumor cells, ROR2 expression tends to be moderate in many cancer types, necessitating the identification of antibodies with exceptional internalization capabilities to ensure sufficient intracellular delivery of the cytotoxic payload. The mode of action of ADCs requires not only specific binding to the target antigen but also efficient internalization of the antibody-antigen complex through receptor-mediated endocytosis, followed by trafficking to lysosomal compartments where the cytotoxic payload is released through linker cleavage in the acidic, protease-rich environment. Importantly, different antibodies recognizing the same antigen can exhibit vastly different internalization rates and efficiencies, even when binding to identical or overlapping epitopes, highlighting the critical need for functional screening approaches that can identify internalizing antibodies early in the discovery process.

Traditional antibody discovery platforms, including phage display, yeast display, and hybridoma technologies, typically focus on identifying antibodies based on binding affinity and specificity without providing information about their functional properties such as internalization efficiency. This limitation necessitates extensive downstream characterization and optimization efforts to identify suitable candidates for ADC development, significantly increasing the time, cost, and resource requirements of the discovery process. Furthermore, many antibody discovery platforms yield non-human antibodies that require humanization to reduce immunogenicity risks in clinical applications, adding another layer of complexity to the development pipeline.

Recent advances in transgenic mouse technology have enabled the generation of mice capable of producing fully human antibodies, addressing the immunogenicity concerns associated with murine or chimeric antibodies. However, these transgenic platforms often face challenges related to reduced immune responses compared to wild-type mice, potentially limiting the diversity and quality of the antibody repertoire generated. Additionally, conventional screening approaches using these platforms still require extensive post-discovery processing, including sequence recovery, recombinant expression, and functional characterization, before determining the suitability of candidate antibodies for ADC applications.

There remains a significant unmet need in the field for improved methods and compositions that can efficiently identify and develop fully human monoclonal antibodies with optimal characteristics for ADC applications against ROR2 and other challenging TAAs. Specifically, there is a need for integrated discovery platforms that combine the advantages of fully human antibody generation with direct functional screening capabilities to rapidly identify internalizing antibodies with potent ADC activity without requiring extensive post-discovery processing steps. Such platforms would greatly accelerate the development of effective ROR2-targeted ADCs and improve the likelihood of clinical success by enabling the selection of candidates with optimal functional properties from the earliest stages of discovery.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel fully human monoclonal antibodies that specifically bind to the extracellular domain of human receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2 (ROR2) with high affinity and demonstrate exceptional internalization capabilities upon binding to ROR2-expressing tumor cells. These antibodies are particularly suited for development as antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) due to their ability to efficiently mediate the intracellular delivery of cytotoxic payloads, resulting in potent and selective killing of ROR2-positive cancer cells while sparing normal tissues that lack ROR2 expression. The invention encompasses twelve distinct clonotypes of anti-ROR2 monoclonal antibodies, designated as GK-1E5, GK-2G8, MK-3B12, GK-5A1, GK-5E1, GK-5G12, GK-6B10, MK-7C3, GK-21D3, GK-22G12, MK-24C10, and MK-24F9, each characterized by unique heavy chain complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) sequences and demonstrating varying degrees of cross-reactivity with ROR2 from relevant toxicology species including cynomolgus monkey and mouse.

The antibodies of the invention exhibit binding affinities to human ROR2 ranging from 0.7 to 347 nanomolar, with six of the twelve clonotypes demonstrating sub-10 nanomolar affinity. Importantly, the invention demonstrates that binding affinity alone does not correlate directly with ADC potency, as some lower-affinity antibodies exhibit superior cell-killing activity compared to higher-affinity counterparts, likely due to differences in epitope recognition and internalization efficiency. Several of the disclosed antibodies, specifically MK-3B12, GK-5A1, GK-5E1, MK-24C10, MK-24F9, and GK-22G12, demonstrate cross-reactivity with cynomolgus monkey ROR2, facilitating preclinical toxicology studies, while GK-5A1 additionally shows strong binding to mouse ROR2, enabling evaluation in murine tumor models.

The invention further provides antibody-drug conjugates comprising any of the disclosed anti-ROR2 monoclonal antibodies conjugated to cytotoxic agents through site-specific conjugation technology. In particular embodiments, the cytotoxic agents are derivatives of the potent anthracycline PNU-159682, conjugated to the antibodies via sortase-mediated antibody conjugation (SMAC) technology at C-terminal LPETG tags engineered into both heavy and light chains. The resulting ADCs demonstrate drug-to-antibody ratios (DAR) between 3 and 4 and exhibit potent in vitro cytotoxicity against ROR2-expressing tumor cell lines with IC50 values ranging from 12.3 to 168 ng/ml, while showing minimal activity against ROR2-negative control cells.

Additionally, the invention encompasses a novel functional screening platform that enables the direct identification of internalizing anti-ROR2 antibodies during the early stages of discovery without requiring prior sequence recovery or recombinant expression. This platform utilizes the Transpo-mAb Display technology in combination with immune libraries derived from immunized transgenic mice expressing fully human antibody variable regions. The method involves generating cellular libraries displaying fully human IgG1 antibodies on the surface of immortalized murine progenitor B cells, followed by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate ROR2-binding clones. Critically, the supernatants from these sorted cellular clones are then directly screened for ADC-like activity using a secondary ADC assay, wherein the secreted antibodies are incubated with ROR2-expressing tumor cells followed by addition of a cytotoxic anti-human Fcγ secondary reagent conjugated to PNU-159682. This approach allows for simultaneous assessment of antigen binding and internalization capability, enabling rapid identification of lead candidates with optimal functional properties for ADC development.

The invention also provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising the disclosed anti-ROR2 antibodies or ADCs in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or diluents, formulated for administration to human patients suffering from ROR2-expressing cancers. Methods of treatment are provided for administering these compositions to patients diagnosed with cancers known to express ROR2, including but not limited to renal cell carcinoma, osteosarcoma, melanoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumors, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, oral squamous cell carcinoma, and pancreatic cancer. The therapeutic methods encompass both monotherapy approaches and combination regimens with other anti-cancer agents or modalities.

Furthermore, the invention includes nucleic acid molecules encoding the heavy and light chain variable regions of the disclosed anti-ROR2 antibodies, expression vectors containing these nucleic acid sequences, and host cells engineered to produce the antibodies or ADCs. Diagnostic methods are also provided for detecting ROR2 expression in biological samples using the disclosed antibodies, facilitating patient selection for ROR2-targeted therapies. The comprehensive nature of the invention addresses multiple aspects of ADC development, from initial antibody discovery through functional characterization to final therapeutic application, providing a complete solution for targeting ROR2-expressing malignancies with improved efficacy and safety profiles.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this invention, the following terms shall have the meanings set forth below unless otherwise specified. The term "antibody" refers to an immunoglobulin molecule that specifically binds to and is thereby defined by its specific antigen. Antibodies include, but are not limited to, polyclonal, monoclonal, multispecific, human, humanized, or chimeric antibodies, including intact immunoglobulins as well as fragments thereof such as Fab, F(ab')2, Fv, scFv, and diabodies that retain specific antigen-binding capability. The term "monoclonal antibody" as used herein refers to an antibody obtained from a population of substantially homogeneous antibodies, i.e., the individual antibodies comprising the population are identical except for possible naturally occurring mutations that may be present in minor amounts. Monoclonal antibodies are highly specific, being directed against a single antigenic site, and can be produced by hybridoma technology or recombinant DNA methods.

The term "fully human antibody" refers to an antibody wherein both the heavy and light chain variable regions are derived entirely from human immunoglobulin gene sequences, without any non-human amino acid residues in the variable domains. This includes antibodies produced by transgenic animals containing human immunoglobulin gene loci, human antibody phage display libraries, or other methods that generate antibodies with completely human variable region sequences. The constant regions may be of human or non-human origin, but the antigen-binding specificity is determined solely by human variable region sequences.

"ROR2" or "receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor 2" refers to the human protein encoded by the ROR2 gene (GenBank accession NP_004551.2), including all naturally occurring variants, isoforms, and post-translational modifications thereof. The term encompasses the full-length transmembrane protein as well as soluble forms containing the extracellular domain. For the purposes of this invention, ROR2 specifically refers to the human protein unless otherwise indicated, and includes orthologs from other species such as cynomolgus monkey (XP_005582291.1) and mouse (NP_038874.3) when explicitly referenced.

"Antibody-drug conjugate" or "ADC" refers to a conjugate of an antibody with a cytotoxic agent (payload) linked through a chemical linker. The antibody component provides target specificity by binding to a tumor-associated antigen, while the cytotoxic agent provides the cell-killing activity. The linker is designed to be stable in circulation but cleavable within the target cell to release the active payload. The drug-to-antibody ratio (DAR) represents the average number of drug molecules conjugated per antibody molecule.

"Internalization" refers to the process by which an antibody-antigen complex is taken up from the cell surface into the interior of the cell through receptor-mediated endocytosis. Efficient internalization is critical for ADC function as it enables the intracellular delivery of the cytotoxic payload to its site of action. Internalization can be assessed through various methods including flow cytometry with pH-sensitive dyes, fluorescence microscopy, or functional assays measuring payload delivery and cell killing.

"Transpo-mAb Display" refers to a mammalian cell-based antibody display platform wherein antibody libraries are stably integrated into the genome of immortalized progenitor B cells using transposon-mediated integration. This technology enables the co-expression of membrane-bound and secreted forms of antibodies from the same genetic construct, allowing for simultaneous surface display for selection and secretion for functional screening without requiring sequence recovery or recombinant expression.

"Sortase-mediated antibody conjugation" or "SMAC" refers to a site-specific conjugation technology that utilizes the bacterial enzyme sortase A to catalyze the transpeptidation reaction between an LPXTG motif engineered into the antibody and an oligoglycine-containing payload. This method enables controlled conjugation at specific sites on the antibody, resulting in homogeneous ADC products with defined DAR values.

"Complementarity-determining regions" or "CDRs" refer to the hypervariable regions within the variable domains of immunoglobulin heavy and light chains that are primarily responsible for antigen recognition and binding. CDRs are defined according to the Kabat numbering system or other standard definitions and include CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3 for both heavy and light chains, with CDR3 typically being the most diverse and critical for antigen specificity.

"Tumor-associated antigen" or "TAA" refers to any antigen that is expressed at higher levels on tumor cells compared to normal tissues, or that is uniquely expressed on tumor cells. TAAs serve as targets for antibody-based therapeutics and can include cell surface receptors, adhesion molecules, or other membrane proteins that are accessible to circulating antibodies.

"Pharmaceutically acceptable carrier" refers to any ingredient that is compatible with the other ingredients of a pharmaceutical formulation and is not deleterious to the recipient thereof. Such carriers include, but are not limited to, buffers, stabilizers, preservatives, isotonic agents, and solubilizers that maintain the stability, solubility, and biological activity of the active pharmaceutical ingredient.

### DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a comprehensive platform for the discovery, development, and therapeutic application of novel fully human monoclonal antibodies targeting human ROR2 for use as antibody-drug conjugates in cancer therapy. The foundation of this invention lies in the successful integration of transgenic mouse immunization with advanced mammalian cell display technology to enable direct functional screening for internalizing antibodies with optimal ADC characteristics. This approach overcomes the traditional limitations of antibody discovery platforms that focus solely on binding affinity without providing information about critical functional properties such as internalization efficiency.

The antibodies of the invention were discovered using H2L2 transgenic mice, which harbor human immunoglobulin heavy and light chain gene loci containing a limited repertoire of human V, D, and J gene segments on a background deficient in endogenous mouse immunoglobulin expression. These mice produce chimeric antibodies consisting of fully human variable regions paired with rat constant regions, enabling the generation of a diverse repertoire of human-sequence antibodies while maintaining proper B cell development and selection processes. The immunization strategy employed recombinant human ROR2 extracellular domain (ECD) fused to a C-terminal Twin-Strep-tag as the immunogen, administered through a prime-boost regimen designed to elicit robust humoral immune responses against conformational epitopes present on the native ROR2 protein.

Following immunization, antigen-specific B lymphocytes were enriched from splenic tissue using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) with the same recombinant ROR2-ECD-Twin-Strep protein used for immunization. This enrichment step significantly increased the frequency of ROR2-reactive B cells in the starting population, improving the efficiency of subsequent library construction. Variable region coding sequences were then amplified by RT-PCR using primers specific for human variable regions and rat constant regions, ensuring the recovery of fully human VH and VL sequences while maintaining the natural pairing of heavy and light chains from individual B cells.

The amplified variable region fragments were cloned into specialized transposable expression vectors designed for the Transpo-mAb Display platform. These vectors contain genomic versions of human IgG1 constant regions that enable alternative splicing to produce both membrane-bound and secreted forms of the antibody from the same genetic construct. This dual-expression capability is critical for the functional screening approach, as it allows for surface display of antibodies on host cells for selection while simultaneously secreting soluble antibody into the culture supernatant for functional testing.

Cellular libraries were generated by stable transposition of the heavy and light chain expression vectors into immortalized murine L11 progenitor B cells, which are incapable of expressing endogenous murine immunoglobulins due to their RAG2-deficient background. The transposition process ensures stable genomic integration of the antibody expression constructs, enabling long-term culture and expansion of antibody-producing clones. Following antibiotic selection to ensure co-expression of both heavy and light chains, the cellular libraries were subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to isolate clones displaying ROR2-specific antibodies on their surface.

The key innovation of the present invention lies in the direct functional screening of supernatants from sorted cellular clones for ADC-like activity without requiring prior sequence recovery or recombinant expression. This was achieved through a secondary ADC assay wherein the secreted antibodies in the culture supernatants were incubated with ROR2-expressing tumor cells, followed by addition of a cytotoxic anti-human Fcγ secondary reagent conjugated to the potent anthracycline derivative PNU-159682. This secondary reagent effectively "piggybacks" onto the primary antibody, entering the cell through the same internalization pathway and delivering its cytotoxic payload intracellularly. This approach provides a direct readout of the internalization capability and functional activity of the primary antibodies, enabling rapid identification of lead candidates with optimal ADC characteristics.

Through this integrated discovery platform, twelve distinct clonotypes of fully human anti-ROR2 monoclonal antibodies were identified and characterized. These antibodies demonstrate a range of binding affinities to human ROR2, from high picomolar to mid-nanomolar KD values, with varying association and dissociation kinetics. Importantly, the invention demonstrates that binding affinity alone does not predict ADC potency, as evidenced by the observation that some lower-affinity antibodies exhibit superior cell-killing activity compared to higher-affinity counterparts. This finding underscores the critical importance of functional screening approaches that assess internalization capability rather than relying solely on binding measurements.

Several of the disclosed antibodies demonstrate valuable cross-reactivity with ROR2 from relevant preclinical species. Specifically, six antibodies (MK-3B12, GK-5A1, GK-5E1, MK-24C10, MK-24F9, and GK-22G12) bind to cynomolgus monkey ROR2, facilitating toxicology studies in this important non-rodent species. Additionally, GK-5A1 shows strong binding to mouse ROR2, with weaker cross-reactivity observed for MK-3B12 and MK-24F9, enabling evaluation in murine tumor models. This cross-species reactivity is particularly valuable for preclinical development, as it allows for comprehensive safety and efficacy assessment in relevant animal models before advancing to human clinical trials.

The antibodies of the invention were further developed into site-specifically conjugated ADCs using sortase-mediated antibody conjugation (SMAC) technology. This approach involves engineering C-terminal LPETG sortase recognition motifs into both heavy and light chains of the antibodies, enabling controlled conjugation of glycine-modified cytotoxic payloads through the enzymatic activity of sortase A. The resulting ADCs demonstrate homogeneous drug-to-antibody ratios between 3 and 4, with consistent conjugation at the engineered sites. When tested against ROR2-expressing tumor cell lines, these ADCs exhibit potent cytotoxic activity with IC50 values ranging from 12.3 to 168 ng/ml, while showing minimal activity against ROR2-negative control cells, demonstrating excellent target specificity.

The therapeutic potential of the disclosed antibodies and ADCs extends across multiple cancer indications where ROR2 is overexpressed, including renal cell carcinoma, osteosarcoma, melanoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumors, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, oral squamous cell carcinoma, and pancreatic cancer. The selective expression pattern of ROR2, with minimal presence in adult normal tissues, provides a favorable therapeutic window for targeting ROR2-expressing tumors while minimizing off-target toxicity. Additionally, the association of ROR2 overexpression with aggressive disease phenotypes and poor prognosis suggests that ROR2-targeted therapies may provide significant clinical benefit to patients with advanced or refractory disease.

The invention also encompasses comprehensive pharmaceutical development aspects, including formulation strategies for the antibodies and ADCs, stability considerations, and administration protocols. The ADCs can be formulated in standard pharmaceutical buffers such as phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with appropriate stabilizers and preservatives to maintain stability during storage and administration. Various routes of administration are contemplated, including intravenous infusion, which is the most common route for monoclonal antibody and ADC therapies, although other parenteral routes may also be suitable depending on the specific clinical indication and patient population.

Diagnostic applications of the disclosed antibodies are also provided, enabling the detection and quantification of ROR2 expression in tumor biopsies and other biological samples. This capability facilitates patient selection for ROR2-targeted therapies, ensuring that treatment is directed toward patients most likely to benefit based on ROR2 expression status. The antibodies can be used in various diagnostic formats including immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, and ELISA, providing flexibility in clinical implementation.

The nucleic acid sequences encoding the heavy and light chain variable regions of the disclosed antibodies are also part of the invention, along with expression vectors containing these sequences and host cells engineered to produce the antibodies or ADCs. These molecular tools enable large-scale production of the therapeutic antibodies under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions for clinical and commercial supply. The availability of defined nucleic acid sequences also facilitates further engineering and optimization of the antibodies, including humanization (though the antibodies are already fully human), affinity maturation, or modification of effector functions as needed for specific therapeutic applications.

The comprehensive nature of the present invention addresses the entire spectrum of ADC development, from initial target validation and antibody discovery through functional characterization, conjugation technology, preclinical evaluation, and clinical application. By providing both the therapeutic agents themselves and the innovative discovery platform that enabled their identification, the invention represents a significant advance in the field of targeted cancer therapy, offering new hope for patients with ROR2-expressing malignancies who currently have limited treatment options.
