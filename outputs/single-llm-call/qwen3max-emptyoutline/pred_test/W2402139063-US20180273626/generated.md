# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of immunology and therapeutic antibody engineering, specifically to methods and compositions for modulating immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated allergic responses through targeted disruption or exchange of IgE bound to its high- and low-affinity receptors. More particularly, the invention provides novel engineered IgE variants that are resistant to binding by anti-IgE therapeutic antibodies such as omalizumab, while retaining the ability to bind to FcɛRI and CD23 receptors. These engineered IgE variants, when co-administered with anti-IgE therapeutics, enable the replacement of endogenous allergen-specific IgE on the surface of effector cells such as basophils and mast cells, thereby maintaining receptor occupancy without triggering allergic activation. The invention further encompasses structural insights into the molecular interactions between omalizumab and IgE, revealing the precise epitope recognized by omalizumab on the Cɛ3 domain of IgE and elucidating the mechanisms by which omalizumab inhibits IgE binding to both FcɛRI and CD23. These structural and functional findings provide a rational basis for the design of next-generation anti-IgE therapeutics with enhanced disruptive capabilities against preformed IgE–receptor complexes. Additionally, the invention includes methods for using these engineered IgE variants in combination with existing anti-IgE therapies to achieve synergistic inhibition of basophil and mast cell activation, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of allergic diseases such as asthma, chronic urticaria, and atopic dermatitis.

## BACKGROUND

Allergic diseases represent a significant global health burden, affecting hundreds of millions of individuals worldwide. These conditions arise from inappropriate immune responses to otherwise harmless environmental antigens, known as allergens, and are mediated primarily by immunoglobulin E (IgE). Upon initial exposure to an allergen, susceptible individuals produce allergen-specific IgE antibodies that bind with high affinity to the α-chain of the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcɛRI) expressed on the surface of mast cells and basophils. Subsequent re-exposure to the same allergen leads to cross-linking of receptor-bound IgE molecules, triggering intracellular signaling cascades that result in the release of inflammatory mediators such as histamine, leukotrienes, and cytokines, which collectively manifest as the symptoms of allergic reactions ranging from mild rhinitis to life-threatening anaphylaxis.

In addition to FcɛRI, IgE also interacts with a low-affinity receptor known as CD23 (FcɛRII), which is expressed on B cells, monocytes, and other immune cells. The IgE–CD23 interaction plays a critical role in regulating IgE production through feedback mechanisms, as well as in antigen presentation and transcytosis of IgE–allergen complexes across epithelial barriers. Both FcɛRI and CD23 bind to distinct but overlapping regions on the Cɛ3 domain of the IgE Fc region, making this domain a key target for therapeutic intervention.

Omalizumab, a humanized monoclonal antibody approved for the treatment of moderate-to-severe allergic asthma and chronic idiopathic urticaria, represents the first-in-class anti-IgE therapy. It functions by binding to free IgE in circulation, thereby preventing its interaction with FcɛRI and CD23. This leads to a reduction in free IgE levels, downregulation of FcɛRI expression on effector cells due to lack of receptor occupancy, and ultimately, decreased cellular sensitivity to allergen challenge. However, despite its clinical efficacy, omalizumab has several limitations. Notably, it does not effectively dissociate preformed IgE–FcɛRI complexes on the surface of mast cells and basophils, which can persist for weeks to months, particularly on tissue-resident mast cells. As a result, the therapeutic onset is delayed, and complete disarming of effector cells requires prolonged treatment.

Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that the depletion of IgE by omalizumab may trigger homeostatic compensatory mechanisms. For instance, reduced IgE occupancy of CD23 on B cells can lead to increased IgE synthesis, potentially counteracting the therapeutic effect. Additionally, studies have shown that basophils from omalizumab-treated patients exhibit heightened sensitivity on a per-IgE basis, possibly due to altered receptor signaling dynamics in the context of partial IgE occupancy. These observations raise concerns that complete IgE removal might inadvertently exacerbate underlying immune dysregulation in some patients.

Structurally, the mechanism by which omalizumab inhibits IgE–receptor interactions has been incompletely understood. Early models proposed that omalizumab binds to an epitope overlapping directly with the FcɛRI-binding site, thereby acting as a classical competitive inhibitor. However, recent biochemical and cellular studies have challenged this view, suggesting instead that omalizumab may act primarily through steric hindrance rather than direct residue competition. Furthermore, the conformational flexibility of the IgE Fc region—particularly the relative orientation of the Cɛ3 and Cɛ4 domains, which adopt "open" and "closed" states associated with FcɛRI and CD23 binding, respectively—adds complexity to the understanding of omalizumab’s binding kinetics and inhibitory mechanism.

Compounding these uncertainties is the role of the Cɛ2 domain in full-length IgE. While omalizumab binds effectively to IgE fragments containing only the Cɛ3–Cɛ4 domains (IgE-Fc3–4), its association rate with full-length IgE is significantly slower, suggesting that the Cɛ2 domains may partially occlude the omalizumab epitope in the native IgE conformation. This conformational masking may explain why omalizumab binds preformed IgE-Fc3–4:FcɛRIα complexes but not full-length IgE:FcɛRIα complexes, highlighting a critical distinction between truncated and intact IgE molecules.

In light of these limitations and mechanistic ambiguities, there remains a pressing need for improved anti-IgE strategies that can more effectively neutralize both circulating and cell-bound IgE, while simultaneously preserving beneficial regulatory functions of IgE–receptor interactions. One promising approach involves the concept of "IgE exchange," wherein endogenous allergen-specific IgE is replaced with a non-pathogenic, omalizumab-resistant IgE variant that maintains receptor occupancy without supporting allergen-induced activation. Such a strategy could theoretically prevent the homeostatic upregulation of IgE synthesis, stabilize receptor expression, and avoid the paradoxical increase in cellular sensitivity observed during conventional anti-IgE therapy.

However, the feasibility of this approach hinges on the identification of IgE mutations that abolish omalizumab binding without compromising receptor affinity—a challenge that requires precise structural knowledge of the omalizumab–IgE interface. Prior mutagenesis studies have implicated several residues in the Cɛ3 domain as critical for omalizumab recognition, but a high-resolution structural definition of the epitope has been lacking. Without such information, rational design of omalizumab-resistant IgE variants remains speculative.

Accordingly, the background art lacks a comprehensive structural and functional characterization of the omalizumab–IgE interaction, as well as validated methods for achieving IgE exchange on human effector cells. The present invention addresses these gaps by providing the first crystal structure of the omalizumab–IgE complex, defining the exact molecular determinants of the epitope, and demonstrating the successful engineering and application of an omalizumab-resistant IgE variant that enables receptor occupancy maintenance during anti-IgE therapy.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel compositions and methods for the treatment of IgE-mediated allergic disorders through the strategic exchange of endogenous allergen-specific IgE with engineered, omalizumab-resistant IgE variants that retain the ability to bind to both FcɛRI and CD23 receptors. Central to the invention is the discovery and structural characterization of the precise epitope on the IgE Cɛ3 domain that is recognized by omalizumab, enabling the rational design of IgE mutants that evade omalizumab binding while preserving receptor interactions. Specifically, the invention discloses an IgE-Fc3–4 variant comprising a substitution at position 419 of the IgE heavy chain, wherein arginine is replaced by asparagine (R419N), which introduces a novel N-linked glycosylation site within the core of the omalizumab epitope. This glycosylation sterically blocks omalizumab access without significantly perturbing FcɛRI or CD23 binding, rendering the variant functionally resistant to omalizumab neutralization.

The invention further provides a method for exchanging cell-surface IgE on human basophils and B cells by co-administering omalizumab and the R419N IgE-Fc3–4 variant. In this method, omalizumab neutralizes and removes endogenous IgE, while the R419N variant simultaneously occupies vacant FcɛRI and CD23 receptors, thereby maintaining receptor occupancy and preventing homeostatic upregulation of IgE synthesis. This exchange is demonstrated ex vivo using human whole blood, where basophils pre-loaded with a traceable IgE species are effectively stripped of native IgE by omalizumab and reloaded with the R419N variant, but not with wild-type IgE-Fc3–4, under identical conditions.

Additionally, the invention reveals that the R419N IgE-Fc3–4 variant acts synergistically with omalizumab to inhibit basophil activation. At concentrations as low as 1 nM, the R419N variant, when combined with therapeutic doses of omalizumab (500 nM), nearly abolishes antigen-induced basophil degranulation, an effect significantly greater than either agent alone. This synergy suggests that receptor occupancy by a non-activating IgE variant may actively suppress FcɛRI signaling pathways, beyond simple competitive inhibition.

The structural basis for these functional effects is elucidated through the determination of the crystal structure of the IgE–omalizumab complex at 2.5 Å resolution. This structure shows that omalizumab binds perpendicularly to the face of the IgE Cɛ3 dimer, contacting 23 IgE residues and burying approximately 725 Å² of surface area. The epitope is located below the FcɛRIα-binding loops and is largely distinct from the receptor interface, with only minor peripheral overlap involving residues P426 and R427. Omalizumab inhibits FcɛRI and CD23 binding primarily through steric clashes rather than direct competition: its light chain sterically conflicts with FcɛRIα at binding site 2, while its heavy chain overlaps extensively with CD23. Quantitative analysis reveals that omalizumab exhibits greater steric overlap with FcɛRIα (1,183 Å³) than disruptive DARPin inhibitors such as E2_79 (401 Å³), yet is far less efficient at dissociating preformed IgE–FcɛRI complexes, likely due to the rigidity of its binding mode and limited accessibility to the constrained interface in the closed IgE conformation.

The invention also clarifies the role of IgE conformation in omalizumab binding. Kinetic studies demonstrate that omalizumab binds equally well to both open and closed conformations of the IgE Cɛ3 domain, as evidenced by similar association and dissociation rates with wild-type IgE-Fc3–4, a closed-conformation mutant (G335C), and IgE-Fc3–4 pre-bound to FcɛRIα. However, in full-length IgE, the Cɛ2 domains reduce the association rate by approximately 15–30-fold by masking one of the two symmetric omalizumab epitopes, though the overall affinity is only modestly affected due to a compensatory slower dissociation rate.

Collectively, the invention provides a comprehensive platform for next-generation anti-IgE therapy, combining structural insights, engineered biologics, and novel treatment paradigms to overcome the limitations of current approaches. The disclosed IgE exchange strategy offers a means to maintain immune homeostasis while neutralizing pathogenic IgE, with potential applications in severe asthma, food allergy, and other IgE-driven conditions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “IgE” refers to immunoglobulin E, a class of antibody involved in allergic reactions and defense against parasitic infections. IgE consists of two heavy chains and two light chains, with the heavy chains containing one variable domain (VH) and four constant domains (Cɛ1–Cɛ4). The Fc region of IgE, responsible for receptor binding, comprises the Cɛ2, Cɛ3, and Cɛ4 domains. For the purposes of this invention, “IgE-Fc3–4” denotes a recombinant fragment of IgE encompassing the Cɛ3 and Cɛ4 domains, which retains the ability to bind FcɛRI and CD23 but lacks the Cɛ2 domain and antigen-binding Fab regions.

The term “omalizumab” refers to a humanized monoclonal antibody of the IgG1κ isotype that specifically binds to the Cɛ3 domain of human IgE, thereby inhibiting its interaction with FcɛRI and CD23. Omalizumab is clinically used under the trade name Xolair® for the treatment of allergic asthma and chronic urticaria.

An “omalizumab-resistant IgE variant” is an engineered form of IgE or IgE-Fc3–4 that exhibits substantially reduced or abolished binding to omalizumab while maintaining the ability to bind to FcɛRI and/or CD23. A specific example is the R419N mutation in the IgE heavy chain, which introduces an N-linked glycosylation site (Asn-Val-Thr) at position 419, leading to steric occlusion of the omalizumab epitope.

“FcɛRI” (high-affinity IgE receptor) is a tetrameric receptor expressed primarily on mast cells and basophils, composed of one α-chain, one β-chain, and two γ-chains. The α-chain contains the IgE-binding domain. “CD23” (low-affinity IgE receptor or FcɛRII) is a type II transmembrane protein expressed on B cells and other immune cells that binds IgE with lower affinity and regulates IgE production and transport.

“IgE exchange” refers to the process of replacing endogenous, allergen-specific IgE bound to FcɛRI or CD23 on the surface of immune cells with an exogenously administered, non-pathogenic IgE variant that does not support allergen-induced activation. This process maintains receptor occupancy while eliminating the risk of allergic response.

The term “synergistic inhibition” describes a combined effect of two agents—here, omalizumab and an omalizumab-resistant IgE variant—that is greater than the sum of their individual effects in suppressing basophil or mast cell activation.

“Steric clash” or “steric overlap” refers to the physical interference between two macromolecules when bound to the same target, such that their atomic volumes occupy the same space, preventing simultaneous binding. This is quantified herein as the volume of atomic overlap in cubic angstroms (Å³).

“Closed conformation” and “open conformation” describe distinct structural states of the IgE Fc region: in the closed state, the Cɛ3 domains are closely apposed, favoring CD23 binding; in the open state, the Cɛ3 domains are splayed apart, facilitating FcɛRI engagement.

### II. MODES OF CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The present invention may be carried out by administering to a subject in need thereof a therapeutically effective amount of an omalizumab-resistant IgE variant, such as IgE-R419N-Fc3–4, in combination with omalizumab or a functionally equivalent anti-IgE antibody. The IgE variant is formulated in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier and delivered via intravenous, subcutaneous, or intramuscular injection, typically at doses ranging from 0.1 nM to 10 μM, depending on the clinical context. Omalizumab is administered according to established dosing regimens based on body weight and baseline IgE levels.

Prior to administration, endogenous cell-surface IgE may be partially depleted using a disruptive IgE inhibitor such as a DARPin (e.g., E2_79 or bi53_79) to accelerate the exchange process, although this step is optional as omalizumab itself can gradually remove surface IgE over time. The IgE variant is designed to bind FcɛRI and CD23 with affinities comparable to wild-type IgE, ensuring stable receptor occupancy without inducing signaling in the absence of allergen.

The R419N mutation is introduced into an IgE-Fc3–4 backbone using standard molecular biology techniques, such as site-directed mutagenesis, and the protein is expressed in mammalian cells (e.g., HEK-293 or CHO) to ensure proper glycosylation. The resulting glycoprotein is purified by affinity chromatography (e.g., Ni-NTA for His-tagged constructs) followed by size-exclusion chromatography to ensure homogeneity.

For diagnostic or research applications, the IgE variant may be labeled with biotin, fluorophores, or other detectable moieties to track receptor occupancy in flow cytometry or imaging assays. In therapeutic settings, unlabeled protein is preferred to minimize immunogenicity.

The invention is particularly suited for patients with severe allergic asthma, chronic spontaneous urticaria, or food allergies who exhibit suboptimal responses to omalizumab monotherapy. By maintaining FcɛRI and CD23 occupancy, the combination therapy may prevent the rebound increase in IgE synthesis and heightened cellular sensitivity sometimes observed during anti-IgE treatment.

### III. EXPERIMENTAL

The crystal structure of the IgE–omalizumab complex was determined by co-crystallizing an engineered IgE-Fc3–4 variant (IgE-G335C-Fc3–4), which is locked in a closed conformation via a disulfide bond at position 335, with omalizumab Fab fragments. Crystals were grown in hanging drops containing 0.2 M lithium sulfate, 0.1 M Tris pH 8.5, and 41% PEG 400, and diffracted to 2.5 Å resolution. The structure was solved by molecular replacement using existing models of IgE-G335C-Fc3–4 and omalizumab Fab, revealing four copies of the interface in the asymmetric unit. Electron density was well resolved at the binding interface, allowing precise mapping of contact residues.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays were performed on a BIAcore X100 instrument to measure binding kinetics. Omalizumab or FcɛRIα was immobilized on a CM5 chip, and various IgE constructs—including full-length IgE, wild-type IgE-Fc3–4, IgE-G335C-Fc3–4, and IgE-R419N-Fc3–4—were injected at multiple concentrations. Association (ka) and dissociation (kd) rates were derived from sensorgrams, and equilibrium dissociation constants (KD) were calculated. The R419N mutant showed no detectable binding to omalizumab, while retaining near-wild-type affinity for FcɛRIα.

For cellular exchange experiments, human whole blood was collected from healthy donors and treated with the DARPin E2_79 (25 μM, 2 h, 37°C) to strip surface IgE. Cells were then reloaded with JW8-IgE (a chimeric human/mouse IgE) and subsequently incubated overnight with omalizumab (25 μM) in the presence or absence of biotinylated IgE-Fc3–4 or IgE-R419N-Fc3–4. Flow cytometry using anti-mouse λ-light chain and anti-biotin antibodies revealed that only the R419N variant could reload onto basophils in the presence of omalizumab.

Basophil activation tests were conducted using purified primary human basophils desensitized with bi53_79 DARPin and resensitized with NP-specific JW8-IgE. Cells were treated for 3 or 6 days with omalizumab (500 nM), IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 (1–100 nM), or both, then challenged with NIP-BSA. Activation was measured by CD63 upregulation via flow cytometry. Combination treatment with 500 nM omalizumab and 1 nM IgE-R419N-Fc3–4 reduced activation by over 90% compared to untreated controls, demonstrating potent synergy.

Steric overlap volumes were computed by structurally aligning the Cɛ3 domains of the IgE–omalizumab, IgE–E2_79, and IgE–FcɛRIα complexes, then calculating atomic overlaps using CCP4 software tools (pdbset, sfall, mapmsk, overlapmap) and Chimera. Omalizumab exhibited 1,183 Å³ of overlap with FcɛRIα, versus 401 Å³ for E2_79, correlating with their relative inefficiencies in disrupting preformed complexes.

These experimental results collectively validate the structural model, confirm the functionality of the R419N variant, and demonstrate the therapeutic potential of IgE exchange as a novel strategy for allergic disease management.
