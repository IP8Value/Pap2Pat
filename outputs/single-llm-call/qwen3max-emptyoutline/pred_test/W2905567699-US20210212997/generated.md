# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to the field of immunology and cancer therapeutics. More specifically, the invention pertains to methods and compositions for modulating macrophage polarization through activation of the integrin CD11b/CD18 (also known as αMβ2 or Mac-1). The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising CD11b agonists, such as leukadherin-1 (LA1), for use in treating neoplastic diseases by repolarizing tumor-associated macrophages from an immunosuppressive phenotype to a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor phenotype. Additionally, the invention includes methods for enhancing anti-tumor immune responses, inhibiting pathological angiogenesis, and improving the efficacy of conventional chemotherapeutic agents through CD11b-mediated regulation of microRNA Let-7a expression and downstream signaling pathways involving c-Myc and IL-6.

### Background Information

Macrophages are versatile immune cells that play critical roles in both innate and adaptive immunity, tissue homeostasis, wound healing, and pathological conditions such as chronic inflammation and cancer. Within the tumor microenvironment, macrophages—often referred to as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)—are among the most abundant immune cell populations and are known to exert profound influence over tumor progression, metastasis, and response to therapy. TAMs typically adopt an immunosuppressive, pro-tumoral phenotype characterized by the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β), pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF-A, PDGF-BB), and enzymes that suppress T-cell function (e.g., arginase-1). This phenotype supports tumor immune evasion, angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and metastasis.

In contrast, classically activated or pro-inflammatory macrophages exhibit anti-tumor properties through the secretion of inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-12, IFN-γ), reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, and the promotion of cytotoxic T-cell responses. The balance between these opposing macrophage phenotypes is dynamically regulated by signals within the tumor microenvironment, including cytokines, growth factors, hypoxia, and interactions with other immune and stromal cells.

Integrins are a family of heterodimeric transmembrane receptors that mediate cell–cell and cell–extracellular matrix adhesion, and also function as bidirectional signaling molecules that regulate cellular activation, migration, survival, and differentiation. Among these, the β2 integrins are expressed exclusively on leukocytes and include CD11a/CD18 (LFA-1), CD11b/CD18 (Mac-1), CD11c/CD18 (p150,95), and CD11d/CD18. CD11b/CD18 is predominantly expressed on myeloid cells—including monocytes, macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells—and binds multiple ligands such as intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), fibrinogen, complement component iC3b, and heparin.

Historically, CD11b has been studied primarily in the context of leukocyte adhesion and transmigration during acute inflammation. However, its role in chronic inflammatory settings such as cancer has remained less defined. Prior art teaches that CD11b is dispensable for myeloid cell recruitment into tumors, despite its well-established role in trafficking during acute inflammation. This apparent paradox suggests that CD11b may serve non-adhesive, signaling functions within the tumor microenvironment that are distinct from its classical role in cell migration.

Recent studies have identified several molecular pathways that regulate macrophage polarization in tumors, including signaling through colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R), phosphoinositide 3-kinase gamma (PI3Kγ), Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK), and toll-like receptors (TLRs). Small-molecule inhibitors targeting these pathways are currently under clinical investigation for cancer immunotherapy. However, none of these approaches directly engage integrin-mediated signaling to reprogram macrophage function.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small non-coding RNAs that post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression by binding to complementary sequences in target mRNAs, leading to mRNA degradation or translational repression. The Let-7 family of miRNAs is evolutionarily conserved and functions as a tumor suppressor by targeting oncogenes such as RAS, HMGA2, and c-Myc. In immune cells, Let-7 has been shown to regulate inflammatory responses, with reduced Let-7 expression associated with enhanced IL-6 production and STAT3 activation—key drivers of immunosuppressive macrophage polarization.

The transcription factor c-Myc is a master regulator of cellular metabolism, proliferation, and differentiation. In macrophages, c-Myc promotes an immunosuppressive phenotype by upregulating genes involved in glycolysis, arginine metabolism, and angiogenesis, while simultaneously repressing pro-inflammatory pathways. Notably, c-Myc and Let-7 engage in a reciprocal regulatory loop: c-Myc suppresses Let-7 transcription, and Let-7 targets c-Myc mRNA for degradation, creating a bistable switch that can lock macrophages into either a pro- or anti-inflammatory state.

Despite these advances, there remains a critical unmet need for therapeutic strategies that can selectively repolarize TAMs toward an anti-tumor phenotype without inducing systemic inflammation or compromising host defense. The prior art does not disclose or suggest that pharmacological activation of CD11b can modulate this Let-7/c-Myc/IL-6 axis to reprogram macrophage function and inhibit tumor growth. Furthermore, while vascular normalization—characterized by increased pericyte coverage and reduced vessel leakiness—has been shown to improve drug delivery and immunotherapy efficacy, no existing approach links integrin signaling in myeloid cells to the regulation of vascular maturation via PDGF-BB expression.

Leukadherin-1 (LA1) is a small-molecule allosteric agonist of CD11b that stabilizes the high-affinity conformation of the integrin, enhancing its binding to ICAM-1 and promoting outside-in signaling. Previous studies have demonstrated LA1’s efficacy in reducing inflammation in models of autoimmune disease and ischemia-reperfusion injury by inhibiting leukocyte extravasation. However, the application of LA1—or any CD11b agonist—for cancer immunotherapy, particularly through modulation of macrophage polarization and tumor vasculature, has not been previously described.

Thus, there exists a significant gap in the art regarding the use of CD11b agonists to therapeutically reprogram the tumor immune microenvironment. The present invention addresses this gap by demonstrating that CD11b activation, whether genetic or pharmacological, induces a pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotype via upregulation of Let-7a, suppression of c-Myc and IL-6, and downregulation of PDGF-BB, thereby inhibiting tumor growth, enhancing T-cell infiltration, and modulating vascular architecture. These findings establish CD11b as a novel and druggable target for cancer immunotherapy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides methods and compositions for treating cancer by activating integrin CD11b on myeloid cells, particularly macrophages, to promote a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor phenotype and inhibit tumor progression. The invention is based on the discovery that CD11b signaling regulates macrophage polarization through a molecular axis involving microRNA Let-7a, the transcription factor c-Myc, and the cytokine IL-6. Specifically, activation of CD11b—either by genetic means or by administration of a CD11b agonist such as leukadherin-1 (LA1)—upregulates Let-7a expression, which in turn suppresses c-Myc and IL-6, leading to decreased expression of immunosuppressive and pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., Arg1, PDGF-BB, TGF-β) and increased expression of pro-inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-α, IL-12, iNOS).

In one aspect, the invention provides a method of treating a subject having a neoplastic disease, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of a CD11b agonist. The CD11b agonist may be leukadherin-1 or a structurally related compound that allosterically activates CD11b/CD18. The neoplastic disease may be selected from the group consisting of lung carcinoma, melanoma, breast cancer, and other solid tumors characterized by abundant tumor-associated macrophages.

In another aspect, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising a CD11b agonist and a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, wherein the composition is formulated for systemic administration (e.g., intravenous, intraperitoneal) and is capable of repolarizing tumor-associated macrophages in vivo.

In a further aspect, the invention includes a method of enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapy in a subject with cancer, comprising co-administering a CD11b agonist and a chemotherapeutic agent (e.g., gemcitabine, taxol). Although CD11b activation alone may reduce tumor growth by promoting immune-mediated suppression, the combination with chemotherapy exploits the dual effects of CD11b signaling: while LA1-treated tumors exhibit reduced vascular normalization (i.e., more immature, leaky vessels), CD11b-deficient tumors show enhanced vascular maturation. Paradoxically, the invention reveals that transient or targeted modulation of this pathway can be leveraged to improve drug delivery when strategically combined with cytotoxic agents.

Additionally, the invention encompasses methods of modulating tumor vasculature by regulating PDGF-BB expression in macrophages via CD11b activation. By suppressing PDGF-BB, CD11b agonists reduce pericyte coverage of tumor blood vessels, maintaining a more immature vascular phenotype that, in certain contexts, may limit tumor perfusion and growth.

The invention also provides diagnostic and prognostic methods based on the expression levels of Let-7a, c-Myc, or IL-6 in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells, which may serve as biomarkers to predict responsiveness to CD11b-targeted therapy.

Collectively, the invention establishes CD11b as a central regulator of macrophage functional plasticity in cancer and provides a novel therapeutic strategy to reprogram the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment into one that supports anti-tumor immunity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### EXAMPLE 1

The present invention is exemplified through a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments that demonstrate the critical role of integrin CD11b in regulating macrophage polarization, tumor immune responses, and vascular architecture. These studies utilize genetic models (CD11b knockout and constitutively active CD11b knock-in mice), pharmacological tools (leukadherin-1), and molecular techniques (siRNA knockdown, miRNA modulation) to elucidate the mechanism by which CD11b activation suppresses tumor growth.

First, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from wild-type (WT) and CD11b-deficient (Itgam−/−) mice and stimulated under basal, IL-4 (anti-inflammatory), or IFNγ/LPS (pro-inflammatory) conditions. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that Itgam−/− macrophages exhibited significantly elevated expression of immunosuppressive genes—including Arg1, Tgfb, Il10, Il6, and Pdgfb—and markedly reduced expression of pro-inflammatory genes such as Ifng, Nos2, and Tnfa, regardless of stimulation condition. This phenotype was recapitulated in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) isolated from Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) tumors grown in Itgam−/− mice, confirming that CD11b deficiency promotes an immunosuppressive transcriptional program in vivo.

To determine whether this effect was due to loss of CD11b expression or function, transient siRNA-mediated knockdown of Itgam in WT macrophages similarly enhanced immunosuppressive gene expression and suppressed pro-inflammatory markers. Moreover, treatment of WT macrophages with neutralizing anti-CD11b antibodies during adhesion to ICAM-1-coated plates induced an immunosuppressive profile, whereas adhesion to ICAM-1 (a CD11b ligand) promoted pro-inflammatory transcription, while adhesion to VCAM-1 (an α4β1 ligand) or suspension culture favored immunosuppression. These data indicate that CD11b ligation, not merely its presence, is required for pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization.

Mechanistically, Itgam−/− macrophages showed reduced phosphorylation of NFκB p65 at Ser536—a key activation mark—in response to LPS, suggesting impaired NFκB signaling. Concurrently, these cells exhibited constitutive phosphorylation of STAT3 and elevated c-Myc expression. Given that IL-6 is a potent activator of STAT3 and a target of Let-7a, the inventors investigated the role of this miRNA. Indeed, Let-7a expression was significantly reduced in Itgam−/− macrophages, and this reduction was reversed by CD11b ligation on ICAM-1. Ectopic expression of Let-7a in Itgam−/− macrophages restored pro-inflammatory gene expression and suppressed immunosuppressive markers, while inhibition of Let-7a in WT macrophages mimicked the Itgam−/− phenotype.

Further experiments demonstrated that c-Myc, which is upregulated in Itgam−/− macrophages, represses Let-7 transcription. Pharmacological inhibition of c-Myc with 10058-F4 restored Let-7a, Let-7d, and Let-7f expression and reversed the immunosuppressive gene signature in Itgam−/− macrophages. This establishes a CD11b → Let-7a → c-Myc/IL-6 regulatory axis that controls macrophage polarization.

In vivo, LLC, B16 melanoma, and PyMT mammary tumors grew significantly faster in Itgam−/− mice compared to WT controls. Tumors in Itgam−/− mice contained fewer CD8+ T cells and more FoxP3+ regulatory T cells, consistent with an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Conversely, mice expressing a constitutively active CD11b (I332G knock-in) exhibited markedly reduced tumor growth, confirming that CD11b activation—not just expression—is sufficient to suppress tumorigenesis.

Importantly, adoptive transfer of Itgam−/− macrophages (either bone marrow-derived or tumor-derived) into tumor-bearing mice accelerated tumor growth, whereas transfer of LA1-treated TAMs completely abrogated tumor development. LA1, a small-molecule CD11b agonist, enhanced macrophage adhesion to ICAM-1 in a CD11b-dependent manner and upregulated Let-7a while suppressing Il6 and Pdgfb. Systemic administration of LA1 inhibited tumor growth in syngeneic models of breast cancer (CL66) and human xenografts (MDA-MB-231), and synergized with taxol or radiation. Critically, LA1 had no effect in Itgam−/− mice, confirming on-target activity.

LA1 treatment also altered tumor vasculature, reducing pericyte coverage (as measured by SMA+, Desmin+, and NG2+ staining) and increasing vessel leakiness—opposite to the normalized vasculature seen in Itgam−/− tumors. This effect was mediated by suppression of PDGF-BB, as imatinib (a PDGFR inhibitor) reversed the enhanced tumor growth and vascular maturation in Itgam−/− mice.

Finally, targeted delivery of anti–Let-7a oligonucleotides to myeloid cells using αvβ3-integrin-targeted nanoparticles phenocopied the Itgam−/− tumor phenotype: increased immunosuppression, vascular normalization, and accelerated tumor growth. However, when combined with gemcitabine, anti–Let-7a treatment enhanced chemotherapy efficacy due to improved tumor perfusion, illustrating the context-dependent therapeutic potential of modulating this pathway.

Together, these findings demonstrate that CD11b activation reprograms macrophages toward an anti-tumor phenotype via Let-7a–mediated suppression of c-Myc and IL-6, leading to inhibition of tumor growth, enhanced T-cell recruitment, and modulation of tumor vasculature. The CD11b agonist LA1 represents a promising therapeutic agent for cancer immunotherapy, either as a monotherapy or in combination with conventional treatments.
