# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The detection of intracellular pathogens presents a persistent and formidable challenge in clinical diagnostics, public health surveillance, and agricultural biosecurity. Intracellular pathogens—such as *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, *Mycobacterium avium* subspecies *paratuberculosis* (MAP), and related mycobacteria—reside within host cells, particularly macrophages and dendritic cells, thereby evading conventional detection methods that rely on the presence of free-floating microbial components in biological fluids like blood or lymph. This sequestration within host cellular compartments renders serological immunoassays largely ineffective, as these assays depend on the recognition of pathogen-derived antigens or antibodies in extracellular matrices, which are often absent or present at undetectable levels during early or latent infection. Consequently, accurate diagnosis of diseases caused by intracellular pathogens frequently necessitates invasive procedures, such as tissue biopsies, or complex laboratory workflows involving cell isolation, lysis, nucleic acid extraction, and amplification-based detection.

Among the most widely used molecular diagnostic techniques is the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), particularly nested PCR (nPCR), which offers high specificity and sensitivity through sequential amplification rounds targeting conserved genomic regions. For example, in the case of MAP—a slow-growing intracellular bacterium implicated in Johne’s disease in ruminants and potentially Crohn’s disease in humans—the insertion sequence IS900 serves as a highly specific genomic marker due to its absence in other mycobacteria and microorganisms. However, the utility of PCR-based methods is significantly constrained by their dependence on high-purity, high-yield DNA templates. The extraction of such DNA from clinical samples, especially those containing low pathogen burdens or complex biological matrices (e.g., blood, inflamed intestinal tissue), requires multistep purification protocols involving enzymatic digestion, organic solvent extraction, and column-based chromatography. These procedures are not only labor-intensive and time-consuming but also prone to sample loss, contamination, and inhibition by co-extracted substances such as heme, polysaccharides, and proteases, which can impair polymerase activity and lead to false-negative or indeterminate results.

Moreover, when pathogen loads are extremely low—as is often the case in early-stage or subclinical infections—direct PCR may fail to detect the target even with optimized extraction. In such scenarios, culture-based enrichment is employed, wherein clinical specimens are inoculated into specialized media to allow viable bacteria to proliferate over extended periods before DNA extraction and PCR. While this approach enhances sensitivity, it introduces substantial delays: for slow-growing organisms like MAP, culture durations can span weeks to months, severely impeding timely diagnosis and therapeutic intervention. Furthermore, not all intracellular pathogens are amenable to in vitro culture; some may be non-viable due to host immune pressure, antibiotic exposure, or inherent fastidiousness, rendering culture-based methods inherently unreliable for comprehensive pathogen detection.

In response to these limitations, alternative diagnostic platforms leveraging nanotechnology have emerged as promising avenues for rapid, sensitive, and robust pathogen detection. Among these, magnetic relaxation switching (MRS) sensors represent a class of nanoscale probes that exploit the magnetic properties of iron oxide nanoparticles to transduce molecular binding events into measurable changes in the nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) signal of surrounding water protons. Traditional MRS assays typically rely on the aggregation or clustering of magnetic nanoparticles upon target binding, which alters the local magnetic field inhomogeneity and consequently shortens the transverse (spin-spin) relaxation time (T2) of water protons. While effective in controlled environments, such clustering-based mechanisms often suffer from poor performance in complex, minimally processed clinical samples due to non-specific aggregation, matrix interference, and limited signal-to-noise ratios.

Recent advances have demonstrated that MRS systems can operate via a non-clustering mechanism, wherein the binding of a single nanoparticle to its target induces a measurable increase in T2 relaxation time (ΔT2+). This counterintuitive “T2 increase” phenomenon arises from subtle changes in the rotational dynamics and hydration shell of the nanoparticle upon target engagement, which modulate the efficiency of magnetic dipole-dipole interactions with surrounding water molecules. Critically, this binding-induced T2 enhancement is amplified by the collective effect on thousands of water molecules, enabling high sensitivity without the need for enzymatic or thermal amplification. Moreover, the magnitude and direction of the ΔT2 response (increase or decrease) can be tuned by controlling the surface valency of the nanoparticle—that is, the density of targeting ligands (e.g., antibodies, oligonucleotides) conjugated to its surface. Low-valency nanoparticles, bearing a sparse distribution of ligands, tend to produce a positive ΔT2 upon target binding, whereas high-valency systems favor clustering and negative ΔT2 responses. This valency-dependent behavior provides a powerful design parameter for optimizing assay sensitivity, particularly for low-abundance targets where maximal signal per binding event is essential.

Despite these conceptual advances, the translation of MRS technology into routine clinical diagnostics has been hindered by a lack of validated, application-specific probe architectures capable of detecting genomic biomarkers directly in crude clinical specimens. Existing MRS systems have primarily focused on protein or whole-cell detection, with limited exploration of nucleic acid sensing in real-world samples. There remains an unmet need for a rapid, single-step, amplification-free method to detect pathogen-specific DNA sequences—such as IS900 in MAP—in minimally processed blood, tissue, or biopsy samples, with sensitivity rivaling or exceeding that of PCR, but without its associated time, cost, and complexity burdens. The development of such a technology would not only accelerate diagnosis but also enable large-scale screening, point-of-care testing, and longitudinal monitoring of intracellular infections in both human and veterinary medicine.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel class of hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS) designed for the rapid, sensitive, and specific detection of genomic biomarkers of intracellular pathogens in minimally processed clinical samples. Unlike conventional PCR-based methods that require extensive DNA purification and enzymatic amplification, the hMRS of the invention operate via a single-step, amplification-free mechanism that directly detects target DNA sequences in crude lysates, thereby significantly reducing assay time, cost, and technical complexity. The hMRS are engineered to exhibit a unique binding-induced increase in the transverse relaxation time (T2) of water protons upon hybridization with a specific genomic target, enabling highly sensitive detection without the need for nanoparticle clustering or secondary signal amplification.

In one embodiment, the hMRS comprise superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles coated with a hydrophilic polymer, such as polyacrylic acid, and functionalized with a low density of oligonucleotide probes complementary to a conserved genomic sequence of the target pathogen. The oligonucleotide probes are selected to bind specifically to a unique insertion sequence, such as IS900 in *Mycobacterium avium* subspecies *paratuberculosis* (MAP), which is not found in other microorganisms, thereby ensuring high specificity. The low valency of the oligonucleotide coating—typically fewer than 100, and preferably between 40 and 60 oligonucleotides per nanoparticle—is critical to the observed T2-increase response, which provides enhanced sensitivity at low target concentrations, making the system ideal for detecting rare genomic markers in clinical samples.

The method of use involves minimal sample preparation: clinical specimens such as peripheral blood, tissue homogenates, or cultured isolates are subjected to a brief thermal lysis step (e.g., 30 minutes at 100°C) to release intracellular DNA, followed by direct addition of the hMRS. After a short incubation period that includes one or two brief heating cycles (e.g., 3 minutes at 95°C) to denature genomic DNA and facilitate probe hybridization, the sample is cooled to room temperature, and the T2 relaxation time is measured using a compact NMR relaxometer. A significant increase in T2 relative to a negative control indicates the presence of the target pathogen.

The invention further provides a complete diagnostic platform that outperforms existing nested PCR (nPCR) methods in speed, sensitivity, and robustness. Specifically, the hMRS can detect a single copy of the MAP genome in crude DNA extracts within 60 minutes, whereas nPCR requires more than 6 hours and fails to detect the target in crude samples due to inhibition. In clinical validation studies using blood samples from Crohn’s disease patients and healthy controls, the hMRS demonstrated superior correlation with clinical diagnosis compared to both direct and culture-based nPCR, correctly identifying positive cases that were missed by PCR due to low bacterial load or culture failure. Additionally, the hMRS can be multiplexed with epitope-sensing MRS (e.g., antibody-conjugated nanoparticles) to provide concurrent detection at both genomic and protein levels, enhancing diagnostic confidence.

The hMRS are stable, scalable, and compatible with portable NMR instrumentation, making them suitable for deployment in resource-limited settings, point-of-care clinics, and veterinary field applications. By eliminating the need for DNA purification, enzymatic reagents, and electrophoresis, the invention reduces per-test cost and technical barriers, while maintaining or exceeding the sensitivity of gold-standard molecular methods. Thus, the present invention represents a transformative advance in the detection of intracellular pathogens, with broad applicability to infectious diseases caused by mycobacteria and other hard-to-diagnose microbes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Design and Synthesis of hMRS

The hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS) of the present invention are engineered through a precise and reproducible synthetic protocol that ensures optimal size, surface chemistry, ligand density, and magnetic properties for sensitive genomic detection. The core of the hMRS consists of superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), typically magnetite (Fe3O4) or maghemite (γ-Fe2O3), which are synthesized via co-precipitation or thermal decomposition methods to yield particles with a narrow size distribution centered around 10–15 nm in diameter. These primary nanoparticles are then coated with a hydrophilic polymer, such as polyacrylic acid (PAA), to enhance colloidal stability in aqueous buffers, prevent non-specific protein adsorption, and provide functional groups for subsequent bioconjugation.

The PAA coating is achieved by ligand exchange or in-situ polymerization, resulting in a core-shell structure with a total hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 78 ± 3 nm, as determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The zeta potential of the PAA-coated nanoparticles is measured to be –35 mV, indicating a highly negative surface charge that contributes to electrostatic repulsion and long-term stability in physiological buffers. The relaxometric properties of the nanoparticles are characterized using a 0.47 T benchtop NMR relaxometer, yielding longitudinal (R1) and transverse (R2) relaxivities of 45 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ and 60 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, respectively, which are suitable for sensitive T2-based detection.

To confer specificity for the target genomic sequence, the PAA-coated nanoparticles are functionalized with oligonucleotide probes complementary to a conserved region of the pathogen’s genome. In the case of *Mycobacterium avium* subspecies *paratuberculosis* (MAP), the probe is designed to hybridize with the IS900 insertion sequence, a 1,451-bp element present in 14–20 copies per MAP genome and absent in other mycobacteria. The selected probe sequence is a 15-nucleotide oligomer (5′-ATGTGGTTGCTGTGT-3′), which corresponds to a unique segment within the IS900 locus and exhibits high binding affinity and specificity under the assay conditions.

Conjugation of the oligonucleotide to the nanoparticle surface is accomplished via copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (“click” chemistry), a highly efficient and bioorthogonal reaction. First, the PAA-coated nanoparticles are modified with propargyl groups using carbodiimide chemistry (e.g., EDC/NHS activation), introducing alkyne functionalities onto the carboxylate-terminated polymer chains. Separately, the oligonucleotide is synthesized with a 5′-azide modification. The azide-functionalized oligonucleotide is then reacted with the propargylated nanoparticles in the presence of a Cu(I)-stabilizing ligand, such as tris(benzyltriazolylmethyl)amine (TBTA), to form a stable 1,2,3-triazole linkage. The reaction is carried out in a bicarbonate buffer (pH 8.4) at room temperature for 3 hours, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C to ensure complete conjugation.

Post-conjugation, the hMRS are purified by dialysis against deionized water using a 6,000–8,000 molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) membrane to remove unreacted reagents, followed by magnetic separation using a MACS column to isolate the functionalized nanoparticles from any aggregates or free oligonucleotides. The average number of oligonucleotides per nanoparticle is quantified by UV-Vis spectrophotometry, measuring absorbance at 260 nm (nucleic acid) and 305 nm (background correction), and is found to be 55 ± 5 oligos per particle. This low valency is deliberately engineered to promote a binding-induced T2 increase rather than clustering-induced T2 decrease, as established in prior studies correlating ligand density with magnetic relaxation response.

The final hMRS formulation is stored in aqueous suspension at 4°C and remains stable for at least six months, with no significant changes in size, zeta potential, or relaxivity. The synthesis protocol is scalable and amenable to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, enabling large-scale production for clinical and commercial use. The resulting hMRS are monodisperse, non-aggregating, and exhibit rapid hybridization kinetics with target DNA, making them ideal for point-of-care diagnostics.

### Discussion

The hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS) disclosed herein represent a paradigm shift in the detection of intracellular pathogens, addressing long-standing limitations of conventional molecular diagnostics through a combination of nanomaterial engineering, biophysical insight, and clinical validation. Central to the invention is the exploitation of a non-clustering, binding-induced T2 increase mechanism, which contrasts sharply with traditional magnetic relaxation assays that rely on nanoparticle aggregation to generate a detectable signal. This fundamental difference confers several critical advantages: first, it eliminates the requirement for dual-probe systems or adjacent binding sites, simplifying assay design to a single, target-specific probe; second, it avoids the pitfalls of non-specific aggregation in complex biological matrices, which often plagues clustering-based methods; and third, it leverages the intrinsic signal amplification of NMR, where a single binding event influences the relaxation of hundreds of thousands of water molecules, enabling single-genome-copy detection without enzymatic amplification.

The deliberate tuning of nanoparticle valency to a low range (approximately 55 oligonucleotides per particle) is a key innovation that underpins the assay’s exceptional sensitivity at low target concentrations. This design choice is informed by the well-established principle that low-valency nanoparticles exhibit a positive ΔT2 response upon target binding, whereas high-valency systems favor cross-linking and negative ΔT2. In the context of intracellular pathogen detection—where the target is often present in vanishingly small quantities within a sea of host DNA—maximizing signal per binding event is paramount. The hMRS achieve this by ensuring that each hybridization event produces a measurable T2 increase, rather than relying on the statistical likelihood of multiple binding events leading to cluster formation.

The compatibility of the hMRS with minimally processed clinical samples is another transformative feature. By functioning effectively in crude DNA lysates—obtained through a simple 30-minute boiling step—the invention bypasses the need for laborious, yield-compromising purification protocols. This not only accelerates the diagnostic workflow (from days or weeks to under an hour) but also preserves low-abundance targets that might be lost during purification. The robustness of the hMRS in the presence of proteins, lipids, and other biological interferents is likely due to the steric protection afforded by the dense PAA coating and the high local salt concentration around the DNA-functionalized nanoparticle, which together shield the probe from nuclease degradation and non-specific binding.

Clinically, the superiority of the hMRS over nested PCR is evident in both analytical and diagnostic performance. The ability to detect a single MAP genome copy in crude blood samples within 60 minutes far exceeds the capabilities of direct nPCR, which fails in crude lysates, and rivals or surpasses culture-based nPCR, which requires 12 weeks of incubation. More importantly, the hMRS demonstrate better correlation with clinical disease status, correctly identifying MAP-positive individuals who were misclassified as negative by PCR due to low bacterial load or culture failure. This enhanced diagnostic fidelity has profound implications for the management of Crohn’s disease and Johne’s disease, where timely and accurate pathogen detection could guide targeted antimicrobial therapy, inform public health interventions, and reduce economic losses in agriculture.

The modular nature of the hMRS platform further extends its utility beyond MAP. By simply changing the oligonucleotide sequence, the same nanoparticle scaffold can be adapted to detect other intracellular pathogens—such as *M. tuberculosis*, *Legionella pneumophila*, or *Chlamydia trachomatis*—using their respective genomic markers. Similarly, the integration of epitope-sensing MRS (e.g., antibody-conjugated nanoparticles) enables multimodal detection, providing orthogonal confirmation of pathogen presence at both DNA and protein levels. This versatility, combined with the potential for portable NMR instrumentation, positions the hMRS as a next-generation diagnostic tool for global infectious disease challenges.

### Methods

The methods employed in the development and validation of the hybridizing magnetic relaxation nanosensors (hMRS) are detailed below, encompassing nanoparticle synthesis, biological sample preparation, assay execution, and data analysis.

**Preparation of IS900-Specific hMRS**: Propargylated polyacrylic acid-coated iron oxide nanoparticles were synthesized by activating carboxyl groups on the polymer with 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), followed by reaction with propargylamine. The propargyl-functionalized nanoparticles were then conjugated to an azide-modified 15-mer oligonucleotide (5′-ATGTGGTTGCTGTGT-3′) via copper-catalyzed click chemistry, using a Cu(I)-TBTA complex as the catalyst. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature for 3 hours, then at 4°C overnight. Unbound reagents were removed by dialysis (6,000–8,000 MWCO) and magnetic separation (Miltenyi LS25 MACS column). Oligonucleotide density was quantified by Nanodrop spectrophotometry (A260/A305), yielding 55 ± 5 oligos per nanoparticle. Hydrodynamic diameter (78 ± 3 nm) and zeta potential (–35 mV) were measured by DLS and laser Doppler velocimetry, respectively. Relaxivities (R1 = 45 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, R2 = 60 mM⁻¹s⁻¹) were determined at 0.47 T using a Bruker Minispec mq20.

**Bacterial Cultures and Clinical Samples**: MAP strains and clinical isolates were cultured in BACTEC 12B* medium at 37°C. Other bacteria (*E. coli*, *S. aureus*, etc.) were grown in nutrient broth. Heat inactivation was performed by autoclaving (10 min, 121°C). Human blood samples from Crohn’s disease patients and healthy controls were collected under IRB approval (University of Florida, protocol #354–2007), with informed consent. Animal tissues from Johne’s disease-positive cattle were obtained from archived collections.

**DNA Extraction**: Crude DNA was prepared by resuspending cell pellets in TE buffer (10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0) and heating at 100°C for 30 minutes. Pure DNA was extracted from crude lysates using phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1) followed by ethanol precipitation.

**hMRS Assay Protocol**: 200 µL of hMRS suspension (6 µg Fe/mL in PBS) was mixed with 1 µL of DNA sample or TE buffer control. The mixture was heated twice at 95°C for 3 minutes each, then cooled to room temperature. T2 relaxation times were measured using a CPMG pulse sequence (1.5 ms echo spacing) on a Bruker Minispec mq20. ΔT2+ was calculated as T2(sample) – T2(control).

**Nested PCR**: nPCR was performed as previously described, with two rounds of amplification targeting IS900 (first round: 398 bp; second round: 298 bp), followed by agarose gel electrophoresis.

**Statistical Analysis**: Data were expressed as mean ± SEM. Specificity was confirmed by two-proportion z-test (SPSS, α = 0.05). ROC analysis was performed using logistic regression, with clinical diagnosis as the classification variable and ΔT2+ or PCR result as the test variable.

These methods collectively enabled the rigorous characterization and validation of the hMRS platform, demonstrating its superiority over existing diagnostic technologies for intracellular pathogen detection.
