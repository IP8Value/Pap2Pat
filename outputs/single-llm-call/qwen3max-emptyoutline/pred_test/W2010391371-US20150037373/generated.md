# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a polyphenylpropanoid polysaccharide complex (PPC) derived from the cones of Pinus sylvestris, and its use as an immunomodulatory agent capable of enhancing antigen-specific T cell responses, particularly CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses, during the primary phase of immune activation. More specifically, the invention provides methods for administering PPC to subjects in order to augment the expansion of antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells during the proliferative phase of a primary immune response, without significantly altering the contraction phase or suppressing activation-induced cell death (AICD). The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising PPC, methods for preparing standardized PPC formulations, and therapeutic regimens that combine PPC with vaccines, immunotherapies, or anti-cancer treatments to potentiate cellular immunity. The disclosed PPC is characterized by its ability to bias immune responses toward a T helper type 1 (Th1) phenotype, enhance interferon-gamma (IFNγ) production, reduce interleukin-2 (IL-2) levels during early T cell activation, and modulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) in activated T cells, all while maintaining the natural apoptotic regulation of the immune response. This invention is particularly relevant to the fields of immunology, vaccinology, oncology, and autoimmune disease management, where controlled enhancement of cellular immunity is therapeutically desirable.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The medicinal use of pinecone extracts dates back to antiquity, with the earliest documented evidence appearing in the first century AD in the seminal work *De Materia Medica* by Pedanius Dioscorides, a Greek physician and pharmacologist who served in the Roman army. Dioscorides described the preparation of aqueous extracts from pinecones and their application in the treatment of kidney and digestive disorders, as well as topical use for skin conditions resembling psoriasis. This historical knowledge persisted in various folk traditions, notably among the inhabitants of Kyushu Island in Japan, who have long consumed a tea brewed from pinecones to combat a wide spectrum of illnesses, including infectious diseases and cancer. These anecdotal reports suggested a broad-spectrum immunomodulatory effect, prompting scientific inquiry into the biological mechanisms underlying such observations.

In recent decades, research has focused on isolating and characterizing the bioactive components responsible for these effects. A key breakthrough was the identification of a polyphenylpropanoid polysaccharide complex (PPC) derived from the cones of *Pinus sylvestris* (Scotch pine). This complex, produced through a controlled alkaline hydrothermal extraction process followed by purification and standardization, has demonstrated significant immunomodulatory properties in both in vitro and in vivo models. Prior studies have established that oral administration of PPC at the time of immunization with model antigens—such as chicken egg ovalbumin (OVA), DNA vaccines, protein vaccines, or dendritic cell-based vaccines—consistently biases the ensuing immune response toward a T helper type 1 (Th1) phenotype. This Th1 bias is characterized by elevated production of interferon-gamma (IFNγ) and suppressed secretion of interleukin-4 (IL-4), effectively inhibiting the development of Th2-mediated allergic responses, such as IgE production against OVA.

Moreover, PPC has been shown to significantly enhance the generation of antigen-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) during secondary immune responses. In murine models, mice receiving PPC concomitantly with immunization exhibited approximately twice the number of antigen-specific CD8+/IFNγ+ T cells compared to control groups, as measured by ELISPOT assays. This enhancement of CTL responses is of critical importance in the context of anti-tumor immunity and defense against intracellular pathogens, where robust CD8+ T cell activity is essential for effective clearance of diseased or infected cells. In vitro studies further revealed that exposure of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) or murine bone marrow-derived cells to PPC induces the rapid differentiation of dendritic-like cells, suggesting that PPC may act, at least in part, by promoting the development and maturation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells (DCs), which are pivotal in initiating and shaping adaptive immune responses.

Despite these promising findings, the precise mechanism by which PPC enhances CD8+ T cell responses remained incompletely understood. Specifically, it was unclear whether PPC exerted its effects primarily during the primary immune response—by enhancing T cell proliferation, survival, or differentiation—or during the memory phase, by promoting the maintenance or reactivation of memory T cells. Additionally, questions persisted regarding PPC’s potential influence on activation-induced cell death (AICD), a critical regulatory process that eliminates expanded T cell clones after pathogen clearance to prevent autoimmunity and maintain homeostasis. Some natural compounds with antioxidant properties have been shown to suppress AICD by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are known to downregulate anti-apoptotic proteins like Bcl-2 and promote the activity of pro-apoptotic factors such as Bim. Given that PPC contains polyphenolic constituents with known antioxidant capabilities, it was plausible that PPC might interfere with AICD, thereby artificially inflating T cell numbers during the contraction phase.

To address these gaps, the present inventors employed the well-established Staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB) superantigen model to dissect the temporal effects of PPC on primary T cell responses. SEB binds directly to MHC class II molecules on antigen-presenting cells and specific Vβ chains of the T cell receptor (notably Vβ8 in BALB/c mice), leading to polyclonal activation and massive proliferation of Vβ8+ T cells without the need for conventional antigen processing. This model provides a synchronized, high-magnitude primary T cell response that is ideal for studying proliferation, cytokine dynamics, ROS production, and apoptosis. Using this system, the inventors sought to determine whether PPC enhances T cell numbers by boosting proliferation during the expansion phase, by inhibiting apoptosis during the contraction phase, or through a combination of both mechanisms. The results of these investigations, detailed herein, reveal that PPC acts predominantly during the early proliferative phase of the primary T cell response to significantly increase the number of both CD4+ and CD8+ Vβ8+ T cells, without substantially altering the natural course of AICD or the expression of key apoptotic regulators such as Bcl-2, Bim, Bax, or Bcl-3 in activated T cells. This refined understanding of PPC’s mechanism of action provides a strong foundation for its rational application in vaccine adjuvancy, cancer immunotherapy, and other clinical settings requiring enhanced cellular immunity.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for enhancing a primary T cell immune response in a subject, comprising administering to the subject an effective amount of a polyphenylpropanoid polysaccharide complex (PPC) derived from *Pinus sylvestris* pinecones, wherein the PPC enhances the expansion of antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells during the proliferative phase of the primary immune response without significantly suppressing activation-induced cell death (AICD) during the contraction phase. The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising PPC in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, wherein the PPC is prepared by alkaline hydrothermal extraction of *Pinus sylvestris* pinecones at pH 12.0 and 121°C for 30 minutes, followed by standardization based on ultraviolet absorbance at 280 nm using an extinction coefficient of 22.8 L·gram⁻¹·cm⁻¹, and subsequent removal of particulates and contaminants to yield a sterile, stable powder suitable for oral administration. The invention also includes a method for biasing an immune response toward a T helper type 1 (Th1) phenotype, comprising co-administering PPC with an antigen or vaccine, resulting in increased serum levels of interferon-gamma (IFNγ), decreased serum levels of interleukin-2 (IL-2), and suppression of antigen-specific IgE production. Additionally, the invention encompasses a method for reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in activated T cells in vivo, comprising administering PPC to a subject undergoing immune activation, wherein the reduction in ROS does not lead to a concomitant increase in intracellular Bcl-2 protein expression in antigen-specific T cells. The invention further provides a method for enhancing the efficacy of a vaccine or immunotherapeutic agent, comprising administering PPC concurrently with or shortly after the administration of the vaccine or immunotherapeutic agent, thereby increasing the magnitude of the primary antigen-specific CD8+ T cell response and subsequently amplifying the secondary memory T cell response. The disclosed methods are particularly useful in the treatment or prevention of cancer, infectious diseases, and conditions requiring enhanced cellular immunity, and are applicable to both human and veterinary subjects.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The present invention is directed to a polyphenylpropanoid polysaccharide complex (PPC) isolated from the cones of *Pinus sylvestris* and its use as an immunomodulatory agent that specifically enhances the proliferative phase of primary T cell responses without interfering with the physiological process of activation-induced cell death (AICD). The PPC of the invention is produced through a defined and reproducible manufacturing process that ensures consistency, purity, and biological activity. The process begins with the collection of mature pinecones from *Pinus sylvestris*, a species commonly known as Scotch pine, which are then thoroughly washed with deionized water to remove surface contaminants. The cleaned pinecones are mechanically shredded to increase the surface area available for extraction. The shredded material is then loaded into a stainless steel reaction vessel, where it undergoes hydrothermal extraction under alkaline conditions. Specifically, the extraction is performed at a pH of 12.0 and a temperature of 121°C for a duration of 30 minutes. This controlled alkaline hydrolysis facilitates the solubilization of polyphenylpropanoid and polysaccharide components while minimizing degradation of thermally labile compounds.

Following extraction, the resulting aqueous solution is subjected to rigorous quality control measures. The concentration of active polyphenylpropanoid constituents is determined spectrophotometrically by measuring the optical density at 280 nm (OD₂₈₀) and applying the formula:  
PPC concentration (mg/mL) = OD₂₈₀ × (1 / 22.8 L·gram⁻¹·cm⁻¹),  
where 22.8 L·gram⁻¹·cm⁻¹ is the empirically determined extinction coefficient for the polyphenylpropanoid fraction of the extract. This standardization step ensures batch-to-batch consistency and allows for precise dosing in therapeutic applications. The liquid extract is then tested for the absence of contaminants, including herbicides, pesticides, heavy metals, endotoxins, and microorganisms, using validated analytical and microbiological methods. Once confirmed to be free of contaminants, the extract is spray-dried to produce a dark brown, odorless, and stable powder that retains its biological activity upon reconstitution. For experimental or therapeutic use, the powdered PPC is suspended in sterile water to a stock concentration of 25 mg/mL (w/v), centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 minutes to remove insoluble particulates, and filtered through a 0.2 μm nylon membrane to ensure sterility. A working stock solution containing 200 μg/mL of polyphenylpropanoids is typically prepared fresh weekly for administration.

The immunomodulatory effects of PPC are most pronounced when administered orally in close temporal proximity to an immune stimulus, such as a vaccine or antigen. In the preferred embodiment, PPC is administered to a subject via oral gavage or in drinking water, beginning at the time of immunization and continuing for at least 24 to 48 hours thereafter. This regimen has been shown to significantly enhance the number of antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in secondary lymphoid organs, such as the inguinal lymph nodes, during the peak of the proliferative phase (approximately 48 hours post-immunization in murine models). Notably, this enhancement is not due to a general mitogenic effect, as administration of PPC alone—without concurrent immune stimulation—does not alter baseline T cell populations. Instead, PPC appears to act synergistically with the activating signal provided by the antigen or superantigen to amplify the clonal expansion of antigen-specific T cells.

The mechanism underlying this proliferative enhancement is multifactorial. One key aspect is the modulation of cytokine profiles during early T cell activation. Administration of PPC at the time of immune stimulation leads to a significant reduction in serum levels of interleukin-2 (IL-2) and a marked increase in interferon-gamma (IFNγ) at 48 hours post-immunization. While IL-2 is traditionally viewed as a T cell growth factor, its early suppression in the context of PPC administration may reflect a shift in the differentiation program of activated T cells toward a more effector-oriented, Th1-biased phenotype, rather than a proliferative defect. The elevated IFNγ levels not only reinforce the Th1 bias but may also contribute to the differential survival of CD8+ versus CD4+ T cells during the contraction phase, as IFNγ has been implicated in promoting CD4+ T cell apoptosis while sparing CD8+ T cells.

Another important feature of PPC is its ability to reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in activated T cells. ROS are generated as metabolic byproducts during T cell activation and play a dual role: they support signaling pathways necessary for proliferation but also contribute to the induction of AICD during the contraction phase. PPC, likely due to its polyphenolic content, functions as an in vivo antioxidant, lowering both the percentage of ROS-positive T cells and the mean fluorescence intensity of ROS per cell. However, contrary to expectations, this reduction in ROS does not translate into increased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 in antigen-specific T cells. Both protein and mRNA levels of Bcl-2 remain suppressed in SEB-activated T cells regardless of PPC treatment, indicating that the ROS reduction achieved by PPC is insufficient to override the transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms that downregulate Bcl-2 during strong T cell activation. Consequently, the expression of pro-apoptotic molecules such as Bim remains elevated, and the rate of AICD—as measured by Annexin V staining—is not significantly altered by PPC. This preservation of natural apoptotic regulation is a critical advantage, as it prevents the accumulation of potentially autoreactive T cell clones and maintains immune homeostasis.

The invention further demonstrates that PPC does not significantly affect the expression of other Bcl-2 family members, such as Bcl-3, Bcl-xL, or Bax, in activated T cells. Although PPC administration alone can upregulate Bcl-3 mRNA in naïve mice—a finding that may relate to basal immune tone—the presence of a strong activating stimulus like SEB overrides this effect, and no difference in Bcl-3 expression is observed between SEB and SEB+PPC groups. This suggests that PPC’s primary site of action is upstream of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway, possibly at the level of antigen presentation or early T cell receptor signaling. Supporting this hypothesis, preliminary data indicate an increase in CD11c+ dendritic cells in the lymph nodes of PPC-treated mice, implying that PPC may enhance the number or function of antigen-presenting cells, thereby increasing the number of T cells that receive a productive activation signal.

In summary, the preferred embodiment of the invention utilizes a standardized, orally administered PPC formulation to selectively amplify the expansion of antigen-specific T cells during the primary immune response, particularly favoring the generation of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes. This is achieved without disrupting the natural contraction phase or inducing aberrant T cell survival, thereby providing a safe and effective means to boost cellular immunity in therapeutic contexts such as vaccination and cancer immunotherapy.

## EXAMPLES

### Preparation of PPC

The polyphenylpropanoid polysaccharide complex (PPC) used in the present invention was prepared according to a standardized protocol designed to ensure reproducibility, purity, and biological activity. Mature pinecones of *Pinus sylvestris* (Scotch pine) were sourced from a commercial supplier, with species identification verified by a qualified botanist. The pinecones were initially washed extensively with deionized water to remove dust, soil, and other extraneous matter. Following washing, the pinecones were mechanically shredded using a commercial-grade shredder to produce fine particulates, thereby increasing the surface area available for extraction. The shredded pinecone material was then transferred into a stainless steel reaction vessel equipped with temperature and pH control systems. An aqueous extraction was performed under alkaline conditions, specifically at a pH of 12.0, maintained by the addition of sodium hydroxide, and a temperature of 121°C, sustained for a duration of 30 minutes. This hydrothermal alkaline extraction process was selected to efficiently solubilize the target polyphenylpropanoid and polysaccharide components while minimizing thermal degradation. Upon completion of the extraction cycle, the resulting liquid was cooled to room temperature and subjected to a series of quality assurance tests. The concentration of active polyphenylpropanoid constituents was determined by measuring the optical density at 280 nanometers (OD₂₈₀) using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. The concentration of PPC in milligrams per milliliter was calculated using the formula:  
PPC concentration (mg/mL) = OD₂₈₀ × (1 / 22.8 L·gram⁻¹·cm⁻¹),  
where 22.8 L·gram⁻¹·cm⁻¹ represents the extinction coefficient specific to the polyphenylpropanoid fraction of the extract. This standardization step is critical for ensuring consistent biological activity across different batches. The liquid extract was then rigorously tested for the absence of contaminants, including but not limited to herbicides, pesticides, heavy metals (e.g., lead, mercury, arsenic, cadmium), endotoxins (using the Limulus amebocyte lysate assay), and viable microorganisms (via aerobic and anaerobic culture methods). Only extracts that met stringent safety specifications were advanced to the next stage. The purified liquid was then subjected to spray-drying using a laboratory-scale spray dryer, which atomizes the liquid into a hot air stream, rapidly evaporating the water and yielding a fine, dark brown, odorless powder. This powder was found to be stable at room temperature for extended periods and retained its immunomodulatory properties upon reconstitution. For experimental use, the powdered PPC was suspended in sterile distilled water to a stock concentration of 25 mg/mL (weight per volume). The suspension was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 minutes to pellet any insoluble particulates, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.2 μm nylon membrane filter to ensure sterility and remove residual aggregates. The resulting clear, sterile solution was stored at 4°C and used to prepare working dilutions. A working stock solution containing 200 μg/mL of polyphenylpropanoids, as determined by the OD₂₈₀ measurement, was prepared fresh each week to maintain potency and prevent degradation. This standardized preparation protocol ensures that the PPC used in all experimental and therapeutic applications is consistent in composition, free of contaminants, and biologically active.

### In Vivo Immune Stimulation

To evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of PPC in a controlled in vivo setting, a murine model of T cell activation was employed using Staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB) as a superantigen. Six to seven-week-old male BALB/c mice were obtained from Charles River Laboratories and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in a ventilated cage system with HEPA-filtered air, maintained at 22°C with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Mice were provided with a standard diet and water ad libitum. All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were randomly assigned to one of four experimental groups: Naive (untreated controls), SEB (immunized with SEB only), PPC only (administered PPC without SEB), and SEB + PPC (immunized with SEB and treated with PPC). On Day 0, mice in the SEB and SEB + PPC groups received an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 100 μg of SEB dissolved in 100 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Mice in the Naive group received an equivalent volume of PBS. Concurrently, mice in the SEB + PPC and PPC only groups were administered PPC via oral gavage. Specifically, each mouse received 100 μL of a 200 μg/mL PPC solution three times on Day 0 (immediately after SEB injection and at two subsequent intervals) and three times on Day 1 (at 10 AM, 1 PM, and 4 PM), totaling six doses over 48 hours. Additionally, PPC was provided ad libitum in the drinking water at a concentration of 200 μg/mL throughout the experimental period. At predetermined time points—48 hours and 96 hours post-SEB injection—mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation. The inguinal lymph nodes from each mouse were aseptically removed and pooled for downstream analyses. Serum was collected via cardiac puncture and stored at -80°C for cytokine quantification. This experimental design allowed for the assessment of PPC’s effects on both the proliferative phase (48 hours) and the contraction phase (96 hours) of the primary T cell response, with careful attention to dosing regimen and timing to mimic clinically relevant administration protocols.

### ELISA Assays

To quantify the systemic cytokine response induced by SEB and modulated by PPC, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) were performed on serum samples collected from experimental mice. Standard ELISA development kits specific for murine interleukin-2 (IL-2), interferon-gamma (IFNγ), and interleukin-12p70 (IL-12p70) were purchased from Peprotech. The assays were conducted strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 96-well microtiter plates were coated overnight at 4°C with capture antibodies specific to each cytokine. Following blocking with assay diluent to prevent non-specific binding, serial dilutions of serum samples and recombinant cytokine standards were added to the wells and incubated for two hours at room temperature. After washing to remove unbound material, detection antibodies conjugated to biotin were added, followed by incubation with streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase (HRP). The enzymatic reaction was developed using a tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate, and the reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid. The absorbance of the resulting colored product was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a BioTek μQuant plate spectrophotometer. Standard curves were generated using known concentrations of recombinant cytokines, and sample cytokine concentrations were interpolated from these curves using GraphPad Prism software. This quantitative approach enabled the precise determination of how PPC administration altered the systemic cytokine milieu during the early phases of T cell activation, revealing significant reductions in IL-2 and elevations in IFNγ in the SEB + PPC group compared to SEB alone, while IL-12p70 levels remained unchanged.

### Lymph Node and Spleen Cell Isolation

For cellular analyses, lymphocytes were isolated from secondary lymphoid organs of experimental mice. Inguinal lymph nodes and spleens were aseptically harvested and placed into sterile plastic bags containing 4 mL of Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS, pH 7.4). The tissues were homogenized using a Seward Stomacher®80 mechanical homogenizer set to medium speed for 60 seconds, which effectively disrupted the tissue architecture while preserving cell viability. The resulting single-cell suspensions were transferred to conical tubes and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 minutes to pellet the cells. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellets were resuspended in 2 mL of HBSS for lymph node cells or 4 mL for spleen cells. To determine total cell yield and viability, 20 μL of the cell suspension was mixed with 380 μL of Millipore’s Muse Count and Viability stain, and analyzed using the Muse Cell Analyzer, which employs fluorescent dyes to distinguish live from dead cells based on membrane integrity. Based on these counts, the cells were then resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 1% L-glutamine to achieve a final concentration of 4 × 10⁶ cells per milliliter. This standardized cell preparation ensured consistent cell numbers across experimental groups for downstream functional and phenotypic assays, including flow cytometry, apoptosis detection, and gene expression analysis.

### Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometric analysis was employed to characterize the phenotype and frequency of T cell subsets in lymph node suspensions. For each mouse, 1 × 10⁶ lymph node cells were aliquoted into 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes and pelleted by centrifugation at 1000 × g for 1 minute. The cell pellets were resuspended in 100 μL of FACS buffer (PBS containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 0.1% sodium azide) and incubated on ice with 1 μL of anti-CD16/32 Fc receptor blocking antibody (clone 93) for 10 minutes to prevent non-specific antibody binding. Following blocking, fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies were added: allophycocyanin (APC)-labeled anti-CD3 (clone 17A2), fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled anti-CD4 (clone GK1.5), phycoerythrin (PE)-labeled anti-CD8 (clone SK1), and peridinin chlorophyll protein-conjugated eFluor710 (PerCP-eFluor®710)-labeled anti-Vβ8 (clone KJ16-133). All antibodies were purchased from eBioscience and used at concentrations optimized for minimal background and maximal signal. The cells were incubated on ice for 30 minutes in the dark, then washed once with 500 μL of FACS buffer and pelleted again. The final cell pellets were resuspended in 200 μL of FACS buffer and analyzed on a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer equipped with CellQuest Pro software. Data were subsequently processed and analyzed using FloJo software to determine the percentage and absolute number of CD3+/Vβ8+, CD4+/Vβ8+, and CD8+/Vβ8+ T cell populations. This high-resolution phenotyping allowed for the precise quantification of SEB-specific T cell expansion and the assessment of PPC’s impact on subset distribution during the primary immune response.

### Detection of Apoptosis

To assess whether PPC influenced activation-induced cell death (AICD), apoptosis in SEB-activated T cells was measured using Annexin V staining, which detects phosphatidylserine exposure on the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane—an early hallmark of apoptosis. Lymph node cells (1 × 10⁶ per mouse) were stained using eBioscience’s Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were suspended in 100 μL of Annexin V binding buffer containing 1 μL of APC-labeled anti-CD3 antibody, 5 μL of PerCP-eFluor®710-labeled anti-Vβ8 antibody, and 1 μL of FITC-labeled Annexin V. This multiparametric staining allowed for the specific identification of apoptotic cells within the SEB-reactive Vβ8+ T cell population. The cells were incubated at room temperature for 10 minutes in the dark, then washed with binding buffer and resuspended in the same buffer for immediate analysis by flow cytometry. The percentage of Annexin V-positive cells within the CD3+/Vβ8+ gate was quantified, providing a direct measure of early apoptotic events. This assay confirmed that while SEB robustly induced apoptosis in Vβ8+ T cells by 48 hours, co-administration of PPC did not significantly alter this apoptotic rate, indicating that PPC does not interfere with the physiological contraction of the T cell response.

### Detection of Reactive Oxygen Species

Intracellular levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in activated T cells were measured using the cell-permeable fluorescent probe CM-H₂DCFDA (5-(and-6)-chloromethyl-2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate, acetyl ester). This probe passively diffuses into cells, where intracellular esterases cleave the acetate groups, and the chloromethyl moiety reacts with glutathione and other thiols, trapping the compound intracellularly. Upon oxidation by ROS, the probe yields a highly fluorescent product that cannot escape the cell, allowing for quantitative assessment of oxidative stress. Lymph node cells (1 × 10⁶ per mouse) were incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes in 100 μL of FACS buffer containing 1 μL of APC-labeled anti-CD3, 5 μL of PerCP-eFluor®710-labeled anti-Vβ8, and 2 μL of a 50 μM CM-H₂DCFDA solution. Following incubation, cells were washed with 500 μL of FACS buffer, pelleted, and resuspended in 200 μL of FACS buffer for flow cytometric analysis. ROS levels were reported both as the percentage of CD3+ T cells exhibiting detectable fluorescence above background (ROS+ cells) and as the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) within the CD3+ population, reflecting the average ROS burden per cell. This dual-parameter analysis revealed that PPC administration significantly reduced both the frequency and intensity of ROS in SEB-activated T cells, demonstrating its in vivo antioxidant activity.

### Detection of Intracellular Bcl-2

To determine whether the reduction in ROS by PPC translated into altered expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, intracellular staining was performed on fixed and permeabilized lymph node cells. Cells previously surface-stained with APC-labeled anti-CD3 and PerCP-eFluor®710-labeled anti-Vβ8 were washed in permeabilization buffer (0.03% saponin in HBSS, referred to as HBSS-SAP) and pelleted. The cell pellets were resuspended in 100 μL of HBSS-SAP containing 1 μL of FITC-labeled anti-Bcl-2 antibody (clone 10C4, eBioscience) and incubated on ice for 60 minutes. Following incubation, cells were washed twice with HBSS-SAP and once with plain HBSS to remove unbound antibody. The cells were then resuspended in 200 μL of HBSS and analyzed by flow cytometry. The fluorescence intensity of Bcl-2 staining was measured specifically within the CD3+/Vβ8+ T cell gate, as well as in non-activated CD3+/Vβ8- T cells for comparison. This analysis showed that while SEB activation dramatically reduced Bcl-2 protein levels in Vβ8+ T cells, PPC co-administration did not restore Bcl-2 expression, indicating that the antioxidant effect of PPC was insufficient to counteract the strong transcriptional and post-translational suppression of Bcl-2 induced by superantigen stimulation.

### Analysis of Gene Expression

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying PPC’s effects on T cell survival, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was used to measure mRNA expression levels of key apoptotic regulators in lymph node cells. Total RNA was isolated using Qiagen’s RNeasy Plus Mini Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol, which includes a genomic DNA elimination step to prevent contamination. RNA yield and purity were assessed by spectrophotometry at 260 nm and 280 nm. One microgram of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using Qiagen’s QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit. qPCR was performed using SABiosciences’ RT² Profiler PCR Arrays and SYBR Green master mix, with validated primer pairs specific for murine Bcl-2, Bcl-3, Bcl-xL, Bax, and Bim. Reactions were run in duplicate on a BioRad iCycler under the following cycling conditions: 95°C for 10 minutes (polymerase activation), followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 1 minute. The housekeeping gene β-actin was used as an internal reference, and relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔ-Ct method, normalized to naïve control mice. This sensitive and quantitative approach confirmed that SEB activation suppressed Bcl-2 mRNA and upregulated Bim mRNA, and that PPC treatment did not significantly alter these expression patterns in activated T cells, further supporting the conclusion that PPC does not modulate the intrinsic apoptotic pathway during the primary T cell response.
