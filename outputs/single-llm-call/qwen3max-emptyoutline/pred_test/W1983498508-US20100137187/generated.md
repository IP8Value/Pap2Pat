## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The treatment of solid tumors, particularly prostate and pancreatic cancers, remains a significant clinical challenge due to the limitations of conventional chemotherapeutic agents, including systemic toxicity, poor tumor selectivity, and the development of drug resistance. In recent years, oligonucleotide-based therapeutics—such as antisense oligonucleotides, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and peptide nucleic acids (PNAs)—have emerged as promising alternatives for targeted cancer therapy by enabling the specific inhibition of oncogenic proteins at the transcriptional or post-transcriptional level. Among these, PNAs offer distinct advantages owing to their uncharged pseudopeptide backbone, which confers high binding affinity for complementary DNA or RNA sequences, resistance to enzymatic degradation by nucleases and proteases, and the ability to form stable triple helices with double-stranded DNA, thereby effectively blocking transcription factor binding and gene expression. Despite these favorable properties, the clinical translation of PNA-based therapeutics has been hindered by their inability to efficiently cross cellular membranes and reach intracellular targets, particularly within the nucleus where transcription factors such as Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) exert their oncogenic functions.

To address this delivery barrier, researchers have turned to cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) as molecular carriers capable of ferrying therapeutic payloads into cells. CPPs are short peptides, typically 5–30 amino acids in length, that can traverse biological membranes through energy-dependent or -independent mechanisms and facilitate the intracellular delivery of conjugated cargoes, including proteins, nucleic acids, and small molecules. Early CPPs, such as the HIV-1 TAT peptide, demonstrated broad cellular uptake but lacked cell or subcellular specificity, often resulting in non-selective distribution and potential off-target effects. Subsequent efforts have focused on engineering CPPs with enhanced targeting capabilities, such as the incorporation of ligands for receptors overexpressed on cancer cells (e.g., prostate-specific membrane antigen, PSMA) or the inclusion of nuclear localization signals (NLS) to direct cargo to the nucleus. NLS peptides, derived from endogenous transcription factors, contain basic amino acid motifs (e.g., lysine and arginine-rich sequences) that are recognized by importin proteins and mediate active nuclear transport via the nuclear pore complex. However, while NLS peptides can enhance nuclear accumulation, their standalone use as CPPs has often resulted in inefficient cellular uptake and sequestration in endosomal compartments, limiting their utility for delivering large or charged cargoes like PNAs.

In the context of prostate and pancreatic cancer, several CPP strategies have been explored. For instance, PSMA-targeted peptides bearing C-terminal glutamate residues have been used to deliver methotrexate-loaded liposomes to prostate cancer cells, leveraging the carboxypeptidase activity of PSMA for internalization. Similarly, the antennapedia-derived Antp peptide has been conjugated to the tumor suppressor p16 to inhibit pancreatic cancer cell growth, and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) peptides have been employed for imaging early pancreatic tumors. Nevertheless, these approaches have not been successfully adapted for PNA delivery, and many suffer from suboptimal efficiency, serum instability, or lack of nuclear targeting. Moreover, previous attempts to use transcription factor NLS peptides alone—such as the Oct6 NLS—for PNA delivery have shown limited success, with poor cellular uptake and predominant endosomal trapping, as documented in prior studies. Additionally, some CPP-PNA conjugates have demonstrated efficacy only under non-physiological conditions, such as serum-free media and at micromolar concentrations, which are impractical for clinical application due to cost and toxicity concerns.

A critical unmet need therefore exists for a novel CPP platform that combines efficient cellular internalization, cancer cell selectivity, and robust nuclear localization to enable the effective delivery of PNA therapeutics to the nucleus of prostate and pancreatic cancer cells. Such a platform must overcome the dual barriers of plasma membrane translocation and endosomal escape while ensuring that the therapeutic payload reaches its site of action—the nucleus—where it can inhibit key oncogenic transcription factors like STAT3. STAT3 is constitutively activated in a wide range of cancers, including prostate and pancreatic malignancies, and drives tumor proliferation, survival, angiogenesis, and immune evasion. Inhibition of STAT3 using antisense PNAs has shown promise in preclinical models, but the lack of an effective delivery system has prevented its clinical advancement. Thus, the development of a rationally designed CPP that synergistically enhances both cellular uptake and nuclear trafficking of PNA cargoes represents a pivotal innovation in the field of targeted cancer therapy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) composition and its use for the efficient intracellular and nuclear delivery of therapeutic payloads, particularly peptide nucleic acids (PNAs), to cancer cells, with specific applicability to prostate and pancreatic carcinomas. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that the fusion of a glutamate-rich peptide sequence, specifically EEEAA (Glu-Glu-Glu-Ala-Ala), to the N-terminus of a nuclear localization signal (NLS) derived from the Oct6 transcription factor results in a chimeric peptide that exhibits dramatically enhanced cellular uptake and nuclear localization compared to either component alone. This chimeric peptide, referred to herein as Glu-Oct6, functions as a highly efficient CPP that facilitates the transport of conjugated cargoes—including PNAs—into the cytoplasm and nucleus of target cancer cells at low nanomolar concentrations, under physiological conditions, and without inducing significant cytotoxicity.

In one aspect, the invention provides a peptide comprising, consisting of, or consisting essentially of the amino acid sequence X₁X₂X₃X₄X₅-Y, wherein X₁, X₂, and X₃ are each independently glutamic acid (E), X₄ and X₅ are each independently alanine (A), and Y is a nuclear localization signal derived from the Oct6 transcription factor. In a preferred embodiment, Y comprises the sequence RKRK (Arg-Arg-Lys-Arg) or a functional variant thereof that retains nuclear import activity. The peptide may further include a linker moiety between the glutamate-rich segment and the NLS to optimize spatial orientation and functional independence of each domain, with alanine residues serving as a natural, non-interfering spacer.

In another aspect, the invention encompasses a conjugate comprising the Glu-Oct6 peptide covalently linked to a therapeutic payload. The payload may be a PNA, an antisense oligonucleotide, a siRNA, a small molecule drug, or a protein. In a particularly preferred embodiment, the payload is a PNA designed to inhibit the expression or function of an oncogenic target, such as STAT3. The conjugate is configured such that the Glu-Oct6 peptide mediates rapid and efficient cellular internalization, followed by nuclear translocation of the payload, enabling potent biological activity at low concentrations.

The invention further provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising the Glu-Oct6 peptide or its conjugate, in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient. These compositions are formulated for administration via routes suitable for cancer therapy, including intravenous, intratumoral, or systemic delivery, and are stable in serum-containing environments.

Methods of treating cancer are also within the scope of the invention. Specifically, the invention provides a method of inhibiting tumor growth or inducing apoptosis in a subject suffering from prostate or pancreatic cancer, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of a Glu-Oct6-PNA conjugate, wherein the PNA is complementary to a target sequence in the mRNA or genomic DNA of an oncogene, such as STAT3. The method leverages the unique ability of the Glu-Oct6 peptide to deliver the PNA to the nucleus, where it forms a triple helix with duplex DNA or hybridizes with mRNA, thereby suppressing oncogene expression and triggering cancer cell death.

The invention is further supported by experimental evidence demonstrating that the Glu-Oct6 peptide achieves up to 100-fold greater cellular uptake than the Oct6 NLS alone, localizes predominantly to the nucleus in prostate (DU-145, LNCaP) and pancreatic (PANC-1) cancer cell lines, and enables the nuclear delivery of a carboxyfluorescein-labeled PNA at concentrations as low as 300 nM. Critically, substitution of glutamic acid residues with phenylalanine, asparagine, or tyrosine abolishes uptake, confirming the sequence specificity of the glutamate-rich motif. Moreover, the Glu-Oct6-anti-STAT3 PNA conjugate induces significant apoptosis (48–69%) in cancer cells at 600 nM, whereas the unconjugated PNA shows negligible activity, underscoring the essential role of the Glu-Oct6 carrier in therapeutic efficacy.

In summary, the invention solves the long-standing problem of inefficient nuclear delivery of PNA therapeutics by providing a rationally engineered, sequence-specific CPP that combines cancer cell penetration with nuclear targeting, thereby enabling potent and selective anticancer activity with minimal off-target effects.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a novel class of cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) engineered through the rational fusion of a glutamate-rich motif with a nuclear localization signal (NLS) derived from the Oct6 transcription factor. This chimeric design overcomes the limitations of conventional CPPs by simultaneously enhancing cellular uptake efficiency and directing the conjugated cargo to the nucleus—the primary site of action for transcription factor inhibitors such as anti-STAT3 peptide nucleic acids (PNAs). The invention is grounded in the discovery that the N-terminal addition of a pentapeptide sequence EEEAA (Glu-Glu-Glu-Ala-Ala) to the Oct6 NLS (RKRK) yields a peptide, termed Glu-Oct6, that exhibits superior performance in terms of internalization kinetics, nuclear accumulation, and functional delivery of therapeutic payloads in prostate and pancreatic cancer cell lines.

The glutamate-rich segment (EEEAA) serves multiple critical functions. First, it appears to engage with cell surface receptors or transporters that are overexpressed in malignant cells, potentially including aberrant glutamate receptors known to be upregulated in various solid tumors. Although the exact mechanism of recognition is not fully elucidated, the strict dependence on glutamic acid residues—demonstrated by the complete loss of uptake upon substitution with Phe, Asn, or Tyr—suggests a specific interaction with a negatively charged or glutamate-sensing moiety on the cancer cell membrane. Second, the alanine residues (AA) act as a flexible, non-polar spacer that decouples the glutamate domain from the NLS, preventing steric interference and allowing each functional unit to operate independently. This design principle is consistent with established practices in protein engineering, where linker peptides are used to maintain the structural and functional integrity of fused domains.

The Oct6 NLS component, comprising the tetrapeptide RKRK, is derived from the bipartite nuclear localization signal of the Oct6 transcription factor, a member of the POU family of DNA-binding proteins. While NLS peptides alone often suffer from poor cellular uptake and endosomal entrapment, the fusion with the EEEAA motif in Glu-Oct6 not only boosts internalization but also promotes escape from endosomal compartments, enabling efficient nuclear translocation. Imaging flow cytometry and live-cell microscopy studies confirm that Glu-Oct6-FITC accumulates in the nucleus of DU-145, LNCaP, and PANC-1 cells, with approximately 50% nuclear localization observed at 250 nM, in stark contrast to control peptides lacking either the glutamate motif or the NLS, which show minimal nuclear presence.

The cellular uptake of Glu-Oct6 is concentration-dependent, with detectable internalization at 30 nM and linear increases in fluorescence intensity up to at least 500 nM. Kinetic analyses reveal rapid entry, with significant uptake occurring within 1 hour and near-maximal levels achieved by 4 hours. Importantly, the process is temperature-sensitive and partially inhibited by sodium azide (NaN₃), indicating involvement of ATP-dependent endocytic pathways, although a non-endocytic component may also contribute. This dual-mechanism uptake profile may enhance versatility across different tumor microenvironments, where energy availability and receptor expression can vary.

The true therapeutic value of Glu-Oct6 lies in its capacity to deliver functional cargoes. When conjugated to a carboxyfluorescein-labeled PNA of inert sequence, Glu-Oct6-PNA-FITC enters both DU-145 and PANC-1 cells and localizes predominantly to the nucleus, with quantitative imaging showing ~70% nuclear colocalization. This nuclear delivery capability is then harnessed for oncogene silencing: a Glu-Oct6-conjugated anti-STAT3 PNA (sequence 13410a) induces robust apoptosis in prostate and pancreatic cancer cells at 600 nM, whereas the unconjugated PNA is ineffective even at 1000 nM. The induction of apoptosis is both dose-dependent and statistically significant (p < 0.01), validating the biological relevance of nuclear PNA delivery.

The invention is not limited to the specific EEEAA-Oct6 sequence. Variants may include conservative substitutions in the alanine linker (e.g., glycine, serine) or extensions of the glutamate tract (e.g., EEEEAA), provided that the core glutamate dependency and nuclear targeting function are preserved. Similarly, the Oct6 NLS may be replaced with other transcription factor NLS motifs (e.g., from SV40 T-antigen, c-Myc, or NF-κB), though the synergistic effect observed with Oct6 suggests a unique compatibility between its sequence and the EEEAA motif. The peptide may be synthesized using standard Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis, purified by HPLC, and conjugated to payloads via amide bond formation, thioether linkages, or click chemistry, depending on the cargo’s chemical nature.

Pharmaceutical formulations of Glu-Oct6 conjugates are prepared in physiologically compatible buffers, optionally with stabilizers (e.g., human serum albumin) or targeting moieties to further enhance tumor specificity. Administration routes include intravenous infusion for systemic therapy or localized injection for accessible tumors. The low effective concentration (nanomolar range) and absence of cytotoxicity in viability assays support a favorable therapeutic index.

In conclusion, the Glu-Oct6 platform represents a paradigm shift in CPP design, moving beyond generic membrane translocation to achieve organelle-specific delivery with sequence-defined precision. Its application to STAT3 inhibition in prostate and pancreatic cancer exemplifies a broader strategy for targeting nuclear oncoproteins with nucleic acid therapeutics, opening new avenues for precision oncology.

### Examples

The following examples illustrate the synthesis, characterization, and biological evaluation of the Glu-Oct6 peptide and its conjugates, demonstrating the principles and advantages of the present invention. These examples are provided for illustrative purposes only and are not intended to limit the scope of the claims.

**Example 1: Synthesis and Characterization of Glu-Oct6 Peptides**

A series of FITC-labeled peptides were synthesized using Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis on an Applied Biosystems Model 433 synthesizer. The peptides included: (1) Glu-FITC (EEE-FITC), (2) Glu-Ala-FITC (EEEAA-FITC), (3) Glu-Lys-FITC (EEEAA-KKK-FITC), (4) Oct6-FITC (RKRK-FITC), and (5) Glu-Oct6-FITC (EEEAA-RKRK-FITC). After cleavage and deprotection, peptides were purified by reverse-phase HPLC and verified by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and amino acid sequencing. All peptides exhibited >95% purity and correct molecular weights. Fluorescein was conjugated to the N-terminus via a lysine side chain to ensure consistent labeling stoichiometry (1:1 dye-to-peptide ratio).

**Example 2: Cellular Uptake in Prostate and Pancreatic Cancer Cell Lines**

DU-145 (prostate adenocarcinoma), LNCaP (prostate carcinoma), and PANC-1 (pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma) cells were cultured in appropriate media and incubated with 30–500 nM of FITC-labeled peptides for 1–48 hours. Flow cytometry analysis, gating on live cells using 7-AAD exclusion, revealed that Glu-Oct6-FITC achieved the highest geometric mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in all cell lines. At 300 nM and 4 hours, Glu-Oct6-FITC showed a 100-fold increase in MFI compared to Oct6-FITC alone and a 10-fold increase over Glu-Lys-FITC. Uptake was rapid, with 60–70% of cells fluorescent at 30 nM after 6 hours. In contrast, PMSA-targeted EEE-PNA and TAT-PNA showed only 30–50% uptake in DU-145 cells, which declined over time for EEE-PNA.

**Example 3: Sequence Specificity of Glutamate Residues**

To assess the role of glutamic acid, analogs of Glu-Oct6-FITC were synthesized with Glu substituted by Phe (Phe-Oct6-FITC), Asn (Asn-Oct6-FITC), or Tyr (Tyr-Oct6-FITC). Incubation with DU-145 cells at 0–1000 nM for 1 hour resulted in negligible uptake (MFI < 100), confirming that glutamate is essential. Competition assays showed that unlabeled Phe-Oct6 did not inhibit Glu-Oct6-FITC uptake, indicating that the recognition mechanism is not saturable by non-glutamate peptides and is thus sequence-specific.

**Example 4: Mechanism of Cellular Uptake**

Uptake experiments at 4°C, 23°C, and 37°C demonstrated strong temperature dependence, with maximal uptake at 37°C. Treatment with 1% NaN₃ reduced Glu-Oct6-FITC uptake by ~25% and Oct6-FITC uptake by >50%, suggesting partial ATP dependence. In contrast, Glu-FITC and Glu-Ala-FITC uptake was unaffected by NaN₃, indicating a non-endocytic pathway for smaller peptides. These data imply that Glu-Oct6 utilizes both energy-dependent and -independent mechanisms, possibly involving initial receptor-mediated endocytosis followed by non-endocytic translocation.

**Example 5: Nuclear Localization Studies**

Imaging flow cytometry and live-cell epifluorescence microscopy using DRAQ5 nuclear stain showed that Glu-Oct6-FITC colocalized with nuclei in >50% of DU-145 and LNCaP cells after 24 hours. Control peptides (Glu-FITC, Oct6-FITC) remained diffuse in the cytoplasm. Quantitative IDEAS software analysis of PANC-1 cells treated with Glu-Oct6-PNA-FITC confirmed ~70% nuclear localization, validating the NLS functionality.

**Example 6: Delivery of Therapeutic PNA and Induction of Apoptosis**

A Glu-Oct6-conjugated anti-STAT3 PNA (Glu-Oct6-13410a) was synthesized and tested against DU-145 and PANC-1 cells. After 48 hours, 600 nM Glu-Oct6-13410a induced 48% and 69% apoptosis, respectively, as measured by Annexin V/PI staining. Unconjugated 13410a PNA caused <1% apoptosis at 1000 nM. These results demonstrate that Glu-Oct6 enables functional nuclear delivery of therapeutic PNAs, leading to potent oncogene inhibition.

## Experimental Procedures

Peptides and peptide-PNA conjugates were synthesized by the Molecular Resources Facility at the University of Medicine and Dentistry of New Jersey using Fmoc chemistry on an Applied Biosystems Model 433 peptide synthesizer. Carboxyfluorescein and carboxydansyl labels were incorporated during synthesis, and final products were purified by HPLC and validated by MALDI-TOF and sequencing. DU-145, LNCaP, and PANC-1 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 or RPMI-1640 with 10% serum. For uptake studies, cells were incubated with 0–1000 nM peptide for 1–48 hours, harvested, and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACScan or LSR II) with 7-AAD or PI for live-cell gating. Geometric MFI was used for quantification due to logarithmic detector amplification. Nuclear colocalization was assessed using DRAQ5 staining and imaging flow cytometry (Amnis ImageStream) with IDEAS 3.0 software. Live-cell imaging was performed on a Zeiss Axiovert 200 microscope with epifluorescence. Apoptosis was quantified by Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and flow cytometry. Statistical analyses were conducted using Kaleidagraph 4.2 and InStat3, with significance defined as p < 0.01 by repeated ANOVA.
