# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of recombinant protein production has long been challenged by the need for efficient, reliable, and cost-effective purification methodologies that yield biologically active, untagged proteins in high purity and quantity. Recombinant proteins are indispensable tools in modern biological research, structural biology, proteomics, and therapeutic development. However, the expression of heterologous proteins in bacterial systems—most commonly *Escherichia coli*—often results in poor solubility, aggregation into inclusion bodies, or rapid degradation by endogenous proteases. To address these issues, researchers have developed a variety of affinity-based purification strategies that rely on the fusion of target proteins to solubility-enhancing tags such as glutathione S-transferase (GST), maltose-binding protein (MBP), N-utilization substance A (NusA), or small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO). These tags not only improve the solubility and stability of otherwise recalcitrant proteins but also facilitate their purification through specific interactions with immobilized ligands, such as glutathione for GST or nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) for polyhistidine (His6) tags.

Despite these advances, the presence of affinity tags can interfere with the functional characterization of the target protein, particularly in enzymatic assays, protein-protein interaction studies, or structural analyses such as X-ray crystallography, where even minor perturbations at the protein termini can disrupt folding or packing. Consequently, there is a strong demand for methods that allow the complete removal of purification tags after isolation. The most common approach involves the use of site-specific proteases—such as thrombin, factor Xa, or tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease—that recognize short, engineered cleavage sequences inserted between the tag and the target protein. While effective in principle, this strategy suffers from several significant drawbacks. First, these exogenous proteases are often expensive, especially when used at the high concentrations required for complete cleavage. Second, they may exhibit suboptimal activity under standard purification conditions, necessitating buffer exchanges or extended incubation times that can compromise protein stability. Third, and perhaps most critically, many of these proteases display limited specificity and can cleave the target protein at secondary sites, particularly if the target contains sequences resembling the recognition motif. This off-target cleavage can lead to heterogeneous protein products, reduced yields, and compromised experimental reproducibility.

Moreover, the post-cleavage removal of the protease itself introduces an additional chromatographic step, typically involving a second affinity column or size-exclusion chromatography, which further complicates the purification workflow and reduces overall efficiency. In high-throughput settings—such as structural genomics consortia or proteomic screening platforms—these limitations become especially problematic, as they increase both time and cost while reducing scalability. Alternative strategies have emerged to circumvent the need for exogenous proteases, including self-cleaving intein systems and elastin-like polypeptide (ELP) tags. Intein-based systems exploit the natural protein splicing activity of inteins, which can be induced by changes in pH, temperature, or the addition of thiols to release the target protein from a chitin-binding domain (CBD) tag. However, intein fusions are often large (>40 kDa), which can negatively impact the solubility of the target protein, and the cleavage conditions (e.g., low pH or high concentrations of dithiothreitol) may be incompatible with sensitive proteins. Similarly, ELP tags undergo reversible phase separation in response to temperature shifts, enabling purification without chromatography, but the requirement for thermal cycling and high salt concentrations can denature or aggregate labile proteins.

Another critical limitation of existing systems is their inability to consistently enhance the expression, solubility, and stability of difficult-to-express proteins—particularly those derived from eukaryotic pathogens such as *Plasmodium falciparum* or intrinsically disordered domains like the cytokine receptor gp130 intracellular domain (ICD). Many of these proteins remain refractory to conventional purification approaches, requiring laborious refolding protocols from inclusion bodies or yielding insufficient quantities for downstream applications. There remains a pressing need for a purification platform that not only simplifies the removal of affinity tags but also actively improves the biophysical properties of the target protein during expression and purification. Ideally, such a system would integrate affinity capture, tag cleavage, and product release into a single, streamlined step, eliminate the need for exogenous enzymes, operate under mild and tunable conditions, and be broadly applicable across a diverse range of protein classes—including structured enzymes, disordered domains, and membrane-associated regions.

The present invention addresses these unmet needs through the development of a novel one-step purification system based on the *Vibrio cholerae* MARTX toxin cysteine protease domain (CPD). Unlike conventional proteases, the CPD is uniquely suited for this application due to its strict dependence on the eukaryotic-specific small molecule inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6) for activation, its exclusive cleavage specificity for leucine residues at the P1 position, and its intrinsic autoprocessing mechanism that minimizes trans-cleavage activity. Because InsP6 is absent in bacterial expression hosts such as *E. coli*, CPD fusion proteins remain catalytically inert during expression and initial purification, preventing premature cleavage. Upon immobilization on Ni-NTA resin via a C-terminal His6 tag, the addition of micromolar concentrations of InsP6 triggers rapid and specific autocleavage at the junction between the target protein and the CPD, releasing the untagged target into solution while the His6-tagged CPD remains bound to the resin. This elegant design eliminates the need for protease removal steps, reduces processing time to under two hours, and ensures high-fidelity tag excision without collateral damage to the target protein. Furthermore, empirical evidence demonstrates that the CPD fusion itself acts as a solubility and stability enhancer, significantly improving the expression yields of challenging proteins such as *P. falciparum* SENP1, mouse macrophage metalloelastase (MMP12), and the CRAC-activation domain (CAD) of STIM1—proteins that are otherwise poorly expressed or highly unstable in bacterial systems. Thus, the CPD-based purification system represents a transformative advance in recombinant protein technology, offering a robust, scalable, and versatile platform for the rapid production of high-quality, untagged proteins for research, industrial, and therapeutic applications.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel, one-step method for the purification of recombinant, untagged proteins from bacterial expression systems, which overcomes the limitations of conventional affinity-tag purification and exogenous protease-mediated cleavage strategies. Central to this invention is the use of a fusion construct comprising a target protein operably linked to a cysteine protease domain (CPD) derived from the *Vibrio cholerae* MARTX toxin, wherein the CPD is fused to a C-terminal polyhistidine (His6) affinity tag. The CPD possesses three critical properties that enable its integration into a streamlined, high-fidelity purification workflow: (1) it is catalytically inactive in the absence of the eukaryotic-specific small molecule inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6); (2) it exhibits absolute specificity for cleavage immediately following a leucine residue at the P1 position; and (3) it functions primarily as an autocatalytic enzyme with minimal trans-cleavage activity, thereby ensuring that cleavage occurs exclusively at the engineered junction between the target protein and the CPD.

In accordance with the invention, a nucleic acid construct encoding the target protein-CPD-His6 fusion is introduced into a bacterial host, such as *Escherichia coli*, and protein expression is induced under standard conditions. Due to the absence of InsP6 in bacterial cytoplasm, the CPD remains in a proteolytically inactive state during expression and cell lysis, preventing unwanted cleavage of the fusion protein. The lysate is then applied to an immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) resin, such as Ni2+-NTA agarose, which selectively binds the C-terminal His6 tag, thereby immobilizing the intact fusion protein on the resin. Following washing to remove non-specifically bound contaminants, the resin-bound fusion protein is treated with a solution containing InsP6. This small molecule allosterically activates the CPD, triggering rapid and specific autocleavage at the leucine residue positioned at the C-terminus of the target protein. As a result, the untagged target protein is released into the supernatant, while the His6-tagged CPD remains immobilized on the resin. The supernatant is then collected, yielding a highly purified preparation of the untagged target protein, free from the affinity tag, the protease, and the need for additional chromatographic steps.

The invention further encompasses a suite of engineered expression vectors that allow precise control over the number and identity of residual amino acids appended to the C-terminus of the target protein following cleavage. By cloning the target gene into different restriction sites within the CPD expression cassette—such as SalI, SacI, or BamHI—the user can generate fusion constructs that leave behind four (Val-Asp-Ala-Leu), two (Glu-Leu), or a single (Leu) amino acid residue, respectively. Additionally, epitope tags such as the hemagglutinin (HA) tag can be incorporated N-terminal to the CPD to facilitate detection or alternative purification strategies without compromising the core functionality of the system. The method is compatible with a wide range of buffer conditions, operates efficiently at temperatures between 4°C and 37°C, and requires only micromolar concentrations of InsP6—an inexpensive and commercially available reagent—thereby ensuring cost-effectiveness and experimental flexibility.

Critically, the CPD fusion tag not only enables simplified purification but also enhances the expression, solubility, and stability of a broad spectrum of target proteins, including those that are traditionally difficult to express in bacterial systems. Examples include intrinsically disordered domains (e.g., gp130 ICD), eukaryotic enzymes from parasitic organisms (e.g., *Plasmodium falciparum* SENP1), and proteolytically sensitive regulatory domains (e.g., STIM1 CAD). In each case, fusion to the CPD-His6 tag resulted in significantly higher yields of soluble, full-length protein compared to conventional His6 or GST tags, and in some instances, enabled the purification of proteins that were previously inaccessible due to aggregation or degradation. The purified untagged proteins are suitable for demanding downstream applications, including enzymatic assays, protein crystallization, and high-throughput screening, as demonstrated by the successful generation of diffraction-quality crystals of *P. falciparum* SENP1 and the functional characterization of active MMP12.

Thus, the invention provides a unified platform that integrates affinity purification, inducible tag cleavage, and product recovery into a single, efficient step, eliminating the need for exogenous proteases, reducing processing time, minimizing sample loss, and improving overall protein quality. This system is particularly well-suited for high-throughput structural and proteomic pipelines, academic laboratories, and industrial bioprocessing environments where simplicity, reliability, and scalability are paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

### TERM DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed to them below, unless otherwise indicated by the context. The term “target protein” refers to any polypeptide of interest that is intended for purification, including but not limited to enzymes, structural proteins, regulatory domains, signaling molecules, receptors, antibodies, and fragments thereof. The target protein may be of prokaryotic, eukaryotic, viral, or synthetic origin, and may be naturally occurring or engineered through mutagenesis, truncation, or fusion. The term “cysteine protease domain (CPD)” denotes the catalytic domain derived from the multifunctional autoprocessing repeats-in-toxin (MARTX) toxin of *Vibrio cholerae*, specifically encompassing amino acid residues 3440 to 3650 of the MARTX toxin from strain N16961, or any functional homolog or variant thereof that retains the ability to undergo InsP6-dependent autocleavage at a leucine residue. The term “inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6)” refers to the phosphorylated derivative of inositol bearing six phosphate groups, also known as phytic acid, which serves as the specific allosteric activator of the CPD and is absent in bacterial cells. The term “affinity tag” designates a peptide or protein sequence that facilitates the purification of a fusion protein through specific binding to a solid support; in the present invention, the affinity tag is a polyhistidine sequence (preferably His6) fused to the C-terminus of the CPD. The phrase “operably linked” indicates that the nucleic acid sequences encoding the target protein and the CPD-His6 tag are joined in-frame such that a single, continuous polypeptide is produced upon translation. The term “autocleavage” or “autoprocessing” describes the intramolecular proteolytic reaction in which the CPD catalyzes the hydrolysis of the peptide bond immediately following a leucine residue at its own N-terminus, resulting in the release of the upstream target protein. The term “trans-cleavage” refers to the intermolecular proteolytic activity of a protease on substrates other than itself, which is minimized in the CPD due to its structural constraints. The term “immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC)” encompasses any chromatographic method that utilizes metal ions—such as Ni2+, Co2+, or Cu2+—chelated to a solid support to bind polyhistidine-tagged proteins. The term “bacterial host” includes any prokaryotic expression system, with *Escherichia coli* being the preferred embodiment, though other Gram-negative or Gram-positive bacteria may also be employed. The term “residual amino acids” refers to the short peptide sequence remaining at the C-terminus of the target protein after CPD-mediated cleavage, the composition of which is determined by the cloning strategy used to construct the fusion gene. The term “solubility-enhancing” describes the observed property of the CPD fusion to increase the proportion of target protein found in the soluble fraction of bacterial lysates, as opposed to aggregated inclusion bodies. The term “one-step purification” signifies a process in which affinity capture, tag cleavage, and product elution are accomplished in a single operational sequence without intermediate chromatographic separations.

### Methods

The present invention provides a comprehensive method for the purification of untagged recombinant proteins from bacterial expression systems, leveraging the unique biochemical properties of the *Vibrio cholerae* MARTX toxin cysteine protease domain (CPD). The method begins with the construction of a recombinant expression vector in which the nucleic acid sequence encoding the target protein is operably linked in-frame to the 5′ end of a nucleic acid sequence encoding the CPD, which itself is fused at its 3′ end to a polyhistidine (His6) affinity tag. A series of specialized expression vectors has been developed to facilitate this process, including pET-CPDSalI, pET-CPDSacI, pET-CPDBamHI, and pET-HA-CPDSalI, each designed to accommodate different cloning strategies and desired C-terminal residues on the target protein. For instance, when the target gene is cloned into the SalI site of pET-CPDSalI, the resulting fusion protein includes four additional amino acids—Val-Asp-Ala-Leu—at the C-terminus of the target protein, with the leucine serving as the P1 cleavage site. Cloning into the SacI site of pET-CPDSacI reduces this extension to two residues (Glu-Leu), while insertion into the BamHI site of pET-CPDBamHI, coupled with the inclusion of a leucine codon in the 3′ primer, results in a single leucine residue appended to the target. The pET-HA-CPDSalI vector further allows the incorporation of an N-terminal HA epitope tag for immunodetection or alternative purification, without interfering with CPD function.

Once the expression construct is assembled, it is introduced into a suitable bacterial host, preferably *Escherichia coli* strains such as BL21(DE3) or derivatives optimized for protein expression. Bacterial cultures are grown in standard media such as Luria-Bertani (LB) or 2xYT broth supplemented with appropriate antibiotics (e.g., 100 µg/mL carbenicillin for pET vectors) at 37°C until mid-log phase (OD600 ≈ 0.6). Protein expression is then induced by the addition of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of 250 µM, and cultures are incubated at temperatures ranging from 16°C to 30°C for 3–8 hours, depending on the stability and solubility requirements of the target protein. Notably, lower induction temperatures (e.g., 16°C for MMP12 or room temperature for PfSENP1) are employed to minimize aggregation and promote proper folding. Following induction, cells are harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in a lysis buffer typically composed of 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 500 mM NaCl, 15 mM imidazole, and 10% glycerol, with optional additives such as 1 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) for disulfide-sensitive proteins or protease inhibitor cocktails. The cell suspension is flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until purification.

Purification is initiated by thawing the lysate and subjecting it to sonication or mechanical disruption to ensure complete cell lysis. The lysate is then clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 30 minutes to remove insoluble debris and inclusion bodies. The supernatant, containing the soluble CPD-His6 fusion protein, is incubated in batch with Ni2+-NTA agarose beads for 2–4 hours at 4°C with gentle agitation. The beads are then pelleted by low-speed centrifugation (1,500 × g), and the supernatant is discarded. The resin is washed three times with lysis buffer to remove non-specifically bound contaminants. At this stage, the immobilized fusion protein is catalytically inert due to the absence of InsP6 in the bacterial lysate and purification buffers.

To initiate cleavage, the washed beads are resuspended in a small volume of lysis buffer (typically 300–500 µL per milliliter of culture), and inositol hexakisphosphate (InsP6) is added to a final concentration of 50–100 µM. The mixture is nutated or gently rotated at either 4°C or room temperature for 1–2 hours, allowing the InsP6 to diffuse into the resin matrix and activate the CPD. Upon activation, the CPD undergoes rapid autocleavage at the leucine residue positioned at the C-terminus of the target protein, releasing the untagged target into the surrounding solution. The beads are then pelleted, and the supernatant—containing the purified target protein—is collected. Additional washes of the resin with lysis buffer are performed to maximize recovery, and these wash fractions are pooled with the initial supernatant. The His6-tagged CPD remains tightly bound to the Ni2+-NTA resin and is not detected in the eluate, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie staining.

The resulting preparation of untagged target protein is typically of high purity, often requiring no further chromatographic steps. In cases where minor contaminants persist—as observed with *Plasmodium falciparum* SENP1—a single round of gel filtration chromatography is sufficient to achieve homogeneity suitable for crystallization. The entire process, from cell lysis to purified protein, can be completed in approximately 7 hours, a dramatic improvement over traditional multi-step protocols involving exogenous proteases and multiple columns. The method is highly adaptable: buffer composition, temperature, and InsP6 concentration can be optimized for individual proteins, and the absence of salt or denaturants in the cleavage step allows direct compatibility with downstream applications. Activity assays confirm that the purified proteins retain full functionality, as demonstrated by the fluorescence of GFP, the proteolytic activity of MMP12 against a fluorogenic substrate, and the successful use of PfSENP1 in crystallography trials. This robust, scalable, and user-friendly platform represents a significant advancement in recombinant protein purification technology.
