# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The cultivation of potato (Solanum tuberosum) has long been a cornerstone of global agriculture, with the crop serving as a primary source of calories and nutrients for billions of people. Among the most widely grown cultivars in Europe is Bintje, a variety first developed in the Netherlands in 1904 and prized for its high yield, adaptability to diverse growing conditions, and excellent culinary qualities. Despite these agronomic advantages, Bintje suffers from a critical limitation: it is functionally female-sterile under normal cultivation conditions. Specifically, while Bintje plants produce flowers that undergo normal anthesis, the ovaries fail to develop into seed berries following pollination, and the flowers typically abscise shortly after blooming. This sterility severely restricts the utility of Bintje in conventional breeding programs, which rely on sexual reproduction to introduce desirable traits such as disease resistance, improved nutritional content, or novel tuber characteristics. As a result, the Bintje gene pool has remained largely unchanged for over a century, rendering it vulnerable to evolving pathogens and limiting opportunities for genetic improvement.

One of the most devastating threats to potato production worldwide is late blight, caused by the oomycete pathogen Phytophthora infestans. This pathogen was responsible for the Irish Potato Famine in the 1840s and continues to cause significant economic losses today. While resistant cultivars such as Sarpo Mira have been developed through traditional breeding, incorporating such resistance into elite but sterile lines like Bintje has proven exceptionally difficult due to the lack of female fertility. Similarly, efforts to enhance the nutritional profile of Bintje—such as introducing anthocyanin pigments for antioxidant benefits—have been hampered by the inability to cross it with donor lines possessing these traits. Consequently, there exists a long-felt but unmet need in the field of plant breeding for a method to restore female fertility to otherwise sterile potato cultivars without permanently altering their genetic identity or requiring complex genomic interventions.

Carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs), including cellulose-binding domains (CBDs), are protein domains commonly found in microbial cellulolytic enzymes. These domains facilitate the binding of enzymes to insoluble cellulose, thereby enhancing the efficiency of cellulose degradation. CBDs are classified into numerous families based on amino acid sequence similarity, with Family 1 CBDs being particularly prevalent in fungal and oomycete systems. Notably, certain phytopathogens, including P. infestans, encode standalone CBD proteins that are not fused to catalytic domains. One such protein, CBD1 from P. infestans (GenBank accession EU179903), is known to associate tightly with the cellulose-containing hyphal cell wall and may play a role in cell wall integrity or assembly. Prior research has suggested that CBDs can interact with plant cell walls when expressed heterologously, sometimes leading to morphological changes. For example, expression of a bacterial Family 3 CBD in transgenic potato led to altered early growth patterns, while other studies in tobacco using different CBMs yielded variable effects on plant development. However, no prior work has demonstrated that expression of a CBD—particularly a Family 1 CBD from a plant pathogen—could overcome reproductive sterility in a major crop cultivar.

The scientific community has long recognized the potential of cell wall-modifying proteins to influence plant development, including fruit and ovary formation. In Solanaceous species such as tomato and potato, fruit development is tightly regulated by hormonal signals, particularly auxin, which promotes cell wall loosening and expansion through the induction of endogenous cell wall-modifying enzymes. Parthenocarpic fruit development—fruit formation without fertilization—has been achieved in tomato through tissue-specific manipulation of auxin biosynthesis or signaling. However, these approaches often require precise genetic constructs and can lead to pleiotropic effects. The idea that a simple carbohydrate-binding domain, devoid of enzymatic activity, could trigger ovary development in a sterile cultivar was entirely unexpected and represents a paradigm shift in our understanding of how non-enzymatic cell wall-interacting proteins can influence plant reproductive biology.

Prior to the present invention, there was no known method to restore functional female fertility to Bintje or similar sterile potato cultivars in a manner that would allow for conventional crossing and subsequent segregation of the fertility-restoring element. Existing approaches to circumvent sterility—such as somatic hybridization or mutagenesis—are technically challenging, inefficient, and often result in undesirable genetic changes. Furthermore, while genetic transformation has been used to introduce disease resistance or other traits into potato, none of these efforts have addressed the fundamental issue of reproductive sterility in elite cultivars. Thus, despite decades of research in plant pathology, molecular biology, and breeding, the problem of Bintje’s sterility remained unsolved, and the broader implication—that a pathogen-derived CBD could serve as a tool for reproductive rescue—was neither anticipated nor suggested by the prior art.

It is within this context that the present invention emerges. The inventors hypothesized that expression of the P. infestans CBD1 protein in potato might alter host-pathogen interactions, potentially by shielding host or pathogen cell walls from enzymatic degradation. Surprisingly, however, the primary and most significant effect observed was not related to disease resistance at all, but rather to the complete restoration of female fertility in the otherwise sterile Bintje cultivar. Transgenic Bintje plants expressing CBD1 not only formed seed berries in the absence of pollination—a phenomenon resembling parthenocarpy—but also produced viable seeds when crossed with fertile donor lines. This unexpected outcome opens new avenues for potato breeding, enabling the introgression of valuable traits into the Bintje background and, by extension, into other sterile cultivars. Critically, because the CBD1 transgene can be segregated out in subsequent generations, the resulting improved varieties can be free of recombinant DNA, addressing regulatory and consumer concerns in markets where transgenic crops face restrictions. The discovery that a single, non-catalytic carbohydrate-binding domain can so profoundly influence plant reproductive development represents a novel and non-obvious contribution to the fields of plant biotechnology, reproductive biology, and crop improvement.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for restoring female fertility to female-sterile potato cultivars through the expression of a cellulose-binding domain (CBD) protein, specifically the Family 1 CBD derived from the late blight pathogen Phytophthora infestans, herein referred to as CBD1. In a preferred embodiment, the CBD1 protein is encoded by the nucleotide sequence set forth in GenBank accession EU179903 and is expressed in potato plants under the control of a constitutive promoter, such as the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter. The invention further encompasses transgenic potato plants of previously female-sterile cultivars, such as Bintje, that have been genetically modified to express the CBD1 protein and thereby exhibit restored female fertility, as evidenced by the formation of seed berries either parthenocarpically or following successful pollination with viable pollen.

A key aspect of the invention is the observation that CBD1-expressing transgenic Bintje plants develop ovaries and produce seed berries even in the absence of fertilization, indicating a parthenocarpic-like phenotype. Upon pollination with pollen from fertile donor lines, these transgenic plants produce larger seed berries containing viable seeds, demonstrating full functional restoration of female fertility. Importantly, the male fertility of the transgenic Bintje plants remains unaffected; they continue to produce non-viable pollen, consistent with the original sterility phenotype of the cultivar. This selective restoration of female function enables the use of transgenic Bintje as a female parent in controlled crosses, facilitating the introduction of desirable traits—such as late blight resistance from Sarpo Mira or anthocyanin pigmentation from Peter Wilcox—into the Bintje genetic background.

The invention further provides a method for developing improved potato varieties that retain the agronomic and culinary qualities of elite sterile cultivars while incorporating novel traits through sexual reproduction. By crossing CBD1-expressing transgenic Bintje plants with donor lines possessing desired characteristics, breeders can generate F1 progeny that segregate for both the introduced traits and the CBD1 transgene. Subsequent generations can be selected to retain the beneficial traits while losing the CBD1 transgene, thereby producing non-transgenic, fertile offspring that are genetically improved versions of the original sterile cultivar. This approach circumvents the need for permanent genetic modification and allows for the development of conventionally bred varieties that meet regulatory and market requirements for non-GMO products.

In addition to its application in Bintje, the invention contemplates the use of CBD1 expression to restore female fertility in other female-sterile potato cultivars, such as Russet Burbank, as well as in other Solanaceous crops where reproductive sterility limits breeding efforts. The mechanism by which CBD1 restores ovary development is not fully understood but is believed to involve interaction with cellulose in the plant cell wall, potentially modulating cell wall properties or signaling pathways that influence reproductive development. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, the practical utility of CBD1 as a fertility-restoring agent is demonstrated by the successful production of late blight-resistant and nutritionally enhanced progeny from crosses involving transgenic Bintje.

The invention also includes diagnostic methods for identifying transgenic plants expressing CBD1, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using primers specific to the transgene or immunolocalization using CBD1-specific antibodies. Furthermore, the invention provides kits comprising the CBD1 expression construct, along with instructions for its use in transforming potato plants and utilizing the resulting transgenics in breeding programs. Overall, the present invention represents a breakthrough in plant breeding technology, enabling the genetic improvement of historically intractable elite cultivars through a simple, transient, and segregatable genetic intervention.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to the surprising discovery that expression of a cellulose-binding domain (CBD) protein, specifically the Family 1 CBD from Phytophthora infestans (CBD1), in female-sterile potato cultivars results in the restoration of female fertility, thereby enabling their use in conventional breeding programs. The invention encompasses nucleic acid constructs encoding the CBD1 protein, transgenic potato plants transformed with such constructs, methods for producing these transgenic plants, and methods for using them to develop improved potato varieties through sexual reproduction.

The CBD1 protein utilized in the present invention is a naturally occurring protein encoded by the genome of Phytophthora infestans, the causal agent of late blight in potato and tomato. The full-length coding sequence of CBD1 is publicly available under GenBank accession number EU179903 and comprises 276 nucleotides encoding a 92-amino acid polypeptide. The protein belongs to Carbohydrate-Binding Module Family 1 (CBM1) and is characterized by a conserved structural fold that facilitates high-affinity binding to crystalline cellulose. Unlike many microbial CBDs that are fused to catalytic domains of cellulolytic enzymes, CBD1 exists as a standalone protein in P. infestans and is associated with the hyphal cell wall, where it may contribute to cell wall integrity or assembly. The amino acid sequence of CBD1 includes conserved aromatic residues that are critical for stacking interactions with glucose rings in cellulose, as well as cysteine residues that form disulfide bonds stabilizing the tertiary structure.

In accordance with the invention, a nucleic acid molecule encoding the CBD1 protein is operably linked to a plant-compatible promoter to drive expression in potato tissues. In a preferred embodiment, the cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) 35S promoter is used due to its strong, constitutive activity in dicotyledonous plants. The expression cassette further includes a transcription termination sequence, such as the nopaline synthase (nos) terminator, and may optionally include a selectable marker gene, such as neomycin phosphotransferase II (nptII), to facilitate the identification of transformed plant cells. The entire expression cassette is typically cloned into a binary vector, such as pBI121, which is compatible with Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, the standard method for introducing foreign DNA into potato.

Transformation of potato plants is carried out using established protocols. In one exemplary method, leaf explants from in vitro-grown Bintje plantlets are co-cultivated with Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 harboring the CBD1 expression vector. Following co-cultivation, explants are transferred to selection media containing kanamycin to eliminate non-transformed cells. Regenerated shoots are rooted and acclimatized to greenhouse conditions. Putative transgenic plants are confirmed by PCR amplification of the transgene using primers specific to the 35S promoter or the CBD1 coding sequence, followed by sequencing to verify the integrity of the insert. Integration sites of the transgene in the potato genome can be determined using techniques such as genome walking, which has revealed that integration occurs at non-coding regions, thereby minimizing the risk of disrupting essential endogenous genes.

Transgenic Bintje plants expressing CBD1 exhibit a striking phenotypic change: they develop seed berries, a trait absent in non-transformed Bintje. These seed berries form even in the absence of pollination, indicating a parthenocarpic-like development. Upon manual pollination with viable pollen from fertile donor lines—such as the late blight-resistant cultivar Sarpo Mira or the purple-skinned, yellow-fleshed cultivar Peter Wilcox—the transgenic plants produce enlarged seed berries containing viable seeds. This demonstrates that CBD1 expression not only initiates ovary development but also supports full seed maturation when fertilization occurs. Immunolocalization studies using CBD1-specific antibodies confirm that the protein is abundantly present in the ovary wall and locule tissues of developing seed berries, suggesting a direct role in reproductive tissue development.

Importantly, the male fertility of transgenic Bintje remains compromised; the plants produce little to no viable pollen, consistent with the original sterility of the cultivar. This selective restoration of female function is advantageous for breeding, as it prevents self-pollination and ensures that all seeds result from controlled crosses with selected donor lines. The resulting F1 progeny segregate for the CBD1 transgene and the traits introduced from the donor parent. For example, crosses between CBD1-expressing Bintje and Sarpo Mira yield progeny that exhibit varying levels of late blight resistance, with many individuals displaying full resistance comparable to Sarpo Mira. Similarly, crosses with Peter Wilcox produce progeny with diverse tuber colors and flesh types, indicating successful complementation of the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway.

A critical feature of the invention is the ability to segregate the CBD1 transgene in subsequent generations. Since the transgene is integrated at a single locus in some lines (e.g., B7) or at a defined non-coding locus in others (e.g., B48), standard Mendelian inheritance applies. By selecting F2 or backcross progeny that lack the CBD1 transgene but retain the desired agronomic traits, breeders can develop non-transgenic, improved varieties that are essentially isogenic to the original Bintje except for the introgressed traits. This addresses a major concern in agricultural biotechnology—the persistence of transgenes in commercial varieties—and provides a pathway to market acceptance in regions with strict GMO regulations.

The invention is not limited to Bintje. Preliminary data indicate that CBD1 expression can also restore female fertility in other female-sterile potato cultivars, such as Russet Burbank, although the vigor of the resulting progeny may vary. Moreover, the principle may extend to other Solanaceous crops, such as tomato, where parthenocarpic fruit development is a desirable trait. The mechanism by which CBD1 influences ovary development is not yet fully elucidated but is hypothesized to involve modulation of cell wall properties. CBD1 may bind to cellulose microfibrils in the ovary wall, altering wall extensibility or triggering signaling cascades that promote cell division and expansion. This hypothesis is supported by the known role of cell wall integrity in developmental processes and the precedent of cell wall-modifying proteins influencing fruit set.

In summary, the detailed description of the invention provides a comprehensive framework for utilizing CBD1 expression as a tool for reproductive rescue in potato breeding. The methods are straightforward, the genetic construct is simple, and the outcomes are highly reproducible across independent transformation events. The invention thus offers a practical, efficient, and scalable solution to a longstanding problem in crop improvement.

## EXAMPLES

### Plant Transformation and Selection

The restoration of female fertility in the Bintje potato cultivar was achieved through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation using a binary vector containing the Phytophthora infestans CBD1 gene under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter. Four-week-old in vitro-grown Bintje plantlets were used as the source of explants. Leaves were excised and cut laterally into segments approximately 5 mm in length. These segments were immersed for 15 minutes in a suspension of Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain LBA4404 harboring the pBI121 vector, which had been induced with 100 μM acetosyringone to activate the virulence genes necessary for T-DNA transfer. Following co-cultivation, the explants were transferred to Murashige and Skoog (MS) basal medium supplemented with 2 mg/L benzylaminopurine (BAP) and 0.1 mg/L naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) for shoot regeneration, along with 100 mg/L kanamycin for selection and 500 mg/L cefotaxime to suppress Agrobacterium overgrowth.

After four to six weeks, kanamycin-resistant shoots emerged from the explants and were excised and transferred to rooting medium (MS basal medium with 0.1 mg/L indole-3-butyric acid). Rooted plantlets were acclimatized to greenhouse conditions by gradual exposure to ambient humidity over a period of two weeks. A total of 42 putative transgenic lines were regenerated, of which 38 were confirmed by PCR using primers specific to the 35S promoter (35S-F: GATAATCATCGCAAGACCGGC; 35S-R: GACGTAAGGGATGACGCACAATCCC). The PCR products were sequenced to ensure the absence of mutations in the CBD1 coding region.

To assess transgene integration, genome walking was performed on two representative lines, B7 and B48, using the APAgene GOLD Genome Walking Kit. Three rounds of nested PCR were conducted with forward primers designed to anneal within the CBD1 gene and the left border of the T-DNA, and reverse primers provided by the kit. For line B7, a single band of approximately 850 bp was obtained, indicating a likely single-copy integration event. Sequencing of this product revealed a truncated left border, but the flanking potato genomic sequence could not be uniquely mapped due to insufficient homology. In contrast, line B48 yielded multiple bands, with one prominent fragment of 720 bp that was sequenced and aligned to potato genome scaffold NW_006239126. The integration site was located at positions 1071,661–1071,733, within a 1089 bp intron of a putative ODORANT1 protein gene (XM_006355444). No coding sequences were disrupted, and only one EST (JG563892) corresponding to the downstream exon has been reported, suggesting that the integration is in a transcriptionally inactive region.

Phenotypic evaluation of the transgenic lines revealed that all CBD1-expressing plants produced seed berries, whereas non-transformed Bintje controls exhibited floral abscission after anthesis. Some lines, such as B48, produced significantly larger seed berries, with up to 200 seeds per berry following outcrossing. Height measurements taken weekly from five weeks after soil transfer showed that certain lines, including B7 and B48, exhibited increased vegetative growth compared to controls, although this trait was not universal across all transformants. Immunolocalization of CBD1 in developing seed berries using peptide-specific antibodies and alkaline phosphatase-conjugated secondary antibodies confirmed abundant protein accumulation in the ovary wall and locule tissues, with no signal detected in control tissues treated with pre-immune serum. These results collectively demonstrate successful transformation, stable integration, and functional expression of CBD1 in Bintje potato, leading to the restoration of female fertility.

### Example 2

To evaluate the utility of CBD1-expressing Bintje as a female parent in breeding, controlled crosses were performed with two donor lines: Sarpo Mira, a cultivar with high-level resistance to late blight, and Peter Wilcox, a cultivar with purple skin and yellow flesh due to anthocyanin and carotenoid pigmentation, respectively. Flowers from transgenic Bintje lines B7 and B48, as well as non-transformed Bintje controls, were emasculated to prevent self-pollination, although this step was largely redundant given the male sterility of Bintje. Fresh pollen from Sarpo Mira or Peter Wilcox was applied to the stigmas of the emasculated flowers using a fine brush. Control crosses involved applying the same donor pollen to non-transformed Bintje flowers.

As expected, all control crosses resulted in floral abscission within three days post-pollination, with no seed berry formation. In contrast, transgenic Bintje flowers retained their ovaries and developed seed berries that matured over a period of six to eight weeks. Seed berries from B7 and B48 crosses with Sarpo Mira averaged 120 and 180 seeds per berry, respectively, while crosses with Peter Wilcox yielded similar numbers. Seeds were harvested, dried, and stored at 4°C for two months to break dormancy before sowing.

A subset of F1 seeds from each cross was planted in the greenhouse, and the resulting progeny were screened for late blight resistance using a detached leaflet assay. Leaflets from ten-week-old plants were inoculated with a mixture of P. infestans sporangia and zoospores (race US-11) and incubated under high humidity at 18°C. Disease progression was scored six days post-inoculation on a scale from 1 (fully resistant) to 5 (fully susceptible). All non-transformed Bintje controls were rated as fully susceptible (score 5), while Sarpo Mira was fully resistant (score 1). Among the 16 progeny from the B7 × Sarpo Mira cross, 13 were fully resistant, two were moderately susceptible, and one was fully susceptible, indicating segregation of resistance genes. Tuber color evaluation revealed that several progeny exhibited purple skin, suggesting complementation of the anthocyanin pathway, which is defective in Bintje. Similarly, crosses with Peter Wilcox produced progeny with a range of tuber colors, including purple, red, and yellow, as well as variations in flesh color, demonstrating the successful transfer of multiple quality traits.

These results confirm that CBD1-expressing Bintje can serve as an effective female parent for introgressing valuable traits into the Bintje background. The high rate of successful seed set and the viability of the resulting progeny underscore the practical utility of this approach in potato breeding programs.

### Example 3

To determine whether the CBD1 transgene could be segregated out in subsequent generations, F2 progeny were generated from self-pollination of F1 plants that were heterozygous for the CBD1 transgene. Since the F1 plants inherited the male sterility of Bintje, they could not be used as pollen donors. Instead, F1 plants were crossed with each other as female parents using pollen from a fertile diploid potato line. Seeds from these crosses were germinated, and the resulting F2 plants were screened by PCR for the presence of the CBD1 transgene. Approximately 25% of the F2 plants lacked the transgene, consistent with Mendelian segregation for a single-locus insertion.

These transgene-negative F2 plants were evaluated for female fertility and late blight resistance. All transgene-negative plants exhibited the original Bintje phenotype of floral abscission and failed to produce seed berries, confirming that the fertility-restoring effect is dependent on CBD1 expression. However, many of these plants retained the late blight resistance and purple tuber traits inherited from Sarpo Mira, demonstrating that the desired agronomic traits can be fixed in the absence of the transgene. Tuber yield and quality assessments showed that the improved lines were comparable to Bintje in terms of yield, dry matter content, and cooking quality, while offering the added benefits of disease resistance and enhanced nutritional value.

This example illustrates the key advantage of the present invention: the ability to use a transient genetic intervention to enable breeding, followed by the elimination of the transgene to produce non-GMO, improved varieties. Such varieties are likely to face fewer regulatory hurdles and greater consumer acceptance, making this approach particularly valuable for public sector breeding programs and markets with stringent GMO policies.
