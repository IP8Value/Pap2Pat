# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The proliferation of system-on-chip (SoC) architectures in modern electronic systems has led to an increasing reliance on third-party intellectual property (3PIP) cores to accelerate design cycles and reduce research and development costs. These 3PIP cores, which may be delivered in soft (register transfer level or RTL), firm (gate-level netlist), or hard (GDSII layout) forms, are integrated into the SoC by the design house to fulfill specific functional requirements. However, this outsourcing model introduces significant security vulnerabilities, particularly the risk that untrusted 3PIP vendors may embed malicious circuitry—commonly referred to as hardware Trojans—into their supplied components. A hardware Trojan typically comprises a trigger mechanism and a payload; the trigger remains dormant under normal operating conditions and activates only upon the occurrence of a rare or specific event, at which point the payload executes its malicious function. Such functions may include the unauthorized exfiltration of sensitive data (e.g., cryptographic keys), degradation of system reliability, or denial-of-service attacks. The stealthy nature of these Trojans, combined with the absence of a trusted golden reference model for comparison during verification, renders their detection exceptionally challenging.

Traditional approaches to hardware Trojan detection have employed a variety of methodologies, including structural and functional analysis, logic testing, formal verification, information flow tracking, and runtime validation. Structural techniques, such as unused circuit identification (UCI), flag RTL code segments that remain unexecuted during simulation as potentially malicious. Functional methods like FANCI (Functional Analysis for Nearly-unused Circuit Identification) and VeriTrust identify nets with weak input-output dependency or those not driven by functional inputs as suspicious. However, it has been demonstrated that sophisticated adversaries can engineer Trojans specifically designed to evade these detection mechanisms by ensuring that all Trojan logic is exercised during standard simulation or by embedding the malicious functionality within seemingly legitimate computational paths. More advanced techniques have leveraged formal methods and information flow tracking (IFT) to verify security properties. For instance, bounded model checking (BMC) has been used to formally verify whether sensitive information leaks to unauthorized outputs or whether critical data is maliciously modified. Commercial tools such as JasperGold Security Path Verification (SPV) employ proprietary path sensitization technologies to prove the absence of illegal information flows from designated secure assets to potential leakage points. Similarly, gate-level information flow tracking (GLIFT) associates each data bit with a taint bit and monitors propagation through Boolean gates to detect unauthorized flows.

Despite these advances, existing techniques suffer from several critical limitations that undermine their robustness against determined adversaries. Formal verification approaches based on BMC are inherently constrained by the number of clock cycles over which the design can be unrolled; Trojans whose triggers activate beyond this bound remain undetected. Moreover, these methods often produce false positives because they verify logical equivalence rather than actual information flow—demonstrated by cases where a signal’s value matches that of a sensitive asset without any causal dependency. GLIFT-based techniques, while capable of tracking actual information propagation, fail to distinguish between legitimate and malicious paths when both terminate at the same authorized output, such as the ciphertext port in an encryption module. This limitation enables Type I Trojans—those that leak secrets through valid output channels via bypass circuits—to evade detection. Additionally, GLIFT is incompatible with designs containing design-for-test (DfT) structures, particularly full-scan chains, due to “taint explosion”: in scan mode, a single tainted flip-flop contaminates the entire scan chain, rendering the analysis meaningless. This precludes the detection of Trojans inserted by rogue DfT vendors, who often have full access to the SoC netlist. Proof-carrying code (PCC) and sensitivity tracing methods assign numerical sensitivity values to signals and assume that any deviation from expected values indicates a breach; however, adversaries can construct leakage paths that preserve the nominal sensitivity signature of authorized outputs, thereby bypassing detection. Furthermore, commercial tools like Jasper SPV require the integrator to specify internal signals of interest for analysis—a task that is infeasible under a black-box model where the internal structure of the 3PIP is unknown. Consequently, Trojans that leak information through side channels, such as power consumption of internal registers, remain undetected because the relevant signals are not monitored.

These shortcomings highlight the urgent need for a Trojan detection framework that operates without requiring white-box knowledge of the IP, is resilient to evasion tactics, functions effectively on DfT-enhanced netlists, and accurately distinguishes malicious from legitimate information flows. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel information flow security (IFS) verification methodology grounded in automatic test pattern generation (ATPG) principles. Unlike prior art, this approach models sensitive assets—not as abstract data entities—but as physical faults (specifically, stuck-at-0 and stuck-at-1 faults) and leverages ATPG algorithms to determine whether these “faults” can be observed at unauthorized points or controlled by untrusted inputs. By doing so, it directly verifies the existence of information flow paths, eliminating false positives arising from coincidental value matching. The framework further incorporates a partial-scan ATPG strategy that dynamically configures scan chains to enable deep sequential analysis without the taint explosion problem of full-scan methods, thereby supporting verification of DfT-inserted designs. Additionally, it introduces innovative techniques—propagation depth analysis and intersect analysis—to differentiate between authorized and malicious paths, even when they share endpoints. Finally, the invention includes a trigger condition extraction mechanism based on finite state machine (FSM) reverse engineering, which is not limited by sequential depth and can recover complex activation sequences that evade bounded formal methods. This comprehensive solution provides a robust, scalable, and practical means for SoC integrators to validate the trustworthiness of third-party IPs in the pre-silicon design phase.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for detecting hardware Trojans in third-party intellectual property (3PIP) cores by verifying compliance with information flow security (IFS) policies. The framework operates on synthesized gate-level netlists and does not require a golden reference model or white-box knowledge of the IP’s internal architecture. Central to the invention is the conceptual modeling of sensitive assets—such as cryptographic keys, privilege tokens, or control registers—as physical faults, specifically stuck-at-0 and stuck-at-1 faults, and the application of automatic test pattern generation (ATPG) algorithms to detect the propagation of these faults to observe points or their activation by control points. This approach directly establishes the existence of information flow paths, thereby overcoming the false positive and false negative limitations of prior art that rely on logical equivalence or abstract taint propagation. The framework is structured into three primary components: confidentiality verification, integrity verification, and trigger condition extraction, each of which is described in detail below.

Confidentiality verification ensures that sensitive information does not leak to unauthorized observe points. An observe point is defined as any primary output or pseudo-primary output (e.g., scan flip-flops in a DfT structure) through which the logical state of an internal signal can be externally observed. The process begins by identifying the asset to be protected—for example, a net carrying a secret key bit in an encryption module—and modeling it as a pair of stuck-at faults (stuck-at-0 and stuck-at-1). The gate-level netlist of the 3PIP is then processed using a commercial ATPG tool, such as Synopsys Tetramax, enhanced with custom scripting to implement a dynamic partial-scan methodology. In this methodology, all flip-flops in the design are initially treated as scannable to maximize controllability and observability during analysis. The ATPG algorithm is executed in sequential mode to generate test patterns that propagate the asset’s logical value (0 or 1) from its source to each potential observe point in its fan-out cone. For each observe point, the algorithm determines whether both fault polarities can be detected, which confirms the existence of a bidirectional information flow path from the asset to that observe point. The output includes the complete propagation path, the sequence of input stimuli required to activate and propagate the asset, and the set of all observe points reachable from the asset.

To distinguish between authorized and malicious observe points, the invention employs two complementary techniques: propagation depth analysis and intersect analysis. Propagation depth analysis is used to detect Type I Trojans, which leak assets through valid output ports (e.g., ciphertext outputs) via shortcut paths that bypass the normal cryptographic operations. In such cases, the propagation depth—the number of logic gates traversed from the asset to the observe point—is significantly shorter for the Trojan path than for the legitimate path. Because the analysis is performed on a synthesized netlist, any redundant logic inserted by an adversary to artificially lengthen the Trojan path is automatically eliminated during synthesis, ensuring that the depth discrepancy remains detectable. Intersect analysis, on the other hand, targets Type II Trojans, which exfiltrate assets through functionally isolated leakage circuits that do not interact with the IP’s authorized logic. This technique compares the fan-in cone of each identified observe point against the fan-in cone of the IP’s legitimate outputs. For instance, in an AES encryption module, the only authorized observe points are the ciphertext ports; any flip-flop or output that can observe the key but lies outside the fan-in cone of these ports is flagged as malicious. This method successfully identifies covert channels, such as those implemented via CDMA modulation or power side channels, by detecting registers that are not part of the encryption datapath yet can capture or transmit the asset.

Integrity verification ensures that trusted assets cannot be influenced by untrusted control points. A control point is defined as any primary input or pseudo-primary input (e.g., scan flip-flops) that can drive internal signals. The process mirrors confidentiality verification but operates in the reverse direction: the asset (e.g., a program counter register in a microprocessor) is modeled as a stuck-at fault, and the ATPG algorithm searches for paths from potential control points in the asset’s fan-in cone that can activate the fault. The algorithm iteratively explores deeper levels of the fan-in hierarchy by temporarily removing scan capability from intermediate flip-flops, thereby enabling sequential ATPG to generate multi-cycle activation sequences. The output includes all control points capable of influencing the asset, along with the activation paths and stimulus vectors. Malicious control points are identified using the same depth and intersect analysis principles: Type I integrity violations exhibit abnormally short activation paths, while Type II violations involve control points that lie outside the fan-out cone of the IP’s legitimate control sources (e.g., a pipeline register that should exclusively drive the program counter).

A critical innovation of the invention is its use of partial-scan ATPG, which overcomes the limitations of both full-scan and full-sequential ATPG. Full-scan ATPG restricts fault propagation to the first level of flip-flops, missing deeper Trojan observe points, while full-sequential ATPG is computationally intractable for large sequential circuits. The partial-scan approach dynamically configures scan chains during analysis—adding scan capability to flip-flops to enable observation and then removing it to allow sequential propagation—thus achieving deep sequential coverage without excessive computational overhead. Importantly, this method remains effective on DfT-inserted netlists because it treats scan flip-flops as pseudo-primary I/Os rather than propagating taint through the entire scan chain, thereby avoiding the taint explosion problem that plagues GLIFT-based techniques.

Once an IFS violation is detected, the framework proceeds to extract the Trojan’s trigger condition. For simple Trojans activated by a specific input value or clock count, the stimulus vector reported by the ATPG analysis directly reveals the trigger. For complex Trojans with finite state machine (FSM)-based triggers, the invention employs an FSM reverse engineering technique. First, the flip-flops associated with the trigger condition (as reported by ATPG) are identified as potential state registers by detecting feedback loops in their combinational logic. Then, a state extraction algorithm systematically determines the input conditions and present states that cause transitions to the trigger state, ultimately reconstructing the full state transition graph (STG). From the STG, the exact sequence of inputs that activates the Trojan is derived. This method is not bounded by sequential depth, enabling it to detect Trojans with arbitrarily long trigger sequences or embedded counters—unlike formal verification, which fails when the trigger exceeds the unrolling limit.

The invention has been experimentally validated on a suite of trust-hub benchmark circuits, including AES-T100, RSA-T100, s35932-T100, and modified variants like AES-T1100(M) with extended trigger sequences. It successfully detected all twelve confidentiality-violating and six integrity-violating Trojans, including those that evade prior art: Type I Trojans leaking through ciphertext outputs, Type II Trojans using power side channels, and scan-mode Trojans inserted by DfT vendors. The framework also demonstrated resilience against evasion tactics, such as redundant logic insertion and sensitivity-preserving leakage paths. While the invention assumes that the design contains no latches or uncontrollable flip-flops (which ATPG cannot handle), it includes a warning mechanism to flag such elements for manual inspection or test point insertion. Overall, the invention provides a robust, automated, and commercially viable solution for pre-silicon Trojan detection that addresses the fundamental limitations of existing approaches.
