# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of hydrometallurgical extraction and recovery of rare earth elements (REEs) and other critical minerals from geologic materials, and more particularly to a novel and environmentally sustainable method for selectively leaching REEs from clay-rich sedimentary rocks, including underclay associated with coal seams and coal utilization byproducts. The invention specifically discloses an organic acid-based leaching system that employs citric acid and its conjugate base, sodium citrate, optionally in combination with monovalent salts such as sodium chloride or ammonium sulfate, to achieve high-yield, selective, and low-impact recovery of ion-exchangeable and weakly bound REEs. This method is designed to operate under mild pH conditions, typically between 3 and 6, to minimize the co-dissolution of undesirable gangue elements and radioactive contaminants such as thorium and uranium, which are commonly found in association with REE-bearing minerals. The invention further encompasses the integration of this leaching process with downstream sorbent-based capture technologies for the efficient concentration and purification of REEs from the resulting pregnant leach solution (PLS). The disclosed technology is particularly applicable to domestic sources of REEs in the United States, such as the vast stockpiles of coal mining waste rock and coal preparation plant refuse found in the Central Appalachian region, thereby offering a strategic pathway to enhance national supply security for these critical materials essential to renewable energy, defense, and advanced manufacturing sectors.

## BACKGROUND

Rare earth elements (REEs), comprising the fifteen lanthanides along with scandium and yttrium, are indispensable components in a wide array of modern technologies, including permanent magnets for electric vehicles and wind turbines, phosphors for lighting and displays, catalysts for petroleum refining, and numerous defense applications. Despite their name, many REEs are relatively abundant in the Earth’s crust; however, they are rarely found in concentrated, economically exploitable deposits. For decades, global REE production has been dominated by China, which has created significant supply chain vulnerabilities for other nations, including the United States. In response, there has been a concerted effort to identify and develop alternative, domestic sources of REEs to mitigate the risk of market disruptions and ensure a secure supply for critical industries.

One promising avenue for domestic REE production lies in the utilization of secondary resources, particularly coal and its associated byproducts. The U.S. Department of Energy has identified coal seams, coal mining waste, and coal utilization byproducts (CUBs)—such as fly ash, bottom ash, and coal preparation plant refuse—as potential reservoirs of REEs. Among these, a specific geologic material known as “underclay” has emerged as a material of high interest. Underclay is a clay-rich sedimentary rock that forms the floor beneath a coal seam and is often excavated and stockpiled as waste during coal mining operations. In the Central Appalachian coal basin, for instance, there exist hundreds of millions of cubic meters of such refuse piles, which represent a vast, untapped resource. Geological studies have shown that these underclays can contain elevated concentrations of REEs, often ranging from 250 to over 350 parts per million (ppm), with a significant portion of these REEs existing in an “ion-adsorbed” or exchangeable form on the surfaces of clay minerals like illite, kaolinite, and halloysite. This form of REE is particularly amenable to extraction via hydrometallurgical methods, as it is not locked within the crystal lattice of refractory minerals like monazite or xenotime, which require aggressive and environmentally damaging acids for dissolution.

Traditional methods for recovering ion-adsorbed REEs, as practiced in southern China from weathered granitic clays, rely heavily on the use of large volumes of ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄) or sodium chloride (NaCl) solutions. While effective for displacing exchangeable cations, these inorganic salt lixiviants suffer from several drawbacks. They often exhibit low selectivity, leading to the co-extraction of a wide range of base metal cations (e.g., Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) that complicate downstream purification. Furthermore, the massive quantities of salt required generate enormous volumes of saline wastewater, posing significant environmental and disposal challenges. There is therefore a critical need for a more selective, efficient, and environmentally benign leaching agent.

Organic acids have long been recognized in soil science and geochemistry for their ability to complex with and mobilize metal ions. Citric acid, a tricarboxylic acid naturally produced by many plants and microorganisms, is of particular interest due to its multiple functional groups—three carboxyl groups and one hydroxyl group—that can act as ligands to form stable complexes with trivalent metal ions, including REEs. Previous research has suggested that citric acid can enhance the leaching of REEs from clays through a dual mechanism: (1) acting as a complexing agent that solubilizes REEs by forming stable aqueous complexes, thereby shifting the equilibrium away from the solid phase, and (2) interacting with the clay surface to alter its zeta potential, which can promote the dispersion of clay particles and increase the accessibility of adsorbed REEs. However, prior art has not provided a complete, optimized, and scalable system for the practical application of citric acid for REE recovery from complex, real-world geologic feedstocks like coal-associated underclays. A key challenge has been balancing the leaching power of the acid with the need to maintain a controlled pH to prevent excessive dissolution of the aluminosilicate clay matrix, which would release large amounts of gangue elements and undermine the process’s selectivity and economic viability. The present invention addresses these gaps by providing a precisely formulated, pH-buffered citrate system that maximizes REE recovery while minimizing the environmental footprint and downstream processing burden.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and highly effective method for the selective leaching and recovery of rare earth elements (REEs) from clay-rich geologic materials, particularly underclay associated with coal seams and other coal utilization byproducts. The core of the invention is a tailored leaching solution composed of a citrate buffer system, which comprises a mixture of citric acid and its conjugate base, sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate, at a total citrate concentration of approximately 0.1 mol/L. This solution is maintained at a mildly acidic pH, preferably within the range of 3 to 6, to optimize the complexation of REEs while suppressing the dissolution of the host clay matrix and the co-extraction of undesirable gangue elements.

In a preferred embodiment, the citrate leaching solution is further amended with a monovalent salt, such as sodium chloride (NaCl) or ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄), at a concentration sufficient to provide a source of competing cations for ion exchange. The addition of the monovalent salt serves a dual purpose: it facilitates the displacement of ion-exchangeable REEs from the negatively charged clay surfaces, and the electrolyte it provides helps to balance the surface charge of the clay particles, promoting their dispersion and thereby increasing the reactive surface area available for complexation with the citrate anions.

The method of the invention involves contacting the particulate clay-rich feedstock with the citrate-based leaching solution under controlled conditions of solid-to-liquid ratio, temperature (typically ambient), and contact time. The process is remarkably effective, capable of recovering up to 30% or more of the total REE content from certain underclay samples, which represents a significant improvement—often an order of magnitude greater—over conventional leaching with ammonium sulfate alone. Crucially, this high recovery is achieved with exceptional selectivity. The citrate leaching solution liberates a much lower concentration of base cations such as aluminum, silicon, and iron compared to strong mineral acids, resulting in a pregnant leach solution (PLS) with a high REE-to-gangue ratio. This selectivity also extends to radioactive elements; the leaching of thorium, a common contaminant in REE ores, is consistently kept below 3% of its total content in the feedstock, which greatly simplifies waste management and regulatory compliance.

The invention is versatile and can be applied in both batch and continuous flow-through modes. In a flow-through configuration, which is more representative of a potential in-situ or heap leaching operation, the method demonstrates that the highest concentrations of REEs are eluted in the initial 20-40 minutes of fluid flow following a “shut-in” period where the leaching solution is held in static contact with the feedstock. This observation suggests that the primary mechanism of extraction is the rapid desorption and complexation of surface-bound and colloidal REEs, rather than the slow dissolution of primary REE-bearing minerals. The shut-in time can be optimized to allow for better wetting and penetration of the solution into the micropores of the material, thereby enhancing overall extraction efficiency.

Furthermore, the invention contemplates the integration of the leaching step with a downstream sorbent capture process for the concentration and purification of REEs from the PLS. A novel amine-based solid polymer sorbent has been shown to effectively bind REEs from the citrate PLS while allowing the lixiviant and alkali/alkaline earth metals to pass through. The captured REEs can then be selectively eluted from the sorbent to produce a high-purity REE fraction. This integrated approach not only provides a complete recovery pathway but also opens the possibility of recycling the citrate lixiviant for multiple leaching cycles, which would significantly improve the process economics and sustainability.

In summary, the present invention offers a transformative technology for REE recovery that is characterized by its high efficiency, superior selectivity, environmental benignity, and applicability to a vast, domestic, and currently underutilized resource base of coal-related wastes. By providing a cost-effective and sustainable method to unlock the REE value from these materials, the invention directly addresses a critical national need for supply security of these essential elements.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for the selective leaching of rare earth elements (REEs) from clay-rich geologic materials using a specifically formulated organic acid-based solution. The method is predicated on the discovery that a buffered citrate system, optionally enhanced with a monovalent salt, can achieve a level of REE recovery and selectivity that far surpasses conventional inorganic salt lixiviants and is competitive with, yet significantly more environmentally friendly than, strong mineral acid leaching. The following detailed description provides a comprehensive account of the invention, including the composition of the leaching solution, the nature of the feedstock, the operational parameters of the leaching process, the underlying mechanisms of action, and the integration with downstream recovery technologies.

The feedstock for the method of the present invention is a clay-rich geologic material. In a preferred embodiment, this material is an underclay, a sedimentary rock that is found in direct contact with a coal seam, typically as the floor rock. Underclays from coal-bearing formations in the Central Appalachian region of the United States, such as the Lower Freeport, Middle Kittanning, and Pittsburgh No. 8 coal seams, are particularly suitable. These materials are often already excavated and stockpiled as waste from historical and current coal mining operations, making them a readily available and low-cost resource. The underclay is characterized by a high clay mineral content, typically exceeding 55% by weight of the bulk material. The predominant clay minerals are from the kaolin group (kaolinite and halloysite) and the mica group (illite). Non-clay mineral components may include quartz, calcite, and ilmenite. Critically, the REEs in these underclays are distributed across various phases: a significant fraction is present as ion-adsorbed or exchangeable cations on the surfaces of the clay minerals; another fraction may be associated with secondary minerals such as iron and manganese oxides or carbonates; and a residual fraction is bound within more recalcitrant primary or diagenetic phosphate minerals like rhabdophane, apatite, churchite, monazite, xenotime, and crandallite. The method of the present invention is specifically designed to target the first two, more labile fractions.

The heart of the invention is the leaching solution, which is an aqueous composition comprising a citrate buffer system. This system is formed by combining citric acid (C₆H₈O₇) and sodium citrate tribasic dihydrate (Na₃C₆H₅O₇·2H₂O) in water. The total concentration of citrate species (i.e., the sum of the concentrations of citric acid and all its dissociated anionic forms) in the solution is maintained at a level that is effective for complexation but not so high as to be economically wasteful. A preferred total citrate concentration is 0.1 mol/L (or 0.1 M). The pH of this solution is a critical parameter and is carefully controlled by the ratio of citric acid to sodium citrate. The invention operates optimally within a pH range of from about 3 to about 6. Within this mildly acidic window, the solution maintains its ability to protonate and displace exchangeable cations while the citrate anion is sufficiently deprotonated to act as an effective chelating agent for the trivalent REE ions. A particularly preferred pH is 5, as it has been found to offer an excellent balance between high REE recovery and minimal gangue dissolution.

In a key aspect of the invention, the citrate leaching solution is amended with a monovalent salt. The monovalent salt serves as a source of cations (e.g., Na⁺ or NH₄⁺) that can compete with the REE³⁺ ions for exchange sites on the negatively charged clay surfaces. This ion exchange process is a primary driver for the initial liberation of the exchangeable REE fraction. Suitable monovalent salts include sodium chloride (NaCl) and ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄). In a preferred embodiment, sodium chloride is used at a concentration of 0.5 mol/L. The presence of this electrolyte not only drives ion exchange but also appears to influence the physical state of the clay suspension. It is hypothesized that the added ionic strength helps to compress the electrical double layer around the clay particles, reducing inter-particle repulsion and potentially aiding in the dispersion of fine aggregates. This increased dispersion exposes more surface area for the citrate anions to access and complex with the REEs, thereby enhancing the overall leaching efficiency beyond what would be expected from simple ion exchange alone.

The leaching process itself can be conducted in a variety of configurations. In a laboratory or pilot-scale batch process, a predetermined mass of the feedstock, which has been comminuted to a particle size of less than 149 µm (100 mesh), is combined with the leaching solution in a vessel such as a polypropylene centrifuge tube. The solid-to-liquid (S:L) ratio is an important variable; ratios of 1% (e.g., 0.5 g of solids in 50 mL of solution) and 10% (e.g., 20 g of solids in 200 mL of solution) have both been demonstrated to be effective. The mixture is then agitated, for example, by rotation on a tube mixer, for a period of time sufficient to reach near-equilibrium. Contact times ranging from 4 to 24 hours at ambient temperature (approximately 20-25°C) have been found to be adequate. At the conclusion of the leaching period, the slurry is separated, typically by centrifugation, and the supernatant liquid, now the pregnant leach solution (PLS), is filtered through a 0.45-micron membrane to remove any fine particulates.

The efficacy of the method is demonstrated by its performance against standard benchmarks. When tested on a Middle Kittanning formation underclay (sample UC-02) containing approximately 353 mg/kg of total REEs, a conventional ammonium sulfate leach recovered only about 7% of the total REE content. In stark contrast, the citrate leaching solution of the present invention, specifically the formulation with 0.1 M total citrate, 0.5 M NaCl, and pH 5 (designated as solution RS-2), recovered nearly 33% of the total REE. This represents a nearly five-fold increase in recovery. Moreover, this superior recovery was achieved with remarkable selectivity. The PLS generated by the RS-2 solution had a REE-to-base cation (Al, Si, Fe) ratio of 2.5, whereas the PLS from a sequential acid digestion using strong mineral acids had a ratio of only 0.2-0.3. This high selectivity translates directly into lower costs and complexity for downstream purification. The method also shows a strong preference for the light and middle REEs (LREEs and MREEs), which are often the most commercially valuable.

### Experimental Studies

The experimental validation of the present invention involved a series of comprehensive studies on underclay samples obtained from the West Virginia Geological and Economic Survey. The samples, designated UC-01, UC-02, UC-03, and UC-06, were sourced from strata associated with the Lower Freeport, Middle Kittanning, and Pittsburgh No. 5 coal seams. A rigorous sample preparation protocol was followed to ensure homogeneity and reproducibility. Each sample was first crushed and then pulverized in a Zr-Ti lined shatter box to a grain size of less than 149 µm. The powder was homogenized using the cone and quarter technique and split into subsamples for various analyses, including X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS), and the leaching experiments themselves.

Mineralogical characterization by XRD confirmed that the samples were indeed clay-rich, with clay minerals constituting over 55% of the bulk. Illite and smectite were the dominant clay phases, with minor amounts of kaolinite and halloysite. The non-clay fraction was primarily quartz, with calcite being prominent in sample UC-06 due to late diagenetic cementation. SEM-EDS analysis was instrumental in identifying the specific REE-bearing mineral phases. All samples contained REE phosphates, including rhabdophane, apatite, churchite, monazite, xenotime, and crandallite. In sample UC-02, REEs were found associated with porous, spongy grains of a crandallite-like aluminum phosphate. In sample UC-04, trace REEs were detected in grain-coating clays associated with framboidal pyrite.

A sequential acid digestion procedure was employed as a baseline to understand the distribution of REEs among different operational fractions (exchangeable, acid-soluble, residual). This involved a stepwise treatment with (NH₄)₂SO₄ (to extract the exchangeable fraction), 1 M HCl (to extract carbonates and some oxides), and warm 1.2 M H₂SO₄ (to attack more resistant phases), with the final residue being fused for total elemental analysis. The results showed that for most samples, the majority of REEs (>65%) resided in the residual fraction, confirming their association with refractory minerals. However, samples UC-02 and UC-03 exhibited a higher proportion (25-35%) in the exchangeable and HCl-soluble fractions, making them prime candidates for the mild leaching approach of the present invention.

The core leaching experiments compared the performance of the novel citrate solutions against the standard (NH₄)₂SO₄ leach. A suite of citrate solutions was tested, all at a total citrate concentration of 0.1 M but varying in pH (3, 4, 5, 6) and with or without the addition of 0.5 M NaCl. The results were unequivocal: the citrate solutions, particularly RS-2 (pH 5, with NaCl), consistently outperformed the ammonium sulfate control across all tested underclay samples. For samples with low exchangeable REE content (<1%), the citrate leach recovered an order of magnitude more REE than the control. The pH study revealed a crucial insight: while the concentration of REEs leached remained relatively constant across the pH 3-6 range, the concentration of gangue elements like Al, Si, and Fe decreased significantly as pH increased. This demonstrates the tunable selectivity of the citrate system; by operating at a higher pH within the optimal range, one can maintain high REE recovery while drastically reducing the impurity load in the PLS.

Flow-through column experiments were conducted to simulate a more industrially relevant scenario, using both powdered samples and fractured core pieces. These experiments introduced the concept of a "shut-in" time—the period during which the leaching solution is held in static contact with the feedstock before flow is initiated. The data showed that longer shut-in times (up to 5 days) led to higher peak REE concentrations in the effluent, indicating that the static contact period allows for more complete wetting and reaction within the pore structure of the material. However, the actual extraction efficiency in these flow-through tests was low (<1% of total REE), likely due to poor fluid transmissivity and limited contact in the intact core material. This highlights a key area for future process development: optimizing the physical form of the feedstock (e.g., through crushing or pelletizing) and the hydrodynamics of the leaching system to bridge the gap between the high efficiencies seen in well-mixed batch tests and the practicalities of a continuous flow operation.

Finally, a proof-of-concept study for downstream processing was performed. A PLS generated by leaching coal preparation plant fines with the RS-2 solution was passed through a column packed with a novel amine-based solid polymer sorbent. The sorbent demonstrated a high affinity for REEs, achieving an uptake of 60-70% for elements up to holmium, with a bias toward LREEs and MREEs. The overall recovery for La to Dy was an impressive 80-100%. This successful demonstration confirms that the citrate PLS is compatible with advanced separation technologies and paves the way for a fully integrated and closed-loop REE recovery process, where the lixiviant can potentially be regenerated and recycled. These experimental studies collectively validate the novelty, efficacy, and practical potential of the citrate-based leaching method disclosed herein.
