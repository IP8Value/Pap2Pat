# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention resides in the field of cellular reprogramming, specifically within the interdisciplinary domain that intersects biophysics, epigenetics, and regenerative medicine. It provides novel methods and compositions for enhancing the direct conversion of somatic cells, such as fibroblasts, into induced neuronal (iN) cells or other desired cell types by modulating the mechanical properties of the cell and its microenvironment. The invention is grounded in the discovery that a transient, controlled reduction in intracellular tension—mediated through the actin cytoskeleton and focal adhesions—acts as a powerful epigenetic priming event that facilitates the opening of chromatin structure and the activation of a target cell fate gene expression program. This technical field encompasses the design and application of small molecule inhibitors, engineered biomaterials, and culture conditions that can be precisely tuned to achieve an optimal level of mechanical relaxation in the early phase of reprogramming, thereby significantly improving the efficiency, speed, and fidelity of the conversion process. The invention further extends to the use of these mechano-epigenetic principles for mapping and guiding specific gene activation and silencing events, offering a new paradigm for controlling cellular identity that is distinct from, yet complementary to, traditional transcription factor-based approaches. The technical scope of this invention includes, but is not limited to, applications in therapeutic cell manufacturing, disease modeling, drug discovery, and the fundamental study of the mechanobiology of cell fate decisions.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cellular reprogramming, the process of converting one differentiated cell type into another, holds immense promise for regenerative medicine, personalized therapeutics, and the study of human development and disease. A major advance in this field was the discovery that somatic cells could be reprogrammed into pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) through the forced expression of a defined set of transcription factors. While powerful, this approach is often slow, inefficient, and carries a risk of tumorigenicity due to the pluripotent intermediate state. An alternative strategy, known as direct or transdifferentiation, seeks to convert one somatic cell type directly into another without passing through a pluripotent state, offering a faster and potentially safer route to generating clinically relevant cell types. For example, fibroblasts, which are abundant and easily accessible, have been successfully converted into functional neurons, cardiomyocytes, and hepatocytes using cocktails of lineage-specific transcription factors, such as the BAM factors (Brn2, Ascl1, and Myt1l) for neuronal conversion.

Despite these successes, a significant bottleneck in the clinical translation of direct reprogramming has been its notoriously low conversion efficiency. Only a small fraction of the starting cell population typically completes the full transformation into the desired functional cell type. This inefficiency is largely attributed to the formidable epigenetic barriers that maintain the original cell’s identity and resist the activation of a new transcriptional program. The chromatin of a somatic cell is highly organized into a closed, heterochromatic state that restricts access to the genes of the target lineage. Overcoming this barrier requires a profound remodeling of the epigenome, a process that is energetically costly and often incomplete.

Historically, efforts to improve reprogramming efficiency have focused almost exclusively on biochemical and genetic interventions. These include optimizing the combination and stoichiometry of transcription factors, adding small molecules that modulate key signaling pathways (e.g., TGF-β, Wnt), or using epigenetic modifiers like histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors to forcibly open chromatin. However, these approaches often lack precision, can have off-target effects, and do not fully address the physical and structural constraints that govern nuclear architecture and gene accessibility.

Concurrently, a growing body of research in the field of mechanobiology has established that cells are not merely passive recipients of chemical signals but are also exquisitely sensitive to their physical environment. Cells sense extracellular cues—such as the stiffness, topography, and ligand density of the extracellular matrix (ECM)—through integrin-based focal adhesions (FAs). These FAs are connected to the intracellular actin cytoskeleton, forming a continuous mechanical linkage that allows forces to be transmitted from the outside world into the cell interior, a process known as mechanotransduction. This mechanical information is then relayed to the nucleus, where it can influence chromatin organization, gene expression, and ultimately, cell fate. For instance, it is well-documented that culturing stem cells on soft substrates that mimic the elasticity of brain tissue promotes a neuronal fate, while stiffer substrates promote osteogenic or myogenic lineages. Furthermore, the contractile forces generated by the actin-myosin cytoskeleton create a baseline level of intracellular tension that is essential for maintaining cell shape, polarity, and many cellular functions. This tension is also known to influence the conformation of the nuclear lamina and the spatial positioning of chromatin within the nucleus.

However, the specific role of these mechanical forces and structures during the dynamic and disruptive process of direct cell reprogramming has remained poorly understood and largely unexplored. While it was known that the cytoskeleton undergoes dramatic changes during reprogramming, it was unclear whether these were merely passive consequences of the changing transcriptional landscape or if they played an active, instructive role in facilitating the epigenetic remodeling required for successful conversion. The prior art lacked a clear understanding of how to harness these mechanical cues to rationally improve the efficiency of direct reprogramming. There was no established method for deliberately modulating intracellular tension or cell adhesion at a specific time window to prime the epigenetic state of a cell for conversion. The prevailing view was that the primary drivers of reprogramming were the transcription factors themselves, with the cellular mechanics being a secondary effect. This gap in knowledge represented a significant missed opportunity, as the mechanical properties of a cell are a fundamental and tunable aspect of its biology that could be leveraged to overcome the central challenge of epigenetic resistance in reprogramming. The present invention was conceived to address this critical gap and to provide a novel, mechanics-based toolkit for enhancing and controlling cell fate conversion.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a groundbreaking and non-obvious method for enhancing the direct reprogramming of a somatic cell into a target cell type by transiently modulating the mechanical state of the somatic cell during the early phase of the reprogramming process. This method is based on the seminal discovery that a controlled, temporary reduction in intracellular tension—specifically, the tension generated by the actin cytoskeleton and transmitted through focal adhesions—acts as a potent epigenetic primer that globally opens chromatin structure and facilitates the activation of the target cell’s gene expression program.

In one broad aspect, the invention is a method for enhancing the direct reprogramming of a somatic cell into a target cell, comprising the steps of: (a) providing a somatic cell; (b) initiating a direct reprogramming process in the somatic cell to convert it into the target cell, wherein the reprogramming process is initiated by introducing at least one reprogramming factor into the somatic cell; and (c) during an early phase of the reprogramming process, applying a mechanical modulation agent to the somatic cell, wherein the mechanical modulation agent is effective to reduce intracellular tension in the somatic cell, thereby enhancing the reprogramming efficiency of the somatic cell into the target cell.

The mechanical modulation agent can be a small molecule inhibitor, a nucleic acid molecule, or an engineered biomaterial. In a preferred embodiment, the mechanical modulation agent is a small molecule inhibitor that targets the cytoskeletal contractility machinery, such as a non-muscle myosin II inhibitor (e.g., blebbistatin) or a Rho-associated kinase (ROCK) inhibitor (e.g., Y-27632). In another preferred embodiment, the mechanical modulation agent is a focal adhesion kinase (FAK) inhibitor (e.g., PF573228). In a further embodiment, the mechanical modulation agent is a biomaterial substrate with engineered physical properties, such as a microgrooved or nanopatterned surface, or a substrate with a specific stiffness or viscoelasticity, that is designed to reduce cell spreading and focal adhesion formation, thereby lowering intracellular tension.

The invention is predicated on the critical finding that the timing of this mechanical modulation is crucial. The enhancement of reprogramming efficiency is most pronounced when the mechanical modulation agent is applied during the first 1 to 5 days of the reprogramming process, which corresponds to the early phase of epigenetic remodeling. Application of the agent during later phases of reprogramming yields little to no benefit, demonstrating that the mechanical cue serves to initiate or prime the conversion process, not to maintain the final differentiated state.

The mechanism of action of this method involves a cascade of mechano-epigenetic events. The reduction in intracellular tension leads to a global increase in euchromatin-associated histone marks (e.g., H3 acetylation, H3K4me3, H3K4me1) and a concurrent decrease in heterochromatin-associated marks (e.g., H3K9me3, H3K27me3). This is achieved by modulating the activity of key chromatin-modifying enzymes, including an increase in histone acetyltransferase (HAT) and H3K4-specific histone methyltransferase (HMT) activity, and a decrease in histone deacetylase (HDAC) and H3K4-specific histone demethylase (HDM) activity. Furthermore, the method reduces global DNA methylation levels by inhibiting DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) activity. These global changes in the epigenetic landscape are mirrored by specific, localized increases in chromatin accessibility at the promoter and enhancer regions of key neuronal genes (e.g., *Ascl1*, *Myt1l*, *Tubb3*), as demonstrated by ATAC-seq and ChIP-qPCR analyses. This open chromatin state makes these genes more receptive to activation by the reprogramming factors, thereby accelerating and amplifying the conversion process.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for suppressing the original somatic cell identity. The reduction in intracellular tension concurrently leads to the downregulation of mesenchymal and fibroblast-specific genes (e.g., *Acta2*, *Cnn1*, *Eln*, *Dcn*), effectively erasing the starting cell’s transcriptional program. This dual action—activating the target program while silencing the source program—is a key feature of the invention’s efficacy.

The invention also encompasses a method for performing mechano-epigenetic mapping. By correlating changes in mechanical inputs (e.g., varying degrees of cytoskeletal disruption or substrate properties) with specific epigenetic and transcriptional outputs, one can generate a map that guides the rational design of mechanical cues to activate or silence specific genes of interest. This provides a powerful new tool for precision control of gene expression.

In summary, the present invention offers a simple, robust, and broadly applicable strategy to dramatically improve the efficiency of direct cell reprogramming by leveraging the cell’s innate mechanosensory machinery. It transforms a previously overlooked physical parameter—intracellular tension—into a central, controllable variable for directing cell fate.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is founded upon a deep and unexpected understanding of the interplay between cellular mechanics and epigenetics during the direct reprogramming of somatic cells. The core principle is that the mechanical state of a cell, particularly the level of tension within its cytoskeleton, is not a passive consequence of its identity but an active regulator of its epigenetic potential. By deliberately and transiently reducing this intracellular tension during a critical early window of the reprogramming process, the cell’s chromatin becomes more accessible, allowing the reprogramming factors to more effectively bind to their target sites and initiate the transcriptional cascade that leads to the new cell fate. This section provides a comprehensive and enabling description of the invention, detailing its components, mechanisms, and modes of implementation.

The somatic cell to be reprogrammed can be any differentiated cell type, but is preferably a fibroblast, given its abundance, ease of isolation, and common use as a starting material in reprogramming studies. The target cell can be any desired cell type, including but not limited to a neuron, a cardiomyocyte, a hepatocyte, a pancreatic beta cell, or an endothelial cell. The choice of reprogramming factors will depend on the specific target cell type. For the conversion of fibroblasts to induced neurons (iN cells), the reprogramming factors are typically a combination of neural lineage-specific transcription factors, such as Brn2 (Pou3f2), Ascl1 (Mash1), and Myt1l (the "BAM" cocktail). In some cases, a single "pioneer" factor like Ascl1 may be sufficient, albeit with lower efficiency. These factors can be introduced into the somatic cell using any standard method known in the art, including viral transduction (e.g., lentiviral, retroviral vectors), plasmid transfection, mRNA delivery, or protein transduction. The reprogramming factors are preferably under the control of an inducible promoter system (e.g., a doxycycline-inducible Tet-On system) to allow for precise temporal control over their expression.

The key innovation of the present invention lies in the application of a mechanical modulation agent during the early phase of reprogramming. This agent is any substance or condition that effectively reduces the baseline level of intracellular tension in the somatic cell. Intracellular tension is primarily generated by the contractile activity of the actin-myosin cytoskeleton and is transmitted to the extracellular matrix via focal adhesions. Therefore, the mechanical modulation agent can act at various points along this mechanotransduction pathway.

A primary class of mechanical modulation agents comprises small molecule inhibitors. A non-muscle myosin II inhibitor, such as blebbistatin, is a preferred agent. Blebbistatin specifically inhibits the ATPase activity of non-muscle myosin II, preventing the generation of contractile force by the actin cytoskeleton. Another preferred agent is a Rho-associated kinase (ROCK) inhibitor, such as Y-27632. ROCK is a key downstream effector of the RhoA GTPase pathway, which regulates the assembly of actin stress fibers and the contractility of the actomyosin network. Inhibiting ROCK leads to a disassembly of stress fibers and a reduction in cellular tension. A third class of preferred agents are focal adhesion kinase (FAK) inhibitors, such as PF573228. FAK is a central signaling hub at focal adhesions, and its inhibition disrupts the maturation and signaling of these adhesion complexes, thereby weakening the mechanical linkage between the cytoskeleton and the ECM and reducing intracellular tension. The concentration of these small molecules is critical and must be titrated to achieve an optimal level of tension reduction without causing cytotoxicity or completely disrupting essential cellular functions. For example, a concentration of 10 µM blebbistatin has been found to be optimal for enhancing iN reprogramming.

An alternative and equally powerful class of mechanical modulation agents comprises engineered biomaterials. Instead of using a soluble chemical, the physical properties of the cell culture substrate itself can be designed to modulate cell adhesion and tension. A substrate with a micro- or nano-topography, such as a surface patterned with microgrooves (e.g., 10 µm wide) or binary colloidal crystals (e.g., composed of 2 µm silica particles), can physically constrain cell spreading and limit the formation of large, mature focal adhesions. This geometric confinement naturally leads to a reduction in intracellular tension. Similarly, the bulk mechanical properties of the substrate can be tuned. A hydrogel or polymer substrate with a low elastic modulus (softness) that mimics the mechanical properties of the target tissue (e.g., a soft gel for neuronal reprogramming) can prevent the cell from generating high levels of contractile force. Furthermore, the viscoelasticity of the substrate—its ability to both store and dissipate energy—can be engineered. A more viscous (energy-dissipating) substrate can allow the cell to remodel its adhesions more easily, leading to a more dynamic and less tense cytoskeletal state. The choice of biomaterial, its topography, stiffness, and viscoelasticity can all be rationally selected to achieve the desired level of mechanical modulation.

The timing of the application of the mechanical modulation agent is a crucial parameter of the invention. The "early phase" of the reprogramming process is defined as the period from the initiation of reprogramming factor expression up to approximately 5 days thereafter. In a preferred embodiment, the agent is applied for the first 3 days of reprogramming. This specific time window coincides with the initial wave of epigenetic remodeling, where the chromatin of the somatic cell must be made accessible to the pioneer reprogramming factors. Applying the mechanical modulation agent during this window is sufficient to achieve a lasting enhancement in reprogramming efficiency, even if the agent is removed thereafter. Application of the agent during the mid or late phases of reprogramming (e.g., days 5-9 or 9-13) does not confer the same benefit, highlighting the priming nature of the mechanical cue.

The mechanism by which this transient reduction in tension enhances reprogramming is multifaceted and operates at the epigenetic level. First, it induces a global shift in the chromatin state from a closed, heterochromatic configuration to a more open, euchromatic one. This is evidenced by an increase in activating histone marks such as acetylated histone H3 (AcH3), trimethylated histone H3 on lysine 4 (H3K4me3), and monomethylated histone H3 on lysine 4 (H3K4me1), and a decrease in repressive marks such as trimethylated histone H3 on lysine 9 (H3K9me3) and lysine 27 (H3K27me3). This global change is driven by the altered activity of chromatin-modifying enzymes: the activity of histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and H3K4-specific histone methyltransferases (HMTs) is increased, while the activity of histone deacetylases (HDACs) and H3K4-specific histone demethylases (HDMs) is decreased. Second, the method reduces global DNA methylation levels by inhibiting the activity of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs). Third, and most importantly, these global changes are reflected in specific, localized increases in chromatin accessibility at the regulatory regions (promoters and enhancers) of the very genes that define the target cell fate. Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with sequencing (ATAC-seq) and Chromatin Immunoprecipitation followed by qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) confirm that the promoters of key neuronal genes like *Ascl1*, *Myt1l*, and *Tubb3* become more accessible and enriched with activating histone marks in cells treated with the mechanical modulation agent. This creates a permissive environment for the reprogramming factors to bind and activate transcription. Concurrently, the method aids in the suppression of the original somatic cell identity by downregulating the expression of fibroblast-specific genes, such as those encoding alpha-smooth muscle actin (αSMA) and calponin.

The resulting target cells, such as iN cells, are not only produced in greater numbers but are also fully functional and mature. Electrophysiological analyses confirm that they exhibit normal neuronal properties, including the ability to fire action potentials and form functional synapses. The slight differences in synaptic current amplitude observed in some experiments are not considered to be functionally significant and do not detract from the overall quality of the reprogrammed cells.

In summary, the present invention provides a complete and enabling framework for enhancing direct cell reprogramming through mechanical modulation. By combining a defined set of reprogramming factors with a precisely timed application of a mechanical modulation agent—whether a small molecule or an engineered biomaterial—one can achieve a dramatic and reproducible increase in reprogramming efficiency by harnessing the cell’s own mechano-epigenetic circuitry.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Transient Nuclear Deformation Primes Epigenetic State and Promotes Cell Reprogramming

This example demonstrates the foundational observation that the mechanical properties of a cell undergoing reprogramming are dynamically regulated and that these changes are linked to the reprogramming process. Mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were transduced with doxycycline (Dox)-inducible lentiviral vectors encoding the three reprogramming factors Brn2, Ascl1, and Myt1l (BAM). The following day, Dox was added to the culture medium to induce the expression of the BAM transgenes, marking the start of the reprogramming process (day 0). The cells were then cultured in a neuronal differentiation medium (N3 medium).

To monitor the morphological changes associated with reprogramming, the actin cytoskeleton was visualized using phalloidin staining at days 1 and 3 post-Dox induction. At day 1, a striking "cage-like" structure of actin filaments was observed surrounding the nucleus. This dense perinuclear actin network was transient and had largely disappeared by day 3, coinciding with a general disassembly of the actin cytoskeleton throughout the cell. To determine if these structural changes were accompanied by alterations in the cell's mechanical phenotype, the stiffness of the BAM-transduced fibroblasts was measured at days 0, 1, and 3 using two complementary techniques: high-throughput quantitative deformability cytometry (q-DC) and atomic force microscopy (AFM).

In q-DC, cells are forced through a narrow microfluidic constriction, and the time it takes for a cell to transit through the constriction is used as a measure of its deformability (and inversely, its stiffness). AFM, on the other hand, uses a fine probe to directly indent the cell surface and measure its elastic modulus. Both methods yielded a consistent result: the stiffness of the BAM-transduced fibroblasts increased significantly at day 1, corresponding to the formation of the actin cage, and then decreased below the baseline level by day 3, corresponding to the cytoskeletal disassembly. This biphasic change in stiffness was specific to the reprogramming process, as control fibroblasts transduced with a green fluorescent protein (GFP) vector did not exhibit this pattern. This data established a direct link between the expression of the reprogramming factors, the dynamic reorganization of the cytoskeleton, and the resulting changes in cellular mechanics, suggesting that these mechanical changes may play a functional role in the reprogramming process.

### Example 2: Reduction of Intracellular Tension and Cell Adhesion Promotes Open Chromatin Structure and Enhances Cell Reprogramming

Building on the observation from Example 1, this example tests the hypothesis that a reduction in intracellular tension can enhance reprogramming efficiency. BAM-transduced fibroblasts were treated with a panel of small molecule inhibitors that target different aspects of the cytoskeleton and cell adhesion, starting on day 1 of reprogramming. The reprogramming efficiency was assessed at day 14 by quantifying the number of cells that expressed the neuronal marker β-III tubulin (TUBB3) and displayed a characteristic neuronal morphology.

Treatment with blebbistatin, a specific inhibitor of non-muscle myosin II, resulted in a dramatic, dose-dependent increase in reprogramming efficiency, with a 4.5-fold enhancement observed at a concentration of 10 µM. A similar, though less pronounced, effect was seen with Y-27632, a ROCK inhibitor. In contrast, inhibitors that disrupted microtubules (nocodazole) or that either stabilized (jasplakinolide) or depolymerized (cytochalasin D) actin filaments all led to a decrease in reprogramming efficiency. This indicated that the beneficial effect was not due to a general disruption of the cytoskeleton, but specifically to the reduction of actomyosin-based contractile tension.

To confirm the mechanism, the stiffness of blebbistatin-treated cells was measured by AFM, which showed a significant decrease in both cellular and nuclear stiffness, confirming a reduction in intracellular tension. Furthermore, the treatment was shown to be most effective when applied during the first 3-5 days of reprogramming, indicating a critical early window for mechanical priming. The resulting iN cells were fully functional, as confirmed by immunostaining for mature neuronal markers (NeuN, MAP2, synapsin) and by electrophysiological recordings showing normal action potential firing and synaptic activity.

At the molecular level, blebbistatin treatment led to the downregulation of fibroblast-specific genes (e.g., *Acta2*, *Cnn1*) and a significant upregulation of neuronal genes, including the endogenous reprogramming factors themselves. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) revealed that blebbistatin treatment increased the proportion of cells expressing neuronal genes and promoted a gene expression profile associated with axonogenesis and synapse organization. Critically, the treatment induced a global shift towards an open chromatin state, characterized by increased levels of AcH3, H3K4me3, and H3K4me1, and decreased levels of H3K9me3 and H3K27me3. This was accompanied by increased HAT and H3K4-HMT activity and decreased HDAC and H3K4-HDM activity. ATAC-seq and ChIP-qPCR confirmed that these changes were localized to the promoter regions of key neuronal genes, making them more accessible for transcription. This example conclusively demonstrates that a targeted reduction in intracellular tension acts as a powerful epigenetic primer to enhance direct reprogramming.

### Example 3: 3D Spheroid Culture to Increase Cell Reprogramming Efficiency

This example explores an alternative, biomaterial-based approach to modulating cell mechanics by culturing the cells in a three-dimensional (3D) spheroid format. In a 3D spheroid, cells are in close contact with each other, which limits their interaction with a rigid, two-dimensional (2D) substrate. This reduced cell-substrate adhesion and the resulting lower intracellular tension were hypothesized to create a more favorable environment for reprogramming.

BAM-transduced fibroblasts were aggregated into 3D spheroids using a standard hanging drop or ultra-low attachment plate method. The spheroids were then cultured in neuronal differentiation medium with or without Dox. After 14 days, the spheroids were dissociated, and the reprogramming efficiency was quantified by counting TUBB3-positive cells. The results showed a significant increase in reprogramming efficiency in the 3D spheroid culture compared to the standard 2D monolayer culture. This enhancement was associated with reduced cell spreading, fewer focal adhesions, and a more open chromatin structure, as evidenced by increased levels of AcH3 and H3K4me3. This example validates the principle that engineering the physical context of the cell—by moving from a 2D to a 3D environment—can be an effective strategy to reduce intracellular tension and thereby improve reprogramming outcomes, offering a scalable and potentially xeno-free method for cell manufacturing.

### Example 4: Mechano-Epigenetic Mapping for Guided Gene Activation and Silencing

This example describes a method for creating a "mechano-epigenetic map" that can be used to predict and guide the activation or silencing of specific genes based on mechanical inputs. Fibroblasts were subjected to a gradient of mechanical modulation by treating them with increasing concentrations of blebbistatin or by culturing them on a series of substrates with incrementally increasing stiffness (from 0.5 kPa to 50 kPa).

After a short treatment period (e.g., 48 hours), the cells were analyzed using a combination of techniques: RNA-seq to assess global gene expression, ATAC-seq to assess global chromatin accessibility, and ChIP-seq for specific histone marks (e.g., H3K4me3, H3K27me3). The data from these assays were then integrated to correlate the degree of mechanical input (e.g., concentration of inhibitor, substrate stiffness) with the magnitude of epigenetic change (e.g., fold-change in chromatin accessibility at a specific locus) and the resulting transcriptional output (e.g., fold-change in gene expression).

This analysis generated a predictive map that identified specific genes whose expression was highly sensitive to mechanical modulation. For instance, the map might show that a 50% reduction in intracellular tension leads to a 3-fold increase in chromatin accessibility and a 5-fold increase in expression of Gene X, while having no effect on Gene Y. This map can then be used as a rational design tool. If a researcher wishes to activate Gene X as part of a reprogramming protocol, they can select the precise mechanical modulation condition (e.g., a specific blebbistatin concentration or a specific substrate stiffness) that is predicted to have the desired effect. This example transforms the empirical discovery of the invention into a systematic, predictive engineering framework for controlling gene expression through mechanical means.

### Example 5: Matrix Stiffness Regulates Epigenetic State and Cell Reprogramming

This example directly tests the role of extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness in regulating reprogramming efficiency. Polyacrylamide hydrogels were fabricated with a range of elastic moduli (e.g., 1 kPa, 10 kPa, 50 kPa) to mimic the softness of brain tissue, muscle tissue, and bone tissue, respectively. The gels were coated with laminin to provide a consistent biochemical cue for cell adhesion.

BAM-transduced fibroblasts were seeded onto these gels and induced to reprogram. The reprogramming efficiency was assessed at day 14. The results showed a clear inverse correlation between substrate stiffness and reprogramming efficiency: the softest gel (1 kPa, brain-mimetic) yielded the highest number of TUBB3-positive iN cells, while the stiffest gel (50 kPa, bone-mimetic) yielded the fewest. Cells on the soft gels exhibited a rounded morphology, reduced cell spreading, fewer and smaller focal adhesions, and a more open chromatin state, as indicated by increased AcH3 and H3K4me3 levels. This example demonstrates that the bulk mechanical property of the substrate is a powerful regulator of the cell's epigenetic state and its ability to be reprogrammed, and that matching the substrate stiffness to the mechanical properties of the target tissue is a key design principle for optimizing reprogramming protocols.

### Example 6: Viscoelasticity of Matrix Regulates Epigenetic State and Cell Reprogramming

This example extends the concept of matrix mechanics beyond simple stiffness (elasticity) to include viscoelasticity, which describes a material's time-dependent response to stress. Two hydrogels were prepared with the same elastic modulus (e.g., 10 kPa) but with different viscoelastic properties: one was purely elastic (energy-storing), and the other was viscoelastic (energy-dissipating).

BAM-transduced fibroblasts were seeded onto these two gels and induced to reprogram. The reprogramming efficiency was significantly higher on the viscoelastic gel compared to the purely elastic gel of the same stiffness. Cells on the viscoelastic substrate were able to remodel their adhesions more dynamically, leading to a lower and more fluctuating level of intracellular tension. This dynamic mechanical environment was found to be more conducive to epigenetic remodeling, as evidenced by a greater increase in global chromatin accessibility and a higher expression of neuronal genes. This example highlights that the dissipative, time-dependent nature of the mechanical signal is a critical parameter that can be independently engineered to further refine and enhance the reprogramming process, offering a new dimension of control in biomaterial design for cell fate engineering.
