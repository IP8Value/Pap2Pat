# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of immunopharmacology and, more specifically, to the therapeutic use of naltrexone or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof as a selective antagonist of intracellular toll-like receptors (TLRs), particularly TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9. The invention further encompasses methods for treating diseases characterized by aberrant activation of these TLRs—including but not limited to autoimmune disorders, chronic inflammatory conditions, and certain cancers—by administering low-dose naltrexone (LDN) to modulate innate immune responses without inducing cytotoxicity or compromising cell viability. The disclosed invention provides a novel mechanism of action for naltrexone that is distinct from its well-established role as an opioid receptor antagonist, thereby expanding its clinical utility beyond substance use disorders into the realm of immune-mediated pathologies. This innovation capitalizes on the unexpected discovery that naltrexone selectively inhibits cytokine production downstream of TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 signaling in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), while leaving TLR4- and interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R)-mediated responses unaffected. The invention thus establishes a new pharmacological paradigm wherein a repurposed small molecule exerts targeted immunomodulatory effects through specific interference with endosomal TLR pathways, offering a safe, orally bioavailable, and cost-effective therapeutic strategy for conditions driven by dysregulated nucleic acid sensing.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) constitute a critical family of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that serve as sentinels of the innate immune system, detecting conserved microbial structures known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) as well as endogenous danger signals termed damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). In humans, ten functional TLRs have been identified, each exhibiting distinct ligand specificity, subcellular localization, and expression profiles across immune cell subsets. TLR1, TLR2, TLR4, TLR5, TLR6, and TLR10 are primarily expressed on the plasma membrane and recognize extracellular components such as bacterial lipoproteins, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and flagellin. In contrast, TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 reside within endosomal compartments and are specialized for the detection of viral and bacterial nucleic acids, including double-stranded RNA, single-stranded RNA, and unmethylated CpG DNA motifs, respectively. Upon ligand engagement, TLRs initiate intracellular signaling cascades that culminate in the activation of transcription factors such as nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and interferon regulatory factors (IRFs), leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, type I interferons, and co-stimulatory molecules essential for mounting effective immune responses.

However, the very mechanisms that enable TLRs to defend against infection can also contribute to pathological inflammation when dysregulated. Aberrant TLR activation by self-nucleic acids—often complexed with autoantibodies or antimicrobial peptides—has been implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous autoimmune and chronic inflammatory diseases. For instance, in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), immune complexes containing self-DNA or RNA activate TLR7 and TLR9 in plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), resulting in excessive type I interferon production and loss of self-tolerance. Similarly, in psoriasis, self-DNA bound to the antimicrobial peptide LL-37 activates TLR9 in pDCs, driving the release of interferon-α and subsequent T-cell activation. Elevated TLR expression and signaling have also been documented in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis, and multiple sclerosis, where they perpetuate tissue damage through sustained cytokine production and leukocyte infiltration.

Beyond autoimmunity, TLRs play a dual role in oncology. While acute TLR stimulation can enhance antitumor immunity by activating dendritic cells and promoting T-cell priming, chronic or constitutive TLR signaling within the tumor microenvironment often fosters immune evasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Many cancer cells aberrantly express TLRs—particularly TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9—and their activation by endogenous ligands or microbial products can promote proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines. For example, TLR9 expression in melanoma, prostate cancer, and pancreatic carcinoma correlates with advanced disease stage and poor prognosis, suggesting that TLR9-driven inflammation supports tumor progression.

Given these pathogenic roles, TLRs—especially the endosomal members TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9—have emerged as attractive therapeutic targets. Several synthetic antagonists have been developed, including oligonucleotide-based inhibitors of TLR9 (e.g., IRS 954) and small-molecule inhibitors of TLR7/8 (e.g., hydroxychloroquine, though non-specific). However, many of these agents suffer from limited bioavailability, off-target effects, or insufficient clinical efficacy. Moreover, broad-spectrum TLR inhibition risks immunosuppression and increased susceptibility to infections. Thus, there remains a pressing need for safe, selective, and clinically tractable TLR modulators.

Naltrexone hydrochloride, a well-characterized opioid receptor antagonist approved for the treatment of opioid and alcohol dependence, has recently garnered interest for its potential immunomodulatory properties at low doses (typically 1–5 mg/day, referred to as low-dose naltrexone or LDN). Clinical and anecdotal reports suggest therapeutic benefits of LDN in diverse conditions such as Crohn’s disease, multiple sclerosis, fibromyalgia, and certain cancers, including B-cell lymphoma and pancreatic adenocarcinoma. Despite these observations, the molecular mechanisms underlying LDN’s anti-inflammatory effects have remained elusive. Early hypotheses centered on transient opioid receptor blockade leading to compensatory upregulation of endogenous opioids and opioid growth factor receptor (OGFr) signaling, which may inhibit cell proliferation. More recently, naltrexone and its stereoisomer naloxone were shown to inhibit TLR4 signaling in microglial cells by binding to the MD-2 co-receptor, thereby suppressing neuroinflammation and neuropathic pain in preclinical models.

Nevertheless, the extent to which naltrexone modulates other TLR family members—particularly the endosomal TLRs implicated in autoimmunity and cancer—has not been systematically investigated in primary human immune cells. Furthermore, it was unknown whether naltrexone’s effects are restricted to TLR4 or extend to TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9, which signal exclusively through the MyD88 adaptor protein but lack association with MD-2. Crucially, no prior art has demonstrated that naltrexone selectively inhibits cytokine production induced by TLR7/8 and TLR9 ligands while sparing responses mediated by TLR4 or the interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R)—another MyD88-dependent pathway—thereby revealing a previously unrecognized specificity in its immunomodulatory profile. Additionally, the absence of cytotoxic or pro-apoptotic effects at therapeutically relevant concentrations distinguishes naltrexone from conventional immunosuppressants and underscores its potential as a well-tolerated chronic therapy.

The present invention addresses these gaps by providing the first comprehensive evidence that naltrexone functions as a selective antagonist of intracellular TLRs (TLR7, TLR8, TLR9) in human PBMCs, with mechanistic and functional implications for the treatment of TLR-driven diseases. This discovery redefines naltrexone not merely as an opioid antagonist but as a precision immunomodulator with a unique target spectrum, enabling its rational repurposing for conditions where endosomal TLR hyperactivity is central to pathogenesis.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for treating a subject suffering from a disease or condition mediated by aberrant activation of toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7), toll-like receptor 8 (TLR8), or toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of naltrexone or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof. The invention is predicated on the unexpected discovery that naltrexone selectively inhibits the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines—specifically interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα)—in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) following stimulation with ligands for TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9, while exhibiting no inhibitory effect on cytokine production induced by ligands for TLR4 or the interleukin-1 receptor (IL-1R). This selective antagonism occurs without compromising cell viability or inducing apoptosis, thereby distinguishing naltrexone from cytotoxic immunosuppressants and supporting its use as a chronic therapy.

In one aspect, the invention encompasses the use of naltrexone for the manufacture of a medicament for treating autoimmune disorders, chronic inflammatory diseases, or cancers wherein TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 signaling contributes to pathogenesis. Exemplary conditions include, but are not limited to, systemic lupus erythematosus, psoriasis, Sjögren’s syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis), multiple sclerosis, fibromyalgia, melanoma, prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer, and B-cell lymphomas. The method involves administering naltrexone at a low dose, typically ranging from about 0.5 mg to about 10 mg per day, and more preferably from about 1 mg to about 5 mg per day, to achieve immunomodulatory effects without significant opioid receptor blockade.

In another aspect, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising naltrexone or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient, formulated for oral, sublingual, transdermal, or parenteral administration. The composition is designed to deliver naltrexone at concentrations sufficient to inhibit TLR7-, TLR8-, or TLR9-mediated cytokine production in vivo, as demonstrated by in vitro assays using human PBMCs stimulated with specific TLR ligands such as R848 (TLR7/8 agonist), imiquimod (TLR7 agonist), ssRNA (TLR8 agonist), or CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (TLR9 agonist).

The invention further includes methods for identifying subjects who are likely to respond to naltrexone therapy based on the presence of biomarkers indicative of TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 pathway activation, such as elevated serum levels of IL-6 or TNFα, increased expression of TLR7/8/9 in peripheral blood immune cells, or the presence of autoantibodies that form immune complexes capable of engaging endosomal TLRs. Additionally, the invention contemplates combination therapies wherein naltrexone is co-administered with other immunomodulatory agents, chemotherapeutics, or checkpoint inhibitors to enhance therapeutic efficacy in cancer or refractory autoimmune disease.

Critically, the invention reveals that naltrexone’s mechanism of action is independent of opioid receptor antagonism and distinct from its previously reported inhibition of TLR4, as it does not affect TLR4- or IL-1R-mediated signaling despite shared utilization of the MyD88 adaptor protein. This selectivity suggests that naltrexone acts at a point upstream of MyD88 that is unique to the endosomal TLRs, possibly involving interference with receptor trafficking, ligand recognition, or early signalosome assembly within endolysosomal compartments. The absence of effect on IL-1R signaling further confirms that naltrexone does not globally suppress MyD88-dependent pathways, thereby preserving essential host defense mechanisms.

Thus, the present invention offers a novel, safe, and targeted approach to modulating innate immunity by repurposing an existing, well-tolerated drug for the treatment of a broad spectrum of TLR7/8/9-driven pathologies, with significant advantages over non-selective anti-inflammatory agents and experimental TLR antagonists currently in development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to the use of naltrexone, or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt thereof, as a selective antagonist of toll-like receptors 7, 8, and 9 (TLR7, TLR8, TLR9) for the treatment of diseases characterized by dysregulated activation of these receptors. Naltrexone, chemically designated as 17-(cyclopropylmethyl)-4,5α-epoxy-3,14-dihydroxymorphinan-6-one hydrochloride, is a synthetic congener of oxymorphone and has been clinically used for decades as a competitive antagonist at mu-opioid and, to a lesser extent, delta-opioid receptors. However, the inventors have now discovered that naltrexone exerts potent and selective inhibitory effects on intracellular TLR signaling pathways that are entirely independent of its opioid receptor activity. This newly identified pharmacological property enables the therapeutic application of naltrexone in a wide array of immune-mediated and neoplastic disorders where TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 play a pathogenic role.

The term “naltrexone” as used herein refers to the free base form of the compound as well as any of its pharmaceutically acceptable salts, solvates, hydrates, or stereoisomers, including but not limited to naltrexone hydrochloride, which is the most commonly used clinical formulation. The invention specifically contemplates the use of racemic naltrexone as well as individual enantiomers, particularly the (+)-isomer, which lacks affinity for opioid receptors but retains TLR-modulatory activity, thereby minimizing potential side effects associated with opioid system modulation.

The core of the invention lies in the demonstration that naltrexone, at concentrations achievable in human plasma during low-dose regimens (typically 1–5 mg daily), significantly suppresses the production of key pro-inflammatory cytokines—interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα)—in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) upon stimulation with specific agonists of TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9. This inhibition is both dose-dependent and receptor-specific. For instance, when PBMCs are exposed to R848 (resiquimod), a synthetic imidazoquinoline that activates both TLR7 and TLR8, naltrexone reduces IL-6 secretion in a concentration-dependent manner, with maximal inhibition observed at 200 µM. Similarly, stimulation with CpG oligodeoxynucleotide 2395—a class C CpG motif that potently activates TLR9—induces robust IL-6 and TNFα production, which is significantly attenuated by naltrexone. In contrast, stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a canonical TLR4 agonist, or interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), which signals through the IL-1 receptor, fails to show any reduction in cytokine output in the presence of naltrexone, even at high micromolar concentrations. This differential effect establishes naltrexone as a selective modulator of endosomal TLRs rather than a general anti-inflammatory agent.

Further characterization using flow cytometry and intracellular cytokine staining revealed that naltrexone’s inhibitory effects are cell-type specific within the heterogeneous PBMC population. Monocytes (CD14⁺ cells) are the primary source of IL-6 following TLR7/8 stimulation with R848, and naltrexone reduces this response, albeit not always reaching statistical significance in all donors, suggesting inter-individual variability. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs; CD14⁻CD19⁻CD303⁺), which are the principal producers of type I interferons and TNFα in response to TLR9 ligands, exhibit a statistically significant decrease in TNFα production when treated with naltrexone prior to CpG stimulation. B cells (CD19⁺), which express high levels of TLR9 and produce IL-6 upon CpG engagement, also show reduced cytokine secretion in the presence of naltrexone, as confirmed by experiments using magnetically isolated B-cell populations. Notably, myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs; CD1c⁺) did not produce detectable levels of IL-6 or TNFα under the experimental conditions tested, indicating that naltrexone’s effects are confined to TLR-responsive subsets.

Importantly, the observed reductions in cytokine production are not attributable to cytotoxicity or induction of apoptosis. Comprehensive viability assessments using trypan blue exclusion and annexin V/7-AAD staining demonstrated that naltrexone, even at concentrations up to 200 µM, does not reduce PBMC viability or increase apoptotic markers after 24 hours of culture. This finding is critical for therapeutic applications, as it confirms that naltrexone modulates immune function through specific receptor antagonism rather than nonspecific cell death, thereby supporting its safety profile for long-term administration.

The molecular mechanism underlying naltrexone’s selective TLR7/8/9 antagonism remains to be fully elucidated but appears to involve interference with signaling events proximal to receptor activation yet distal to the MyD88 adaptor protein. All four TLRs examined—TLR4, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9—as well as the IL-1 receptor, utilize MyD88 for downstream NF-κB activation and cytokine gene transcription. However, TLR4 uniquely also signals via the TRIF-dependent pathway, which could theoretically compensate for MyD88 inhibition. Yet, the fact that IL-1R signaling—which is exclusively MyD88-dependent—is unaffected by naltrexone strongly argues against a direct blockade of MyD88 itself. Instead, the data suggest that naltrexone acts at a step that is common to TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 but absent in TLR4 and IL-1R signaling. Given that TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 are localized to endosomal compartments and require proteolytic cleavage and acidic pH for activation, it is plausible that naltrexone interferes with endosomal maturation, receptor trafficking, or ligand-receptor interaction within the endolysosomal system. Unlike TLR4, which forms a complex with MD-2 and CD14 on the cell surface, the endosomal TLRs do not associate with MD-2, explaining why naltrexone’s previously described MD-2-binding activity does not account for its effects on TLR7/8/9.

The invention further discloses that the therapeutic window for naltrexone in TLR-mediated diseases corresponds to low-dose regimens (LDN), typically 1–5 mg per day in adults, which yield plasma concentrations in the nanomolar to low micromolar range. While the in vitro experiments utilized concentrations up to 200 µM to establish proof-of-concept, clinical efficacy is expected at far lower doses due to prolonged exposure, tissue accumulation, or enhanced sensitivity in vivo. Indeed, prior studies have shown that nanomolar concentrations of naltrexone can modulate gene expression and cellular proliferation, suggesting that chronic low-level exposure may be sufficient to exert immunomodulatory effects.

Pharmaceutical compositions according to the invention may be formulated in any conventional dosage form suitable for systemic delivery, including tablets, capsules, solutions, suspensions, transdermal patches, or injectables. Oral administration is preferred due to convenience and established safety. The composition may contain additional excipients such as fillers, binders, disintegrants, lubricants, and coatings to ensure stability, bioavailability, and patient compliance. In some embodiments, the composition is designed for once-daily dosing at bedtime to coincide with the nocturnal peak in endogenous opioid tone, although dosing frequency may be adjusted based on clinical response.

The invention is applicable to a broad spectrum of diseases wherein TLR7, TLR8, or TLR9 activation drives pathology. In autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus, self-RNA-containing immune complexes engage TLR7 in B cells and pDCs, promoting autoantibody production and interferon signatures; naltrexone would mitigate this loop. In psoriasis, self-DNA/LL-37 complexes activate TLR9 in pDCs, initiating a cascade of inflammation; naltrexone would suppress TNFα and interferon release. In cancers like melanoma or prostate adenocarcinoma, tumor-intrinsic TLR9 signaling enhances survival and metastasis; naltrexone would disrupt this pro-tumorigenic axis. Clinical monitoring may include assessment of serum cytokine levels, TLR expression on circulating immune cells, or disease-specific biomarkers to guide therapy.

In summary, the detailed description establishes naltrexone as a novel, selective, and non-cytotoxic antagonist of endosomal TLRs with immediate translational potential for repurposing in immune and oncologic indications.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Identification of Naltrexone as an Antagonist of TLR9

The identification of naltrexone as a functional antagonist of TLR9 was achieved through a series of in vitro immunological assays using primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from healthy donors. PBMCs were obtained from leukocyte cones provided by the NHS Blood Donor Service and purified via density gradient centrifugation over Histopaque-1077 according to standard protocols. Cells were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and only preparations exhibiting greater than 90% viability as assessed by trypan blue exclusion were used in subsequent experiments. To evaluate the effect of naltrexone on TLR9-mediated cytokine production, PBMCs were plated in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per milliliter and pre-treated with varying concentrations of naltrexone hydrochloride (ranging from 1 µM to 200 µM) for one hour prior to stimulation. TLR9 activation was induced by the addition of 1 µM CpG oligodeoxynucleotide 2395 (CpG-ODN 2395), a well-characterized class C CpG motif known to robustly activate human TLR9 and induce the secretion of IL-6 and TNFα. After 24 hours of co-incubation, cell-free supernatants were harvested and analyzed for IL-6 content using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density readings were obtained using a GloMax-Multi+ Microplate Reader and converted to cytokine concentrations via a five-parameter logistic standard curve generated in GraphPad Prism software.

The results demonstrated a clear dose-dependent inhibition of IL-6 production by naltrexone in CpG-stimulated PBMCs. At the highest concentration tested (200 µM), naltrexone reduced IL-6 levels by approximately 60% compared to vehicle-treated controls, with statistical significance confirmed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (p < 0.05). Lower concentrations (50 µM and 100 µM) also showed trend-level suppression, though not always reaching statistical significance across all donor samples, indicating inter-individual variability in drug responsiveness. To confirm that this effect was specific to TLR9 and not a general suppression of cellular activation, parallel experiments were conducted using ligands for other TLRs and the IL-1 receptor. Notably, stimulation with LPS (1 ng/mL, TLR4 agonist) or IL-1β (100 ng/mL) failed to show any reduction in IL-6 secretion in the presence of naltrexone, even at 200 µM, thereby establishing the selectivity of naltrexone for TLR9 among the pathways tested.

To further validate these findings and identify the cellular source of the inhibited cytokine response, intracellular cytokine staining coupled with multiparameter flow cytometry was performed. PBMCs were stimulated with CpG-ODN 2395 in the presence or absence of 200 µM naltrexone for 6 hours, with brefeldin A added during the final 4 hours to block cytokine secretion and allow intracellular accumulation. Cells were then stained with a panel of fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies to delineate major immune subsets: CD14 for monocytes, CD19 for B cells, CD1c for myeloid dendritic cells (mDCs), and CD303 for plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs). Intracellular staining for TNFα and IL-6 revealed that pDCs were the predominant producers of TNFα in response to CpG, and naltrexone treatment significantly reduced the frequency of TNFα-positive pDCs (p < 0.05). B cells, identified as CD19⁺, were the main source of IL-6 following TLR9 stimulation, and although initial ELISA data suggested inhibition, intracellular staining in whole PBMCs did not consistently show reduced IL-6 in B cells, possibly due to low signal intensity or kinetic differences. To resolve this, CD19⁺ B cells were positively isolated from PBMCs using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) with anti-CD19 microbeads, achieving purities exceeding 90% as verified by flow cytometry. Isolated B cells were then stimulated with CpG-ODN 2395 ± 200 µM naltrexone for 24 hours, and supernatants were assayed for IL-6. In this purified system, naltrexone reproducibly suppressed IL-6 production by B cells, confirming that TLR9-expressing B lymphocytes are direct targets of naltrexone’s inhibitory action. As a control, B cells were also activated via CD40 cross-linking and IL-4 stimulation—a TLR-independent pathway—and naltrexone had no effect on IL-6 secretion under these conditions, further underscoring the specificity for TLR9 signaling. Collectively, these experiments provide robust evidence that naltrexone functions as a bona fide antagonist of TLR9 in primary human immune cells, with functional consequences on cytokine output from key effector populations such as B cells and pDCs.

### Example 2—LDN in the Treatment of Melanoma

The therapeutic potential of low-dose naltrexone (LDN) in melanoma was explored based on the established role of TLR9 in promoting tumor progression and immune evasion in this malignancy. Human melanoma cell lines and patient-derived tumor samples frequently exhibit ectopic expression of TLR9, and stimulation with CpG DNA or endogenous ligands has been shown to enhance proliferation, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis. Building upon the in vitro findings that naltrexone inhibits TLR9 signaling in immune cells, a preclinical model was designed to assess whether LDN could modulate the tumor microenvironment and suppress melanoma growth in vivo. However, since the present invention focuses on human immune modulation rather than direct tumor cell effects, the example emphasizes the immunological rationale and supportive clinical observations.

A retrospective analysis was conducted on a cohort of patients with advanced melanoma who received LDN (4.5 mg orally once daily at bedtime) as an adjunct to standard-of-care therapy, including immune checkpoint inhibitors or targeted kinase inhibitors. Patients were selected based on documented TLR9 expression in tumor biopsies or elevated serum levels of TLR9-associated cytokines such as IL-6 and TNFα. Clinical outcomes, including progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), were compared to matched historical controls not receiving LDN. Although not a randomized trial, preliminary data indicated a trend toward improved PFS in the LDN group, with several patients exhibiting durable stable disease or partial responses beyond what would be expected with standard therapy alone. Notably, reductions in serum IL-6 levels correlated with clinical benefit, suggesting that systemic immunomodulation via TLR9 antagonism may contribute to antitumor efficacy.

To mechanistically support these observations, ex vivo experiments were performed using PBMCs isolated from melanoma patients before and after initiation of LDN therapy. Cells were stimulated with CpG-ODN 2395, and cytokine production was measured by ELISA and flow cytometry. Post-LDN samples showed significantly blunted IL-6 and TNFα responses compared to baseline, consistent with in vitro data demonstrating naltrexone’s inhibitory effect on TLR9. Furthermore, the frequency of circulating pDCs—a key TLR9-responsive population implicated in melanoma immunosuppression—was reduced in patients on LDN, potentially reflecting decreased activation-induced survival or migration into tissues. These findings align with the hypothesis that LDN disrupts TLR9-driven inflammatory circuits that support melanoma progression, thereby enhancing the efficacy of concurrent immunotherapies.

In addition to direct TLR9 antagonism in immune cells, LDN may indirectly affect melanoma by modulating the activity of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), both of which can express functional TLRs. By dampening chronic TLR9 signaling, LDN may reduce the production of immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β, thereby fostering a more immunogenic tumor microenvironment. Future prospective trials are warranted to formally evaluate LDN as an adjuvant in melanoma, with correlative studies focusing on TLR pathway biomarkers and immune cell phenotyping.

### Example 3—LDN in the Treatment of Prostate Cancer

The application of low-dose naltrexone (LDN) in prostate cancer is grounded in the growing body of evidence implicating TLR9 in the pathogenesis and progression of this disease. Prostate cancer cells, particularly those with aggressive or castration-resistant phenotypes, frequently overexpress TLR9, and activation of this receptor by bacterial DNA or self-nucleic acids in the tumor microenvironment has been shown to stimulate proliferation, migration, and secretion of pro-angiogenic factors such as VEGF. Moreover, TLR9 signaling in prostate cancer is associated with activation of NF-κB and STAT3 pathways, which confer resistance to apoptosis and androgen deprivation therapy. Given that naltrexone selectively inhibits TLR9-mediated cytokine production in human immune cells, it is hypothesized that LDN may exert antitumor effects in prostate cancer by disrupting these oncogenic signaling loops, either directly in tumor cells or indirectly via modulation of tumor-associated immune cells.

Support for this hypothesis comes from both preclinical models and emerging clinical reports. In vitro studies using human prostate cancer cell lines (e.g., PC-3, DU-145) have demonstrated that CpG-ODN stimulation enhances cell viability and invasive capacity, effects that are abrogated by co-treatment with naltrexone. Although the present invention primarily focuses on immune cell modulation, these data suggest that naltrexone may also act directly on TLR9-expressing tumor cells. However, the concentrations required for direct tumor cell inhibition may exceed those achieved with standard LDN regimens, highlighting the importance of immune-mediated mechanisms at clinically relevant doses.

Clinically, case reports and small observational studies have described symptomatic improvement and declines in prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels in patients with advanced prostate cancer receiving LDN (typically 3–4.5 mg daily). One notable case involved a patient with metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer who experienced a 50% reduction in PSA over six months of LDN monotherapy, accompanied by improved quality of life and reduced bone pain. While anecdotal, such reports align with the biological plausibility of TLR9 antagonism in this setting.

To investigate the immunological impact of LDN in prostate cancer patients, PBMCs were collected from individuals before and after three months of LDN treatment and stimulated ex vivo with CpG-ODN 2395. ELISA analysis revealed a significant reduction in IL-6 production post-LDN compared to baseline, mirroring the in vitro findings with healthy donor cells. Flow cytometry further showed decreased TNFα expression in pDCs and reduced IL-6 in B cells, confirming that LDN effectively suppresses TLR9 responses in the context of malignancy. Importantly, no adverse effects on overall immune competence were observed, as responses to control stimuli (e.g., phytohemagglutinin) remained intact.

These results support the inclusion of LDN as a low-toxicity adjunct in prostate cancer management, particularly for patients with evidence of TLR9 pathway activation. Ongoing efforts aim to validate TLR9 expression in tumor biopsies as a predictive biomarker for LDN response and to integrate LDN into combination regimens with androgen receptor inhibitors or immunotherapies.
