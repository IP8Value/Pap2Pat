## BACKGROUND

Modern air-to-air and surface-to-air missile guidance systems face significant challenges when engaging highly maneuverable targets, particularly those capable of executing complex evasion strategies such as weaving, jinking, or bang-bang acceleration profiles. Traditional proportional navigation (PN) guidance laws, while computationally simple and effective against non-maneuvering or slowly maneuvering targets, suffer from degraded performance when confronted with agile threats that can rapidly change their acceleration vectors. This degradation stems from PN’s fundamental assumption that both the missile and target will maintain constant velocity vectors for the remainder to the engagement—a condition encapsulated in the concept of zero effort miss (ZEM). When a target executes evasive maneuvers, this assumption is violated, leading to increased miss distances and potential mission failure.

To address this limitation, researchers have developed augmented proportional navigation (APN) guidance laws that incorporate estimates of target acceleration into the guidance command calculation. While APN demonstrates improved performance against specific maneuver types—particularly step accelerations—it introduces significant practical challenges. Accurate real-time estimation of arbitrary target acceleration profiles remains an unsolved problem in many operational scenarios, especially when sensor data is noisy, delayed, or corrupted by environmental effects such as radome refraction. Moreover, estimation errors can compound over time, potentially causing the guidance system to diverge and produce catastrophic miss distances. Consequently, there exists a critical need for guidance approaches that can effectively counter maneuvering targets without relying on explicit target acceleration estimation.

Alternative approaches have emerged that attempt to circumvent the estimation problem altogether. Switched bias proportional navigation (SBPN) employs sliding mode theory to introduce a bias term based solely on the sign of the line-of-sight (LOS) rotation rate, thereby approximating APN-like performance without requiring acceleration estimates. Geometric guidance methods parameterize trajectories in specialized reference frames such as the Frenet-Serret frame, though these often assume knowledge of target acceleration vectors or are restricted to low-speed engagements. While these approaches represent important advances, they typically demonstrate limited robustness across diverse maneuver types and environmental conditions, and often fail to generalize well to realistic operational scenarios involving high-speed, high-acceleration targets with complex evasion strategies.

The challenge is further compounded by practical hardware limitations inherent in real-world missile systems. Radome effects, where the protective dome covering the seeker introduces refraction errors that depend on look angle, create parasitic attitude loops that degrade guidance performance. Aerodynamic constraints limit maximum achievable acceleration as a function of dynamic pressure, which varies with altitude and velocity. Flight control system lags and actuator dynamics introduce additional delays between commanded and actual acceleration responses. These factors collectively create a complex, nonlinear, and partially observable environment that traditional guidance laws struggle to navigate effectively.

Recent advances in machine learning, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), have demonstrated promise in developing adaptive guidance and control systems that can learn optimal policies through simulated experience. RL frameworks enable direct mapping from sensor observations to control commands without requiring explicit models of target behavior or environmental dynamics. However, conventional RL approaches often lack the ability to generalize across diverse engagement scenarios or adapt to novel target behaviors encountered during operation. Meta-reinforcement learning (meta-RL) addresses this limitation by training agents across ensembles of environments, enabling rapid adaptation to new conditions through learned internal representations of environmental variations.

Despite these advances, existing RL-based guidance approaches typically optimize end-to-end mappings from observations directly to acceleration commands, which may not leverage the proven effectiveness of established guidance laws like PN. Furthermore, such approaches often require extensive training data and computational resources, and may produce policies that are difficult to analyze, verify, or certify for safety-critical applications. There remains a need for hybrid approaches that combine the theoretical foundations of classical guidance laws with the adaptive capabilities of modern machine learning techniques, while maintaining interpretability and robustness across diverse operational conditions.

The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel line-of-sight curvature methodology that enhances any LOS-based guidance law without requiring explicit target acceleration estimation. By dynamically shaping the observed LOS vector through a learned policy, the invention enables guidance systems to implicitly account for target maneuvers, environmental disturbances, and system limitations while preserving the mathematical structure and computational efficiency of established guidance laws. This approach represents a significant departure from both traditional estimation-based augmentation methods and end-to-end RL guidance systems, offering improved performance, reduced control effort, and enhanced robustness across diverse engagement scenarios.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel guidance system and method for improving missile accuracy against maneuvering targets without requiring explicit estimation of target acceleration. The invention achieves this through a line-of-sight (LOS) curvature policy that dynamically modifies the observed LOS vector before it is processed by a conventional guidance law such as proportional navigation (PN). This LOS curvature policy is implemented as a deep recurrent neural network optimized using reinforcement meta-learning (meta-RL) to maximize guidance performance across diverse engagement scenarios and target behaviors.

In accordance with the invention, a guidance system receives navigation system outputs including relative position and velocity vectors between the missile and target, seeker measurements, and other relevant state information. These observations are processed by a LOS curvature policy that generates Euler 321 attitude parameters representing a desired rotation of the LOS vector. This rotation is converted to a direction cosine matrix (DCM) that transforms the original LOS unit vector into a curved LOS vector. The curved LOS vector is then used as input to a conventional guidance law such as PN, which computes the required missile acceleration command based on the modified LOS information.

The LOS curvature policy is trained using a meta-RL framework that exposes the policy to an ensemble of engagement scenarios varying in initial conditions, target maneuver types (including bang-bang, weave, and jinking maneuvers), aerodynamic parameters, radome characteristics, and environmental conditions. Through this training process, the policy learns to implicitly infer target behavior patterns from observation history and adjust the LOS curvature accordingly to optimize guidance performance. The recurrent architecture of the neural network enables the policy to maintain internal state representations that capture temporal dependencies in target motion, allowing for sophisticated responses to complex maneuver sequences.

The invention demonstrates superior performance compared to both conventional PN and augmented PN (APN) guidance laws across a wide range of test conditions. Specifically, the PN-LOSC (proportional navigation with line-of-sight curvature) guidance system achieves significantly reduced miss distances while requiring less control effort than either benchmark approach. This dual advantage stems from two key mechanisms: first, the LOS curvature policy provides enhanced robustness to radome refraction effects by intelligently filtering spurious LOS rotation signals; second, the policy develops sophisticated responses to target maneuvers that minimize unnecessary control activity while maintaining accurate intercept trajectories.

Importantly, the invention maintains compatibility with existing guidance law architectures, requiring only the insertion of the LOS curvature module between the seeker/radome subsystem and the guidance law computation. This modular design enables straightforward integration into current missile systems without requiring extensive redesign of established guidance algorithms. Furthermore, because the invention does not rely on explicit target acceleration estimation, it avoids the stability and convergence issues associated with estimation-based augmentation methods.

The invention is particularly well-suited for engagements involving high-speed, high-acceleration targets such as supersonic cruise missiles, where traditional guidance laws struggle to maintain accuracy due to the combination of aggressive target maneuvers and practical system limitations. The approach demonstrates consistent performance improvements across varying altitude ranges, dynamic pressure conditions, and target aerodynamic characteristics, making it applicable to both endoatmospheric and exoatmospheric intercept scenarios.

Additional advantages of the invention include its ability to satisfy multiple guidance constraints simultaneously, such as impact angle requirements, load factor limitations, and thermal protection considerations. The learned LOS curvature policy naturally balances these competing objectives through its reward function optimization, producing guidance commands that respect system capabilities while maximizing intercept probability. Future extensions of the invention could incorporate six-degree-of-freedom dynamics, more sophisticated aerodynamic models, and additional sensor modalities to further enhance performance in realistic operational environments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive guidance system and methodology for enhancing missile accuracy against maneuvering targets through dynamic line-of-sight (LOS) curvature. The system architecture integrates a learned LOS curvature policy with conventional guidance laws to achieve superior performance without requiring explicit target acceleration estimation. The following detailed description outlines the system components, mathematical formulations, training methodology, and operational characteristics that enable this novel approach.

The guidance system operates within a three-dimensional engagement scenario involving a missile pursuing a maneuvering target. The engagement begins with initial conditions defined by relative position vector r_TM = r_T - r_M and relative velocity vector v_TM = v_T - v_M, where r_M and v_M represent the missile's position and velocity vectors, respectively, and r_T and v_T represent the target's corresponding vectors. The system considers skewed head-on engagement geometries where the initial missile and target velocity vectors are not necessarily aligned with the relative position vector, creating complex three-dimensional interception problems that challenge traditional guidance approaches.

The core innovation of the invention lies in the LOS curvature policy c: o → u, which maps navigation system observations o to Euler 321 attitude parameters u that define a rotation for modifying the observed LOS vector. The observation vector o includes comprehensive state information comprising relative position and velocity components, LOS rotation rates, missile and target speed estimates, look angle measurements, and other relevant navigation outputs. Specifically, the observation vector incorporates normalized relative position components, LOS rotation rate components in body-fixed coordinates, missile speed normalized by maximum achievable speed, look angle measurements, and time-to-go estimates derived from closing velocity calculations.

The action vector u generated by the LOS curvature policy is scaled and interpreted as Euler 321 rotation angles φ_LOSC, θ_LOSC, and ψ_LOSC, which are constrained to reasonable operational limits through appropriate scaling factors. These Euler angles define a rotation sequence that transforms the original LOS unit vector λ = r_TM / ||r_TM|| into a curved LOS vector λ_LOSC through application of the corresponding direction cosine matrix C(φ_LOSC, θ_LOSC, ψ_LOSC). The mathematical relationship is expressed as λ_LOSC = C(φ_LOSC, θ_LOSC, ψ_LOSC)λ, where the direction cosine matrix is constructed according to standard Euler 321 rotation conventions.

The curved LOS vector λ_LOSC is then processed by a conventional guidance law, specifically true proportional navigation (TPN) in the preferred embodiment, though the invention is compatible with any guidance law that utilizes LOS-derived inputs. The TPN guidance law computes the commanded acceleration perpendicular to the missile velocity vector according to the relationship a_M_COM = N × (v_M × ω_LOSC), where N represents the navigation gain (typically set to 3 for optimal performance), v_M is the missile velocity vector, and ω_LOSC represents the LOS rotation rate calculated from the curved LOS vector λ_LOSC. The LOS rotation rate is computed as ω_LOSC = (r_TM × v_TM_LOSC) / ||r_TM||², where v_TM_LOSC represents the relative velocity vector adjusted for the curved LOS geometry.

The LOS curvature policy is implemented as a deep recurrent neural network with four hidden layers, where the second layer employs gated recurrent units (GRUs) to maintain internal state representations that capture temporal dependencies in the observation sequence. Both the policy network and the associated value function network incorporate identical recurrent architectures to enable effective credit assignment across time steps during the reinforcement learning optimization process. The networks utilize hyperbolic tangent (tanh) activation functions on all hidden layers to ensure smooth, differentiable mappings between observations and actions.

Training of the LOS curvature policy occurs through a reinforcement meta-learning framework that exposes the agent to an ensemble of diverse engagement scenarios. Each training episode randomly samples from distributions of initial conditions including heading error (0-15 degrees), initial range (5-20 km), target speed (400-600 m/s), missile speed (800-1200 m/s), altitude (0-20 km), and target maneuver characteristics. The target maneuver model randomly selects between bang-bang, weave, and jinking acceleration profiles with equal probability, where each maneuver type exhibits varying acceleration magnitudes, durations, and switching frequencies. Target maximum acceleration capability is dynamically adjusted based on dynamic pressure considerations, reflecting realistic aerodynamic limitations of maneuvering vehicles.

The reinforcement learning optimization employs a customized implementation of proximal policy optimization (PPO) with adaptive hyperparameter tuning to maintain stable learning dynamics. The PPO algorithm utilizes a clipped surrogate objective function that prevents large policy updates between iterations, ensuring bounded Kullback-Leibler divergence between successive policy distributions. The objective function combines two surrogate terms: the standard probability ratio multiplied by advantage estimates, and a clipped version of the same product, with the final objective taking the minimum of these two terms to provide conservative policy updates.

The reward function guiding the optimization process comprises multiple components designed to balance competing objectives. A primary terminal reward component provides positive reinforcement proportional to the inverse of final miss distance, encouraging accurate intercepts while avoiding numerical instability near zero miss distances. An intermediate reward component penalizes excessive LOS curvature magnitude to discourage unnecessary modifications that might degrade performance in benign scenarios. Additional reward shaping includes penalties for excessive control effort and constraints on maximum allowable acceleration commands to ensure realistic system behavior.

Episode termination occurs when the closing velocity becomes negative, indicating that the missile has passed the target without achieving intercept. The simulation environment employs variable time-stepping integration to balance computational efficiency with accuracy requirements, using 20 ms time steps for ranges greater than 80 meters and reducing to 0.2 ms time steps during the final homing phase to achieve sub-meter miss distance measurement accuracy. Fourth-order Runge-Kutta integration ensures numerical stability and accuracy throughout the engagement simulation.

The missile and target dynamics models incorporate realistic aerodynamic effects including drag forces that vary with dynamic pressure and vehicle characteristics. Missile drag is modeled through a quadratic relationship with velocity, incorporating reference drag coefficients and cross-sectional area parameters. Target drag modeling includes both powered scenarios (constant speed maintenance) and unpowered ballistic trajectories with randomized drag characteristics to represent diverse threat types. Maximum achievable acceleration for both vehicles is dynamically constrained by dynamic pressure considerations, reflecting the physical limitations of aerodynamic control surfaces.

Radome effects are modeled through a look-angle-dependent refraction model that introduces parasitic attitude loops characteristic of real-world seeker systems. The model calculates azimuthal and elevation refraction errors as functions of look angle, with stochastic parameters sampled at the beginning of each episode to represent manufacturing variations and environmental uncertainties. Gaussian white noise is added to LOS measurements to simulate seeker tracking errors, and first-order low-pass filters model seeker bandwidth limitations and measurement lag.

Flight control system dynamics are represented through cascaded first-order lag models that account for both flight control computer processing delays and actuator response times. The missile's commanded acceleration is filtered through these dynamics before being applied to the equations of motion, ensuring realistic representation of control system limitations. Load factor constraints prevent excessive structural stresses by limiting maximum achievable acceleration to safe operational limits.

Experimental validation demonstrates the superiority of the PN-LOSC guidance system across diverse test conditions. Comparative testing against conventional PN and APN benchmarks reveals consistent performance improvements in both accuracy and control efficiency. Under high-target-acceleration conditions (30g maximum capability), PN-LOSC achieves median miss distances of approximately 0.8 meters compared to 1.5 meters for APN and 2.2 meters for PN, while simultaneously reducing average control effort by 25% compared to APN and 15% compared to PN. These improvements are particularly pronounced for close-intercept scenarios (miss distances < 1 meter), where PN-LOSC demonstrates nearly double the success rate of conventional approaches.

Analysis of the learned LOS curvature policy reveals sophisticated behavioral patterns that explain its superior performance. During weave maneuvers, the policy demonstrates range-dependent curvature adjustment, applying minimal modification at long ranges to conserve energy while increasing curvature magnitude during terminal homing to counteract target evasion attempts. This intelligent resource allocation strategy maintains higher missile velocity and acceleration capability throughout the engagement by minimizing unnecessary drag-inducing control activity. Against bang-bang maneuvers, the policy develops anticipatory curvature adjustments that effectively lead the target's expected motion, reducing the guidance system's reaction time requirements.

Robustness testing confirms the policy's effectiveness across varying environmental conditions and system parameters. Performance degrades gracefully with increasing radome refraction severity, maintaining significant advantages over conventional approaches even under extreme refraction conditions. Altitude variations from sea level to 20 kilometers show consistent performance improvements, demonstrating the policy's ability to adapt to changing dynamic pressure conditions. Target drag model variations, representing both powered cruise missiles and unpowered ballistic threats, reveal the policy's versatility across diverse threat classes.

The invention's modular architecture enables straightforward integration with existing missile guidance systems. Implementation requires only the insertion of the LOS curvature module between the seeker/radome subsystem and the guidance law computation, preserving established software architectures and verification procedures. The learned policy operates entirely on standard navigation system outputs, requiring no additional sensor inputs or computational infrastructure beyond what is already available in modern missile systems.

Future extensions of the invention could incorporate six-degree-of-freedom dynamics to account for roll coupling effects and more sophisticated aerodynamic interactions. Additional sensor modalities such as infrared imaging or radar Doppler measurements could provide enhanced target characterization capabilities to further improve policy performance. Multi-objective optimization could explicitly balance competing requirements such as impact angle constraints, thermal protection limits, and electronic warfare considerations to produce guidance commands optimized for specific mission profiles.

The invention represents a significant advancement in missile guidance technology by combining the theoretical foundations of classical guidance laws with the adaptive capabilities of modern machine learning techniques. Through dynamic LOS curvature, the system achieves unprecedented levels of accuracy and efficiency against maneuvering targets while maintaining compatibility with existing system architectures and avoiding the pitfalls of explicit target acceleration estimation. This approach opens new possibilities for autonomous guidance systems that can adapt to evolving threat environments while maintaining robust, predictable performance across diverse operational scenarios.
