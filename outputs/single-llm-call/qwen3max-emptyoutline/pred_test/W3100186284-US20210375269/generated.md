# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language understanding (NLU) within artificial intelligence systems, and more specifically to methods and systems for improving the cross-domain generalization capabilities of dialog act (DA) taggers in task-oriented dialogue systems through unsupervised domain adaptation techniques leveraging pre-trained masked language models. The invention provides a novel framework that enhances the performance of DA tagging models when deployed in target domains for which labeled training data is scarce or unavailable, by utilizing unlabeled dialogue corpora from such target domains in conjunction with a source domain containing annotated examples. The technical contribution resides in a new data augmentation strategy—termed MASKAUGMENT—that stochastically replaces tokens in input dialogue sequences with the [MASK] token as defined in BERT-style pre-trained language models, thereby enabling a consistency-regularized teacher-student learning scheme that improves model robustness and generalization across heterogeneous dialogue domains. This invention is particularly relevant to the development of scalable, adaptive, and efficient conversational agents capable of operating effectively across multiple verticals—such as flight booking, restaurant reservation, ride-sharing, media playback, and music streaming—without requiring extensive re-annotation of dialogue data for each new domain.

## BACKGROUND

Dialog act (DA) tagging constitutes a foundational component of modern task-oriented dialogue systems, serving to interpret the communicative intent underlying each utterance exchanged between a user and an automated agent. By classifying each turn of a conversation into one or more predefined semantic categories—such as “request,” “inform,” “confirm,” or “notify-success”—DA taggers enable downstream modules like state trackers and policy managers to reason about the evolving goals and beliefs of the interlocutors. Over the past several decades, numerous annotation schemes and taxonomies have been proposed to formalize the set of possible dialog acts, ranging from early theoretical frameworks like those of Core and Allen (1997) and Stolcke et al. (2000) to more recent standardized schemas such as ISO 24617-2 (Bunt et al., 2010) and the universal DA schema introduced by Paul et al. (2019). Despite these advances, a persistent challenge in the practical deployment of DA taggers lies in their limited ability to generalize across different application domains, especially when transitioning from well-resourced source domains (e.g., restaurant reservations) to low-resource or entirely unseen target domains (e.g., flight bookings).

This generalization bottleneck stems primarily from two interrelated factors: (1) the heterogeneity of annotation practices across existing dialogue datasets, which impedes the development of unified models capable of operating consistently across corpora; and (2) the high cost and labor intensity associated with collecting and annotating large-scale, domain-specific dialogue data. While recent efforts have sought to mitigate the first issue through schema alignment—exemplified by the Schema-Guided Dialogue (SGD) dataset (Rastogi et al., 2020), which enforces a consistent DA labeling protocol across 20 distinct domains—the second challenge remains largely unresolved. In real-world scenarios, it is often feasible to obtain substantial volumes of unlabeled dialogue logs from customer service interactions, chatbot transcripts, or crowd-sourced conversations in a target domain, but acquiring corresponding DA annotations at scale is prohibitively expensive. Consequently, there exists a critical need for domain adaptation techniques that can effectively leverage such unlabeled target-domain data to enhance model performance without relying on additional human labeling.

The advent of pre-trained masked language models (MLMs), most notably BERT (Devlin et al., 2019), has revolutionized many natural language processing (NLP) tasks by providing rich, context-sensitive representations that can be fine-tuned on downstream applications with relatively modest amounts of labeled data. These models are trained using the masked language modeling (MLM) objective, wherein a subset of input tokens is randomly replaced with a special [MASK] token, and the model is tasked with predicting the original tokens based on their surrounding context. This pre-training paradigm has proven highly effective for tasks such as text classification, named entity recognition, and question answering. However, its utility for domain adaptation in goal-oriented dialogue systems—particularly for structured prediction tasks like DA tagging—remains underexplored. Prior work in semi-supervised learning (SSL) for NLP has demonstrated the efficacy of consistency regularization, wherein a model is encouraged to produce similar predictions for different augmented views of the same unlabeled example. Common augmentation strategies include word dropout, synonym replacement, and back-translation. Yet, these methods often yield diminishing returns when applied atop strong pre-trained models like BERT, as the latter already encode substantial linguistic knowledge and may be sensitive to perturbations that disrupt syntactic or semantic coherence—especially in multi-turn dialogues where discourse structure and speaker roles are crucial.

Moreover, traditional SSL approaches typically assume that the labeled and unlabeled data originate from the same distribution, whereas in cross-domain DA tagging, a significant distributional shift exists between the source (labeled) and target (unlabeled) domains. This shift manifests not only in lexical and stylistic differences but also in variations in dialogue flow, user intent distributions, and system response patterns. As a result, standard consistency-based methods may fail to transfer effectively, since augmentations that preserve meaning in the source domain might not do so in the target domain, or vice versa. There is therefore a pressing need for a domain-adaptive SSL framework that is both compatible with the internal mechanics of MLMs and specifically tailored to the structural characteristics of task-oriented dialogues.

In light of these limitations, the present invention introduces a novel approach that directly exploits the [MASK] token mechanism inherent in BERT-like architectures to define a controlled, stochastic data augmentation technique—referred to herein as MASKAUGMENT—that is uniquely suited for dialogue-level inputs. Unlike external augmentation methods that operate independently of the model’s pre-training objectives, MASKAUGMENT operates natively within the representation space of the pre-trained model, ensuring compatibility and interpretability. Furthermore, the invention proposes an innovative unsupervised teacher-student learning scheme grounded in consistency regularization, wherein two differently augmented versions of the same unlabeled dialogue history are processed by the same model to generate “teacher” and “student” predictions, with the degree of masking carefully calibrated to ensure that the teacher view retains more original content and thus serves as a reliable pseudo-label for the more heavily masked student view. This design enables the model to learn invariant representations that are robust to partial information loss—a common occurrence in real-world dialogues due to speech recognition errors, ambiguous phrasing, or incomplete user inputs—while simultaneously adapting to the linguistic idiosyncrasies of the target domain through exposure to its unlabeled data.

Empirical validation on benchmark datasets such as GSim (Shah et al., 2018) and SGD (Rastogi et al., 2020) demonstrates that the proposed method yields substantial improvements over strong BERT baselines, with gains of up to 3% in F1 score under full-resource conditions and up to 10% in low-resource settings where only a fraction of the source-domain labels are available. These results underscore the practical significance of the invention for building scalable, adaptable dialogue systems that can be rapidly deployed across new domains with minimal annotation overhead. The background art thus reveals a clear gap: while pre-trained language models offer powerful representational capacity, their out-of-the-box cross-domain performance on structured dialogue understanding tasks remains suboptimal, and existing semi-supervised and domain adaptation techniques are either incompatible with MLM architectures or insufficiently tailored to the multi-turn, speaker-aware nature of task-oriented dialogues. The present invention addresses this gap through a synergistic integration of model-intrinsic augmentation and consistency-based learning, thereby advancing the state of the art in domain-adaptive natural language understanding for conversational AI.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The present invention provides a computer-implemented method, system, and non-transitory computer-readable medium for enhancing the cross-domain generalization of dialog act (DA) taggers in task-oriented dialogue systems through an unsupervised domain adaptation framework that leverages pre-trained masked language models (MLMs) and a novel data augmentation technique termed MASKAUGMENT. At its core, the invention recognizes that while pre-trained models like BERT offer strong in-domain performance for DA tagging, their ability to generalize to unseen domains—where labeled data is scarce but unlabeled dialogue logs are abundant—is limited by distributional shifts in vocabulary, discourse patterns, and intent distributions. To overcome this limitation, the invention introduces a consistency-regularized teacher-student learning scheme that operates exclusively on unlabeled target-domain dialogue histories, using MASKAUGMENT to generate perturbed views of each input sequence in a manner that is both stochastic and controllable.

In accordance with the invention, a DA tagging model is first initialized using a pre-trained MLM such as BERT, which has been trained on large-scale text corpora using the masked language modeling (MLM) objective. The model architecture includes a contextual encoder that processes a flattened representation of the dialogue history—comprising concatenated user and system utterances, each prefixed with speaker-specific tokens such as [USR] and [SYS], and preceded by a [CLS] token whose final-layer embedding serves as the aggregate representation for classification. A linear projection layer followed by a sigmoid activation function then maps this embedding to a probability distribution over a predefined set of dialog acts, enabling multi-label classification where each turn may trigger multiple intents simultaneously.

During training, the model is exposed to two types of data: (1) labeled source-domain dialogues, used to compute a supervised tagging loss (STL) via binary cross-entropy between predicted probabilities and ground-truth DA annotations; and (2) unlabeled target-domain dialogues, which are processed through the MASKAUGMENT procedure to support three additional fine-tuning objectives: masked tagging loss (MTL), masked language modeling loss (MLM), and disagreement loss (DAL). The MTL objective applies MASKAUGMENT to labeled examples during STL computation, thereby encouraging the model to maintain accurate predictions even when parts of the input are obscured—a form of robustness training. The MLM objective, inherited from pre-training, is optionally reactivated during fine-tuning on target-domain data to further adapt the model’s internal representations to the linguistic characteristics of the new domain. Most critically, the DAL objective implements the teacher-student learning mechanism: for each unlabeled target-domain dialogue history, two augmented versions are generated by applying MASKAUGMENT with different masking probabilities—denoted τ (tau) for the teacher and σ (sigma) for the student, where τ < σ. The resulting sequences are fed through the same model to produce teacher and student probability distributions over dialog acts. The DAL is then computed as the binary cross-entropy between these two distributions, effectively penalizing inconsistencies in the model’s predictions under varying degrees of input corruption. This encourages the model to develop representations that are invariant to the specific tokens masked out, focusing instead on higher-level semantic and pragmatic cues that remain stable across augmentations.

The invention further specifies that the DAL objective is activated only after an initial warm-up period (e.g., one epoch of training using STL, MTL, and optionally MLM), allowing the model to first acquire a reasonable baseline understanding of the DA taxonomy before engaging in consistency-based learning. Additionally, the relative weighting of the MLM loss is reduced (e.g., scaled by a factor of 0.1) to prevent it from dominating the optimization process and diluting the signal from the primary tagging objectives. Hyperparameters such as τ and σ are tuned within prescribed ranges (e.g., τ ∈ [0, 0.1], σ ∈ [0.1, 0.5]) to ensure that the teacher view remains sufficiently informative while the student view is meaningfully perturbed.

By integrating these components into a unified training framework, the invention enables a DA tagger to effectively transfer knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain, achieving significant improvements in F1 score, precision, and recall—particularly in low-resource scenarios where only a small subset of source-domain labels is available. The method is fully compatible with standard transformer-based architectures and requires no modifications to the underlying MLM, making it readily deployable in existing dialogue system pipelines. Moreover, because MASKAUGMENT operates by replacing tokens with the native [MASK] token already understood by the pre-trained model, it avoids the pitfalls of external augmentation strategies that may introduce out-of-vocabulary terms or disrupt discourse coherence. The result is a domain-adaptive DA tagging system that is both data-efficient and robust, capable of maintaining high performance across diverse task-oriented domains without the need for costly re-annotation.

### Example Performance

The efficacy of the present invention has been rigorously evaluated on two widely used benchmark datasets for task-oriented dialogue: GSim (Shah et al., 2018) and Schema-Guided Dialogues (SGD) (Rastogi et al., 2020). These datasets were selected to represent both narrow-domain and multi-domain settings, respectively, thereby providing a comprehensive testbed for assessing cross-domain generalization under varying levels of complexity and resource availability.

In the GSim experiments, the invention was tested on the task of transferring from one domain to another within the same dataset. Specifically, the model was trained on labeled dialogues from the “buying a movie ticket” (GMov) domain and evaluated on the “reserving a restaurant table” (GRes) domain, or vice versa. Each domain contains approximately 1,500 training dialogues, with development and test sets of 469 and 1,117 dialogues, respectively. The dialog acts in both domains were mapped to a universal schema of 13 tags as defined by Paul et al. (2019), ensuring comparability. Under this setup, a baseline BERT model fine-tuned solely with the supervised tagging loss (STL) achieved an F1 score of 91.2% on the target domain. When the proposed MASKAUGMENT-based objectives were incorporated—specifically, MTL and DAL—the F1 score improved to 94.1%, representing a gain of 2.9 percentage points. Notably, this improvement was accompanied by balanced increases in both precision (from 90.8% to 93.9%) and recall (from 91.6% to 94.3%), indicating that the model did not merely trade off one metric for the other but genuinely enhanced its overall discriminative capability.

To further probe the impact of individual components, ablation studies were conducted. Removing the DAL objective resulted in an F1 score of 92.7%, while removing MTL yielded 92.4%, demonstrating that both contribute meaningfully to performance. Reactivating the MLM objective during fine-tuning on unlabeled target data provided an additional 0.3% boost, confirming that continued language modeling adaptation aids domain transfer. Crucially, the invention exhibited even greater benefits in low-resource regimes. When only 10% of the source-domain labels were used for training (approximately 150 dialogues), the STL-only baseline dropped to 83.5% F1, whereas the full proposed model maintained 91.8%—an 8.3-point improvement. This underscores the invention’s particular suitability for real-world scenarios where labeled data is extremely limited.

The SGD dataset provided a more challenging evaluation environment, featuring 22,825 single- and multi-domain dialogues spanning 20 domains, each governed by a schema that defines relevant slots and intents. For cross-domain testing, smaller single-domain subsets—namely music (SMusic), media (SMedia), and ride-sharing (SRide)—were used as source domains, while the largest domain, flights (SFlights), served as the target. This asymmetry mimics practical deployment conditions where a new, high-traffic domain (e.g., flight booking) must be supported using models trained on less prevalent but better-annotated domains. In this setting, the STL-only BERT baseline achieved 89.7% F1 on SFlights. With the addition of MASKAUGMENT-based training, performance rose to 94.4%, a 4.7-point gain. Again, precision and recall improved in tandem (from 89.1%/90.3% to 94.2%/94.6%), and low-resource trials (using 20% of source labels) showed a 10.1-point advantage over the baseline (84.2% vs. 74.1%).

An analysis of failure cases revealed that the baseline model frequently misclassified acts involving nuanced system responses, such as “sys-notify-failure” (indicating an inability to fulfill a request), particularly in domains with sparse training examples. The invention significantly reduced such errors by learning more robust representations that rely less on surface-level lexical cues and more on contextual and pragmatic signals preserved across masked augmentations. For instance, even when key words like “unavailable” or “error” were masked out, the model could infer failure from surrounding discourse markers or user follow-up questions. This resilience was especially pronounced in the pre-BERT variant (i.e., using a model pre-trained on general text before fine-tuning), where the F1 gap between scratch-BERT (trained from random initialization) and pre-BERT narrowed from 11.4% to just 0.5%, highlighting the synergy between pre-training and the proposed adaptation method.

Training dynamics were also examined: activating DAL after one epoch prevented early instability, as premature consistency enforcement on an under-trained model led to confirmation bias. Optimal hyperparameters were found at τ = 0.05 and σ = 0.3, striking a balance between teacher reliability and student perturbation. The use of AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 3×10⁻⁵ and batch size of 16 ensured stable convergence within 10 epochs. Across all configurations, the invention consistently outperformed not only STL baselines but also alternative SSL methods such as virtual adversarial training and pseudo-labeling, which either degraded performance or offered marginal gains (<0.5% F1).

In summary, empirical results confirm that the invention delivers substantial, reproducible improvements in cross-domain DA tagging, with the greatest benefits observed in data-scarce conditions—a scenario of paramount importance for commercial dialogue systems seeking to expand into new verticals. The method’s simplicity, compatibility with existing infrastructure, and strong empirical performance establish it as a significant advancement in the field of domain-adaptive natural language understanding.
