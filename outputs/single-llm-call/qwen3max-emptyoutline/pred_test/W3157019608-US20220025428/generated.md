# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of non-volatile digital data storage, and more specifically to a novel platform for encoding, storing, and retrieving digital information using nucleic acid molecules arranged in spatially addressable nanostructures. The invention provides a system and method for representing binary data as the presence or absence of specific single-stranded DNA extensions on staple strands that form part of a DNA origami scaffold, wherein the spatial arrangement of these extensions defines a matrix of addressable bits that can be optically read using super-resolution microscopy techniques. The invention further encompasses integrated error-correction and data-encoding schemes that enable robust recovery of digital information from noisy optical readouts, thereby establishing a complete and scalable architecture for nucleic acid-based memory that does not rely on sequencing for data retrieval. This platform, referred to herein as digital Nucleic Acid Memory (dNAM), leverages the programmability of DNA nanotechnology, the high information density of nucleic acids, and the parallelism of optical imaging to create a durable, high-density, and editable memory system suitable for archival storage and specialized applications requiring redundancy and copy number.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The exponential growth of global digital data has placed unprecedented strain on existing storage technologies, which are approaching fundamental physical and economic limits. Conventional non-volatile memory materials, including magnetic tape and solid-state drives, suffer from finite lifespans, high energy consumption during operation and maintenance, and diminishing returns in areal density scaling. In response, researchers have explored alternative storage media, with deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) emerging as a particularly promising candidate due to its extraordinary information density—estimated at up to 215 petabytes per gram—and its potential for millennial-scale stability under appropriate environmental conditions. Early approaches to DNA-based data storage have primarily relied on synthesizing unique oligonucleotide sequences in which digital information is encoded directly into the nucleotide sequence itself, and subsequently recovered through high-throughput sequencing. While this method has demonstrated feasibility for archival storage, it is inherently constrained by the cost, speed, and error profiles associated with both DNA synthesis and sequencing technologies. Moreover, editing stored data in such systems typically requires re-synthesis of the entire oligonucleotide pool, rendering the process inefficient and impractical for dynamic data management.

Parallel advances in DNA nanotechnology have enabled the precise spatial organization of DNA molecules into complex two- and three-dimensional structures through the technique of DNA origami. In this approach, a long single-stranded viral scaffold, such as the M13 bacteriophage genome, is folded into a predetermined shape by hundreds of short synthetic staple strands that bind to specific regions of the scaffold via complementary base pairing. Critically, individual staple strands can be extended with additional single-stranded domains that protrude from the origami surface, providing addressable docking sites for secondary probes. This spatial control has been exploited in super-resolution microscopy, particularly in the DNA-Points Accumulation for Imaging in Nanoscale Topography (DNA-PAINT) technique, where transient binding of fluorescently labeled imager strands to these docking sites generates blinking events that can be localized with nanometer precision, thereby reconstructing the underlying nanostructure below the diffraction limit of light.

Despite these advances, the integration of DNA origami and super-resolution microscopy for digital data storage has remained unexplored. Prior art in nucleic acid memory has not decoupled the data encoding mechanism from the nucleotide sequence content, nor has it leveraged the spatial addressability of DNA nanostructures for bit-level representation. Furthermore, existing error-correction strategies in DNA storage have been adapted from classical information theory without accounting for the unique noise characteristics of optical readout systems, such as incomplete staple incorporation, fluorophore bleaching, background fluorescence, and orientation ambiguity of immobilized nanostructures. There remains a critical need for a DNA-based memory platform that combines the durability and density of nucleic acids with the editability, parallelism, and direct optical readability of spatially encoded nanostructures, supported by tailored error-correction algorithms that ensure reliable data recovery under realistic experimental conditions. The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a paradigm shift in nucleic acid memory: instead of storing data in the sequence of free-floating oligonucleotides, data is stored as the presence or absence of structural extensions on origami-integrated staple strands, creating a physical, spatially resolved binary matrix that can be read in a massively parallel fashion using super-resolution microscopy, without the need for sequencing.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a system and method for digital nucleic acid memory (dNAM) that enables the writing, storage, and optical reading of digital information using DNA origami nanostructures. In accordance with the invention, digital data is encoded into a plurality of DNA origami structures, each comprising a scaffold strand and a set of staple strands, wherein a subset of the staple strands are extended with single-stranded docking domains that serve as addressable data bits. The presence of a docking domain at a predefined spatial location within the origami structure represents a binary “1,” while its absence represents a binary “0.” These spatial locations are arranged in a regular matrix pattern, with inter-bit distances on the order of 10 nanometers or less, enabling high areal information density. The origami structures are deposited onto a substrate and imaged using DNA-PAINT super-resolution microscopy, wherein fluorescently labeled imager strands transiently bind to the docking domains, generating localized blinking events that are reconstructed into a spatial map of bit positions. A custom localization processing algorithm converts this map into a binary matrix corresponding to the original encoded data. To ensure robustness against experimental noise—including false negatives due to incomplete staple incorporation or fluorophore failure, and false positives from background binding—the invention employs a multi-layer error-correction scheme that combines fountain codes at the message level with a bi-level parity and checksum system at the origami level. The error-correction scheme is further enhanced by orientation-invariant encoding, allowing the decoding algorithm to correctly interpret the data matrix regardless of the rotational orientation of the origami on the substrate. The dNAM platform is fully editable, as data can be modified by replacing specific staple strands in the synthesis step, and supports massive parallelism, as tens of thousands of origami structures can be imaged simultaneously in a single field of view. The invention thus provides a complete, scalable, and sequencing-free alternative for high-density, durable digital data storage using nucleic acids.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

As used herein, the term “digital Nucleic Acid Memory” or “dNAM” refers to a system and method for encoding, storing, and retrieving digital information using spatially addressable DNA origami nanostructures, wherein binary data is represented by the presence or absence of single-stranded docking domains on staple strands. The term “DNA origami” denotes a nanostructure formed by the folding of a long single-stranded DNA scaffold, typically derived from the M13 bacteriophage genome, through hybridization with a set of shorter synthetic staple strands designed to bind specific regions of the scaffold and induce a desired two- or three-dimensional conformation. An “extended staple strand” is a staple strand that includes an additional single-stranded domain beyond the portion required for scaffold binding; this extension serves as a docking site for imager strands in the DNA-PAINT readout process. A “data domain” or “docking domain” refers to the single-stranded extension of an extended staple strand that is accessible for hybridization with a complementary imager strand. The term “binary state” describes the representation of a bit value, where the presence of a data domain at a predefined spatial location corresponds to a logical “1” and the absence corresponds to a logical “0.” A “data matrix” is a two-dimensional grid of spatially addressable bit positions defined by the arrangement of potential docking sites on a DNA origami structure. The term “DNA-PAINT” refers to the super-resolution microscopy technique known as DNA-Points Accumulation for Imaging in Nanoscale Topography, which relies on the transient binding of fluorescently labeled imager strands to complementary docking strands to generate stochastic blinking events that can be localized with nanometer precision. A “droplet” is a unit of encoded data generated by a fountain code algorithm, comprising one or more original data segments combined via binary XOR operations. The term “fountain code” denotes a class of erasure codes that enable the generation of a potentially infinite stream of encoded packets from a finite set of source symbols, such that the original data can be recovered from any sufficiently large subset of the encoded packets. A “parity bit” is an error-detection bit computed as the modulo-2 sum of a subset of data bits, used to verify the integrity of the data. A “checksum” is a value derived from a block of data to detect errors, typically computed as a sum or hash of the data bits. The term “orientation marker” refers to a set of predefined bits within the data matrix that allow the decoding algorithm to determine the rotational orientation of the origami structure on the substrate. The term “localization” refers to the estimated position of a fluorescent emitter, as determined by fitting the point spread function of a blinking event in a DNA-PAINT recording. A “template matching” algorithm is a computational method that compares an observed data structure to a set of predefined templates to identify the best match based on similarity metrics. The term “areal information density” refers to the amount of digital information stored per unit area of substrate, typically expressed in gigabits per square centimeter (Gbit/cm²). All other terms used herein have their ordinary meanings as understood by those skilled in the art of molecular biology, nanotechnology, and information theory.

### Nucleic Acid Architecture

The nucleic acid architecture of the present invention is centered on the design and assembly of DNA origami nanostructures that serve as physical carriers of digital information. Each origami structure is composed of a single-stranded DNA scaffold, preferably the 7249-nucleotide M13mp18 genome derived from the M13 bacteriophage, and a set of synthetic staple strands ranging in length from 20 to 60 nucleotides. The staple strands are designed to hybridize to specific, non-overlapping regions of the scaffold strand, thereby inducing the folding of the scaffold into a predetermined two-dimensional shape. In the preferred embodiment, the origami adopts a rectangular raft-like conformation measuring approximately 90 nanometers by 70 nanometers, although other geometries—including triangular, hexagonal, or three-dimensional polyhedral forms—are contemplated within the scope of the invention. The key innovation lies in the strategic modification of a subset of staple strands to include single-stranded extensions that project outward from the plane of the origami, creating spatially addressable docking sites for data readout.

The spatial arrangement of these docking sites is defined by a regular grid, referred to herein as the data matrix, which is embedded within the overall origami design. In the exemplary implementation, the data matrix comprises a 6×8 grid of 48 potential docking sites, with adjacent sites spaced approximately 10 nanometers apart. This spacing is chosen to be resolvable by current DNA-PAINT super-resolution microscopy techniques, which can achieve localization precisions of 5 nanometers or better under optimal conditions. Each site in the matrix corresponds to a specific staple strand position in the origami blueprint; during the synthesis phase, the presence or absence of a docking domain at that site is determined by the selection of either an extended staple strand (for a “1”) or a standard, non-extended staple strand (for a “0”). The docking domain itself is a single-stranded sequence of 8 to 12 nucleotides, designed to be orthogonal to all other sequences in the system to prevent cross-hybridization, and complementary to a fluorescently labeled imager strand used in the DNA-PAINT readout process.

The origami structure is further engineered to ensure that the data matrix is oriented with the docking domains facing away from the substrate during deposition, thereby maximizing accessibility for imager strand binding. This is achieved through controlled surface chemistry during the deposition process, wherein the origami is attached to the glass coverslip via electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged DNA backbone and a positively charged surface created by glow discharge treatment. Empirical measurements indicate that approximately 60% of deposited origami adopt the desired orientation, with the remaining structures having their data domains obscured by the substrate and thus excluded from the readout. To facilitate accurate image registration and drift correction, the origami mixture may include a small fraction of fiducial marker structures, such as sharp triangular origami or gold nanoparticles, which provide stable reference points throughout the imaging session.

The modularity of the DNA origami platform enables straightforward editing of stored data. Since each bit is represented by an independent staple strand, modifying the stored information requires only the replacement of specific staple strands in the synthesis mixture, without altering the scaffold or the overall origami architecture. This contrasts sharply with sequence-based DNA storage, where data editing necessitates complete re-synthesis of the oligonucleotide pool. Furthermore, the physical separation of data bits across multiple origami structures provides inherent redundancy, as the loss or corruption of a single origami affects only a small fraction of the total stored information. The scalability of the system is limited primarily by the number of unique origami designs that can be synthesized and distinguished, which in turn is governed by the size of the index field within the data matrix and the fidelity of the optical readout process.

### Dyes

The optical readout of dNAM relies on the use of fluorescent dyes conjugated to single-stranded DNA imager strands that are complementary to the docking domains on the origami. In the preferred embodiment, the dye Cy3B is employed due to its high photon yield, photostability, and compatibility with total internal reflectance fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy. The imager strand consists of a 10-nucleotide sequence complementary to the docking domain, with the Cy3B fluorophore attached to the 5′ or 3′ end via a standard phosphoramidite chemistry linkage. The concentration of the imager strand in the imaging buffer is carefully optimized to balance binding kinetics and signal-to-noise ratio; typically, a concentration of 3 nM is used to ensure that the transient binding events are sparse enough to be resolved as individual blinking events, yet frequent enough to accumulate sufficient localizations within a reasonable imaging time.

The imaging buffer further contains an oxygen-scavenging system to prolong the lifetime of the fluorophore and reduce photobleaching. This system comprises protocatechuic acid (PCA) and protocatechuate 3,4-dioxygenase (PCD), which work together to remove dissolved oxygen from the solution. Additionally, the antioxidant Trolox (6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid) is included to suppress triplet-state formation and blinking artifacts. The buffer is formulated in 0.5× TBE (Tris-borate-EDTA) with 18 mM MgCl₂ to maintain the structural integrity of the DNA origami during imaging. The combination of these components creates a reducing environment that maximizes the number of photons emitted per binding event, thereby improving the localization precision of the DNA-PAINT technique.

While Cy3B is the exemplar dye in the present invention, other fluorophores compatible with DNA-PAINT are also contemplated, including Alexa Fluor 647, ATTO 655, and CF dyes. Moreover, the use of multiple spectrally distinct dyes enables multiplexed readout, wherein different docking domains are assigned to different imager strands labeled with different colors. This approach can increase the bit depth per spatial location, effectively allowing more than one bit of information to be stored at each site in the data matrix. For instance, a four-color system could encode two bits per site (00, 01, 10, 11), quadrupling the information density without changing the physical spacing of the docking domains. The choice of dye and labeling strategy is thus a critical parameter in optimizing the performance of the dNAM system for specific applications, balancing factors such as readout speed, resolution, and information density.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

As a proof-of-concept demonstration of the dNAM platform, the message “Data is in our DNA!\n” was encoded, stored, and retrieved using the methods described herein. The message, comprising 19 ASCII characters (152 bits), was first converted to binary and then segmented into 10 non-overlapping data segments of 16 bits each. A fountain code algorithm was applied to generate 15 droplets, each consisting of a random XOR combination of one or more data segments, ensuring that the original message could be recovered from any subset of 10 or more correctly decoded droplets. Each droplet was then encoded onto a unique DNA origami design using a 6×8 data matrix, with the following allocation of bits: 16 bits for the droplet data, 4 bits for a unique binary index (0000 to 1110), 4 bits for orientation markers, 4 bits for checksums, and 20 bits for parity checks, totaling 48 bits per origami.

Fifteen distinct sets of staple strands were synthesized, with extended strands corresponding to the “1” positions in each design matrix and standard strands for the “0” positions. Each origami was assembled separately by mixing 22 nM M13mp18 scaffold with a 10-fold excess of standard staples and a 50-fold excess of extended staples in 1× TAE buffer with 18 mM MgCl₂, followed by a thermal annealing cycle from 90°C to 25°C over 12 hours. The assembled origami were purified by agarose gel electrophoresis and stored at 4°C in the dark. Equal molar amounts of all 15 origami designs were mixed to a total concentration of 0.33 nM per design and deposited onto a glow-discharged glass coverslip within a sticky-slide flow cell. The sample was imaged using DNA-PAINT on a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope equipped with a 561 nm laser and an EMCCD camera, acquiring 40,000 frames at 300 ms exposure time.

The resulting blinking events were localized using the ThunderSTORM plugin for ImageJ, and the localizations were rendered into a super-resolution image using Picasso-Render software. A custom localization processing algorithm identified clusters of localizations corresponding to individual origami, fitted them to a 6×8 grid model, and converted the signal intensities into binary matrices using an empirically derived threshold. The binary matrices were passed to the decoding algorithm, which first corrected errors at the origami level using the parity and checksum bits, then extracted the droplet and index data, and finally reconstructed the original message using the fountain code decoder. Despite an average of 7.3 errors per origami, the multi-layer error-correction scheme successfully recovered all 15 droplets, enabling 100% reconstruction of the original message from a single field of view containing approximately 4500 origami structures. Subsampling analysis indicated that only ~750 correctly decoded origami were required to achieve near-certain message recovery, demonstrating the robustness and efficiency of the dNAM encoding strategy.

## Materials and Methods

### Buffers

Two primary buffers are utilized in the dNAM workflow: a deposition buffer and an imaging buffer. The deposition buffer consists of 0.5× TBE (Tris-borate-EDTA) and 18 mM MgCl₂ in nuclease-free water, and is used for the deposition of DNA origami onto glass coverslips. The imaging buffer is prepared by supplementing the deposition buffer with 60 nM protocatechuate 3,4-dioxygenase (PCD), 1 mM Trolox, 3 nM Cy3B-labeled imager strands, and 10 mM protocatechuic acid (PCA). The PCA is added immediately prior to the start of the DNA-PAINT recording to activate the oxygen-scavenging system. All buffer components are obtained from commercial vendors and prepared according to standard protocols, with pH adjusted to 8.0–8.3 as needed.

### Encoding Algorithm

The encoding algorithm implements a multi-layer error-correction scheme that operates at both the message level and the origami level. At the message level, a fountain code based on the Soliton distribution is used to divide the input message into k equal-sized segments and generate a stream of droplets, each formed by XOR-ing a random subset of segments. The number of segments per droplet is selected to optimize the probability of successful decoding from a minimal number of received droplets. At the origami level, each droplet is mapped onto a 6×8 data matrix, with additional bits allocated for indexing, orientation, checksum, and parity. The index bits uniquely identify each origami design, enabling the decoder to associate droplets with their correct positions in the message. The orientation bits are placed at the corners of the matrix in a rotationally invariant pattern, allowing the decoder to determine the correct orientation of the origami regardless of its rotational state on the substrate. Checksum bits are computed as the modulo-2 sum of all data bits in predefined rows or columns, while parity bits are calculated over overlapping subsets of data bits to enable error localization. The final encoded matrix is used to generate the staple strand sequences for origami assembly, with extended strands synthesized for “1” positions and standard strands for “0” positions.

### DNA Origami Folding

DNA origami structures are assembled by mixing 22 nM M13mp18 scaffold DNA with a 10-fold molar excess of standard staple strands and a 50-fold molar excess of extended staple strands in 100 µL of 1× TAE buffer containing 18 mM MgCl₂. The mixture is subjected to a thermal annealing protocol in a Mastercycler nexus thermal cycler: 1 minute at 90°C, 2 minutes at 80°C, followed by a gradual cooldown from 80°C to 25°C over 12 hours. The assembled origami are purified by electrophoresis on a 0.8% agarose gel in 0.5× TBE with 8 mM MgCl₂ at 4°C, and the band corresponding to correctly folded origami is excised and crushed to extract the DNA. The purified origami are stored at 4°C in the dark until use. Fiducial marker origami, such as sharp triangles, are prepared in parallel using the same protocol with their respective staple sets.

### Glass Coverslip Preparation

Borosilicate glass coverslips are cleaned by sonication in 0.1% (v/v) Liquinox detergent for 1 minute, followed by sonication in nanopure water for 1 minute. The coverslips are then dried at 40°C for at least 30 minutes. Fiducial markers are deposited by applying 200 µL of 0.2 pM silanized gold nanoparticles to the coverslip for 10 minutes at room temperature, followed by rinsing with methanol and nanopure water. The labeled coverslips are stored at 40°C until use. For DNA-PAINT imaging, a sticky-slide flow cell is glued to the coverslip, creating a microfluidic channel with a volume of approximately 50 µL.

### Fluorescence Microscopy

DNA-PAINT imaging is performed on an inverted Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope equipped with a TIRF illumination system, a 100× oil-immersion objective (NA 1.49), and a ProEM EMCCD camera. The sample is illuminated with a 561 nm laser in TIRF mode to generate an evanescent field that excites fluorophores within ~200 nm of the coverslip surface. Images are acquired at 300 ms exposure time (~3 frames per second) for a total of 40,000 frames per recording. The microscope is equipped with an autofocus system to maintain focus throughout the acquisition, and the imaging buffer is introduced into the flow cell immediately before recording. The raw image stack is saved in TIFF format for subsequent processing.

### DNA-PAINT Fluorophore Localization

Fluorophore localizations are identified from the raw image stack using the ThunderSTORM plugin for ImageJ, with wavelet-based filtering and Gaussian fitting to determine the centroid positions of blinking events. The localization list is then rendered into a super-resolution image using the Picasso-Render software package, with drift correction applied using the fiducial markers. The lateral uncertainty of each localization is recorded and used in the subsequent grid-fitting algorithm to weight the contribution of each localization to the final position estimate.

### Localization Data Processing

A custom algorithm processes the localization list to identify individual origami structures and convert them into binary matrices. The algorithm first selects random localizations and clusters nearby points within a radius R, accepting clusters that meet intensity and isolation criteria. Each cluster is then fitted to a 6×8 grid model using a maximum likelihood estimation that accounts for localization uncertainties, background noise, and global drift errors. The grid parameters—including center position, rotation angle, and emitter intensities—are optimized using the L-BFGS-B method. The fitted intensities are thresholded to produce a binary matrix, which is passed to the decoding algorithm. A parallel template matching step is used for quality control, comparing each observed origami to the 15 known design templates to quantify error rates, but this step is not used in the actual data decoding process.

### Decoding Algorithm

The decoding algorithm operates in two stages. In the first stage, errors in the binary matrix are corrected using the parity and checksum bits. A weight is assigned to each bit based on the number of parity/checksum discrepancies it participates in, and a greedy search algorithm iteratively flips the highest-weight bits until a consistent matrix is found or a maximum number of flips (9 in the exemplary implementation) is reached. The orientation bits are then used to rotate the matrix to its canonical orientation. In the second stage, the fountain code is decoded by maintaining a priority queue of droplets sorted by their degree (number of constituent segments). Droplets of degree one are used to recover original segments, which are then XOR-ed with higher-degree droplets to reduce their degree, repeating until all segments are recovered or the queue is exhausted.

## Data Simulation Test

To evaluate the theoretical performance of the dNAM encoding and decoding algorithms, simulations were conducted using randomly generated messages of varying lengths (160 to 12,800 bits). Messages were encoded into origami using the same multi-layer error-correction scheme as in the experimental implementation, with the number of data bits per origami adjusted to accommodate larger index fields for longer messages. Simulated origami were subjected to random bit errors at rates ranging from 0 to 9 errors per origami, and the decoding success rate was measured over 40 independent trials per condition. The results showed that the algorithm robustly recovered the entire message when the average error rate was below 7.4 errors per origami, with a sharp decline in performance beyond this threshold. Notably, even with an origami recovery rate as low as 63%, the message recovery rate remained at 100% for error rates below the threshold, demonstrating the effectiveness of the fountain code redundancy. The simulations also confirmed that the areal information density of the system scales linearly with message size up to approximately 5000 bytes, beyond which the overhead of indexing bits begins to dominate, imposing a practical limit of 64 kB for the prototype architecture.
