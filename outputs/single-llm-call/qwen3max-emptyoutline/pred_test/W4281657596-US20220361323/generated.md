## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of neural interface devices and, more specifically, to methods and systems for fabricating three-dimensional (3D) microelectrode arrays using high-resolution two-photon lithography. The invention further encompasses biomimetic electrode geometries, porous stimulating electrodes, and integrated fabrication processes that enable wafer-scale production of flexible, high-aspect-ratio neural probes suitable for both acute and chronic implantation in small and large animal models, as well as potential human clinical applications. The disclosed technology bridges advanced additive manufacturing with conventional thin-film microfabrication to produce customizable, high-channel-count neural recording and stimulation devices with micron-scale precision.

## BACKGROUND

Neural interface technologies have long been constrained by the trade-offs between spatial resolution, mechanical compliance, biocompatibility, and fabrication scalability. Traditional microelectrode arrays—such as the Utah array or Michigan probes—are typically fabricated using silicon-based micromachining techniques that yield rigid, planar, or linear structures with limited geometric flexibility. While these devices have enabled significant advances in neuroscience and neuroprosthetics, they suffer from several inherent limitations. First, their rigidity induces chronic inflammatory responses due to mechanical mismatch with soft neural tissue, leading to glial scarring and signal degradation over time. Second, their fixed geometries cannot conform to the complex three-dimensional architecture of the brain or peripheral nerves, limiting the density and specificity of neural recordings or stimulations. Third, conventional photolithographic processes restrict feature resolution to tens of microns, preventing the creation of truly cellular-scale interfaces that could minimize tissue damage and improve signal-to-noise ratios (SNR).

Two-photon polymerization (2PP), also known as direct laser writing (DLW), has emerged as a promising alternative for fabricating microscale 3D structures with sub-micron resolution. This technique exploits the nonlinear absorption of femtosecond laser pulses to initiate localized photopolymerization only at the focal point of a high-numerical-aperture objective, enabling true 3D voxel-by-voxel construction of arbitrary shapes within a photosensitive resin. Despite its high resolution and design freedom, 2PP has historically been limited by slow writing speeds, making it impractical for mass production or large-area devices. Recent innovations, including resonant scanning mirrors and optimized laser parameters, have accelerated printing speeds by one to two orders of magnitude, rendering 2PP viable for functional device fabrication.

However, integrating 2PP-fabricated structures into functional electronic devices—particularly neural electrodes—presents significant challenges. Most critically, the polymer scaffolds produced by 2PP are electrically insulating and must be metallized to serve as conductive electrodes. Conventional metallization techniques, such as directional sputtering or evaporation, fail to uniformly coat high-aspect-ratio or porous 3D structures, resulting in discontinuous or non-conformal metal layers that compromise electrical performance. Additionally, post-metallization insulation and selective exposure of electrode sites require precise, damage-free patterning methods compatible with delicate 3D geometries. Traditional approaches like photolithographic masking followed by reactive ion etching (RIE) or wet chemical etching are ill-suited for complex topographies and risk damaging fine features.

Furthermore, the mechanical properties of 2PP-printed polymers—while sufficient for static applications—often lack the stiffness required for penetrating neural tissue without buckling, especially for electrodes exceeding several hundred microns in length. This limitation is particularly acute in small animal models such as songbirds or mice, where dense neuronal packing demands ultrafine electrodes (<20 µm cross-section) to avoid triggering extensive immune responses. Yet, reducing electrode diameter inherently reduces mechanical robustness, creating a fundamental design conflict between miniaturization and insertability.

Prior attempts to address these issues have included hybrid fabrication strategies combining silicon micromachining with polymer coatings, insertion shuttles to assist penetration, or carbon fiber electrodes. While effective in specific contexts, these approaches lack the geometric versatility, scalability, or integration capability needed for next-generation neural interfaces. For instance, carbon fiber arrays offer excellent chronic stability but suffer from low fabrication yield and poor channel count. Silicon-based 3D electrodes provide high channel density but are brittle and incompatible with curved or moving tissues. Flexible polymer arrays mitigate mechanical mismatch but often sacrifice resolution and aspect ratio.

Moreover, existing methods for creating stimulating electrodes focus primarily on increasing surface area through roughening, nanostructuring, or electrochemical deposition of high-capacitance materials like iridium oxide (IrOx). These techniques, however, do not fundamentally alter the planar nature of the electrode-tissue interface. True 3D protruding structures—such as vertical pillars or porous scaffolds—could enhance charge injection capacity while maintaining minimal tissue displacement, but their fabrication on flexible substrates has remained elusive due to process incompatibilities between high-temperature semiconductor processing and temperature-sensitive polymers.

The present invention addresses these unmet needs by providing a fully integrated, wafer-scale fabrication platform that combines high-speed two-photon lithography with standard thin-film processing to produce customizable, high-aspect-ratio, 3D microelectrode arrays. By leveraging a resonant-scanning 3D direct laser writer, the invention enables rapid printing of complex electrode geometries—including biomimetic needle tips inspired by mosquito proboscises and macroporous architectures for enhanced charge injection—directly onto flexible polyimide substrates. A key innovation lies in the use of a sacrificial chromium layer for trace definition, which survives the aggressive development conditions of 2PP resins that would dissolve conventional photoresist masks. Subsequent non-directional sputter deposition ensures conformal metallization of intricate 3D prints, while femtosecond laser ablation—performed on the same optical platform used for printing—enables precise, maskless opening of electrode sites without thermal damage or redeposition. This unified workflow eliminates the need for multiple alignment steps, reduces process complexity, and preserves the fidelity of micron-scale features.

The resulting devices exhibit exceptional mechanical compliance, biocompatibility, and electrical performance, enabling high-SNR single-unit recordings in both zebra finches and mice, as well as faithful capture of local field potentials synchronized to natural behaviors such as respiration. Critically, the biomimetic “mosquito needle” geometry permits dura-penetrating implantation without craniotomy—a significant surgical advantage that reduces trauma and accelerates recovery. Additionally, the porous electrode variant demonstrates a five-fold increase in surface area compared to solid counterparts, offering substantial gains in charge storage capacity for neuromodulation applications.

In summary, the background art lacks a scalable, high-resolution method for fabricating 3D neural electrodes with arbitrary geometries, conformal metallization, and integrated insulation on flexible substrates. The present invention overcomes these deficiencies by synergistically combining high-speed two-photon lithography, robust thin-film processing, and precision femtosecond laser ablation into a single, manufacturable platform.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive method and system for fabricating three-dimensional (3D) microelectrode arrays with unprecedented geometric flexibility, resolution, and integration on flexible substrates. At the core of the invention is a high-speed two-photon lithography platform that utilizes a resonant scanning mirror to accelerate the direct laser writing of polymer microstructures by one to two orders of magnitude compared to conventional galvanometer-based systems. This enables the practical, wafer-scale production of complex 3D electrode scaffolds with micron-scale precision in minutes rather than hours.

The fabrication process begins with the deposition of a polyimide base layer on a silicon wafer, followed by the sputtering of a chromium (Cr) sacrificial layer. Unlike traditional photoresist-based lift-off processes, which are incompatible with the aggressive solvents used to develop two-photon polymerized resins, the Cr layer serves as a robust etch mask that withstands the entire fabrication sequence. Standard photolithography is then employed to pattern the Cr layer, defining the layout of metal traces and electrode sites. After resist removal, the wafer undergoes oxygen plasma cleaning to enhance adhesion for subsequent 3D printing.

A liquid photopolymer—specifically, a modified formulation of the commercially available OrmoComp® resin, augmented with a photoinitiator (TPO), a stabilizer (BHT), and fluorescein for real-time fluorescence monitoring—is applied to the wafer. Using a femtosecond-pulsed infrared laser focused through a high-numerical-aperture objective, 3D electrode structures are written directly onto the predefined Cr-patterned locations according to a digital CAD model. The printer incorporates an automated surface-finding algorithm that uses fluorescence feedback from the photoresist-substrate interface to achieve micron-level vertical alignment, ensuring strong adhesion between the printed structure and the underlying substrate. Each 16-channel array of 350 µm tall electrodes is printed in approximately 10 minutes, with potential for further acceleration through parallelized printing.

Following development in a dedicated solvent and a UV post-cure to enhance crosslinking, the wafer is subjected to a non-directional sputter deposition of titanium and platinum (Ti/Pt). This isotropic metallization process uniformly coats both the pre-patterned metal traces and the 3D-printed polymer structures, creating continuous conductive pathways from the electrode tips to external contact pads. The Cr sacrificial layer is then selectively etched away in a heated chromium etchant, lifting off excess metal and leaving behind only the desired conductive features.

To electrically isolate the traces and shanks, a conformal layer of parylene C is deposited via vapor-phase polymerization. Crucially, the invention employs femtosecond laser milling—using the same resonant-scanning optical system as the 3D printer, but with a high-pulse-energy amplified fiber laser—to selectively ablate the parylene C at the electrode tips. This maskless, athermal ablation process removes a 2–3 µm thick volume encompassing all electrode tips in under one minute per array, exposing the underlying platinum without melting, cracking, or redepositing debris. Real-time monitoring of ultraviolet emission during ablation allows for precise calibration of laser power, ensuring consistent tip exposure across devices.

For final device release, the high-pulse-energy laser is again employed to cut along the device perimeter, severing the polyimide and parylene layers. Individual probes are then lifted off the wafer in warm water and bonded to Omnetics connectors via anisotropic conductive film (ACF), enabling immediate compatibility with commercial neural recording systems such as Intan or Open Ephys.

The invention further discloses two key embodiments enabled by this fabrication platform: (1) biomimetic “mosquito needle” electrodes featuring sharp, tapered tips inspired by insect proboscises, which reduce insertion forces and permit transdural implantation in songbird brains without dura removal; and (2) macroporous stimulating electrodes with internal voids ranging from 40 µm² to 400 µm² in cross-section, which increase effective surface area by up to five-fold compared to solid equivalents, thereby enhancing charge injection capacity for neuromodulation.

Electrophysiological validation demonstrates that the devices achieve mean impedances of ~200 kΩ at 1 kHz and record high-SNR single-unit activity in zebra finch HVC and respiration-locked local field potentials in mouse olfactory bulb. The combination of high resolution, mechanical compliance, and geometric customization positions the invention as a transformative tool for neuroscience research, neural prosthetics, and bioelectronic medicine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a novel method and system for fabricating three-dimensional (3D) microelectrode arrays using high-speed two-photon lithography integrated with standard thin-film microfabrication processes. The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, including materials, process sequences, device geometries, and performance characteristics. It will be understood by those skilled in the art that the invention is not limited to the particular embodiments described herein but encompasses all equivalents and modifications falling within the scope of the appended claims.

The foundation of the invention is a custom-built, high-speed 3D direct laser writer based on two-photon polymerization (2PP). Unlike conventional galvanometer-scanned 2PP systems, which are limited by the inertia of moving mirrors and thus constrained to writing speeds on the order of millimeters per second, the present system employs a resonant scanning mirror operating at kilohertz frequencies. This enables raster scanning of the laser focus at speeds exceeding 100 mm/s, accelerating the printing of complex 3D structures by one to two orders of magnitude. As a result, a cubic millimeter of space filled with near-micron resolution features can be printed in approximately one minute, making wafer-scale production feasible.

The photopolymer used in the printing process is a modified formulation of OrmoComp® (Micro Resist Technology), a commercially available organically modified ceramic (ORMOCER®) resin known for its glass-like mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and optical clarity. To optimize the resin for 2PP, the base OrmoComp® is supplemented with 2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphine oxide (TPO) as a photoinitiator to enhance two-photon absorption efficiency, 3,5-di-tert-butyl-4-hydroxytoluene (BHT) as a radical scavenger to suppress unwanted polymerization, and fluorescein as a fluorescent tracer. The fluorescein enables real-time monitoring of the printing process via fluorescence imaging: as the laser writes, unpolymerized regions emit green fluorescence when excited by the same infrared laser, allowing the system to detect the resin-substrate interface with sub-micron precision. This capability is critical for achieving reliable adhesion between the 3D-printed structure and the underlying substrate, as misalignment by even a few microns can lead to delamination during subsequent processing or implantation.

The fabrication process begins with a prime-grade 75 mm silicon wafer bearing 300 nm of thermal oxide. A 6 µm thick layer of HD MicroSystems PI2611 polyimide is spin-coated onto the wafer and cured at 350°C in a nitrogen atmosphere to form a flexible, biocompatible base substrate. An adhesion promoter (HD Microsystems VM652) is applied to the wafer edge prior to polyimide deposition to prevent peeling during high-temperature curing. Next, a 500 nm thick chromium (Cr) layer is sputter-deposited onto the polyimide surface under 3 mTorr DC conditions. Chromium is selected as the sacrificial layer—not only for its excellent etch selectivity relative to polyimide and metals—but because it remains intact during the aggressive development step required to remove unpolymerized OrmoComp®. Conventional lift-off processes using organic photoresists (e.g., AZ series) fail in this context, as the developer (Ormodev) dissolves the resist, causing catastrophic loss of pattern fidelity.

To define the metal trace pattern, AZ-1512 photoresist is spin-coated onto the Cr layer and exposed through a photomask using a Süss Microtec MJB4 mask aligner with a 350 W mercury arc lamp at a dose of 70 mJ/cm². The photomask, designed in-house using CAD software and written via direct laser lithography on a Heidelberg system, contains the layout of all conductive traces, electrode sites, and bond pads. After development in AZ 300 MIF, the exposed Cr is etched using room-temperature Transene Chromium Etchant 1020 (a ceric ammonium nitrate/nitric acid mixture), transferring the trace pattern into the Cr layer. The remaining photoresist is stripped via sonication in acetone at 37 kHz for 5 minutes, leaving a Cr-etched template on the polyimide.

Before 3D printing, the wafer undergoes a 90-second oxygen plasma treatment in a March plasma etcher (300 mTorr, 100 W RF power) to activate the surface and improve print adhesion. The wafer is then mounted in the 3D printer, and the modified OrmoComp® resin is dispensed over the surface. The microscope objective is immersed in the liquid resin, and the femtosecond laser—typically a Coherent Chameleon Discovery system emitting 100 fs pulses at 780 nm wavelength and 80 MHz repetition rate, set to ~40 mW average power—is focused through a 20× Nikon immersion objective (NA 0.7) to initiate polymerization at the focal point. The resonant scanner rapidly moves the laser focus in the x-y plane while a piezoelectric stage controls the z-position, building the 3D structure voxel by voxel according to an STL file derived from the original CAD model.

A critical feature of the printing process is the automated surface-finding routine. As the objective approaches the substrate, the fluorescence signal from the fluorescein-doped resin increases sharply at the interface due to changes in refractive index and confinement. This fluorescence peak is detected in real time and used to calibrate the z-zero position for each electrode site, ensuring that the first layer of the 3D print adheres directly to the polyimide surface. Without this feedback, variations in wafer flatness or resin thickness could cause the print to float above the substrate, leading to mechanical instability.

After printing, the wafer is submerged in Ormodev developer for 12 hours to dissolve unpolymerized resin, followed by an isopropanol rinse. A 10-minute UV cure at 395 nm (2.8 mW/mm²) is then applied to further crosslink the polymer network, enhancing mechanical rigidity and chemical resistance. The wafer undergoes a second oxygen plasma clean to prepare the surface for metallization.

Metallization is performed via sputter deposition of a 15 nm titanium adhesion layer followed by 200 nm platinum in an Angstrom Engineering system. The sputtering is conducted under elevated pressure (3 mTorr for Ti, 10 mTorr for Pt) to promote non-directional, quasi-isotropic deposition. This ensures that even high-aspect-ratio or porous 3D structures receive uniform metal coverage on all surfaces, including interior walls and undersides—features that would remain uncoated in directional evaporation processes. The Ti/Pt stack provides excellent conductivity, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility, making it ideal for chronic neural interfaces.

The Cr sacrificial layer is then removed by immersing the wafer in Transene Chromium Etchant 1020 at 60°C for 15 minutes with agitation. Multiple fresh etchant baths are used sequentially to prevent re-deposition of dissolved Cr, and thorough deionized water rinsing follows to eliminate residual contaminants. This lift-off step defines the final metal traces and electrode structures, as only regions protected by the 3D-printed polymer or underlying Cr pattern retain metal.

Next, a 3 µm thick layer of parylene C is conformally deposited via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) in a Labcoater 4200 system. Parylene C is chosen for its excellent dielectric properties, moisture barrier performance, and biocompatibility. However, the electrode tips must be exposed to enable electrical contact with neural tissue. Instead of using photolithography and etching—which would require aligning a mask to micron-scale 3D features—the invention employs femtosecond laser ablation using a Coherent Monaco amplified fiber laser emitting 1035 nm pulses with up to 40 µJ energy at 1 MHz repetition rate. This laser is co-aligned with the 2PP writing laser in the same optical path, allowing seamless switching between printing and ablation modes.

During ablation, the laser raster-scans a 2–3 µm thick volume encompassing all electrode tips. The ultrashort pulse duration ensures an athermal ablation mechanism: material is removed via direct Coulomb explosion rather than melting, preventing thermal damage to surrounding structures or redeposition of molten debris. Real-time detection of ultraviolet emission from the ablation plume—collected via photomultiplier tubes in a standard two-photon detection configuration—provides feedback for calibrating laser fluence. When ablation reaches the underlying platinum, the emission spectrum shifts, signaling completion. This entire process takes less than one minute per 16-channel array and yields clean, debris-free electrode sites, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopy showing platinum enrichment at the tips.

For device singulation, the same high-pulse-energy laser is used to cut along the programmed outline of each probe, severing the polyimide and parylene layers. The wafer is then placed in warm water, causing the individual devices to float free due to differential thermal expansion. Finally, Omnetics connector pads on the flex cable are bonded to a miniature connector using anisotropic conductive film (ACF 7371 from 3M), completing the assembly.

Two specialized embodiments demonstrate the versatility of this platform. First, biomimetic “mosquito needle” electrodes are fabricated by designing sharp, tapered tips (resembling the proboscis of a mosquito) in the CAD model. These tips reduce insertion forces by concentrating stress at a fine point, enabling penetration through the dura mater of songbird brains without surgical removal—a significant advance that minimizes trauma and simplifies implantation. Second, macroporous stimulating electrodes are created by printing 3D scaffolds containing interconnected voids. During metallization, elevated-pressure sputtering ensures interior coating, yielding electrodes with up to five times the surface area of solid equivalents. Electrochemical testing shows a ~2× increase in charge storage capacity for solid pyramids versus planar electrodes, with porous variants offering even greater potential pending optimization of internal accessibility.

Electrophysiological validation confirms the functionality of the devices. In zebra finches, acute implants in HVC (a song-control nucleus) yield single-unit spikes with signal-to-noise ratios exceeding 8. In mice, recordings from the olfactory bulb capture local field potentials that precisely track the animal’s breathing rhythm, demonstrating high temporal fidelity. Impedance spectroscopy reveals a mean impedance of 200 kΩ at 1 kHz across 16 channels, suitable for extracellular recording. Future enhancements may include iridium oxide electrodeposition to further lower impedance or amorphous silicon carbide encapsulation to increase stiffness for longer electrodes.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, scalable, and highly customizable platform for fabricating 3D neural electrodes with micron-scale resolution, conformal metallization, and integrated insulation—all on flexible substrates compatible with chronic implantation. By unifying high-speed 2PP, resilient thin-film processing, and precision laser ablation, the method overcomes longstanding barriers in neural interface engineering and opens new avenues for high-density, volumetric neural recording and stimulation.
