# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of radiation therapy for the treatment of cancer and, more specifically, to a novel method and system for delivering spatially fractionated high-dose radiation in three dimensions within a tumor volume while minimizing radiation exposure to surrounding healthy tissues. The invention provides an advanced form of lattice radiotherapy—hereinafter referred to as “3D Lattice Radiotherapy” or “LRT”—that leverages modern radiation delivery platforms, including intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT), robotic radiosurgery systems, and charged particle beam therapy, to generate a controlled array of localized high-dose regions (“vertices”) interspersed with low-dose regions (“valleys”) throughout the interior of a target tumor. This approach capitalizes on emerging radiobiological phenomena such as the bystander effect and abscopal response, which are triggered by highly heterogeneous dose distributions and can lead to enhanced tumor cell death even in regions receiving sublethal radiation doses. Unlike conventional two-dimensional GRID radiotherapy, which delivers high-dose “pipes” primarily to superficial tissue layers and often irradiates normal structures outside the clinical target volume, the 3D LRT technique confines all high-dose vertices strictly within the tumor boundaries and achieves rapid dose fall-off between vertices and beyond the tumor margin. The invention is particularly suited for the treatment of bulky, hypoxic, or radioresistant tumors that are poorly responsive to standard uniform-dose radiotherapy regimens. Furthermore, the disclosed methodology enables integration of 3D LRT as an induction therapy prior to conventional or hypofractionated radiotherapy, thereby potentially amplifying therapeutic efficacy without increasing normal tissue toxicity. The invention encompasses both the technical implementation of the 3D dose lattice using clinically available linear accelerators and treatment planning systems, as well as the underlying biological rationale supporting its use as a paradigm-shifting oncologic intervention.

## BACKGROUND

Radiation therapy has long been a cornerstone of cancer treatment, relying on the principle that ionizing radiation induces lethal DNA damage in malignant cells while sparing adjacent normal tissues through precise targeting and fractionation. Conventional external beam radiotherapy typically employs relatively uniform dose distributions across the entire tumor volume, delivered in multiple fractions to allow normal tissue repair between sessions. However, certain tumor types—particularly those that are large, hypoxic, or inherently radioresistant—often exhibit poor response to such homogeneous dosing strategies. In these cases, alternative approaches that exploit non-uniform spatial dose patterns have been explored to overcome therapeutic resistance.

One such historical approach is GRID radiotherapy, first introduced in the 1930s using orthovoltage X-ray units equipped with physical shields containing arrays of circular or square apertures (typically 0.5–1.5 cm in diameter, spaced 1–2 cm apart). This technique produced a two-dimensional pattern of alternating high- and low-dose regions across the skin surface and superficial tumor layers. Despite its limited adoption due to technological constraints and concerns about normal tissue exposure, GRID therapy demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy in select patients with advanced, bulky tumors, often resulting in dramatic tumor regression even when only a fraction of the tumor received direct high-dose irradiation. These observations defied classical radiobiological models based solely on direct cellular kill and suggested the involvement of indirect mechanisms.

Recent advances in radiobiology have elucidated key phenomena that may explain the efficacy of spatially fractionated radiotherapy. Notably, the **bystander effect**—whereby unirradiated or low-dose-irradiated cells exhibit biological responses due to signals from nearby high-dose-irradiated cells—has been implicated in GRID-induced tumor control. Studies have identified specific molecular mediators released from high-dose zones, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), and ceramide, which propagate pro-apoptotic signals to neighboring tumor and endothelial cells in shielded (low-dose) regions. Additionally, compelling preclinical and clinical evidence points to a systemic **abscopal effect**, wherein localized GRID irradiation triggers immune-mediated regression of distant, non-irradiated metastases. These findings collectively suggest that the therapeutic impact of spatially fractionated radiotherapy extends far beyond the physical boundaries of the high-dose regions.

Despite these promising biological insights, the clinical utility of traditional 2D GRID therapy remains constrained by significant dosimetric limitations. The high-dose “pipes” generated by 2D GRID extend perpendicularly from the skin surface into the body, often depositing maximum dose in superficial tissues that lie outside the actual tumor volume. Consequently, critical normal structures near the tumor—such as the spinal cord, esophagus, or lung parenchyma—may receive unintended high doses, increasing the risk of toxicity. Moreover, because the dose pattern is fixed in two dimensions and aligned with the beam’s entry direction, it cannot conform to the complex three-dimensional geometry of deep-seated or irregularly shaped tumors. As a result, substantial volumes of healthy tissue surrounding the tumor are unnecessarily irradiated, limiting the feasibility of dose escalation or combination with other therapies.

Modern radiation oncology has witnessed the advent of highly conformal delivery technologies capable of sculpting complex 3D dose distributions with sub-millimeter precision. These include multileaf collimator (MLC)-based IMRT and VMAT systems (e.g., Varian’s RapidArc), robotic radiosurgery platforms (e.g., Accuray’s CyberKnife), and particle beam therapy with pencil-beam scanning (e.g., proton or carbon ion systems). These modalities enable the creation of multiple, tightly focused high-dose spots (“vertices”) distributed throughout a target volume, with steep dose gradients between them. Such capabilities open the door to transforming the concept of spatially fractionated radiotherapy from a crude 2D surface technique into a sophisticated 3D intratumoral strategy.

Prior art does not disclose a systematic method for designing and delivering a three-dimensional lattice of high-dose vertices within a tumor volume that simultaneously satisfies three critical criteria: (1) confinement of all high-dose regions strictly within the gross tumor volume (GTV) or clinical target volume (CTV); (2) generation of pronounced peak-to-valley dose differentials (e.g., peak doses ≥12 Gy with valley doses ≤3 Gy) sufficient to activate radiobiological bystander and immune responses; and (3) minimization of dose to surrounding normal tissues through rapid fall-off beyond the lattice structure. While stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) and radiosurgery employ focal high-dose delivery, they aim for uniform coverage of the target and do not intentionally create internal low-dose valleys. Similarly, microbeam radiation therapy (MRT)—an experimental synchrotron-based technique using micron-scale beams—shares conceptual similarities but is not feasible in standard clinical settings due to infrastructure requirements.

Thus, there exists a clear and unmet need for a clinically implementable, biologically informed radiation therapy technique that harnesses the therapeutic potential of spatial dose heterogeneity while overcoming the anatomical and dosimetric shortcomings of historical GRID approaches. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel 3D Lattice Radiotherapy framework that integrates advanced delivery hardware, optimized treatment planning, and emerging radiobiological principles to achieve superior tumor control with reduced toxicity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for delivering three-dimensional lattice radiotherapy (3D LRT) to a tumor volume in a patient, wherein a plurality of localized high-dose radiation regions—referred to as “vertices”—are strategically positioned within the interior of the tumor, separated by intervening low-dose regions—referred to as “valleys”—to create a spatially fractionated dose distribution that maximizes tumor cell kill through both direct radiation effects and indirect biological mechanisms, while minimizing radiation exposure to surrounding healthy tissues. The invention is predicated on the discovery that confining high-dose vertices exclusively within the tumor volume and achieving a peak-to-valley dose ratio of at least 4:1 (e.g., peak doses of 12–18 Gy and valley doses of ≤3 Gy) significantly enhances therapeutic efficacy compared to conventional uniform-dose radiotherapy or historical two-dimensional GRID techniques.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a method of treating a tumor in a patient, the method including: (a) acquiring imaging data of the patient to define a three-dimensional tumor volume; (b) determining a lattice configuration comprising a number, spatial arrangement, and separation distance of high-dose vertices within the tumor volume, wherein each vertex is configured to receive a radiation dose of at least 12 Gy, and wherein the separation between adjacent vertices is selected to ensure that the dose in the intervening valleys does not exceed 3 Gy; (c) generating a treatment plan using a radiation therapy planning system, the plan configured to deliver the lattice configuration via a radiation delivery apparatus selected from the group consisting of a multileaf collimator-equipped linear accelerator operating in IMRT or VMAT mode, a robotic radiosurgery system, and a charged particle beam therapy system with spot-scanning capability; and (d) delivering the treatment plan to the patient, thereby inducing tumor regression through a combination of direct cellular damage in high-dose vertices and bystander-mediated apoptosis in low-dose valleys.

In another aspect, the invention provides a radiation therapy system comprising: a radiation source capable of delivering high-energy photons or charged particles; a beam-shaping device operatively coupled to the radiation source, the beam-shaping device selected from a dynamic multileaf collimator, a robotic arm with variable collimators, or a magnetic scanning nozzle; a treatment planning computer programmed to calculate a 3D dose lattice within a user-defined tumor volume, the lattice comprising discrete high-dose vertices separated by low-dose valleys, with all vertices contained entirely within the tumor volume; and a delivery control unit configured to execute the treatment plan by dynamically modulating the beam-shaping device during irradiation to conform the high-dose regions to the predetermined vertex locations.

The invention further encompasses the use of 3D LRT as an induction therapy prior to a course of conventional or hypofractionated radiotherapy. In this embodiment, a single fraction of 3D LRT is administered to the tumor to initiate robust bystander and immune signaling cascades, followed within days or weeks by standard radiotherapy designed to uniformly treat the entire tumor volume. This sequential approach leverages the initial high-dose lattice to “prime” the tumor microenvironment, rendering it more susceptible to subsequent radiation, thereby improving overall tumor control without increasing cumulative normal tissue dose.

Key advantages of the invention include: (1) strict intratumoral confinement of high-dose regions, eliminating unnecessary irradiation of superficial or peripheral normal tissues; (2) preservation of the peak-to-valley dose architecture essential for triggering radiobiological bystander and abscopal effects; (3) compatibility with existing clinical radiotherapy platforms without requiring hardware modifications; (4) adaptability to tumors of varying size, shape, and location through customizable lattice parameters; and (5) potential for integration into multimodal treatment regimens involving chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted agents.

The invention is applicable to a wide range of solid tumors, particularly those that are bulky (>100 cc), hypoxic, or resistant to conventional radiotherapy, including but not limited to non-small cell lung cancer, pancreatic adenocarcinoma, sarcomas, head and neck cancers, and recurrent gynecological malignancies. By transforming spatially fractionated radiotherapy from a historical curiosity into a precision-guided, biologically rational treatment modality, the present invention represents a significant advancement in the field of radiation oncology.

## DESCRIPTION OF INVENTION

The present invention introduces a novel and clinically viable approach to spatially fractionated radiotherapy through the development and implementation of three-dimensional Lattice Radiotherapy (3D LRT). This technique fundamentally reimagines the delivery of high-dose radiation by replacing the outdated two-dimensional GRID paradigm with a volumetric array of precisely localized high-dose vertices embedded entirely within the tumor mass. The core innovation lies not merely in the geometric arrangement of dose peaks, but in the deliberate orchestration of radiobiological responses—both local and systemic—through controlled spatial heterogeneity, all while adhering to stringent normal tissue sparing constraints made possible by modern delivery technology.

The foundation of 3D LRT rests on three interdependent pillars: anatomical precision, dosimetric optimization, and biological intent. Anatomically, the method begins with high-resolution imaging—typically computed tomography (CT), optionally fused with positron emission tomography (PET) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)—to delineate the gross tumor volume (GTV) and, if applicable, the clinical target volume (CTV). Unlike 2D GRID, which projects a fixed aperture pattern onto the skin regardless of tumor depth or shape, 3D LRT requires that every high-dose vertex be placed exclusively within the defined tumor boundary. This ensures that the maximum radiation dose is deposited where it is therapeutically relevant, rather than in overlying or adjacent normal structures.

Dosimetrically, the lattice is engineered to achieve a pronounced peak-to-valley dose differential. Based on preclinical and clinical evidence, the invention specifies that each vertex should receive a minimum dose of 12 Gray (Gy), with preferred embodiments utilizing doses between 15 and 18 Gy in a single fraction. Concurrently, the dose in the valleys—the regions between vertices—must remain at or below 3 Gy to preserve the spatial fractionation effect while avoiding excessive cumulative dose to any single normal cell population. The separation distance between adjacent vertices is a critical parameter, determined by the physical characteristics of the delivery system (e.g., beam penumbra, focal spot size) and the desired valley dose. For photon-based systems such as linear accelerators with dynamic multileaf collimators (MLCs), a center-to-center vertex spacing of 1.5 to 3.0 cm is typically sufficient to achieve the required dose fall-off. In robotic systems like CyberKnife, which use small circular collimators (e.g., 5–15 mm diameter) and non-coplanar beam arrangements, similar or slightly larger separations may be employed to maintain valley doses below threshold. Importantly, the vertex dose distribution need not be perfectly spherical; ellipsoidal or irregular high-dose islands are permissible as long as they remain fully contained within the tumor and maintain adequate separation from neighbors.

Biologically, the invention exploits the non-targeted effects of ionizing radiation. When tumor cells in a high-dose vertex undergo catastrophic damage, they release a cascade of signaling molecules—including TNF-α, TRAIL, and ceramide—that diffuse into surrounding low-dose regions, inducing apoptosis in otherwise radioresistant or hypoxic cells. This bystander effect effectively extends the cytotoxic reach of the radiation beyond the physical high-dose zones. Furthermore, the inflammatory milieu created by 3D LRT may activate dendritic cells and promote T-cell infiltration, potentially leading to systemic anti-tumor immunity manifesting as abscopal regression of metastatic lesions. These mechanisms are maximized when high-dose vertices are distributed throughout the tumor volume, ensuring broad coverage of the tumor microenvironment.

The invention is implemented using commercially available radiation therapy platforms without modification. In one embodiment, a Varian TrueBeam or similar linear accelerator equipped with a high-definition MLC is used in RapidArc (volumetric modulated arc therapy) mode. Multiple non-coplanar arcs (e.g., 4–8) are planned, with the MLC dynamically shaping the beam to sequentially “paint” each vertex location as the gantry rotates. The treatment planning system (e.g., Eclipse) is instructed to optimize for maximum dose heterogeneity within the GTV while constraining doses to organs-at-risk (OARs) according to standard tolerance limits. In another embodiment, the Accuray CyberKnife system is employed, leveraging its robotic arm and real-time image guidance to deliver 40–60 non-coplanar beams per vertex through a small fixed collimator (e.g., 7.5 mm). The system’s Synchrony respiratory tracking ensures that vertex positions remain accurate despite tumor motion, preserving the integrity of the lattice during delivery. A third embodiment envisions the use of proton or carbon ion therapy with pencil-beam scanning, wherein the Bragg peak is precisely positioned at each vertex location without the need for physical collimation, offering even sharper dose gradients and negligible exit dose.

Validation studies confirm the deliverability and accuracy of 3D LRT plans. Dosimetric verification using Gafchromic film in anthropomorphic phantoms demonstrates agreement between planned and measured dose distributions within 3% dose difference and 1 mm distance-to-agreement, confirming clinical feasibility. Comparative analyses against 2D GRID show that 3D LRT achieves equivalent or superior tumor dose coverage while significantly reducing integral dose to normal tissues—particularly in deep-seated tumors where 2D GRID would irradiate large volumes of healthy parenchyma.

The invention also contemplates sequential treatment protocols. In a preferred clinical application, a single fraction of 3D LRT (e.g., 15 Gy to vertices) is administered as induction therapy, followed 1–7 days later by a standard course of conventionally fractionated radiotherapy (e.g., 1.8–2.0 Gy per fraction to the entire tumor). Preclinical data suggest that the initial high-dose lattice “primes” the tumor by disrupting vasculature, increasing oxidative stress, and upregulating death receptors, thereby sensitizing residual cells to subsequent radiation. This approach may improve local control in radioresistant histologies without increasing late toxicity, as the total dose to normal tissues remains governed by the conventional phase.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, translatable framework for 3D Lattice Radiotherapy that merges cutting-edge delivery technology with mechanistic radiobiology to overcome the limitations of historical spatially fractionated techniques. By ensuring that high-dose regions are confined to the tumor, valleys are sufficiently low to maintain fractionation, and normal tissues are spared through conformal delivery, 3D LRT offers a safe, effective, and biologically rational strategy for treating challenging malignancies.

## EQUIVALENTS

The present invention has been described in terms of specific embodiments and illustrative examples; however, it will be understood by those skilled in the art that various modifications, substitutions, and alterations may be made without departing from the spirit and scope of the invention as defined by the appended claims. The term “3D Lattice Radiotherapy” or “LRT” as used herein refers broadly to any radiation therapy technique that delivers a spatially fractionated dose distribution comprising multiple discrete high-dose regions (vertices) entirely contained within a tumor volume, separated by low-dose regions (valleys), wherein the peak dose is at least 12 Gy and the valley dose does not exceed 3 Gy, regardless of the specific delivery platform employed. Thus, while the examples provided utilize photon-based linear accelerators (e.g., Varian RapidArc) and robotic radiosurgery systems (e.g., CyberKnife), the invention equally encompasses implementations using charged particle therapy (protons, carbon ions) with spot-scanning nozzles, as well as future radiation delivery technologies capable of generating conformal, high-gradient dose distributions.

Furthermore, the lattice configuration—including the number of vertices, their spatial arrangement, separation distance, and individual dose levels—is not rigidly fixed but may be optimized based on tumor size, shape, location, histology, and proximity to critical structures. For instance, a small tumor (<50 cc) may be treated with only one or two high-dose vertices, whereas a large, irregular mass may require dozens of vertices arranged in a quasi-random or optimized pattern rather than a symmetric grid. The invention does not require mathematical regularity in the lattice; the term “lattice” is used descriptively to convey the concept of a structured array of dose peaks, not to impose geometric constraints.

Similarly, the biological mechanisms invoked—bystander effect, abscopal response, vascular disruption—are presented as explanatory frameworks supported by current evidence, but the therapeutic benefit of 3D LRT does not depend on the exclusive operation of any single pathway. The invention remains valid even if additional or alternative radiobiological processes are discovered to contribute to its efficacy.

The use of 3D LRT as an induction therapy prior to conventional radiotherapy is a preferred embodiment, but the invention also includes standalone applications of 3D LRT as definitive treatment, as well as combinations with chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted molecular agents. Real-time motion management systems such as Synchrony (Accuray) or Calypso (Varian) are advantageous for maintaining lattice fidelity in moving targets but are not strictly necessary for static tumors.

Finally, all numerical values provided (e.g., 12 Gy peak dose, 3 Gy valley dose, 2 cm vertex separation) represent practical ranges derived from current clinical and preclinical data; minor deviations outside these ranges that still achieve the core objective of intratumoral high-dose confinement with significant peak-to-valley differentiation are considered equivalent under the doctrine of equivalents. Accordingly, the full scope of the invention is defined not by the illustrative details herein but by the breadth of the claims that follow.
