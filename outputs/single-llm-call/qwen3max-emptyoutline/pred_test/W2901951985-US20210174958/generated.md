# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with early detection playing a critical role in improving patient outcomes and reducing the overall burden of disease. Despite significant advances in diagnostic technologies and public health initiatives promoting population-level screening, adherence to existing cancer screening protocols remains suboptimal, and many cancers are still diagnosed at advanced stages when therapeutic options are limited and survival rates are poor. For example, colorectal cancer (CRC), which is among the most prevalent malignancies globally, continues to be the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths in both men and women in the United States. Alarmingly, nearly 60% of CRC cases are detected only after regional or distant metastases have occurred, significantly diminishing the likelihood of curative intervention. Similarly, pancreatic cancer—a disease with an even more aggressive clinical course—is diagnosed at late stages in approximately 80% of patients, underscoring the urgent need for more effective and accessible early detection strategies.

Current standard-of-care screening methods for cancer, including colonoscopy for CRC, mammography for breast cancer, and low-dose computed tomography for lung cancer, are often associated with limitations that hinder their widespread adoption and effectiveness. These limitations include invasiveness, procedural discomfort, logistical complexity, high cost, and variable sensitivity and specificity—particularly for early-stage disease and precancerous lesions. As a result, there is a growing interest in the development of minimally invasive, blood-based assays that can detect cancer at its earliest and most treatable stages. Among these emerging approaches, liquid biopsies based on circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) have garnered considerable attention from both academic researchers and commercial entities.

cfDNA refers to fragmented DNA molecules that are released into the bloodstream through processes such as apoptosis and necrosis of cells throughout the body. In individuals with cancer, a fraction of this cfDNA originates from tumor cells and is referred to as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). ctDNA carries molecular signatures of the tumor, including somatic mutations, chromosomal rearrangements, copy number variations (CNVs), and epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation patterns. These tumor-derived alterations distinguish ctDNA from cfDNA derived from non-malignant tissues and provide a potential basis for cancer detection. Moreover, studies have shown that ctDNA fragments tend to be shorter on average than cfDNA from healthy individuals, offering another potential discriminatory feature.

However, the utility of ctDNA-based liquid biopsies for early cancer detection faces several significant challenges. Chief among these is the extremely low abundance of ctDNA in the total cfDNA pool, particularly in early-stage disease and premalignant conditions. In late-stage cancers, ctDNA may constitute 5–10% or more of the total cfDNA, but in early-stage tumors, this fraction can be as low as 0.01% to 1.0%, and even lower in precancerous states. This low tumor fraction (TF) poses substantial technical hurdles for detection, requiring ultra-deep sequencing and highly sensitive analytical methods to distinguish true tumor-derived signals from background noise and sequencing artifacts. Furthermore, biological phenomena such as clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP)—a condition in which hematopoietic stem cells acquire somatic mutations that can mimic tumor-derived variants—can introduce false-positive signals that confound interpretation. Practical considerations, including the volume of blood required for analysis and the associated costs of high-depth sequencing, also present barriers to the implementation of ctDNA-based assays in large-scale population screening programs.

In light of these limitations, alternative approaches to cfDNA-based cancer detection have been proposed that move beyond the exclusive focus on ctDNA. These approaches recognize that the presence of a tumor can induce systemic changes in the host that are reflected not only in tumor-derived DNA but also in the broader cfDNA landscape. Emerging evidence suggests that cancer cells interact extensively with surrounding and distant cellular populations, including immune cells, fibroblasts, and platelets, within the tumor microenvironment and systemically. This “tumor education” of host cells can lead to alterations in gene expression, epigenetic states, and cellular turnover that are detectable in the circulation. For instance, platelets from cancer patients have been shown to harbor distinct messenger RNA (mRNA) profiles compared to those from healthy individuals, and immune cell populations exhibit altered apoptosis patterns and relative abundances in cancer patients. These systemic changes may be captured in cfDNA through fragmentation patterns, methylation signatures, and other genomic features that reflect the cellular origins and physiological states of the DNA-releasing cells.

Critically, because cfDNA fragmentation and methylation patterns are influenced by the chromatin structure and transcriptional activity of the cells from which they originate, the cfDNA profile can serve as a composite readout of the body’s cellular composition and functional state. Thus, even in the absence of detectable tumor-specific mutations, the global cfDNA landscape may contain subtle but informative signals of early neoplasia. However, identifying these signals requires analytical methods capable of discerning complex, high-dimensional patterns across the entire genome without prior assumptions about specific genomic loci or alterations. Machine learning (ML) offers a powerful framework for this purpose, enabling the discovery of disease-associated patterns directly from raw genomic data.

The application of ML to cfDNA-based cancer detection, however, is not without its own challenges. A primary concern is the risk of overfitting and the learning of spurious associations driven by confounding variables rather than true biological signals. Confounders—factors that correlate with both the disease label and technical or demographic variables—can severely compromise the generalizability and clinical validity of ML models. Common confounders in biomarker studies include age, sex, batch effects from laboratory processing, institutional differences in sample collection and handling, and temporal variations in assay performance. If not properly controlled, these confounders can lead to inflated performance estimates during model development and failure to generalize to new, independent populations. Traditional cross-validation (CV) methods, such as random k-fold CV, often fail to account for these structured sources of variation, resulting in overly optimistic assessments of model performance.

To address these challenges, there is a pressing need for robust analytical frameworks that integrate unbiased, genome-wide cfDNA profiling with rigorous ML methodologies designed to mitigate the influence of confounding variables. Such frameworks must be capable of capturing both tumor-derived and non-tumor-derived signals, operating effectively at low sequencing depths to enable cost-effective population screening, and demonstrating consistent performance across diverse clinical and technical contexts. The present invention provides such a framework, offering a novel approach to early cancer detection that leverages the full informational content of cfDNA while ensuring the reliability and generalizability of the resulting diagnostic models.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides systems, methods, and compositions for the detection and diagnosis of cancer, particularly early-stage cancer, using a machine learning-based analysis of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) profiles derived from whole-genome sequencing of plasma samples. Unlike conventional liquid biopsy approaches that focus exclusively on tumor-derived mutations or copy number alterations, the invention utilizes an unbiased, genome-wide representation of cfDNA that captures both tumor-intrinsic and tumor-extrinsic biological signals. This approach enables the identification of complex, high-dimensional patterns in cfDNA that are associated with the presence of cancer, including signals arising from systemic changes in host cell populations induced by the tumor microenvironment.

The invention encompasses a comprehensive analytical pipeline that begins with the extraction and sequencing of cfDNA from a blood sample, followed by the generation of a feature vector representing the cfDNA profile across the entire genome. In one embodiment, features are derived by counting the number of sequencing fragments overlapping each protein-coding gene, thereby simultaneously capturing information related to copy number variation and epigenetic states reflected in cfDNA fragmentation patterns. The feature vectors are then normalized to account for technical biases such as GC content, mappability, and sequencing depth, and standardized for input into machine learning models.

A key innovation of the invention lies in its use of specialized cross-validation procedures designed to rigorously control for known confounding variables that can compromise the generalizability of machine learning models. These confounder-aware cross-validation methods include binned-age cross-validation to account for age-related differences in cancer prevalence and cfDNA profiles; k-batch cross-validation to mitigate batch-to-batch technical variability; ordered k-batch cross-validation to address temporal drift in laboratory processes; and balanced k-batch cross-validation to control for institution-specific biases in sample collection and handling. By evaluating model performance under these stringent validation schemes, the invention ensures that the resulting classifiers are robust to real-world sources of variation and capable of generalizing to new populations.

The machine learning models employed in the invention may include support vector machines, logistic regression, or other suitable classification algorithms, optionally combined with dimensionality reduction techniques such as principal component analysis or truncated singular value decomposition. The models are trained to distinguish between cancer and non-cancer samples based on the cfDNA feature vectors, with performance metrics such as area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and sensitivity at fixed specificity used to assess diagnostic accuracy.

In exemplary embodiments, the invention has demonstrated high performance in the detection of early-stage colorectal cancer, achieving a mean sensitivity of 85% at 85% specificity using standard k-fold cross-validation, and maintaining robust performance—ranging from 71% to 85% sensitivity at 85% specificity—under various confounder-controlled cross-validation schemes. Importantly, the invention operates at relatively low sequencing depths (approximately 9X genome coverage), making it cost-effective and feasible for population-level screening, and utilizes modest blood volumes (e.g., 250 μL of plasma), enhancing patient compliance.

The invention is not limited to colorectal cancer but is broadly applicable to the detection of other malignancies and pathological conditions. The genome-wide, unbiased nature of the cfDNA profiling approach allows for the discovery of disease-specific signatures without requiring prior knowledge of target mutations or biomarkers. Furthermore, the framework can be extended to incorporate additional analytes, such as proteins or other nucleic acid species, to provide orthogonal signals that further enhance diagnostic performance.

In summary, the present invention provides a novel, robust, and scalable platform for early cancer detection that overcomes the limitations of existing liquid biopsy approaches by leveraging the full informational content of cfDNA in conjunction with rigorous machine learning methodologies designed to ensure clinical validity and generalizability.

## TERMS

As used herein, the term “circulating cell-free DNA” or “cfDNA” refers to fragmented DNA molecules that are present in the bloodstream and originate from the natural turnover of cells throughout the body. cfDNA includes both tumor-derived DNA (circulating tumor DNA or ctDNA) and DNA derived from non-tumor cells, such as hematopoietic, stromal, and other somatic cells. The term encompasses DNA fragments of varying lengths, typically ranging from approximately 150 to 200 base pairs, although shorter and longer fragments may also be present.

The term “tumor fraction” or “TF” refers to the proportion of cfDNA in a sample that is derived from tumor cells. TF is expressed as a percentage or decimal fraction and represents a critical parameter in liquid biopsy applications, as it influences the detectability of tumor-derived signals. In early-stage cancer, TF is typically very low, often less than 1%, whereas in advanced disease, TF may be substantially higher.

“Whole-genome sequencing” or “WGS” as used herein denotes a sequencing methodology that generates reads distributed across the entire human genome, as opposed to targeted sequencing approaches that focus on specific genomic regions. The WGS employed in the present invention is performed at relatively low depth, typically in the range of 5X to 15X genome coverage, which balances cost-effectiveness with sufficient signal for machine learning-based pattern recognition.

A “feature vector” refers to a numerical representation of a biological sample, wherein each element of the vector corresponds to a quantifiable attribute or measurement derived from the sample. In the context of the present invention, feature vectors are constructed from cfDNA sequencing data, with each feature representing the normalized count of sequencing fragments overlapping a specific genomic region, such as a protein-coding gene.

“Machine learning” or “ML” encompasses a set of computational algorithms that enable systems to automatically learn and improve from experience without being explicitly programmed. In the present invention, ML refers specifically to supervised learning techniques used for classification tasks, wherein models are trained on labeled data (e.g., cancer vs. non-cancer samples) to predict the class label of new, unseen samples.

“Cross-validation” or “CV” is a statistical method for evaluating the performance and generalizability of machine learning models. Standard k-fold CV involves partitioning the dataset into k subsets, training the model on k-1 subsets, and testing on the remaining subset, with the process repeated k times. The present invention extends this concept to include specialized CV procedures designed to control for specific confounding variables, as detailed herein.

A “confounding variable” or “confounder” is a factor that is associated with both the outcome of interest (e.g., cancer status) and one or more predictor variables (e.g., technical or demographic features), potentially leading to spurious associations if not properly accounted for. Examples of confounders in cfDNA-based cancer detection include age, sex, batch effects, institutional source, and processing date.

“Sensitivity” refers to the ability of a diagnostic test to correctly identify individuals with the disease (true positive rate), calculated as the number of true positives divided by the sum of true positives and false negatives. “Specificity” refers to the ability to correctly identify individuals without the disease (true negative rate), calculated as the number of true negatives divided by the sum of true negatives and false positives. Performance is often evaluated at a fixed specificity (e.g., 85%) to assess the corresponding sensitivity.

The term “area under the curve” or “AUC” refers to the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, a graphical plot that illustrates the diagnostic ability of a binary classifier system as its discrimination threshold is varied. AUC values range from 0.5 (no discriminative ability) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination), with higher values indicating better performance.

“Dimensionality reduction” refers to techniques used to reduce the number of random variables under consideration by obtaining a set of principal variables. In the present invention, methods such as principal component analysis (PCA) and truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) may be applied to transform high-dimensional feature vectors into a lower-dimensional space while preserving the most informative variance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. CIRCULATING ANALYTES AND CELLULAR DECONSTRUCTION WITH BIOLOGICAL ASSAYS

The foundation of the present invention lies in the recognition that circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) serves as a rich and dynamic reservoir of biological information that reflects not only the presence of tumor-derived genetic material but also the systemic physiological responses elicited by neoplastic processes. Traditional approaches to liquid biopsy have predominantly focused on the identification of tumor-specific genomic alterations—such as point mutations, insertions, deletions, and copy number variations—within the ctDNA fraction of cfDNA. While these approaches have demonstrated utility in monitoring treatment response and detecting minimal residual disease in advanced cancer settings, their application to early cancer detection is hampered by the extremely low abundance of ctDNA in early-stage disease and the technical challenges associated with distinguishing true tumor-derived signals from background noise and biological artifacts.

In contrast, the present invention adopts a holistic view of cfDNA as a composite analyte that integrates signals from multiple cellular sources, including both malignant and non-malignant cell populations. This perspective is grounded in the growing body of evidence demonstrating that cancer induces profound alterations in the host organism that extend far beyond the tumor mass itself. These systemic changes, often referred to as “tumor education,” manifest in the form of altered gene expression profiles, epigenetic reprogramming, and shifts in cellular composition and turnover within the immune system, stromal compartment, and other tissues. Critically, these alterations are imprinted onto the cfDNA landscape through mechanisms that govern DNA fragmentation, methylation, and release into the circulation.

For example, studies have shown that platelets from cancer patients exhibit distinct mRNA expression profiles compared to those from healthy individuals, a phenomenon attributed to the uptake of tumor-derived biomolecules and subsequent activation of signaling pathways. Similarly, immune cell populations in cancer patients display altered apoptosis patterns and relative abundances, with clinical implications for prognosis and therapeutic response. These systemic changes are not merely bystander effects but represent active biological responses to the presence of neoplasia that can be harnessed for diagnostic purposes.

The cfDNA fragmentation pattern—defined by the distribution of fragment lengths and genomic positions—provides a direct readout of the chromatin structure and transcriptional activity of the cells from which the DNA originates. Nucleosomes, the fundamental units of chromatin, protect approximately 147 base pairs of DNA from nuclease digestion, resulting in cfDNA fragments that are typically multiples of this length. However, the precise fragmentation pattern varies depending on the cell type, physiological state, and disease context. In cancer, alterations in nucleosome positioning, histone modifications, and DNA methylation can lead to cell-type-specific fragmentation signatures that are detectable in the circulation.

Moreover, the methylation status of cfDNA reflects the epigenetic landscape of the source cells, providing another layer of information that can be exploited for cancer detection. Tumor cells often exhibit global hypomethylation coupled with locus-specific hypermethylation, particularly at promoter regions of tumor suppressor genes. These methylation changes can be detected in cfDNA and used to infer the presence of malignancy. However, rather than focusing on predefined methylation markers, the present invention leverages the genome-wide methylation and fragmentation patterns inherent in cfDNA sequencing data to discover novel, data-driven signatures of cancer.

By moving beyond the narrow focus on ctDNA and embracing the full complexity of cfDNA as a systemic biomarker, the present invention enables the detection of early-stage cancer through the integration of both tumor-derived and non-tumor-derived signals. This approach is particularly well-suited for the detection of cancers that shed minimal amounts of ctDNA, such as early-stage colorectal cancer, where traditional mutation-based assays may lack sufficient sensitivity. The biological assays employed in the present invention are designed to capture this rich informational content through whole-genome sequencing of cfDNA, followed by sophisticated computational analysis to extract meaningful patterns from the high-dimensional data.

### II. SAMPLE PREPARATION

The successful implementation of the present invention begins with the careful collection, processing, and preparation of biological samples to ensure the integrity and quality of cfDNA for downstream analysis. In accordance with the invention, blood samples are collected from subjects using standard venipuncture techniques, with plasma separated from cellular components through centrifugation. The use of EDTA as an anticoagulant is preferred to minimize ex vivo DNA degradation and preserve the native cfDNA profile. Plasma samples are then stored at −80°C until further processing to prevent degradation and maintain sample stability.

For cfDNA extraction, the invention employs commercially available kits optimized for the isolation of low-molecular-weight DNA from plasma, such as the MagMAX cfDNA Isolation Kit (Applied Biosystems). This kit utilizes magnetic bead-based technology to efficiently capture cfDNA fragments while minimizing contamination from high-molecular-weight genomic DNA derived from lysed white blood cells. The extraction protocol is carefully controlled to ensure consistent yield and purity across samples, with quality control measures implemented to verify the absence of genomic DNA contamination and assess cfDNA concentration and fragment size distribution.

Following extraction, cfDNA is converted into sequencing libraries using established protocols, such as the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs). This process involves end repair, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification to generate libraries compatible with next-generation sequencing platforms. The library preparation is performed under stringent conditions to minimize technical artifacts and ensure uniform representation of cfDNA fragments. Quality control checks, including assessment of library size distribution and quantification, are conducted prior to sequencing to confirm library integrity and suitability for analysis.

Sequencing is carried out on high-throughput platforms, such as the Illumina sequencing system, using paired-end sequencing chemistry to generate reads of sufficient length for accurate alignment and fragment reconstruction. The sequencing depth is optimized to balance cost-effectiveness with analytical sensitivity, with typical coverage ranging from 5X to 15X of the human genome. This relatively low depth is a deliberate design choice that enables the processing of large sample volumes at a fraction of the cost of ultra-deep sequencing approaches, making the invention suitable for population-level screening applications.

After sequencing, raw reads are subjected to a series of bioinformatic processing steps to generate high-quality aligned data. Reads are aligned to the human reference genome (e.g., GRCh38) using robust alignment algorithms such as BWA-MEM, with parameters optimized for cfDNA fragment characteristics. Quality control metrics are then applied to filter out low-quality alignments, remove duplicate reads, and assess overall data quality. Samples failing to meet predefined quality thresholds—such as insufficient read depth, excessive GC bias, or evidence of contamination—are excluded from further analysis to ensure the reliability of downstream results.

This rigorous sample preparation workflow ensures that the cfDNA profiles used for machine learning analysis are representative of the true biological state of the subject, free from technical artifacts that could confound interpretation. The consistency and reproducibility of this workflow are critical to the success of the invention, as they provide the foundation for the robust and generalizable diagnostic models described herein.

### III. EXAMPLE SYSTEMS

The present invention may be implemented within a variety of integrated systems designed to facilitate the end-to-end process of cfDNA-based cancer detection, from sample collection to diagnostic output. In one exemplary embodiment, the system comprises a sample collection module, a laboratory processing module, a bioinformatics analysis module, and a machine learning classification module, all interconnected through a secure data management infrastructure.

The sample collection module includes standardized protocols and materials for blood draw, plasma separation, and sample storage, ensuring consistency across different collection sites and operators. This module may be deployed in clinical settings, such as hospitals, clinics, or mobile health units, enabling broad access to the screening test.

The laboratory processing module encompasses the physical infrastructure and automated workflows for cfDNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing. This module may be centralized in a high-throughput reference laboratory or distributed across multiple regional facilities, with quality control measures implemented to ensure uniformity of processing. Automation is employed wherever possible to minimize human error and enhance reproducibility.

The bioinformatics analysis module consists of a suite of computational tools and pipelines for processing raw sequencing data into normalized feature vectors. This module handles tasks such as read alignment, quality control, feature extraction, and normalization, with algorithms optimized for cfDNA characteristics. The output of this module is a standardized dataset ready for input into the machine learning classification module.

The machine learning classification module implements the core analytical engine of the invention, comprising the trained models and cross-validation frameworks described herein. This module receives the normalized feature vectors and applies the appropriate classification algorithm to generate a diagnostic prediction, along with associated confidence metrics. The output is presented in a user-friendly format suitable for clinical interpretation, such as a probability score or binary classification (cancer vs. non-cancer).

In another embodiment, the system may be implemented as a cloud-based platform, allowing remote access to the analytical tools and enabling real-time updates to the machine learning models as new data becomes available. This architecture supports scalability, facilitates collaboration across institutions, and ensures that the latest scientific advancements are rapidly integrated into the diagnostic workflow.

### IV. MACHINE LEARNING TOOLS

The machine learning tools employed in the present invention are selected and optimized to address the unique challenges of cfDNA-based cancer detection, including high dimensionality, low signal-to-noise ratio, and the presence of confounding variables. The primary classification algorithms considered include support vector machines (SVM) and logistic regression, both of which have demonstrated strong performance in high-dimensional biomedical classification tasks.

Support vector machines are particularly well-suited for this application due to their ability to handle high-dimensional feature spaces and their robustness to overfitting when properly regularized. SVMs work by finding the optimal hyperplane that separates cancer and non-cancer samples in the feature space, with the margin between the classes maximized to enhance generalization. Various kernel functions, such as linear, polynomial, or radial basis function (RBF) kernels, may be explored to capture non-linear relationships in the data.

Logistic regression, while simpler in form, offers advantages in terms of interpretability and computational efficiency. It models the probability of cancer as a logistic function of the input features, with regularization techniques (e.g., L1 or L2 penalties) applied to prevent overfitting and promote feature selection. The choice between SVM and logistic regression is determined empirically through cross-validation, with the best-performing model selected for final deployment.

In addition to the base classifiers, the invention incorporates dimensionality reduction techniques to mitigate the curse of dimensionality and enhance model performance. Principal component analysis (PCA) and truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) are employed to project the high-dimensional feature vectors into a lower-dimensional space that captures the majority of the variance in the data. The number of components retained is optimized through cross-validation to balance information retention with noise reduction.

Hyperparameter optimization is performed using random search or grid search within the training data of each cross-validation fold, ensuring that the model configuration is tailored to the specific characteristics of the dataset. All machine learning implementations leverage established software libraries such as Scikit-learn, which provide robust, well-tested implementations of the algorithms and utilities.

The integration of these machine learning tools within a rigorous cross-validation framework ensures that the resulting models are not only accurate but also robust to real-world sources of variation, as detailed in the following sections.

### V. SELECTION OF INPUT FEATURES

The selection of input features is a critical step in the development of machine learning models for cfDNA-based cancer detection, as it directly influences the model’s ability to capture relevant biological signals while minimizing noise and redundancy. In the present invention, features are derived from whole-genome sequencing data through a gene-centric featurization strategy that counts the number of sequencing fragments overlapping each annotated protein-coding gene.

This approach offers several advantages over alternative featurization methods. First, by aggregating fragment counts at the gene level, the method simultaneously captures information related to copy number variation (through deviations in expected fragment counts) and epigenetic states (through gene-specific fragmentation patterns influenced by chromatin structure and transcriptional activity). Second, the use of protein-coding genes as genomic bins provides a biologically meaningful framework that aligns with known functional elements of the genome, enhancing the interpretability of the resulting features. Third, the gene-centric approach reduces the dimensionality of the data compared to base-pair-level analyses, making it computationally tractable while retaining sufficient resolution for pattern recognition.

Prior to model input, the raw feature counts are subjected to a series of normalization steps to correct for technical biases. These include normalization by gene length and mappability to account for differences in the expected number of fragments per gene, as well as Loess GC bias correction to adjust for sequence-content-dependent variations in sequencing efficiency. Additionally, per-sample normalization is performed by dividing each feature count by the trimmed mean of the sample’s feature vector (excluding the top and bottom 10% of counts), ensuring that the feature vectors are comparable across samples.

Categorical variables such as age, sex, and institutional source are also incorporated into the feature set using one-hot encoding, allowing the model to account for these factors during training. However, the primary focus remains on the genome-wide cfDNA features, which are expected to contain the most informative signals for cancer detection.

The final feature vector thus represents a comprehensive, normalized profile of the cfDNA landscape, integrating both continuous genomic features and categorical demographic variables. This rich input representation enables the machine learning models to learn complex, high-dimensional patterns that discriminate between cancer and non-cancer samples.

### VI. USE OF MACHINE LEARNING MODEL FOR MULTI-ANALYTE ASSAYS

While the present invention primarily focuses on cfDNA as the sole analyte, the machine learning framework is inherently extensible to multi-analyte assays that incorporate additional biomarkers to enhance diagnostic performance. The modular design of the feature vector allows for the seamless integration of other data types, such as protein concentrations, metabolite levels, or RNA expression profiles, provided that these measurements are available for the same set of samples.

For example, protein biomarkers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) for colorectal cancer or CA-19-9 for pancreatic cancer could be included as additional features in the model. Similarly, circulating RNA species, including microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs, have been shown to carry cancer-specific signatures and could be incorporated into the feature set. The machine learning algorithms would then learn to combine these orthogonal signals in a data-driven manner, potentially improving sensitivity and specificity beyond what is achievable with cfDNA alone.

The key requirement for successful multi-analyte integration is the availability of high-quality, matched measurements across all analytes for the training and validation datasets. The normalization and standardization procedures would need to be adapted to account for the different scales and distributions of the various data types, but the core machine learning framework remains unchanged. This flexibility positions the present invention as a foundational platform for the development of comprehensive, multi-modal cancer detection assays.

### VII. CLASSIFIER GENERATION

The generation of robust and generalizable classifiers is a central aspect of the present invention, achieved through a systematic process of model training, validation, and selection. The process begins with the division of the dataset into training and test sets according to the chosen cross-validation procedure. Within each training fold, the feature vectors are standardized by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, with extreme outliers replaced by the 99th percentile value to minimize the influence of technical artifacts.

The standardized training data is then used to train candidate machine learning models, with hyperparameters optimized through random search within the training fold. Multiple model configurations—including different combinations of dimensionality reduction techniques and classification algorithms—are evaluated, and the best-performing model is selected based on its performance on the validation set.

This process is repeated for each fold of the cross-validation procedure, resulting in a set of performance estimates that reflect the model’s ability to generalize to new data. The final classifier is generated by retraining the selected model configuration on the entire dataset, using the optimal hyperparameters identified during cross-validation. This final model is then ready for deployment in clinical or research settings.

The use of multiple, confounder-aware cross-validation procedures ensures that the classifier is not only accurate but also robust to real-world sources of variation, as detailed in the following sections.

### VIII. CANCER DIAGNOSIS AND DETECTION

The primary application of the present invention is the diagnosis and detection of cancer, with a particular emphasis on early-stage disease that is amenable to curative intervention. The invention has been validated in the context of colorectal cancer, where it has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for the detection of stages I and II disease—the very stages that are most challenging to detect with current screening methods but offer the greatest opportunity for improved outcomes.

In a multicenter, international study involving 546 CRC patients and 271 non-cancer controls, the invention achieved a mean sensitivity of 85% at 85% specificity using standard k-fold cross-validation. More importantly, when evaluated under stringent, confounder-controlled cross-validation schemes, the performance remained robust, with sensitivity ranging from 71% to 85% at 85% specificity. This demonstrates that the classifier is not merely capturing spurious associations driven by confounding variables but is instead learning genuine biological signals of cancer.

The invention’s ability to detect early-stage cancer is particularly noteworthy given its operation at relatively low sequencing depth (~9X), which makes it cost-effective and feasible for population-level screening. The use of modest blood volumes (250 μL of plasma) further enhances patient compliance and accessibility.

While the initial validation focused on colorectal cancer, the underlying principles of the invention are broadly applicable to other malignancies. The genome-wide, unbiased nature of the cfDNA profiling approach allows for the discovery of cancer-specific signatures without requiring prior knowledge of target mutations or biomarkers. Future work will focus on extending the framework to other cancer types and evaluating its performance in prospective clinical trials.

### IX. INDICATIONS

The present invention is indicated for use as a minimally invasive screening tool for the early detection of cancer in asymptomatic individuals, particularly those at average or increased risk based on age, family history, or other risk factors. The initial indication is for colorectal cancer screening in adults aged 50 to 84 years, a population for whom current screening methods have suboptimal adherence and performance.

The invention may also be used as an adjunct to existing diagnostic procedures to improve the accuracy of cancer detection, or as a monitoring tool to assess treatment response and detect recurrence in cancer patients. Additionally, the framework can be adapted for the detection of other pathological conditions, such as inflammatory diseases, autoimmune disorders, or organ transplant rejection, where systemic changes in cfDNA profiles may provide diagnostic insights.

The ultimate goal of the invention is to enable population-level cancer screening that is accessible, affordable, and effective, thereby reducing cancer mortality through earlier detection and intervention.
