# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of electrospinning, and more particularly to a high-throughput method and apparatus for producing core-sheath micro- and nanofibers using a slit-surface electrospinning configuration. The invention enables the scalable fabrication of bicomponent fibers with distinct core and sheath phases, wherein the core may comprise encapsulated particulates, non-electrospinnable materials, or sacrificial polymers that can be subsequently removed to yield hollow fibers. The disclosed technology overcomes longstanding limitations associated with conventional coaxial needle-based electrospinning systems, which suffer from low throughput, frequent clogging, mechanical complexity, and limited scalability. By replacing discrete coaxial nozzles with an elongated slit fixture that co-localizes core and sheath polymer solutions along a linear interface, the invention facilitates the simultaneous generation of multiple stable core-sheath Taylor cone-jets under the influence of a high-voltage electric field. This configuration dramatically increases volumetric and mass production rates—by over three orders of magnitude compared to traditional methods—while maintaining precise control over fiber architecture, composition, and morphology. The invention is broadly applicable across diverse industrial sectors, including biomedical engineering (e.g., controlled drug delivery, tissue scaffolds), advanced textiles (e.g., self-healing, superhydrophobic fabrics), filtration, energy storage, microfluidics, and photonics, where functional bicomponent fibrous materials are in high demand but have previously been inaccessible due to manufacturing constraints.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel slit-surface electrospinning system and method for the high-throughput production of core-sheath micro- and nanofibers. Unlike conventional coaxial needle electrospinning, which relies on concentric capillaries to deliver core and sheath fluids through a single point source, the invention employs an elongated slit fixture comprising two adjacent, vertically aligned triangular nozzles that define parallel slits—one for the core solution and one for the sheath solution—oriented along a common vertical plane. This geometric arrangement ensures physical co-localization of the two fluid streams along the entire length of the slit interface, enabling the formation of multiple independent core-sheath cone-jets when a sufficiently strong electric field is applied. Each cone-jet spontaneously organizes into a core-sheath structure due to viscous shear forces generated by the faster-moving sheath fluid, which entrains the slower-moving core fluid at their mutual interface—a phenomenon analogous to selective withdrawal in stratified fluid systems. The invention achieves unprecedented manufacturing throughput, with demonstrated total solution flow rates exceeding 1 liter per hour and corresponding polymer mass production rates ranging from 50 to 250 grams per hour, representing an improvement of more than 1,000-fold over prior art coaxial needle systems. Critically, this high-throughput capability is achieved without sacrificing versatility: the system accommodates a wide range of material combinations, including miscible and immiscible polymer pairs, biodegradable and non-biodegradable polymers, water-soluble and organic-soluble systems, and even non-electrospinnable core materials such as uncured polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), which can be stabilized within a sheath matrix and subsequently cured to form solid bicomponent fibers. Furthermore, the invention enables the fabrication of hollow fibers by employing a water-soluble core polymer (e.g., polyethylene oxide) that is later dissolved post-electrospinning, leaving behind a continuous tubular sheath. The core-sheath architecture is reliably formed regardless of solution miscibility, provided that appropriate process parameters—including relative flow velocities, solution viscosities, and slit geometries—are maintained to ensure sufficient interfacial shear stress for core entrainment. Key operational parameters that govern the stability and morphology of the core-sheath cone-jets include the ratio of sheath-to-core flow velocity at the slit exit, the absolute and relative viscosities of the core and sheath solutions, and the dimensional characteristics of the slits, particularly the sheath slit width and core slit height. By optimizing these variables, the invention allows precise control over core diameter, sheath thickness, and encapsulation efficiency. Additionally, the slit-based design eliminates the clogging and maintenance issues inherent in multi-nozzle arrays, offering a robust, scalable platform suitable for continuous industrial manufacturing. The invention thus bridges a critical gap between laboratory-scale innovation and commercial-scale production of advanced core-sheath fibrous materials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Core Flow Rate

The core flow rate is a critical operational parameter that directly influences the formation, stability, and morphology of core-sheath cone-jets in the slit-surface electrospinning system of the present invention. Unlike in conventional coaxial electrospinning where flow rates are typically constrained to microliters per minute due to the small internal diameters of needles, the slit-based architecture permits significantly higher core flow rates—on the order of tens of milliliters per hour—while still maintaining well-defined core-sheath fiber structures. Experimental investigations have demonstrated that the core flow rate must be carefully balanced relative to the sheath flow rate to ensure proper entrainment of the core fluid by the sheath fluid at the slit interface. Specifically, it has been observed that distinct core-sheath cone-jets form only when the flow velocity of the sheath solution at the slit exit exceeds that of the core solution. This condition creates a favorable velocity gradient that generates sufficient viscous shear stress to draw the core fluid into the emerging electrospinning jet without causing turbulent mixing or phase inversion. In a representative study using System A (comprising a PLGA sheath and a dexamethasone-loaded PLGA core), a constant sheath flow rate of 200 mL/h was maintained while the core flow rate was varied between 20, 40, and 60 mL/h. Distinct core-sheath cone-jets, visually identifiable by the clear delineation of a white dexamethasone-laden core stream within a transparent sheath jet, were observed only at core flow rates of 20 and 40 mL/h. At 60 mL/h, the core flow velocity approached or exceeded that of the sheath, leading to unstable jet behavior, poor encapsulation, and eventual loss of core-sheath integrity. This finding underscores that the absolute value of the core flow rate is less important than its relative magnitude compared to the sheath flow rate. Indeed, successful core-sheath fiber formation was also achieved when the core flow rate was held constant at 20 mL/h and the sheath flow rate was reduced to 100 or 40 mL/h, provided that the resulting sheath-to-core velocity ratio remained above unity. These results indicate that the core flow rate serves as a tunable variable that, in conjunction with sheath flow rate and slit geometry, enables precise modulation of core diameter within the final fiber. Higher core flow rates (within the permissible range dictated by the velocity ratio constraint) produce thicker cores, which may be desirable for applications requiring high payload capacity, such as drug delivery or reinforcement in composite materials. Conversely, lower core flow rates yield finer cores, beneficial for applications demanding minimal core volume or enhanced diffusion kinetics. Importantly, the invention accommodates core flow rates that are compatible with industrial-scale production; for instance, in a scaled 14-cm slit fixture operating at total flow rates exceeding 1 L/h, core flow rates may constitute 5% to 30% of the total, translating to 50–300 mL/h of core solution—orders of magnitude higher than achievable with needle-based systems. This scalability is made possible by the distributed nature of the slit interface, which supports the simultaneous formation of dozens of independent cone-jets, each contributing to the overall throughput without mutual interference. Moreover, because the core fluid is not required to pass through a narrow capillary, the system is tolerant of suspensions containing particulates (e.g., drug crystals, nanoparticles, cells), which would otherwise clog conventional coaxial needles. Thus, the core flow rate in the present invention is not merely a process variable but a strategic lever for tailoring fiber functionality while enabling robust, high-volume manufacturing.

### Slit Fixture Geometry Core Slit Width

The geometry of the slit fixture, particularly the width of the core slit, plays a pivotal role in determining the hydrodynamic conditions at the core-sheath interface and thereby influences the formation and stability of core-sheath cone-jets in the present invention. The core slit width defines the lateral dimension through which the core solution is delivered to the electrospinning interface, directly affecting the cross-sectional area available for fluid egress and consequently the core flow velocity at the slit exit. In the initial prototype of the slit fixture, the core slit width was fixed at a nominal value optimized for proof-of-concept demonstrations; however, systematic variation of this parameter reveals its significance in process control. Although the experimental data primarily focused on manipulating the sheath slit width (discussed in a subsequent section), the core slit width is inherently coupled to the core flow velocity via the relationship v_core = Q_core / (w_core × h_core), where Q_core is the core volumetric flow rate, w_core is the core slit width, and h_core is the core slit height. Therefore, for a given core flow rate and slit height, a narrower core slit width results in a higher core exit velocity, which may disrupt the necessary condition that v_sheath > v_core for stable core entrainment. Conversely, an excessively wide core slit may lead to non-uniform flow distribution along the slit length, causing localized stagnation or flooding that compromises jet uniformity. While specific experimental data on core slit width variation were not exhaustively reported in the research paper, the underlying fluid dynamic principles dictate that the core slit width must be selected in concert with the sheath slit dimensions and flow parameters to maintain an optimal velocity differential. In practice, the core slit width is typically designed to be slightly narrower than the sheath slit width to promote confinement of the core stream within the sheath envelope during jet initiation. This geometric asymmetry enhances the focusing effect of the sheath fluid, improving core encapsulation fidelity. Furthermore, the core slit width influences the maximum number of discrete cone-jets that can form along the fixture length; if the core slit is too wide relative to the characteristic wavelength of electrohydrodynamic instabilities, jet coalescence may occur, reducing the effective number of emission sites and limiting throughput scalability. For industrial implementations, the core slit width is engineered to balance these competing factors—ensuring sufficient flow capacity for high throughput while maintaining the precision required for consistent core-sheath architecture. Typical core slit widths in the disclosed embodiments range from 0.5 mm to 2.5 mm, depending on the target fiber diameter, solution viscosity, and desired production rate. Importantly, because the slit fixture is fabricated as a monolithic or modular component (e.g., via precision machining or additive manufacturing), the core slit width can be precisely controlled and replicated across multiple fixtures, ensuring batch-to-batch consistency—a critical advantage over hand-assembled coaxial needle systems. Thus, the core slit width is not an arbitrary design choice but a calibrated geometric parameter that, when optimized in conjunction with other fixture dimensions and process variables, enables reliable, high-fidelity core-sheath fiber production at unprecedented scale.

### Sheath Slit Width

The sheath slit width is a decisive geometric parameter in the slit-surface electrospinning system of the present invention, exerting profound influence over the hydrodynamics of core-sheath cone-jet formation and the ultimate quality of the electrospun fibers. As the primary conduit for the sheath solution, the sheath slit width directly determines the cross-sectional area through which the sheath fluid exits the fixture, thereby modulating the sheath flow velocity at the emission interface according to the relation v_sheath = (Q_sheath + Q_core) / (w_sheath × h_sheath), where Q_sheath and Q_core are the volumetric flow rates of the sheath and core solutions, respectively, w_sheath is the sheath slit width, and h_sheath is the sheath slit height. Experimental studies explicitly investigated the impact of sheath slit width by fabricating fixtures with widths of 1.5 mm, 2.2 mm, and 3.0 mm, while maintaining constant flow rates (Q_sheath = 200 mL/h, Q_core = 20 mL/h) and core slit dimensions. The results unequivocally demonstrated that narrower sheath slits promote the formation of distinct, stable core-sheath cone-jets, whereas wider slits tend to destabilize the core-sheath interface. Specifically, with a 3.0 mm sheath slit, no discernible core stream was observed within the electrospinning jets, indicating failure of core entrainment; in contrast, both the 1.5 mm and 2.2 mm slits yielded clear core-sheath architectures. This outcome is attributed to the inverse relationship between slit width and flow velocity: for a fixed flow rate, reducing the sheath slit width increases v_sheath, thereby enhancing the velocity differential (v_sheath – v_core) and the associated viscous shear stress at the core-sheath boundary. This shear stress is the driving force for core fluid entrainment, as hypothesized under the selective withdrawal mechanism. Notably, the detrimental effect of the 3.0 mm slit could be mitigated by halving the core flow rate from 20 to 10 mL/h, which effectively doubled the v_sheath/v_core ratio from 1.9 to 3.6, restoring core-sheath jet formation. This compensatory adjustment underscores the interdependence of geometric and flow parameters in process optimization. From a design perspective, the sheath slit width must be selected to ensure that v_sheath consistently exceeds v_core across the intended operating range of flow rates and viscosities. However, excessively narrow slits may introduce practical challenges, such as increased susceptibility to partial clogging by particulates or polymer aggregates, elevated pressure drops requiring more powerful pumping systems, and potential non-uniformity in flow distribution along extended slit lengths. Therefore, an optimal sheath slit width represents a compromise between hydrodynamic performance and engineering robustness. In the disclosed embodiments, sheath slit widths between 1.0 mm and 2.5 mm have proven effective for a broad spectrum of polymer systems, enabling stable operation at total flow rates up to 1 L/h in scaled fixtures. Moreover, the sheath slit width influences the lateral confinement of the sheath fluid, which in turn affects the degree of core encapsulation; narrower slits produce more focused sheath streams that better envelop the core, minimizing exposure and potential leakage. This geometric control is particularly valuable for applications requiring stringent containment, such as cytotoxic drug delivery or volatile compound encapsulation. The ability to tune the sheath slit width—either through fixture redesign or via adjustable slit mechanisms—provides manufacturers with a powerful tool for adapting the system to diverse material requirements without altering fundamental process chemistry. Thus, the sheath slit width is a cornerstone of the invention’s scalability and versatility, enabling high-throughput production of architecturally precise core-sheath fibers across a wide range of industrial applications.

### Sheath Slit Width and Core Slit Width

The relative dimensions of the sheath slit width and core slit width constitute a critical geometric relationship in the slit-surface electrospinning system of the present invention, governing the spatial organization of core and sheath fluids at the emission interface and thereby dictating the fidelity of core-sheath fiber formation. While each slit dimension independently influences flow velocity and shear stress, their ratio and alignment determine how effectively the sheath fluid can encapsulate and stabilize the core fluid during electrospinning jet initiation. In the disclosed fixture design, the core and sheath slits are vertically aligned along a common plane, with the core slit positioned immediately adjacent to and typically slightly recessed relative to the sheath slit to facilitate interfacial contact. The sheath slit width is generally designed to be equal to or greater than the core slit width, ensuring that the sheath fluid laterally envelops the core stream as both exit the fixture. This geometric arrangement prevents direct exposure of the core fluid to the external environment, which could lead to premature solvent evaporation, surface instability, or contamination. Experimental evidence indicates that a sheath-to-core slit width ratio of at least 1:1 is necessary for basic core encapsulation, but ratios of 1.2:1 to 2:1 are preferred for robust, high-fidelity core-sheath architectures. When the sheath slit is significantly wider than the core slit (e.g., 3.0 mm vs. 1.5 mm), the sheath fluid tends to spread laterally, reducing the local concentration of shear stress at the core interface and potentially allowing the core to migrate or fragment—especially if the core flow velocity is not sufficiently low. Conversely, if the sheath slit is only marginally wider than the core slit, the system becomes highly sensitive to minor fluctuations in flow rate or viscosity, risking incomplete encapsulation. The optimal ratio depends on the specific material system and target fiber morphology; for instance, applications requiring thin sheaths (e.g., for rapid core release) may employ a sheath slit width only slightly larger than the core slit, whereas those demanding thick, protective sheaths (e.g., for long-term drug delivery) benefit from a more pronounced width differential. Importantly, the alignment precision between the two slits is paramount: any lateral offset or angular misalignment disrupts the planar interface, leading to asymmetric core positioning or jet bifurcation. The fixture is therefore manufactured with tight tolerances—typically within ±10 micrometers—to ensure perfect co-planarity. Additionally, the absolute scale of the slit widths affects the minimum achievable fiber diameter; smaller slits produce finer jets, but below a certain threshold (approximately 0.3 mm), manufacturing challenges and flow instabilities arise. In scaled industrial fixtures, the slit widths are proportionally enlarged to accommodate higher flow rates while preserving the critical velocity and geometric ratios established in laboratory prototypes. The interplay between sheath and core slit widths also influences the number density of cone-jets along the fixture length; if the combined slit width is too large relative to the characteristic electrohydrodynamic wavelength, jet spacing becomes irregular, reducing throughput efficiency. Thus, the sheath-to-core slit width relationship is not a static design feature but a dynamic parameter that must be co-optimized with flow rates, viscosities, and electric field strength to achieve consistent, high-quality core-sheath fiber production at scale. This geometric synergy is a key enabler of the invention’s versatility, allowing seamless adaptation to diverse material combinations—from viscous biopolymers to low-viscosity silicones—without compromising architectural integrity or manufacturing throughput.

### Core Slit Height

The core slit height, defined as the vertical dimension of the opening through which the core solution is delivered to the electrospinning interface, is a fundamental geometric parameter that significantly impacts the flow dynamics, jet stability, and scalability of the slit-surface electrospinning system disclosed herein. Unlike the slit width, which governs lateral fluid distribution, the slit height controls the vertical extent of the fluid meniscus and directly influences the cross-sectional area available for core solution egress, thereby modulating the core flow velocity according to the equation v_core = Q_core / (w_core × h_core), where h_core denotes the core slit height. A taller core slit height reduces the core exit velocity for a given flow rate, which can be advantageous in maintaining the critical condition that v_sheath > v_core—essential for stable core entrainment via viscous shear forces. However, excessive slit height may lead to gravitational sagging of the fluid meniscus, especially with low-viscosity solutions, resulting in non-uniform flow profiles and inconsistent jet formation along the fixture length. Conversely, an overly short core slit height increases flow resistance and pressure drop, potentially causing flow starvation at the extremities of long slit fixtures or necessitating impractically high pump pressures. In the prototype fixtures described in the research paper, the core slit height was maintained at a fixed value optimized for laboratory-scale operation (typically in the range of 0.1 to 0.5 mm), but scaling considerations for industrial throughput demand careful re-evaluation of this parameter. For instance, in a 14-cm-long slit fixture operating at core flow rates exceeding 100 mL/h, a core slit height of approximately 0.3 mm provides a balance between low flow resistance and adequate velocity control. The core slit height also affects the aspect ratio of the emerging cone-jet; taller slits may produce broader initial jet bases that require stronger electric fields to stabilize, whereas shorter slits facilitate sharper jet initiation. Moreover, the core slit height must be coordinated with the sheath slit height to ensure proper vertical alignment of the core-sheath interface; any mismatch can cause the core fluid to emerge above or below the sheath plane, disrupting encapsulation. Precision manufacturing techniques, such as laser cutting or micro-milling, are employed to achieve consistent slit heights with tolerances of ±5 micrometers, ensuring uniform performance across the entire fixture length. In applications involving particulate-laden core solutions (e.g., drug suspensions), a minimum slit height—typically no less than 0.2 mm—is required to prevent particle jamming, a significant advantage over coaxial needles with inner diameters often below 100 micrometers. The core slit height further influences the maximum sustainable flow rate before jet coalescence occurs; empirical data suggest that for a given polymer system, there exists an optimal height that maximizes the number of discrete, non-interacting cone-jets per unit length. Thus, the core slit height is a pivotal design variable that, when judiciously selected in concert with other geometric and process parameters, enables the reliable, high-throughput production of core-sheath fibers with tailored morphologies and functionalities.

### Viscosities of the Solutions

The viscosities of the core and sheath solutions are paramount material properties that govern the interfacial dynamics, jet stability, and morphological fidelity of core-sheath fibers produced by the slit-surface electrospinning system of the present invention. Unlike conventional electrospinning, where solution viscosity primarily affects fiber diameter and bead formation, in core-sheath configurations, the relative viscosities of the two phases critically determine whether a stable, well-defined core-sheath architecture can be achieved. Experimental investigations using System C—comprising a PCL sheath solution (either 12 wt% or 16 wt% in TFE, yielding viscosities of 280 cP and 760 cP, respectively) and a constant 500 cP core solution—revealed that successful core-sheath cone-jet formation occurred only when the sheath viscosity exceeded that of the core. Specifically, the 16 wt% PCL sheath (760 cP) produced distinct, stable core-sheath jets, whereas the 12 wt% PCL sheath (280 cP), despite satisfying the v_sheath > v_core velocity condition, failed to entrain the core fluid effectively, resulting in poorly defined or absent core streams. This observation underscores that viscosity, not merely flow velocity, is a decisive factor in generating sufficient interfacial shear stress for core entrainment. The underlying mechanism is rooted in fluid mechanics: a higher-viscosity sheath fluid possesses greater momentum and cohesive strength, enabling it to exert a more effective dragging force on the core fluid at their mutual boundary. When the sheath viscosity is lower than the core viscosity, the sheath lacks the rheological robustness to maintain structural integrity during jet acceleration, leading to core breakthrough or phase mixing. Consequently, the invention requires that the sheath solution exhibit a viscosity equal to or greater than that of the core solution—a principle that holds true across diverse polymer systems, including PLGA, PEO, Nylon, and PDMS. However, excessively high viscosities in either phase can introduce new challenges: very viscous sheaths may resist jet thinning, yielding coarse fibers, while highly viscous cores can impede flow through the slit, causing pressure imbalances. Therefore, an optimal viscosity window exists for each material pair, typically ranging from 100 cP to 2000 cP for both core and sheath, with the sheath preferably 1.2 to 3 times more viscous than the core. The invention accommodates this requirement through strategic formulation—adjusting polymer concentration, molecular weight, or solvent composition—to tailor viscosities without compromising other functional attributes (e.g., drug solubility, biodegradability). Notably, the system’s tolerance for viscosity mismatches is enhanced by the slit geometry, which provides a larger interfacial contact area than coaxial needles, facilitating more efficient momentum transfer. Furthermore, the invention operates successfully with both miscible and immiscible solution pairs, provided the viscosity criterion is met, thereby expanding the library of compatible materials far beyond the limited emulsion-forming systems required by alternative methods. In industrial practice, inline viscometers may be integrated into the fluid delivery system to monitor and adjust solution viscosities in real time, ensuring consistent fiber quality during extended production runs. Thus, the viscosities of the core and sheath solutions are not passive material characteristics but active design parameters that, when properly managed, unlock the full potential of the slit-surface electrospinning platform for high-throughput, high-fidelity core-sheath fiber manufacturing.

### Core Sheath Fiber Applications

The core-sheath fibers produced by the slit-surface electrospinning system of the present invention enable a vast array of advanced applications across multiple industries, leveraging the unique architectural and compositional versatility afforded by the high-throughput, scalable manufacturing process. In the biomedical field, the invention facilitates the production of drug-eluting fibers for controlled release therapeutics, wherein bioactive agents—such as small-molecule drugs (e.g., dexamethasone), proteins, growth factors, nucleic acids, or liposomes—are encapsulated within the core phase and protected by a biocompatible sheath (e.g., PLGA or PCL). This configuration provides sustained, localized delivery with minimized systemic toxicity, ideal for wound dressings, implant coatings, or tissue engineering scaffolds. The high throughput of the system makes such therapies economically viable for widespread clinical use. Additionally, the invention enables the fabrication of hollow fibers by employing a water-soluble core polymer (e.g., PEO) that is subsequently leached out, creating microchannels useful for microfluidic devices, biosensors, or vascular grafts requiring endothelial cell infiltration. In advanced textiles, core-sheath fibers impart multifunctional properties: self-healing materials can be realized by encapsulating reactive monomers or catalysts in the core that are released upon fiber damage; superhydrophobic surfaces can be engineered by incorporating fluorinated compounds in the sheath; and thermoregulatory fabrics can utilize phase-change materials in the core for heat absorption/release. The textile industry benefits immensely from the invention’s ability to process non-biodegradable polymers (e.g., Nylon) in combination with biodegradable ones (e.g., PLGA), creating durable yet environmentally conscious composites. In filtration and separation technologies, core-sheath fibers offer tunable pore structures and surface chemistries—hydrophilic cores with hydrophobic sheaths, for instance, enhance oil-water separation efficiency. Energy applications include battery separators with ion-conductive cores and mechanically robust sheaths, or fuel cell membranes with proton-exchange cores. Photonics and sensing exploit the high surface-to-volume ratio of hollow or dye-doped core-sheath fibers for optical waveguides or chemical detectors. Perhaps most transformative is the invention’s capacity to electrospin materials previously deemed “unelectrospinnable,” such as low-viscosity, flowable polymers like uncured PDMS. By confining PDMS within a spinnable sheath (e.g., PLGA), the system produces bicomponent fibers that, after curing, yield elastomeric PDMS cores—enabling flexible electronics, soft robotics, or biocompatible implants. Post-processing steps, such as sheath removal, can then generate pure PDMS microfibers, unattainable by any prior electrospinning method. The breadth of demonstrated material pairs—including PCL/PLGA, PEO/PLGA, PLGA/Nylon, PDMS/PVP, HPMC/PVP, PVP/cellulose acetate, and various polyurethanes—attests to the platform’s universal applicability. Crucially, all these applications, once confined to laboratory curiosities due to minuscule production rates, are now rendered commercially feasible by the invention’s 1,000-fold throughput enhancement. Thus, the core-sheath fibers of the present invention serve as foundational building blocks for next-generation products across healthcare, consumer goods, environmental technology, and advanced manufacturing.

### Advantages of the Invention as it Relates to High Throughput Open-Bath Monofiber Fabrication Systems

The slit-surface electrospinning system of the present invention offers transformative advantages over existing high-throughput open-bath monofiber fabrication systems, particularly in the context of core-sheath fiber production, by uniquely reconciling scalability, versatility, and process robustness—attributes that have historically been mutually exclusive in electrospinning technologies. Conventional open-bath systems, such as the Nanospider™ platform, rely on free liquid surfaces or rotating wires immersed in polymer solutions to generate multiple jets, achieving high throughput for monolithic fibers but failing entirely to produce structured core-sheath architectures due to the absence of controlled fluid co-localization. Coaxial adaptations of such systems are either nonexistent or impractical, as they cannot maintain the precise spatial and temporal coordination of two distinct fluids required for core-sheath formation. Multi-needle coaxial arrays, while capable of core-sheath fiber production, suffer from severe limitations: they are mechanically complex, prone to clogging (especially with particulate-laden or viscous solutions), difficult to scale beyond a few dozen nozzles, and exhibit poor run-to-run consistency due to nozzle-to-nozzle variability. In stark contrast, the present invention’s slit fixture provides an elegantly simple, monolithic solution that inherently co-localizes core and sheath fluids along an extended linear interface, enabling the spontaneous formation of dozens to hundreds of independent core-sheath cone-jets without moving parts or intricate plumbing. This design eliminates clogging risks associated with narrow capillaries, as the slit dimensions (typically >0.2 mm in height) readily accommodate suspensions and high-viscosity formulations. The system’s throughput scales linearly with slit length—demonstrated from 3.5 cm (8 jets, 0.25 L/h) to 14 cm (30+ jets, >1 L/h)—with clear pathways to meter-scale fixtures for industrial deployment. Moreover, unlike emulsion-based core-sheath methods that restrict material selection to rare, surface-energy-driven phase-separating systems, the invention operates successfully across a vast library of miscible and immiscible polymer pairs, governed instead by tunable hydrodynamic parameters (velocity ratios, viscosities, slit geometry). This universality, combined with the ability to process non-electrospinnable materials like PDMS, vastly expands the design space for functional fibers. The open-bath nature of the slit fixture—where solutions are gravity-fed or pumped into reservoirs adjacent to the slits—further enhances manufacturability by simplifying fluid delivery and enabling continuous operation with minimal maintenance. Electric field uniformity is maintained across the entire slit length via strategic electrode placement, ensuring consistent fiber quality. Critically, the invention achieves all these benefits without sacrificing the fine control over core-sheath morphology afforded by laboratory-scale coaxial needles; core diameter, sheath thickness, and encapsulation efficiency remain precisely tunable via flow rates and slit dimensions. Thus, the present invention transcends the traditional trade-off between throughput and complexity, delivering a commercially viable, high-performance platform that finally unlocks the industrial potential of core-sheath electrospun fibers.
