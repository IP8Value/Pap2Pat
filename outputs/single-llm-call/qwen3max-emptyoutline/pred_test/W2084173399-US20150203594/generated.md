# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of diagnostic immunoassays has long relied on solid support materials to immobilize biomolecules such as antibodies, antigens, and enzymes for the detection of analytes in biological samples. Historically, synthetic polymeric substrates derived from nonrenewable petroleum-based resources have dominated this domain due to their tunable physicochemical properties, ease of manufacturing, and compatibility with various surface functionalization chemistries. However, the widespread use of these conventional polymers raises significant environmental concerns, including issues related to non-biodegradability, accumulation in ecosystems, and dependence on finite fossil fuel reserves. In response to growing sustainability imperatives and regulatory pressures, there has been a concerted effort within the scientific and industrial communities to identify and develop alternative support materials derived from renewable, biocompatible, and environmentally benign sources.

Among natural polymers, cellulose—a linear polysaccharide composed of β(1→4)-linked D-glucose units—has emerged as a particularly promising candidate due to its abundance, low cost, non-toxicity, biodegradability, and inherent hydrophilicity. Cellulose and its derivatives have been explored extensively for biomedical applications, including wound dressings, drug delivery systems, and tissue engineering scaffolds. In the context of diagnostic platforms, nitrocellulose—a chemically modified form of cellulose—has been widely adopted as a membrane support in lateral flow assays and Western blotting techniques. Despite its utility, nitrocellulose suffers from several critical limitations that hinder its broader applicability: it exhibits relatively poor mechanical strength, is highly flammable, and demonstrates strong non-specific adsorption of proteins due to its heterogeneous surface chemistry and high porosity. These drawbacks can lead to reduced assay sensitivity, increased background noise, and compromised reproducibility, thereby limiting the reliability of diagnostic outcomes.

To overcome these challenges, researchers have turned to nanocellulosic materials, particularly nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC), which consists of entangled networks of cellulose nanofibrils with diameters typically ranging from 5 to 100 nanometers and lengths extending into the micrometer scale. NFC is produced through the mechanical or chemical disintegration of plant-based cellulose fibers, resulting in a gel-like aqueous suspension that can be processed into dense, transparent, and mechanically robust films. These NFC films possess a unique combination of properties highly desirable for diagnostic applications: high specific surface area, excellent mechanical integrity, optical clarity, and a surface rich in hydroxyl groups amenable to chemical modification. Importantly, NFC films prepared from precursor pulps with low negative surface charge exhibit reduced water uptake and enhanced structural stability in aqueous environments—a crucial attribute for maintaining dimensional integrity during liquid-phase immunoassays.

Nevertheless, the native hydroxyl-rich surface of NFC presents its own set of challenges. The high density of hydrophilic groups renders the material inherently water-absorbent, which can lead to swelling, loss of mechanical strength, and uncontrolled diffusion of analytes into the bulk matrix—phenomena that are detrimental to surface-confined detection schemes where precise spatial localization of biorecognition elements is essential. Moreover, the unmodified NFC surface lacks specific chemical functionalities for the stable and oriented immobilization of biomolecules such as antibodies. Random physical adsorption of proteins onto hydrophilic surfaces often results in denaturation, loss of biological activity, and non-specific binding, all of which compromise assay performance.

To address these limitations, surface engineering strategies have been employed to introduce reactive functional groups onto NFC films. One particularly effective approach involves the TEMPO (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl)-mediated oxidation of cellulose, a selective catalytic process that converts primary hydroxyl groups at the C6 position of glucose units into carboxyl groups. This transformation not only imparts a negative surface charge but also provides anchor points for subsequent bioconjugation reactions. However, while TEMPO-oxidized NFC exhibits enhanced reactivity, it simultaneously becomes more hydrophilic and water-sensitive due to electrostatic repulsion between negatively charged carboxylate groups, which promotes water ingress and film swelling. This paradox—where increased functionality comes at the cost of reduced water resistance—has posed a significant barrier to the practical implementation of NFC in diagnostic devices that must operate reliably in aqueous media.

Conventional methods for biomolecule immobilization on solid supports often rely on physical adsorption, covalent coupling via carbodiimide chemistry (e.g., EDC/NHS activation), or affinity-based capture systems (e.g., streptavidin-biotin). While effective on many synthetic polymers, these techniques have not been systematically optimized for the unique topological and chemical characteristics of NFC films. Furthermore, traditional deposition methods such as manual spotting or dip-coating lack precision, consume large volumes of expensive reagents (e.g., purified antibodies), and are ill-suited for high-throughput manufacturing. Inkjet printing has emerged as a compelling alternative, offering contactless, digital, and programmable deposition of picoliter-scale droplets with high spatial resolution. This technology enables precise control over the surface concentration and patterning of biomolecules, minimizes reagent consumption, and reduces the risk of cross-contamination—attributes that are especially valuable in the development of multiplexed diagnostic arrays.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remains in the art: there is no known method that successfully integrates the water-resistant structural integrity of low-charge NFC films with a surface-specific, topologically controlled chemical activation strategy that enables efficient, covalent, and oriented immobilization of antibodies while minimizing non-specific binding. Prior attempts to functionalize NFC for diagnostic use have either compromised mechanical stability through excessive oxidation or failed to achieve sufficient conjugation efficiency due to inadequate surface activation. Moreover, existing approaches do not leverage the full potential of inkjet printing for scalable, high-fidelity biomolecule patterning on nanocellulosic supports.

Accordingly, there exists a pressing need in the field for an innovative diagnostic platform based on renewable cellulose materials that simultaneously achieves (i) robust water resistance to maintain structural integrity during aqueous processing, (ii) controlled surface chemistry for specific and stable biomolecule conjugation, (iii) minimal non-specific protein adsorption to ensure high signal-to-noise ratios, and (iv) compatibility with advanced deposition techniques such as inkjet printing for scalable manufacturing. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by providing a novel method for the topological modification of water-resistant NFC films through a sequential, surface-confined TEMPO-mediated oxidation followed by EDC/NHS activation, thereby creating a high-performance biointerface uniquely suited for next-generation immunoassays.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for preparing a functionalized nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC) film suitable for use as a solid support in diagnostic immunoassays, wherein the NFC film exhibits enhanced water resistance, controlled surface chemistry, and high efficiency for the covalent immobilization of biomolecules such as antibodies. The method comprises the steps of: (a) providing a water-resistant NFC film prepared from a low-charge precursor pulp, wherein the NFC film possesses a dense, compact microstructure with reduced porosity and limited water penetration; (b) subjecting the surface of the NFC film to a time-controlled TEMPO-mediated oxidation under conditions that selectively introduce carboxyl groups predominantly on the outermost layer of the film without substantially compromising its internal water-resistant structure; (c) activating the carboxylated surface via carbodiimide chemistry using 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) to generate amine-reactive NHS-ester functionalities; and (d) covalently immobilizing amine-containing biomolecules, such as antibodies or proteins, onto the activated surface through nucleophilic acyl substitution.

In a preferred embodiment, the water-resistant NFC film is prepared by mechanical disintegration of bleached hardwood pulp followed by pressure filtration and consolidation, resulting in a film with a low initial surface charge (typically less than 100 μeq/g) and high mechanical integrity in aqueous environments. The TEMPO-mediated oxidation is performed for a short duration (e.g., 10 to 300 seconds) in an aqueous solution containing TEMPO, sodium bromide, and sodium hypochlorite at pH 10, which ensures that oxidation occurs primarily at the film-air or film-liquid interface rather than penetrating deeply into the bulk matrix. This surface-selective modification preserves the water-resistant core of the NFC film while creating a thin, functionalized outer layer rich in carboxyl groups.

The EDC/NHS activation step converts the surface carboxyl groups into NHS-esters, which are highly reactive toward primary amines present in lysine residues of proteins and antibodies. This activation is carried out in a mildly acidic buffer (e.g., 10 mM sodium acetate, pH 5) to optimize the efficiency of ester formation while minimizing hydrolysis. The resulting activated NFC film provides a stable platform for the covalent attachment of biomolecules, as demonstrated by quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation (QCM-D) monitoring, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and fluorescence imaging techniques.

A key advantage of the invention is the ability to immobilize biomolecules via inkjet printing, enabling precise spatial patterning with minimal reagent consumption. The activated NFC film accepts printed droplets of antibody solutions (e.g., antihuman IgG) and facilitates covalent bonding upon contact, with unreacted NHS-esters subsequently quenched by ethanolamine treatment to prevent non-specific adsorption. The immobilized antibodies retain their biological activity, as confirmed by specific binding to target antigens (e.g., human IgG) in subsequent immunoassay steps, while non-specific binding is minimized through the use of blocking agents such as Superblock.

The functionalized NFC film of the invention exhibits superior performance characteristics compared to conventional diagnostic supports: it maintains structural integrity in aqueous media due to its water-resistant core, provides high-density, oriented immobilization of antibodies via covalent linkages, demonstrates low non-specific protein adsorption after blocking, and is compatible with high-resolution, contactless deposition techniques. These attributes make the invention particularly well-suited for applications in point-of-care diagnostics, multiplexed biosensors, and high-throughput screening platforms where reliability, sensitivity, and sustainability are paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a method for producing a functionalized nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC) film that serves as an advanced solid support for diagnostic immunoassays. The method strategically combines the inherent water resistance of low-charge NFC films with a surface-specific chemical activation protocol to create a biointerface that optimally balances structural stability, biomolecule conjugation efficiency, and assay specificity. The following detailed description outlines the preparation of the NFC substrate, the sequential surface modification steps, the immobilization of biomolecules, and the characterization of the resulting functionalized films.

The starting material for the invention is a water-resistant NFC film prepared from bleached hardwood pulp, preferably birch, which is subjected to mechanical disintegration to produce a nanofibrillar network. The pulp is first processed through a Masuko grinder for multiple passes (typically five) to initiate fiber delamination, followed by high-pressure homogenization using a Microfluidics M110P fluidizer for additional passes (e.g., six) to achieve individualized nanofibrils with diameters in the range of 5–50 nm. The resulting NFC gel, at a solids content of approximately 1–2 wt%, is then filtered under pressure (e.g., 2.5 bar) through a membrane to remove excess water and form a wet film. This wet film is further consolidated by rolling with a smooth metal pin to enhance fibril entanglement and reduce macrovoids, and then dried between absorbent blotting boards under mild pressure to yield a transparent, flexible, and mechanically robust dry film. Critically, the precursor pulp used in this process exhibits a low negative surface charge (typically less than 100 μeq/g as measured by conductometric titration), which contributes to the film’s dense microstructure and limited water uptake. When immersed in water for extended periods (e.g., several days) with intermittent ultrasonication, the film remains largely intact, demonstrating superior wet strength compared to NFC films derived from highly charged pulps.

The first step in surface functionalization is the TEMPO-mediated oxidation of the NFC film. Aqueous solutions containing 0.13 mmol TEMPO, 4.7 mmol sodium bromide, and 5.65 mmol sodium hypochlorite per 100 mL of water are prepared, and the pH is adjusted to 10 using 1 M hydrochloric acid. Square pieces of the dry NFC film (e.g., 2.5 × 2.5 cm²) are immersed in this oxidizing solution for a precisely controlled duration ranging from 10 to 300 seconds. The short reaction time is a critical parameter that ensures oxidation occurs predominantly at the film surface rather than penetrating into the bulk. Longer durations (e.g., >300 s) lead to excessive carboxylation throughout the film thickness, resulting in swelling and loss of mechanical integrity. After the designated oxidation period, the reaction is quenched by adding ethanol (10% v/v) to consume residual hypochlorite, followed by extensive rinsing with deionized water to remove reaction byproducts. The resulting TEMPO-oxidized NFC film exhibits a significant increase in surface carboxyl content, as confirmed by conductometric titration (e.g., from 72 μeq/g in the unmodified film to 237 μeq/g after 300 s oxidation), while retaining its overall structural coherence due to the protected water-resistant core.

Following oxidation, the carboxylated NFC film is activated using EDC/NHS chemistry to generate amine-reactive esters. The film is immersed in a solution containing 0.1 M EDC and 0.4 M NHS in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5) for 20 minutes at room temperature. Under these mildly acidic conditions, EDC facilitates the formation of an O-acylisourea intermediate with surface carboxyl groups, which is then stabilized by NHS to form NHS-esters. These esters are significantly more stable toward hydrolysis than the initial O-acylisourea and exhibit high reactivity toward primary amines. After activation, the film is rinsed thoroughly with deionized water to remove excess reagents and then dried between blotting boards to prevent buckling. The successful formation of NHS-esters is verified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), which detects a distinct nitrogen signal (absent in unmodified and carboxylated films) corresponding to the NHS moiety, along with an increase in the carbonyl component of the C1s spectrum.

The activated NFC film is then used for the immobilization of biomolecules, such as bovine serum albumin (BSA) or antihuman IgG antibodies. Covalent conjugation occurs when the NHS-ester groups react with primary amines (e.g., ε-amino groups of lysine residues) on the protein surface, forming stable amide bonds and releasing NHS as a byproduct. This reaction can be performed either by direct immersion (physical adsorption followed by covalent coupling) or by inkjet printing. For inkjet printing, purified antibodies (e.g., antihuman IgG at 1 mg/mL in 10 mM phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) are loaded into a clean inkjet cartridge of a piezoelectric printer (e.g., Epson R800). The activated NFC film (e.g., 2 × 4 cm²) is placed in the CD-printing tray, and the antibody solution is printed using software that allows selection of a single cartridge (with other cartridges filled with buffer to maintain printhead function). The picoliter-scale droplets deposit the antibody precisely onto the activated surface, where immediate covalent bonding occurs. Post-printing, the film is treated with 0.1 M ethanolamine (pH 8.5) for 15 minutes to quench any unreacted NHS-esters by converting them to inert ethylamide groups, thereby minimizing non-specific binding in subsequent assay steps.

The biological activity of the immobilized antibodies is preserved, as demonstrated by specific antigen capture. For example, antihuman IgG printed on the activated NFC film specifically binds human IgG added in a subsequent incubation step, while control films without antibodies show negligible binding. Non-specific adsorption is further suppressed by treating the surface with a commercial blocking agent (e.g., Superblock) that occupies remaining active sites. The entire process—from NFC film preparation to antibody immobilization—is conducted under ambient conditions without the need for specialized equipment beyond standard laboratory apparatus and a commercially available inkjet printer, making it highly accessible for diagnostic device manufacturing.

Characterization of the functionalized films employs multiple complementary techniques. Contact angle measurements show that TEMPO oxidation increases surface hydrophilicity (contact angle decreases from 44° to <15°), while EDC/NHS activation partially restores hydrophobicity due to ester formation. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) reveals nanoscale topographical changes: unmodified NFC shows a fibrillar network with RMS roughness of ~35 nm; TEMPO oxidation introduces a gel-like surface layer with increased roughness (~45 nm); and EDC/NHS activation yields a smoother, more consolidated surface (~50 nm roughness) consistent with reduced hydration. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) confirms uniform antibody distribution after inkjet printing, with fluorescence intensity correlating directly with printed patterns. QCM-D monitoring of model NFC thin films on sensor crystals provides real-time quantification of mass changes during each modification step, confirming covalent binding through resistance to alkaline stripping (e.g., BSA remains bound after pH 10 rinse only on activated surfaces).

The resulting functionalized NFC film represents a significant advancement over prior art supports. Unlike nitrocellulose, it offers superior mechanical strength and reduced flammability. Compared to fully oxidized NFC hydrogels, it maintains dimensional stability in water due to its protected core. The surface-specific activation ensures that conjugation occurs only at the interface where analyte interaction takes place, minimizing waste of expensive reagents and enhancing assay reproducibility. The compatibility with inkjet printing enables rapid prototyping and scalable production of multiplexed diagnostic arrays. Overall, the invention provides a sustainable, high-performance platform that meets the stringent requirements of modern immunoassay technologies.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Preparation of Water-Resistant NFC Films**

Bleached birch hardwood pulp was mechanically processed using a Masuko supermasscolloider with five passes at 1500 rpm. The resulting slurry was further disintegrated using a Microfluidics M110P high-pressure homogenizer at 1500 bar for six passes. The obtained NFC gel, at approximately 1.5 wt% solids, was filtered through a 0.45 μm polyethersulfone membrane under 2.5 bar pressure for 2 hours to form a wet film. The wet film was rolled five times with a stainless-steel rolling pin to enhance consolidation and then sandwiched between two sheets of Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The assembly was placed under a 5 kg weight and allowed to dry at ambient temperature (23°C, 50% relative humidity) for 48 hours. The resulting dry NFC film was transparent, flexible, and exhibited a basis weight of 45 g/m². When immersed in deionized water for 72 hours with ultrasonication (30 s every 6 hours), the film retained over 90% of its original dimensions, confirming its water-resistant nature.

**Example 2: Surface-Specific TEMPO-Mediated Oxidation**

Aqueous oxidation solution was prepared by dissolving 0.13 mmol TEMPO and 4.7 mmol NaBr in 100 mL deionized water, followed by addition of 5.65 mmol NaClO and pH adjustment to 10.0 with 1 M HCl. Square pieces (2.5 × 2.5 cm²) of the NFC film from Example 1 were immersed in this solution for 30 seconds with gentle agitation. The reaction was quenched by adding 10 mL ethanol, and the films were rinsed with 1 L deionized water over 30 minutes. Conductometric titration of the oxidized film showed a carboxyl content of 169 μeq/g, compared to 72 μeq/g for the unmodified film. Contact angle measurement immediately after drying showed a value of 12°, indicating high surface hydrophilicity. XPS analysis revealed no detectable bromine or chlorine residues, confirming complete removal of oxidation chemicals.

**Example 3: EDC/NHS Activation and NHS-Ester Formation**

The TEMPO-oxidized NFC film from Example 2 was immersed in a solution of 0.1 M EDC and 0.4 M NHS in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) for 20 minutes at 25°C. The film was then rinsed with 500 mL deionized water and dried as in Example 1. XPS survey scans showed a nitrogen atomic percentage of 2.1%, absent in control films, confirming NHS incorporation. High-resolution C1s spectra exhibited a new peak at 288.2 eV corresponding to the ester carbonyl of NHS, with a relative area increase of 18% compared to the carboxylated film. QCM-D monitoring of model NFC thin films during activation showed a frequency decrease of 25 Hz, corresponding to a mass uptake of 4.9 mg/m², consistent with NHS-ester formation.

**Example 4: Covalent Immobilization of BSA and Antibody Activity**

EDC/NHS-activated NFC films were incubated with 100 μg/mL BSA in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.0) for 30 minutes. After rinsing with buffer, the films were treated with 10 mM NaCl at pH 10.0 for 5 minutes to remove electrostatically bound protein. QCM-D measurements showed that 4.9 mg/m² of BSA remained bound after alkaline rinse, compared to complete removal from TEMPO-oxidized (non-activated) films, confirming covalent attachment. For antibody immobilization, antihuman IgG (100 μg/mL in phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) was adsorbed onto activated films for 30 minutes, followed by ethanolamine quenching and Superblock treatment. Subsequent exposure to 10 μg/mL human IgG resulted in specific binding of 0.93 mg/m², while control films showed less than 0.1 mg/m² non-specific adsorption.

**Example 5: Inkjet Printing of Antibodies on Activated NFC Films**

Antihuman IgG was labeled with FITC (fluorescein isothiocyanate) by mixing 1 mg antibody with 0.1 mg FITC in 1 mL carbonate buffer (pH 9.0) for 1 hour at 4°C, followed by purification using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters (30 kDa cutoff). The labeled antibody (1 mg/mL in phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) was loaded into a clean black ink cartridge of an Epson R800 printer. Activated NFC films (2 × 4 cm²) were printed with a checkerboard pattern using Print-CD software. After printing, films were treated with ethanolamine (0.1 M, pH 8.5) for 15 minutes and dried. CLSM imaging (488 nm excitation, 520 nm emission) showed sharp, high-contrast fluorescence patterns corresponding exactly to the printed design, with no diffusion beyond droplet boundaries. AFM topography revealed a uniform protein layer covering the nanofibrillar structure, with RMS roughness of 25 nm, indicating conformal coating. The printed antibodies retained full binding capacity, as demonstrated by specific capture of human IgG in subsequent assays.
