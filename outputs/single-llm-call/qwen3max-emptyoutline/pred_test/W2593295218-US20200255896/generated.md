# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of noninvasive prenatal screening and, more specifically, to an improved method and system for detecting fetal chromosomal aneuploidies, including trisomies 21, 18, and 13, as well as sex chromosome aneuploidies and microdeletion syndromes, from maternal plasma using cell-free DNA (cfDNA) analysis. The invention further encompasses a highly sensitive, automated, next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based assay that integrates advanced sample preparation protocols, proprietary bioinformatics algorithms, and statistical modeling to achieve unprecedented discrimination between affected and unaffected pregnancies. In particular, the invention provides a robust clinical laboratory-developed test (LDT) that minimizes false-positive results by identifying and accounting for confounding biological factors such as maternal chromosomal microduplications, global maternal copy number variations, and mosaicism. The technical domain of this invention spans molecular diagnostics, clinical genomics, bioinformatics, reproductive medicine, and automated high-throughput laboratory systems. The disclosed methods are designed for use in certified clinical reference laboratories and are intended to serve as a highly accurate screening tool during early pregnancy, thereby reducing unnecessary invasive procedures while maintaining high positive predictive value (PPV) across a range of fetal chromosomal abnormalities.

## BACKGROUND

Noninvasive prenatal screening (NIPS) has emerged as a transformative advancement in prenatal care, offering a safer alternative to traditional invasive diagnostic procedures such as amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling (CVS), which carry a small but significant risk of procedure-related miscarriage—estimated at 0.5% to 1.0%. Conventional screening methods, including maternal serum screening and nuchal translucency ultrasound, have long been limited by suboptimal detection rates and high false-positive rates, often leading to unnecessary anxiety and invasive follow-up testing. The advent of NIPS, which leverages the analysis of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) fragments circulating in maternal plasma, has dramatically improved the sensitivity and specificity for detecting common fetal aneuploidies, particularly trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome), and trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome). Commercially available NIPS platforms employ various technological approaches, including single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-based genotyping, chromosome-specific sequencing (CSS), and massive parallel shotgun sequencing (MPSS), all of which have demonstrated high analytical performance in controlled settings.

Despite these advances, first-generation NIPS assays continue to face critical limitations in real-world clinical practice. A primary concern is the persistent occurrence of false-positive results, which directly impacts the positive predictive value (PPV) of the test. The PPV is inherently dependent not only on assay specificity but also on the prevalence of the condition in the tested population. Given the relatively low prevalence of trisomies 13, 18, and even 21—even in high-risk cohorts—the theoretical PPV for a test with 99.9% specificity remains suboptimal: approximately 90% for trisomy 21, 67% for trisomy 18, and only 53% for trisomy 13. Empirical data from clinical laboratories corroborate these predictions, with reported PPVs ranging from 39% to 93% depending on the trisomy and the specific assay used. These false positives arise from a combination of biological and technical factors. Biologically, phenomena such as confined placental mosaicism, vanishing twin syndrome, maternal or fetal mosaicism, maternal malignancies, and—critically—maternal constitutional chromosomal duplications can lead to elevated cfDNA signals that mimic fetal aneuploidy. Technically, sequencing biases, particularly those related to GC content variation across chromosomes, can distort read counts and compromise analytical accuracy, especially for GC-rich chromosomes like 13 and 18.

Prior art NIPS methods have largely failed to systematically address these confounders. While some platforms incorporate basic GC correction, they often lack the resolution to distinguish whole-chromosome aneuploidies from localized maternal copy number variants (CNVs). Furthermore, existing assays typically do not perform comprehensive genomic review of all chromosomes upon detection of a potential aneuploidy, thereby missing global copy number abnormalities that may originate from maternal tissues such as uterine fibroids, which are known to shed aneuploid DNA into the maternal circulation. Additionally, many current NIPS workflows rely on manual or semi-automated steps in sample preparation, library construction, and data analysis, introducing variability that reduces assay precision and increases the likelihood of indeterminate or erroneous results. The absence of stringent, automated quality control metrics and dynamic fetal fraction estimation further limits the reliability of sex chromosome aneuploidy detection and microdeletion screening.

Professional guidelines from major medical societies consistently emphasize that NIPS remains a screening—not diagnostic—test and that positive results must be confirmed by invasive cytogenetic analysis. However, clinical practice reveals that a significant proportion of patients and providers treat NIPS results as definitive, sometimes leading to pregnancy termination without confirmatory testing. This underscores the urgent need for a next-generation NIPS platform that not only achieves higher analytical separation between affected and unaffected cases but also actively identifies and mitigates the root causes of false positives. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by integrating full automation, enhanced cfDNA extraction, optimized sequencing chemistry, sophisticated GC bias correction, proprietary biostatistical modeling, and a comprehensive genomic review protocol that evaluates both localized and genome-wide copy number patterns. This holistic approach enables the reliable exclusion of maternal-derived artifacts, thereby significantly elevating the PPV and clinical utility of noninvasive prenatal screening.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel, highly accurate, and fully automated noninvasive prenatal screening (NIPS) assay for the detection of fetal chromosomal aneuploidies, including trisomies 21, 18, and 13, sex chromosome aneuploidies, and select microdeletion syndromes, from maternal plasma. The core innovation lies in the integration of a proprietary end-to-end workflow that combines advanced wet-lab automation, high-yield cell-free DNA (cfDNA) preparation, optimized next-generation sequencing (NGS) chemistry, and a sophisticated bioinformatics pipeline featuring dynamic GC correction, robust statistical modeling, and comprehensive genomic anomaly detection. Unlike conventional NIPS methods, the disclosed assay achieves complete analytical separation between affected and unaffected pregnancies through the use of stringent Z-score thresholds—specifically, a Z-score cutoff of ≤4 for classifying a sample as unaffected and >8 for classifying it as affected—with an intermediate zone (4 < Z ≤ 8) triggering mandatory manual review. This clear demarcation is enabled by exceptional assay consistency, achieved through full automation of all manual processes, including plasma isolation, cfDNA extraction, library preparation, quantification, normalization, and pooling.

A key aspect of the invention is its ability to identify and exclude false-positive results arising from maternal biological confounders. The bioinformatics system generates chromosomal ideograms that visualize normalized read counts across the entire genome at high resolution, allowing trained analysts to distinguish whole-chromosome duplications (indicative of true fetal trisomy) from localized maternal microduplications. When an elevated Z-score is detected for a target chromosome, the system automatically flags the case for ideogram review before reporting; if the duplication is confined to a subchromosomal region, the result is interpreted as a maternal copy number variant rather than a fetal aneuploidy. Similarly, the invention incorporates a genome-wide surveillance protocol wherein any sample with a positive Z-score for chromosomes 13, 18, or 21 is automatically evaluated for concurrent copy number changes on multiple other chromosomes. The presence of such global abnormalities—often indicative of maternal conditions such as uterine fibroids or occult malignancies—triggers exclusion of the result from clinical reporting, thereby preventing false-positive diagnoses.

The assay further includes enhanced methods for fetal sex determination and sex chromosome aneuploidy detection, utilizing dual fetal fraction estimation based on X-chromosome underrepresentation in female pregnancies and Y-chromosome overrepresentation in male pregnancies. For Y-chromosome analysis, the system employs a curated set of high-performance 50-kilobase bins that exhibit strong differential signal between male and female samples, combined with a training-derived model that correlates normalized Y-read counts with fetal fraction. Sex chromosome aneuploidy calls are made by comparing X- and Y-based fetal fraction estimates against empirically validated thresholds derived from large training datasets. Additionally, the invention supports the detection of microdeletion syndromes, such as DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion), by applying the same bin-based counting and Z-score methodology to predefined genomic regions, with total read counts summed across disease-relevant coordinates.

The method is implemented as a laboratory-developed test (LDT) suitable for high-volume clinical reference laboratories, with built-in quality controls ensuring that only samples meeting minimum read depth (>9 million reads), fetal fraction (>5% for reporting), and DNA integrity criteria are reported. The entire workflow—from blood collection in Streck Cell-Free DNA BCT tubes to final clinical interpretation—is standardized and automated, minimizing human error and inter-run variability. Validation studies demonstrate 100% analytical discrimination between affected and unaffected samples in both singleton and twin pregnancies, with clinical implementation yielding positive predictive values (PPVs) of 98–100% for trisomy 21, 92–96% for trisomy 18, and 69% for trisomy 13, with the latter expected to improve as unexplained false positives are investigated. By proactively identifying and mitigating the primary sources of false-positive NIPS results, the present invention represents a significant advancement in prenatal screening accuracy, safety, and clinical reliability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a comprehensive, automated, and highly accurate noninvasive prenatal screening (NIPS) platform for the detection of fetal chromosomal abnormalities from maternal plasma. The method is designed to overcome the limitations of prior art NIPS assays by integrating innovations in sample handling, sequencing technology, bioinformatics, and clinical interpretation. The following detailed description outlines the components, procedures, and analytical frameworks that constitute the invention.

The assay begins with the collection of whole blood from a pregnant individual into specialized blood collection tubes, specifically Cell-Free DNA BCT tubes (Streck, Omaha, NE), which stabilize nucleated cells and prevent genomic DNA contamination of the plasma fraction. Blood samples are processed within four days of collection to maintain cfDNA integrity. Plasma isolation is performed entirely on an automated liquid handling system, such as the Tecan EVO 200, which executes a two-step centrifugation protocol: first, at 2,500 x g for 10 minutes at 22°C to separate plasma from whole blood, followed by a second centrifugation at 3,200 x g for 20 minutes at 22°C to remove residual cellular debris. This dual-spin protocol ensures high-purity plasma, which is critical for minimizing background noise in downstream sequencing.

From 4 mL of isolated plasma, cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is extracted using a high-yield, magnetic bead-based chemistry, such as DynaMax (Thermo Fisher Scientific), on an automated purification platform like the Kingfisher Flex System. This proprietary extraction method maximizes cfDNA recovery while maintaining fragment size distribution, which is essential for consistent library preparation. The extracted cfDNA is then converted into sequencing-ready libraries using a commercial kit optimized for low-input DNA, such as the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. During library amplification, a unique 10-base barcode is introduced via the reverse PCR primer, enabling multiplexing of up to 12 samples per sequencing lane. The forward primer is universal across all samples, ensuring uniform amplification efficiency. PCR conditions are tightly controlled—initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 seconds, followed by 10 cycles of 98°C for 10 seconds, 65°C for 30 seconds, and 72°C for 30 seconds, concluding with a 5-minute extension at 72°C—to minimize amplification bias and preserve quantitative representation of cfDNA fragments.

Post-PCR, libraries are purified using AMPure XP beads at a 1:1 bead-to-sample ratio to remove primer dimers and unincorporated nucleotides. Purified libraries are quantified using a fluorescence-based dsDNA assay, such as Quant-iT PicoGreen, read on a microplate reader like the Infinite 200 PRO. Samples are then normalized to a precise concentration of 2 nM and pooled in groups of 12. Each pool is denatured, diluted to 15 pM, and spiked with 5% PhiX control to enhance base calling accuracy during sequencing. Clonal amplification and cluster generation occur on a cBot instrument using high-output flow cells, followed by sequencing on a HiSeq 2500 system in single-read mode (36 cycles for genomic reads plus 10 cycles for index sequencing). The assay requires a minimum of 9 million high-quality reads per sample, with all bases achieving a Q-score >30, ensuring sufficient depth for robust statistical analysis at approximately 0.6X genome coverage.

The bioinformatics pipeline is a cornerstone of the invention. Raw sequencing reads are aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) and partitioned into fixed-size genomic bins—typically 50 kilobases for sex chromosome analysis and variable sizes for autosomes, selected to avoid repetitive or homologous regions. Each bin’s read count is normalized by the total autosomal read count for that sample to account for differences in sequencing depth. Crucially, the system applies a two-stage GC correction process: first, using a local polynomial regression (LOESS) algorithm based on hg19 GC content data, and second, employing a proprietary statistical smoothing technique to further reduce residual bias. This dual correction is especially critical for chromosomes 13 and 18, which are GC-rich and prone to underrepresentation in uncorrected data.

Following normalization and GC correction, chromosome representation (chrRep) is calculated for each autosome as the ratio of its total read count to the sum of read counts across all 22 autosomes. A Z-score is then computed for each chromosome using the formula Z = (x − μ) / σ, where x is the sample’s chrRep, μ is the plate-specific median chrRep derived from a large training set, and σ is the median absolute deviation (MAD) from that same training set. The training set comprises over 5,400 clinically confirmed unaffected samples, ensuring stable and representative baseline parameters. For clinical reporting, a Z-score ≤4 is classified as unaffected, >8 as affected, and values between 4 and 8 trigger manual review.

The manual review process is a critical innovation. Upon detection of an intermediate or high Z-score, the system generates a chromosomal ideogram displaying normalized bin counts across the entire genome. Analysts inspect this ideogram to determine whether the elevated signal reflects a whole-chromosome duplication (consistent with fetal trisomy) or a localized increase (suggestive of maternal microduplication). If the latter is observed, the case is flagged as a potential maternal CNV, and the ordering physician is contacted for optional maternal confirmatory testing (e.g., microarray on buffy coat DNA). Similarly, any sample with a positive Z-score for chromosomes 13, 18, or 21 is automatically scanned for concurrent copy number changes on other chromosomes. The presence of multiple elevated or suppressed Z-scores across the genome indicates a global abnormality, likely of maternal origin, and such results are not reported as fetal aneuploidies.

For sex chromosome analysis, the system uses 50-kb bins across chromosomes X and Y. Samples with fewer than 4.5 million reads are excluded. Fetal sex is determined by absolute Y-chromosome read count thresholds. Fetal fraction (FF) is estimated using two independent methods: for female fetuses, FF = 2 × (1 − Xi / Xf), where Xi is the sample’s median X-chromosome representation and Xf is the run-specific median for female samples; for male fetuses, Y-based FF is calculated using a linear model derived from training data correlating normalized Y-bin counts with X-based FF estimates. Sex chromosome aneuploidies are called by comparing X- and Y-based FF estimates against thresholds established from 649 training samples and validated on 590 characterized samples.

Microdeletion detection follows the same bin-counting principle, with predefined genomic coordinates (e.g., 22q11.2 for DiGeorge syndrome) used to sum read counts and compute region-specific Z-scores. The assay is validated for use from gestational week 10 onward, with optimal performance between weeks 10 and 15. Samples with fetal fraction <5% or failing quality metrics are reported as “no result,” prompting recollection. The entire workflow is fully automated, minimizing human intervention and ensuring reproducibility. This integrated system delivers unprecedented analytical and clinical performance, significantly reducing false positives and enhancing the reliability of prenatal screening.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Assay Development

The development of the disclosed NIPS assay began with the optimization of every step in the workflow to maximize consistency, yield, and analytical separation. Initial efforts focused on plasma isolation and cfDNA extraction. Comparative studies demonstrated that the dual-centrifugation protocol on the Tecan EVO 200 yielded plasma with significantly lower genomic DNA contamination than single-spin methods, as measured by the ratio of long to short DNA fragments. Subsequent evaluation of cfDNA extraction chemistries revealed that DynaMax on the Kingfisher Flex provided superior recovery of short cfDNA fragments (<150 bp)—the predominant species of fetal origin—compared to silica-column or other bead-based methods. This high-yield extraction was critical for achieving adequate fetal fraction, particularly in early gestation.

Library preparation was optimized using the NEBNext Ultra kit, with titration experiments determining that 10 PCR cycles provided maximal library complexity without introducing significant amplification bias. The introduction of a unique 10-base barcode in the reverse primer enabled efficient multiplexing while maintaining demultiplexing accuracy. Quality control metrics were established at each stage: post-extraction cfDNA concentration, post-PCR library yield, and post-normalization pool concentration. Automated quantification using PicoGreen ensured precise normalization, eliminating a major source of inter-sample variability present in manual workflows.

Sequencing parameters were refined to balance cost and performance. A 36-cycle single-read run on the HiSeq 2500 was selected as it provided sufficient mappability for 50-kb binning while keeping reagent costs manageable. The inclusion of 5% PhiX spike-in was found to improve base calling in low-diversity cfDNA libraries. A minimum of 9 million reads per sample was established as the threshold for reliable Z-score calculation, based on power analysis showing that this depth provided >95% confidence in chromosome representation estimates.

The bioinformatics pipeline underwent iterative refinement. Initial bin definitions were generated by excluding regions with high homology or repetitive content, as identified by BLAST alignment against the reference genome. A training set of 5,406 unaffected samples was used to establish baseline chromosome representations and calculate MADs for Z-score normalization. GC correction was implemented in two stages: first, LOESS smoothing based on published methods, and second, a proprietary algorithm that applied additional smoothing to residual outliers. This dual correction was validated using samples with known trisomies: without correction, trisomy 13 samples often fell below the Z=4 threshold; after correction, all trisomy 13 samples exceeded Z=8, demonstrating complete analytical separation.

Thresholds for clinical reporting were determined through analysis of 2,085 verification samples, including 69 trisomy 21, 20 trisomy 18, and 17 trisomy 13 cases. No unaffected sample had a Z-score >4, and no affected sample had a Z-score <8, establishing the basis for the ≤4/≥8 classification system. The intermediate zone (4–8) was reserved for cases requiring manual review, primarily to detect maternal CNVs. Ideogram visualization was developed to display normalized bin counts as a function of chromosomal position, enabling rapid visual assessment of duplication extent. This development phase culminated in a fully automated, end-to-end assay with unprecedented analytical precision.

### Example 2: Assay Verification and Validation

Verification and validation studies were conducted to confirm the analytical performance of the assay. The verification cohort included 2,085 samples: 1,990 unaffected and 95 with confirmed aneuploidies (69 trisomy 21, 20 trisomy 18, 17 trisomy 13). All unaffected samples had Z-scores ≤4, and all affected samples had Z-scores ≥8, demonstrating 100% analytical sensitivity and specificity within this dataset. GC correction was shown to be essential for chromosomes 13 and 18: uncorrected data resulted in 65% of trisomy 13 samples falling below Z=4, whereas corrected data placed all above Z=8. Chromosome 21, with normal GC content, showed excellent separation even without correction, but correction further improved consistency.

A separate validation set of 552 samples—including 21 trisomy 21, 10 trisomy 18, 1 trisomy 13, and 1 XO—replicated these findings. Additionally, 115 twin gestation samples (10 trisomy 21, 4 trisomy 18, 13 trisomy 13) were analyzed. Despite the expected dilution of fetal signal in twins, all aneuploid twin samples had Z-scores >11, and all unaffected twins had Z-scores <4, indicating superior discrimination in twin pregnancies, possibly due to higher total fetal DNA load.

Fetal sex determination was validated using 372 samples with known fetal sex (188 male). The assay achieved 99.7% concordance, with the single discordant case having a fetal fraction of 2.75%—below the 5% reporting threshold. This demonstrated that the assay’s fetal fraction estimation and sex calling are robust when quality thresholds are met.

Sex chromosome aneuploidy detection was validated using 649 training samples to establish decision boundaries between normal and abnormal X/Y fetal fraction estimates, followed by confirmation on 590 characterized samples. Microdeletion detection was validated using synthetic spike-in controls and clinical samples with known 22q11.2 deletions, confirming the ability to detect subchromosomal events with Z-scores <−7.

Concordance testing was performed by splitting 100 volunteer samples between the inventors’ laboratory and Sequenom. Results were 100% concordant, including one trisomy 21 case identified by both labs. These studies collectively confirmed the assay’s high analytical validity, reproducibility, and robustness across diverse sample types and conditions.

### Example 3: Clinical Implementations

The assay was implemented clinically in a high-volume reference laboratory, with over 10,713 samples tested in the initial rollout. Samples were accepted from gestational week 10 onward, with 90% collected between weeks 10 and 15. The overall test failure rate was 0.88%, primarily due to low fetal fraction (0.59%) or technical issues (0.29%).

Among 10,000 clinical samples, 180 (1.8%) were initially flagged as high-risk: 1.0% for trisomy 21, 0.36% for trisomy 18, 0.21% for trisomy 13, and 0.17% for sex aneuploidies. Follow-up data revealed that the assay’s review protocols successfully mitigated false positives. Two cases with intermediate Z-scores (5.11 for chr21, 6.93 for chr18) were identified as maternal microduplications via ideogram review and confirmed by maternal microarray. One case with Z=21 for trisomy 21 was found to have global copy number changes (elevated Z on chr3,9,21; suppressed on chr4,6,11); maternal fibroids were later confirmed, and the fetus was euploid. These cases would have been false positives in conventional NIPS but were correctly excluded by the invention’s review protocols.

Positive predictive values (PPVs) were substantially improved: 98% for trisomy 21 (42/43 confirmed), rising to 100% when excluding the case with maternal abnormalities; 92% for trisomy 18 (23/25 confirmed), improving to 96% after excluding a teratoma-related false positive; and 69% for trisomy 13 (9/13 confirmed). For sex chromosome aneuploidies, PPV was 86% for Turner syndrome, but would be 100% if excluding a case of maternal mosaicism that was flagged by abnormally high fetal fraction (>50%).

The assay also detected mosaic aneuploidies, with Z-scores ranging from 3.57 to 30.78, corresponding to mosaic fractions from 25% to 75% trisomic cells. While mosaicism was not predicted prospectively, the high Z-scores prompted invasive testing that confirmed the diagnosis. In twin pregnancies, four trisomy 21 cases were identified; three were confirmed as discordant (one affected, one unaffected twin), and one involved a teratoma.

These clinical data demonstrate that the invention’s integrated approach—combining automation, advanced bioinformatics, and mandatory genomic review—significantly enhances the real-world accuracy of NIPS, reducing false positives and increasing clinician and patient confidence in results.

## EQUIVALENTS

The present invention is not limited to the specific embodiments, protocols, reagents, instruments, or computational methods described herein. Those skilled in the art will recognize that numerous modifications and substitutions may be made without departing from the spirit and scope of the invention as defined by the claims. For example, while the disclosed assay utilizes the Tecan EVO 200 for plasma isolation and the Kingfisher Flex for cfDNA extraction, equivalent automated liquid handling and nucleic acid purification systems from other manufacturers (e.g., Hamilton STAR, QIAsymphony, Chemagic) may be employed with appropriate protocol adjustments. Similarly, although the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit is specified for library construction, alternative low-input or cfDNA-optimized library kits (e.g., KAPA HyperPrep, Swift Biosciences Accel-NGS) may be substituted, provided they maintain the required fidelity and yield.

The sequencing platform described is the Illumina HiSeq 2500, but the invention is equally applicable to other Illumina instruments (e.g., NovaSeq, NextSeq) or even non-Illumina NGS platforms (e.g., MGI, Ion Torrent) that can generate sufficient read depth and quality for bin-based counting. The use of 36-cycle single-read sequencing is illustrative; longer read lengths or paired-end sequencing may be used to improve mappability, particularly in repetitive regions, though they may increase cost. The 50-kilobase bin size for sex chromosome analysis is a practical choice balancing resolution and statistical power, but bin sizes may be adjusted (e.g., 25 kb, 100 kb) based on desired sensitivity and computational resources.

The bioinformatics pipeline employs LOESS-based GC correction followed by proprietary smoothing, but other normalization techniques (e.g., quantile normalization, principal component analysis for batch effect removal) may be incorporated. The Z-score calculation uses median absolute deviation (MAD) for robustness, but standard deviation or other dispersion metrics could be used in alternative implementations. The training set size of 5,406 samples is sufficient for stable parameter estimation, but larger or smaller training sets may be used depending on available data and desired precision.

The clinical thresholds (Z ≤4 unaffected, Z >8 affected) are derived from empirical data but may be adjusted in different populations or for different indications. The fetal fraction cutoff of 5% for reporting is a conservative standard; lower thresholds (e.g., 4%) may be used in research settings or with enhanced bioinformatics. The invention’s core principles—automation, comprehensive genomic review, and confounder detection—are platform-agnostic and may be implemented in various diagnostic contexts, including oncology (circulating tumor DNA analysis) or transplant monitoring (donor-derived cfDNA).

Furthermore, the specific microdeletion regions targeted (e.g., 22q11.2) may be expanded to include other clinically relevant loci (e.g., 1p36, 15q11.2), and the sex chromosome aneuploidy detection algorithm may be extended to rare karyotypes (e.g., 48,XXYY). The requirement for maternal follow-up testing in suspected maternal CNV cases is a clinical best practice but may be modified based on institutional protocols or patient consent.

In summary, the invention encompasses all methods and systems that achieve highly accurate, noninvasive prenatal screening through the integration of automated sample processing, advanced sequencing, and intelligent bioinformatics capable of distinguishing true fetal aneuploidies from maternal and technical confounders, regardless of the specific reagents, instruments, or algorithms used to implement these functions.
