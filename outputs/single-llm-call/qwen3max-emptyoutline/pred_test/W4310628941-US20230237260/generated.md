# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Anomaly detection constitutes a foundational task in machine learning with profound implications across diverse domains such as industrial quality control, cybersecurity, financial fraud prevention, and healthcare diagnostics. The core objective of anomaly detection is to identify observations that deviate significantly from the norm or expected patterns within a given dataset. These anomalous instances often represent critical events—such as manufacturing defects, network intrusions, fraudulent transactions, or rare medical conditions—that demand immediate attention and intervention. Despite decades of research and development, anomaly detection remains a challenging problem due to the inherent rarity of anomalies, the high dimensionality of modern datasets, and the dynamic nature of real-world environments where data distributions continuously evolve.

Traditional approaches to anomaly detection can be broadly categorized into three paradigms based on the availability and nature of labeled training data: supervised, unsupervised, and semi-supervised learning. In the fully-supervised setting, both normal and anomalous samples are explicitly labeled during training, enabling the construction of binary classifiers that distinguish between these two classes. However, this approach faces significant practical limitations, primarily due to the substantial cost and effort required to obtain comprehensive labeled datasets, especially when anomalies are rare events. Moreover, supervised methods often struggle with class imbalance, where the overwhelming majority of samples belong to the normal class, necessitating specialized techniques such as weighted loss functions, resampling strategies, or cost-sensitive learning to achieve acceptable performance.

At the opposite end of the spectrum lies the fully-unsupervised anomaly detection paradigm, which operates without any labeled examples. Methods in this category typically assume that the vast majority of data points are normal and attempt to model the underlying distribution of normal instances, flagging deviations from this model as potential anomalies. Popular unsupervised techniques include statistical approaches like Gaussian mixture models, distance-based methods such as Local Outlier Factor (LOF), density-based approaches like Isolation Forest, and reconstruction-based methods using autoencoders or other generative models. While unsupervised methods eliminate the need for costly labeling, they often suffer from reduced detection accuracy compared to their supervised counterparts, particularly in complex, high-dimensional spaces where the distinction between normal variations and true anomalies becomes increasingly ambiguous.

The semi-supervised anomaly detection framework represents a pragmatic middle ground that seeks to leverage the benefits of both supervised and unsupervised approaches while mitigating their respective drawbacks. In this paradigm, a limited set of labeled examples—typically consisting predominantly of normal instances, though sometimes including some anomalous samples—is available alongside a larger pool of unlabeled data. This setting aligns closely with many real-world scenarios where obtaining a small number of verified normal samples is feasible, but comprehensive labeling of all potential anomalies is prohibitively expensive or impractical. Traditional semi-supervised anomaly detection methods have employed various strategies, including one-class classification frameworks that utilize only normal labeled samples, positive-unlabeled (PU) learning approaches that work with exclusively anomalous labeled examples, and more recent deep learning-based methods that combine supervised signals with self-supervised or contrastive learning objectives.

However, a critical limitation of existing semi-supervised anomaly detection frameworks is their implicit assumption that the labeled and unlabeled data originate from identical underlying distributions. This assumption, while mathematically convenient, frequently fails to hold in practical applications due to various forms of distribution mismatch or shift. For instance, in manufacturing quality control, the labeled dataset might contain only historical defect types, while newly introduced production processes generate previously unseen anomaly patterns that appear exclusively in the unlabeled data. Similarly, in financial fraud detection, criminal tactics continuously evolve to circumvent existing detection systems, resulting in novel fraud patterns that were not present during the initial labeling phase. Additionally, human labeling practices often introduce systematic biases, as labelers tend to confidently annotate only clear-cut, easily identifiable cases while leaving ambiguous or borderline instances unlabeled. These distribution mismatches between labeled and unlabeled data can severely degrade the performance of conventional semi-supervised learning methods, which rely heavily on the assumption of distributional consistency to generate reliable pseudo-labels and propagate labels effectively.

Recent advances in semi-supervised learning, such as FixMatch, VIME, and other consistency-based regularization approaches, have demonstrated impressive results in standard classification tasks under the assumption of distributional alignment between labeled and unlabeled data. However, when applied directly to anomaly detection scenarios with distribution mismatch, these methods often exhibit suboptimal performance due to their susceptibility to confirmation bias—the tendency to reinforce initial assumptions derived from the limited labeled data rather than adapting to the true underlying distribution of the unlabeled data. Alternative approaches based on domain adaptation or transfer learning have been proposed to address distribution shifts, but these typically require substantial amounts of source domain data or additional assumptions about the nature of the distribution shift, making them less suitable for the data-scarce regime characteristic of semi-supervised anomaly detection.

Furthermore, the challenge of hyperparameter selection in semi-supervised anomaly detection is exacerbated by the limited availability of labeled validation data. Conventional hyperparameter tuning procedures require holding out a portion of the already scarce labeled data for validation purposes, effectively reducing the amount of training data available for model learning. This trade-off between validation and training data is particularly problematic in anomaly detection contexts where every labeled example carries significant informational value. Existing methods often rely on heuristic parameter settings or require prior knowledge about anomaly prevalence rates, neither of which may be available in practical deployment scenarios.

The limitations of current approaches highlight the need for a robust semi-supervised anomaly detection framework that can effectively handle distribution mismatches between labeled and unlabeled data while operating efficiently with minimal labeled examples. Such a framework should incorporate mechanisms to reduce dependence on the specific characteristics of the labeled data distribution, provide reliable pseudo-labeling even when the unlabeled data contains novel anomaly types not present in the labeled set, and enable automatic hyperparameter selection without requiring additional validation data. Addressing these challenges would significantly enhance the practical applicability of semi-supervised anomaly detection in real-world scenarios where data distributions are inherently dynamic and labeling resources are constrained.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel semi-supervised anomaly detection framework designated as Semi-supervised Pseudo-labeler Anomaly Detection with Ensembling (SPADE) that addresses the critical challenge of distribution mismatch between labeled and unlabeled data in practical anomaly detection scenarios. Unlike conventional semi-supervised learning methods that assume identical distributions for labeled and unlabeled data, SPADE is specifically designed to maintain robust performance even when these distributions differ substantially, as commonly occurs in real-world applications such as evolving manufacturing defects, adaptive financial fraud schemes, and biased human labeling practices.

The core innovation of SPADE lies in its decoupled architecture that separates the pseudo-labeling mechanism from the primary anomaly detection model. Rather than relying on a single binary classifier trained on limited labeled data to generate pseudo-labels—a approach that tends to overfit to the labeled data distribution and perform poorly under distribution shift—SPADE employs an ensemble of One-Class Classifiers (OCCs) as its pseudo-labeler component. This ensemble-based pseudo-labeler operates independently of the main predictor, utilizing only the representation space learned by a shared encoder to assign pseudo-labels to unlabeled samples through a consensus mechanism. Specifically, unlabeled samples receive positive (anomalous) pseudo-labels only when all OCCs in the ensemble agree on their anomalous nature, negative (normal) pseudo-labels only when all OCCs concur on their normal status, and remain unlabeled otherwise. This unanimous voting scheme ensures high precision in pseudo-label assignment while maintaining robustness against distribution shifts that might confuse individual classifiers.

SPADE's architecture comprises four key components working in concert: (i) a data encoder that maps input features into a latent representation space; (ii) a predictor that outputs anomaly scores based on these representations; (iii) the aforementioned ensemble-based pseudo-labeler that assigns reliable pseudo-labels to unlabeled data; and (iv) a projection head that facilitates representation learning through self-supervised objectives. The encoder and predictor are trained jointly using a composite loss function that combines supervised learning on labeled data, pseudo-supervised learning on confidently pseudo-labeled samples, and self-supervised learning on the entire dataset. This multi-objective training strategy enables the model to leverage both explicit supervision from labeled examples and implicit structural information from unlabeled data, while the self-supervised component helps learn robust representations that generalize well across distribution shifts.

A particularly innovative aspect of SPADE is its automated hyperparameter selection mechanism that eliminates the need for validation data—a critical advantage in data-scarce semi-supervised settings. Instead of requiring users to manually tune threshold parameters for pseudo-label assignment or sacrificing precious labeled samples for validation purposes, SPADE employs a partial matching approach based on Wasserstein distance minimization. This method automatically determines optimal thresholds by matching the distribution of anomaly scores from labeled normal and anomalous samples to corresponding subsets of unlabeled data, ensuring that pseudo-label assignments reflect the true underlying data structure rather than arbitrary parameter choices. In scenarios where only one class of labeled samples is available (such as positive-unlabeled or negative-unlabeled settings), SPADE seamlessly adapts by employing Otsu's method to determine the missing threshold in an unsupervised manner.

Experimental validation across multiple benchmark datasets and realistic distribution mismatch scenarios demonstrates that SPADE consistently outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods, achieving significant improvements in Area Under the Curve (AUC) metrics—up to 10.6% on tabular data and 3.6% on image data compared to the best alternative approaches. The framework shows particular strength in handling novel anomaly types not present in the labeled training data, maintaining high detection performance even when unlabeled data contains completely unseen defect patterns or fraud schemes. Furthermore, SPADE exhibits robustness to various forms of labeling bias, including scenarios where labeled data consists only of easily identifiable samples or high-risk instances selected through active learning protocols.

The practical significance of SPADE extends beyond theoretical performance gains, as it directly addresses fundamental challenges faced by practitioners deploying anomaly detection systems in dynamic real-world environments. By reducing dependence on extensive labeled datasets, accommodating distribution shifts without manual intervention, and eliminating the need for validation data during hyperparameter selection, SPADE enables more efficient, cost-effective, and reliable anomaly detection across diverse application domains. The framework's modular design also facilitates integration with various neural network architectures and self-supervised learning objectives, making it adaptable to different data modalities including tabular records, images, and potentially other structured data types.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive semi-supervised anomaly detection framework that specifically addresses the pervasive challenge of distribution mismatch between labeled and unlabeled data in practical deployment scenarios. The framework, designated as Semi-supervised Pseudo-labeler Anomaly Detection with Ensembling (SPADE), introduces several novel architectural and algorithmic innovations that collectively enable robust anomaly detection performance even when the underlying data distributions of labeled and unlabeled samples differ substantially. The following detailed description outlines the complete system architecture, operational methodology, and implementation specifics of the SPADE framework.

The SPADE framework operates on a dataset comprising both labeled training samples D_l = {(x_i^l, y_i^l)}_{i=1}^{n_l} and unlabeled training samples D_u = {x_j^u}_{j=1}^{n_u}, where x ∈ X represents feature vectors drawn from potentially different distributions P_X^l and P_X^u respectively, and y ∈ {0,1} denotes binary labels indicating normal (0) or anomalous (1) status. Unlike conventional semi-supervised learning approaches that assume P_X^l = P_X^u, SPADE explicitly accommodates scenarios where P_X^l ≠ P_X^u, which commonly arise in real-world applications due to factors such as temporal evolution of anomaly patterns, selective labeling practices, or domain shifts between data collection phases.

The core architecture of SPADE consists of four interconnected components that work synergistically to achieve robust anomaly detection: (i) a data encoder h: X → H that transforms input features into a latent representation space; (ii) a predictor q: H → [0,1] that outputs anomaly scores based on these representations; (iii) a pseudo-labeler v: H → {0,1,-1} that assigns pseudo-labels to unlabeled samples through an ensemble consensus mechanism; and (iv) a projection head g: H → G that facilitates representation learning through self-supervised objectives. Each component serves a distinct purpose while contributing to the overall robustness and effectiveness of the framework.

The data encoder h constitutes the foundational component of the SPADE architecture, responsible for mapping raw input features x ∈ X into a lower-dimensional latent representation r = h(x) ∈ H. The choice of encoder architecture depends on the data modality being processed. For tabular data, a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) with appropriate depth and width is employed, typically configured with two hidden layers where the dimensionality of each hidden layer is set to half the original feature dimension. For image data, convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures such as ResNet-18 are utilized, leveraging their proven ability to extract hierarchical visual features from complex image structures. The encoder is trained end-to-end through backpropagation, receiving gradient signals from both the predictor and projection head components, thereby learning representations that simultaneously support accurate anomaly scoring and effective self-supervised learning.

The predictor q operates on the latent representations produced by the encoder to generate anomaly scores that indicate the likelihood of each sample being anomalous. Implemented as a simple feedforward neural network or linear classifier depending on the complexity requirements, the predictor outputs values in the range [0,1] where higher scores correspond to greater anomaly likelihood. During training, the predictor receives supervision from both the original labeled samples and the pseudo-labeled samples generated by the pseudo-labeler component. This dual supervision mechanism enables the predictor to benefit from the limited explicit labels while also leveraging the structural information contained in the larger unlabeled dataset.

The pseudo-labeler v represents one of the most innovative aspects of the SPADE framework, specifically designed to address the vulnerability of conventional pseudo-labeling approaches to distribution mismatch. Rather than using the predictor itself or a similarly trained binary classifier to generate pseudo-labels—a practice that tends to propagate biases from the limited labeled data—the pseudo-labeler employs an ensemble of K independent One-Class Classifiers (OCCs) that operate solely on the latent representations produced by the encoder. Each OCC o_k: H → ℝ (k = 1,...,K) is trained exclusively on the labeled normal samples D_l^0 = {(x_i^l, y_i^l) ∈ D_l | y_i^l = 0}, learning to model the distribution of normal instances in the representation space. The ensemble size K is typically set to 5 in practical implementations, providing sufficient diversity while maintaining computational efficiency.

The pseudo-label assignment process follows a strict consensus mechanism to ensure high precision in pseudo-label generation. For each unlabeled sample x_j^u, the pseudo-labeler computes the outputs of all K OCCs on its latent representation r_j^u = h(x_j^u). A positive pseudo-label (v(r_j^u) = 1) is assigned only if all OCCs unanimously classify the sample as anomalous, which occurs when o_k(r_j^u) > η_p^k for all k = 1,...,K, where η_p^k represents the positive threshold for the k-th OCC. Similarly, a negative pseudo-label (v(r_j^u) = 0) is assigned only if all OCCs unanimously classify the sample as normal, corresponding to o_k(r_j^u) ≤ η_n^k for all k = 1,...,K, where η_n^k denotes the negative threshold for the k-th OCC. Samples that fail to achieve unanimous consensus among the OCC ensemble are assigned the unknown label (v(r_j^u) = -1) and excluded from pseudo-supervised training. This conservative approach prioritizes pseudo-label precision over recall, ensuring that only highly confident assignments contribute to model training and thereby maintaining robustness against distribution shifts that might confuse individual classifiers.

The determination of threshold parameters η_p^k and η_n^k constitutes another key innovation of the SPADE framework, addressing the critical challenge of hyperparameter selection in data-scarce semi-supervised settings. Conventional approaches typically require either manual specification of these thresholds or allocation of precious labeled samples for validation-based tuning—both of which are impractical in real-world anomaly detection scenarios. SPADE instead employs an automated threshold selection mechanism based on partial distribution matching using Wasserstein distance minimization. Specifically, for each OCC k, the positive threshold η_p^k is determined by solving the optimization problem:

η_p^k = argmin_η D_W(P_{o_k(r)|r∈D_l^1}, P_{o_k(r)|r∈D_u, o_k(r)>η})

where D_W denotes the Wasserstein distance between two probability distributions, P_{o_k(r)|r∈D_l^1} represents the distribution of OCC outputs on labeled anomalous samples, and P_{o_k(r)|r∈D_u, o_k(r)>η} corresponds to the distribution of OCC outputs on unlabeled samples exceeding threshold η. This formulation identifies the threshold that minimizes the distributional discrepancy between known anomalous samples and the subset of unlabeled samples deemed anomalous by the OCC, effectively aligning the pseudo-labeling behavior with the true underlying data structure.

Similarly, the negative threshold η_n^k is determined by:

η_n^k = argmin_η D_W(P_{o_k(r)|r∈D_l^0}, P_{o_k(r)|r∈D_u, o_k(r)≤η})

matching the distribution of OCC outputs on labeled normal samples to the subset of unlabeled samples classified as normal. This partial matching approach leverages the fundamental principle that samples of the same class should exhibit similar statistical properties in the representation space, enabling automatic threshold calibration without requiring additional validation data or prior knowledge about anomaly prevalence rates.

In scenarios where only one class of labeled samples is available—such as positive-unlabeled (PU) settings where only anomalous samples are labeled, or negative-unlabeled (NU) settings where only normal samples are labeled—SPADE adapts its threshold selection strategy accordingly. When labeled samples of one class are absent, the corresponding threshold is determined using Otsu's method, an unsupervised thresholding technique that minimizes intra-class variance in the OCC output distribution. For instance, in PU settings where only positive labeled samples D_l^1 are available, η_p^k is computed using the partial matching approach described above, while η_n^k is determined via Otsu's method applied to the OCC outputs on the entire unlabeled dataset D_u. This adaptive thresholding mechanism ensures that SPADE remains effective across diverse semi-supervised learning scenarios without requiring manual configuration adjustments.

The projection head g serves as the fourth component of the SPADE architecture, facilitating representation learning through self-supervised objectives that complement the supervised and pseudo-supervised learning signals. The specific implementation of the projection head depends on the data modality and available self-supervised learning techniques. For tabular data, a simple MLP architecture is employed, while for image data, more sophisticated approaches such as those used in CutPaste or contrastive learning frameworks may be integrated. The projection head is trained using auxiliary self-supervised losses that encourage the encoder to learn representations capturing meaningful structural relationships within the data, independent of explicit label information.

The complete SPADE framework is trained through an iterative optimization process that alternates between pseudo-label generation and model parameter updates. The training procedure begins by initializing the encoder h, predictor q, and projection head g with random parameters. The labeled dataset is partitioned into positively labeled samples D_l^1 and negatively labeled samples D_l^0 based on their ground truth labels. For each epoch of training, the following steps are executed:

First, the current encoder parameters are used to compute latent representations for all training samples. Then, for each of the K OCCs in the pseudo-labeler ensemble, the threshold parameters η_p^k and η_n^k are automatically determined using the partial matching approach (or Otsu's method when appropriate). The pseudo-labeler is constructed by applying the consensus voting mechanism to assign pseudo-labels to unlabeled samples based on the current OCC ensemble outputs and threshold parameters.

Next, the encoder h, predictor q, and projection head g are updated by minimizing a composite loss function that combines three distinct objectives:

L_total = L_sup + αL_pseudo + βL_self

where L_sup represents the supervised loss on labeled samples, L_pseudo denotes the pseudo-supervised loss on confidently pseudo-labeled samples, and L_self corresponds to the self-supervised loss on the entire dataset. The hyperparameters α and β control the relative importance of pseudo-supervised and self-supervised learning components, respectively.

The supervised loss L_sup is computed as the binary cross-entropy between predicted anomaly scores and true labels for all labeled samples:

L_sup = -∑_{(x,y)∈D_l} [y log q(h(x)) + (1-y) log(1-q(h(x)))]

The pseudo-supervised loss L_pseudo applies the same binary cross-entropy formulation but only to unlabeled samples that received confident pseudo-labels from the pseudo-labeler:

L_pseudo = -∑_{x∈D_u} 1{v(h(x)) ∈ {0,1}} [v(h(x)) log q(h(x)) + (1-v(h(x))) log(1-q(h(x)))]

where 1{·} denotes the indicator function that equals 1 when its argument is true and 0 otherwise. This formulation ensures that only samples with unanimous consensus among the OCC ensemble contribute to pseudo-supervised training, maintaining high pseudo-label quality.

The self-supervised loss L_self depends on the specific pretext task employed for representation learning. For reconstruction-based approaches, L_self might correspond to mean squared error between original inputs and reconstructed outputs. For contrastive learning methods, L_self could represent the InfoNCE loss that encourages similar samples to have closer representations while pushing dissimilar samples apart in the embedding space. The choice of self-supervised objective is guided by the data modality and available computational resources, with the goal of learning representations that capture intrinsic data structure independent of label information.

Training continues until convergence criteria are met, typically defined as no improvement in the total training loss for five consecutive epochs. Throughout training, the pseudo-labeler is updated at each epoch based on the current encoder representations and labeled data, ensuring that pseudo-label assignments remain aligned with the evolving model understanding of normal and anomalous patterns.

The operational workflow of SPADE during inference involves processing new test samples through the trained encoder and predictor components to generate final anomaly scores. Given a test sample x_test, the anomaly score is computed as q(h(x_test)), where h represents the trained encoder and q denotes the trained predictor. These scores can then be thresholded according to application-specific requirements to produce binary anomaly classifications, or used directly for ranking purposes in scenarios where relative anomaly severity is important.

Several implementation considerations enhance the practical effectiveness of the SPADE framework. Computational efficiency is maintained by retraining the OCC ensemble only once per epoch rather than at every training iteration, and by using relatively simple OCC implementations such as Gaussian Distribution Estimators (GDE) that provide good performance with minimal computational overhead. The framework's modular design allows for easy integration with various neural network architectures and self-supervised learning objectives, making it adaptable to different data modalities and application requirements. Additionally, the conservative pseudo-labeling strategy ensures that the framework remains robust even when the unlabeled dataset contains substantial contamination from novel anomaly types not present in the labeled training data.

The effectiveness of SPADE has been validated through extensive experimental evaluation across multiple benchmark datasets and realistic distribution mismatch scenarios. Results demonstrate consistent superiority over existing state-of-the-art methods, with particularly notable improvements in scenarios involving novel anomaly types, labeling bias, and temporal distribution shifts. The framework's ability to automatically calibrate hyperparameters without validation data, combined with its robust pseudo-labeling mechanism and effective integration of self-supervised learning, makes it particularly well-suited for practical deployment in real-world anomaly detection applications where data distributions are inherently dynamic and labeling resources are constrained.
