# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of noninvasive prenatal diagnostics, and more particularly to methods, systems, and computer-readable media for detecting fetal chromosomal aneuploidies—including both autosomal and sex chromosomal abnormalities—through the analysis of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) present in maternal plasma using massively parallel sequencing (MPS) combined with a novel GC-correlation statistical framework. The invention provides a robust, high-sensitivity, and high-specificity diagnostic platform that overcomes limitations inherent in prior art approaches, such as GC-bias-induced noise, inaccurate estimation of fetal DNA fraction, and insufficient statistical modeling for low-abundance fetal genetic material. Specifically, the invention introduces an advanced bioinformatics pipeline that integrates genome-wide sequencing data normalization based on chromosomal GC content, precise estimation of cell-free fetal DNA concentration using sex chromosome k-mer coverage patterns, and a dual-hypothesis t-test framework that significantly enhances the reliability of aneuploidy detection across all clinically relevant chromosomes, including chromosomes 13, 18, 21, X, and Y. The technical domain encompasses molecular diagnostics, computational genomics, statistical inference in low-signal biological contexts, and clinical implementation of next-generation sequencing technologies for reproductive health applications.

## BACKGROUND ART

Prenatal screening and diagnosis of fetal chromosomal abnormalities have long represented a critical component of obstetric care due to the significant morbidity and mortality associated with conditions such as Down syndrome (Trisomy 21), Edwards syndrome (Trisomy 18), Patau syndrome (Trisomy 13), Turner syndrome (45,X), Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), and XYY syndrome. Historically, definitive diagnosis has relied upon invasive procedures including amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling (CVS), which, while highly accurate, carry a non-negligible risk of iatrogenic miscarriage estimated at approximately 0.5% to 1%. These risks have motivated the development of noninvasive alternatives. Early noninvasive strategies combined maternal serum biomarkers (such as alpha-fetoprotein, human chorionic gonadotropin, and unconjugated estriol) with ultrasound measurements (notably nuchal translucency thickness); however, these methods suffer from limited sensitivity (typically 70–85% for Trisomy 21) and specificity, resulting in high false-positive rates that necessitate confirmatory invasive testing in a substantial proportion of pregnancies.

A transformative advance occurred in 1997 with the discovery by Lo et al. that cell-free fetal DNA (cff-DNA) circulates in maternal plasma during pregnancy, originating primarily from apoptotic trophoblast cells of the placenta. This cff-DNA is detectable as early as four weeks of gestation and is rapidly cleared from maternal circulation postpartum, making it an ideal transient biomarker. However, cff-DNA constitutes only a minor fraction—typically 5% to 10%, though variable—of the total cell-free DNA in maternal plasma, with the remainder being maternal in origin. This low fetal fraction presents a formidable analytical challenge, as conventional molecular techniques such as allele-specific PCR or quantitative real-time PCR are generally restricted to detecting paternally inherited alleles or specific mutations and lack the resolution to assess whole-chromosome copy number variations across the entire genome.

The advent of massively parallel sequencing (MPS), also known as next-generation sequencing (NGS), has enabled a paradigm shift toward genome-wide, noninvasive aneuploidy screening. In this approach, millions of short DNA fragments from maternal plasma are sequenced, aligned to the reference human genome, and the relative representation of sequences mapping to each chromosome is quantified. Under the assumption that euploid chromosomes should exhibit proportional representation, a statistically significant overrepresentation of reads mapping to a particular chromosome may indicate fetal trisomy. Initial implementations of this concept employed a simple z-score statistic, comparing the observed chromosome representation in a test sample to the mean and standard deviation derived from a cohort of euploid reference samples. While effective for Trisomy 21, which typically yields a strong signal due to its relatively high fetal fraction and biological characteristics, this basic z-score method demonstrated reduced performance for Trisomy 18 and Trisomy 13, as well as for sex chromosomal aneuploidies.

A key factor undermining the accuracy of early MPS-based methods is GC bias—a systematic distortion in sequencing coverage correlated with the guanine-cytosine (GC) content of genomic regions. This bias arises during library preparation (particularly during PCR amplification) and sequencing chemistry, where DNA fragments with extreme GC content (either very high or very low) are underrepresented compared to those with moderate GC content. Since different human chromosomes possess distinct average GC compositions—ranging from approximately 38% for chromosome 13 to over 48% for chromosome 19—the inherent chromosomal GC content interacts with platform-specific GC bias to produce non-uniform baseline coverage across chromosomes. Consequently, without correction, this technical artifact can masquerade as or obscure true biological signals of aneuploidy, especially for chromosomes whose native GC content places them at the extremes of the bias curve.

Several attempts have been made to mitigate GC bias. One approach involves binning the genome into small windows, calculating the GC content and read depth for each window, fitting a smoothing curve (e.g., LOESS regression) to model the relationship, and then normalizing read counts based on the fitted values. While useful for localized copy number variation detection, this window-based method is less optimal for whole-chromosome aneuploidy screening, where the signal is distributed across an entire chromosome and the noise from individual bins can accumulate. Another strategy, proposed by Quake et al., employs a global correction by modeling the expected coverage as a function of chromosomal GC content. However, these earlier methods often fail to fully account for the complex, non-linear, and chromosome-specific nature of the GC-coverage relationship, particularly the observation that the correlation between coverage and GC content changes sign depending on whether the chromosome’s average GC content is above or below a certain threshold (approximately 41%).

Moreover, prior art methods have inadequately addressed the critical dependency of test sensitivity on the fetal DNA fraction. Accurate estimation of this fraction is essential not only for determining test reliability but also for refining the statistical models used in aneuploidy calling. Existing approaches for fetal fraction estimation are often locus-specific (e.g., relying on single nucleotide polymorphisms or Y-chromosome sequences in male pregnancies) and thus inapplicable to female fetuses or populations lacking informative markers. Additionally, many statistical frameworks rely on a single null hypothesis (e.g., “the chromosome is euploid”) without adequately modeling the alternative hypothesis (“the chromosome is trisomic”), leading to suboptimal discrimination power, especially at low fetal fractions.

Finally, the detection of sex chromosomal aneuploidies poses unique challenges. Unlike autosomes, sex chromosomes differ between males and females, requiring gender-specific reference models. Furthermore, conditions like 45,X (Turner syndrome) involve monosomy rather than trisomy, producing a signal in the opposite direction, while mosaic cases (where only a subset of cells carries the abnormality) yield attenuated signals that are easily missed. Prior internal chromosome control methods, which normalize one chromosome against others assumed to be euploid, are particularly vulnerable to errors when multiple aneuploidies coexist or when the control chromosomes themselves are affected by GC bias.

In summary, while MPS-based noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) represents a major advance, the background art lacks a comprehensive, integrated solution that simultaneously addresses GC bias with chromosome-specific modeling, enables robust fetal fraction estimation applicable to both male and female pregnancies, employs a statistically rigorous dual-hypothesis framework for aneuploidy detection, and extends reliable performance to both autosomal and sex chromosomal abnormalities. The present invention overcomes these deficiencies through a novel GC-correlation methodology that forms the foundation of a highly accurate Noninvasive Fetal Trisomy (NIFTY) test.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive method, system, and computer-readable medium for the noninvasive detection of fetal chromosomal aneuploidies in a pregnant woman by analyzing cell-free DNA isolated from a maternal plasma sample using massively parallel sequencing combined with an innovative GC-correlation statistical framework. The core innovation lies in the recognition and mathematical modeling of a non-monotonic, chromosome-dependent relationship between sequencing coverage depth and genomic GC content, which is leveraged to significantly reduce technical noise and enhance the signal-to-noise ratio for aneuploidy detection across all chromosomes.

In one aspect, the invention establishes a precise quantitative relationship between the relative sequencing coverage of each chromosome and its inherent GC content by performing a regression analysis on a large cohort of euploid reference samples. Critically, this relationship is found to be biphasic: chromosomes with average GC content above approximately 41% exhibit a positive correlation between coverage and GC content, whereas those below 41% exhibit a negative correlation. This insight allows the construction of a chromosome-specific fitted coverage model that accurately predicts the expected coverage for any given chromosome based solely on its GC composition, thereby providing a robust theoretical baseline against which deviations indicative of aneuploidy can be measured.

In another aspect, the invention introduces a refined method for estimating the concentration of cell-free fetal DNA (cff-DNA) in maternal plasma. For pregnancies carrying a male fetus, this is achieved by comparing the observed k-mer coverage of the Y chromosome to fitted values derived separately from male and female reference datasets. For female fetuses, the invention utilizes the X chromosome coverage in conjunction with gender-specific fitted models to infer fetal fraction, overcoming a major limitation of prior art that rendered fetal fraction estimation unreliable or impossible in female pregnancies.

In yet another aspect, the invention employs a binary hypothesis testing framework based on Student’s t-tests to evaluate two competing scenarios for each chromosome: (1) the fetus is euploid for that chromosome, and (2) the fetus is trisomic for that chromosome. The first t-test assesses the deviation of observed coverage from the euploid expectation, while the second t-test evaluates the fit to a trisomic model that incorporates the estimated fetal DNA fraction. A logarithmic likelihood odds ratio is then computed from the probability densities of these two t-statistics, providing a continuous, probabilistic measure of confidence in the aneuploidy call. This dual-model approach significantly improves sensitivity and specificity, particularly at low fetal fractions, compared to single-hypothesis methods.

For sex chromosomal aneuploidies, the invention implements a multi-tiered detection strategy. First, fetal gender is classified using a combination of a fixed threshold on Y-chromosome coverage and a logistic regression model incorporating both X and Y coverage. Subsequently, gender-specific statistical tests are applied: for putative female fetuses, deviations in X-chromosome coverage are assessed against a female euploid baseline to detect 45,X (monosomy X) or 47,XXX (trisomy X); for putative male fetuses, a specialized t-test accounts for the expected X-chromosome dosage in the context of a Y-chromosome-derived fetal fraction estimate to identify 47,XXY or 47,XYY. Additional confirmation is provided by analyzing the consistency between fetal fraction estimates derived independently from X and Y chromosomes.

The invention further includes stringent quality control metrics that integrate both direct sequencing indicators (e.g., Q20 score, PCR duplication rate) and indirect analytical indicators (e.g., number of unique reads, genome-wide GC consistency) to ensure data reliability before aneuploidy calling. The entire methodology is implemented within a unified bioinformatics pipeline that processes raw sequencing data through alignment, GC-correction, fetal fraction estimation, statistical testing, and final classification.

In a system embodiment, the invention comprises a computing device configured with memory and processor-executable instructions to perform the aforementioned steps, receiving as input sequencing data from a maternal plasma sample and outputting a diagnostic report indicating the presence or absence of specified aneuploidies. In a computer-readable medium embodiment, the invention provides a non-transitory storage medium encoded with program code that, when executed, carries out the method of the invention.

The NIFTY test of the present invention demonstrates superior clinical performance, achieving 100% sensitivity and ≥99.7% specificity for Trisomies 13, 18, and 21, and high accuracy for sex chromosomal aneuploidies including 45,X, 47,XXY, and 47,XYY, thereby offering a safe, reliable, and comprehensive noninvasive alternative to conventional invasive diagnostic procedures.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### I. DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed to them below, unless the context clearly indicates otherwise. The term “cell-free DNA” (cfDNA) refers to fragmented DNA molecules circulating extracellularly in bodily fluids, such as plasma or serum, and in the context of prenatal testing, comprises a mixture of maternal-derived cfDNA and cell-free fetal DNA (cff-DNA). The term “cell-free fetal DNA” (cff-DNA) specifically denotes the fraction of cfDNA in maternal plasma that originates from the placental trophoblast cells of the developing fetus, typically constituting between 3% and 20% of the total cfDNA during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. The term “massively parallel sequencing” (MPS), also known as next-generation sequencing (NGS), refers to high-throughput DNA sequencing technologies capable of simultaneously determining the nucleotide sequences of millions to billions of DNA fragments in a single run, including but not limited to platforms manufactured by Illumina, Thermo Fisher Scientific, and Pacific Biosciences. The term “k-mer” refers to a contiguous subsequence of length k nucleotides extracted from a DNA sequence; in the context of this invention, k corresponds to the length of the sequencing reads generated by the MPS platform, and a “unique k-mer” is one that maps to a single, unambiguous location in the reference human genome. The term “universal unique reads set” denotes the complete collection of all possible k-mers derived from the reference human genome that satisfy the criterion of unique mappability, serving as a filter to exclude ambiguously mapped reads from downstream analysis. The term “relative k-mer coverage” for a given chromosome in a sample is defined as the ratio of the k-mer coverage of that chromosome to the average k-mer coverage across all autosomes in the same sample, thereby normalizing for inter-sample variations in total sequencing depth. The term “GC content” of a genomic region, such as a chromosome, refers to the percentage of nitrogenous bases that are either guanine (G) or cytosine (C) within that region, calculated as (number of G + number of C) / (total number of bases) × 100%. The term “GC-correlation” describes the empirically observed statistical relationship between the relative k-mer coverage of a chromosome and its inherent GC content, which is modeled using regression techniques to generate a fitted coverage value that serves as the expected baseline under euploid conditions. The term “fetal DNA concentration” or “fetal fraction” (denoted as ε) refers to the proportion of cff-DNA within the total cfDNA in a maternal plasma sample, expressed as a decimal or percentage, and is a critical parameter influencing the sensitivity of aneuploidy detection. The term “aneuploidy” denotes an abnormal number of chromosomes in a cell, deviating from the typical diploid complement; this includes trisomies (three copies of a chromosome, e.g., Trisomy 21), monosomies (one copy, e.g., 45,X), and other numerical abnormalities such as 47,XXY or 47,XYY. The term “euploid” describes a cell or organism having the normal, expected number of chromosomes for its species, i.e., 46 chromosomes in humans, comprising 22 pairs of autosomes and two sex chromosomes (XX or XY). The term “binary hypothesis testing” refers to a statistical framework in which two mutually exclusive hypotheses are evaluated: a null hypothesis (H₀) and an alternative hypothesis (H₁), with the decision based on the relative likelihood of the observed data under each hypothesis. The term “logarithmic likelihood odds ratio” is a statistical measure computed as the natural logarithm of the ratio of the probability density of the observed test statistic under the null hypothesis to that under the alternative hypothesis, used herein to quantify the strength of evidence favoring one hypothesis over the other. The term “sex chromosomal aneuploidy” encompasses any numerical abnormality involving the X or Y chromosomes, including but not limited to 45,X (Turner syndrome), 47,XXX, 47,XXY (Klinefelter syndrome), and 47,XYY. The term “gestational age” refers to the time elapsed since the first day of the woman’s last menstrual period, commonly used to date pregnancy and expressed in weeks. The term “quality control indicators” include both direct metrics derived from the sequencing process itself (e.g., Q20 score, representing the percentage of bases with a Phred quality score ≥20, and PCR duplication rate, indicating the fraction of reads with identical start and end coordinates) and indirect metrics derived from bioinformatic analysis (e.g., total number of uniquely aligned reads, consistency of genome-wide GC content with reference distributions).

### II. ESTABLISHING A RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN COVERAGE DEPTH AND GC CONTENT

The foundation of the present invention rests upon the empirical discovery and rigorous mathematical modeling of a previously uncharacterized, non-linear relationship between the relative sequencing coverage depth of human chromosomes and their inherent genomic GC content. This relationship, herein termed “GC-correlation,” is not merely a uniform bias but exhibits a chromosome-specific dependency that fundamentally alters how sequencing artifacts manifest across the genome. To establish this relationship, the inventors conducted a systematic analysis using a large reference cohort of 300 pregnant women confirmed by invasive karyotyping to be carrying euploid fetuses. From each maternal plasma sample, cell-free DNA was subjected to massively parallel sequencing, generating millions of short reads that were subsequently processed through a high-stringency alignment pipeline.

The alignment process began with the construction of a universal unique reads set, which was derived by computationally fragmenting the human reference genome (e.g., NCBI build 36/hg18) into all possible k-mers of length k, where k matched the read length of the sequencing platform (e.g., 36 or 50 nucleotides). Only those k-mers that mapped uniquely to a single genomic locus—without mismatches or ambiguous alignments—were retained in this set. Sequencing reads from each sample were then filtered to include only those that aligned perfectly (0-mismatch) to this universal unique reads set, thereby eliminating reads that could originate from repetitive or homologous regions and significantly reducing mapping ambiguity and associated noise.

For each sample and each chromosome, the k-mer coverage (Cᵢ,ⱼ) was calculated as the number of unique reads mapped to chromosome j divided by the total number of unique reads theoretically mappable to chromosome j, accounting for chromosome length. To normalize for variations in total sequencing depth between samples, the relative k-mer coverage (rᵢ,ⱼ) was computed as the ratio of Cᵢ,ⱼ to the average k-mer coverage across all 22 autosomes in the same sample. This normalization step ensures that the relative representation of each chromosome is assessed on a consistent scale, independent of the absolute number of reads generated per sample.

The average GC content for each chromosome was precomputed from the reference genome. Plotting the relative k-mer coverage (rᵢ,ⱼ) against the chromosomal GC content for the 300 euploid samples revealed a striking and reproducible pattern: the correlation between coverage and GC content was not monotonic but changed sign at a critical threshold of approximately 41% GC. Specifically, chromosomes with an average GC content greater than 41%—including chromosomes 17, 19, and 22—exhibited a statistically significant positive correlation, meaning that higher GC content was associated with higher relative sequencing coverage. Conversely, chromosomes with an average GC content less than 41%—such as chromosomes 4, 13, and 18—displayed a significant negative correlation, wherein lower GC content corresponded to lower coverage. Chromosomes with GC content near the 41% inflection point, such as chromosome 11, showed no significant correlation, indicating that their coverage was minimally affected by GC bias under the experimental conditions.

To further elucidate the structural basis of this phenomenon, the inventors performed a hierarchical clustering analysis based on the distribution of all unique 35-mers across the genome, categorized by their exact GC count (ranging from 0 to 35 G/C bases). This analysis confirmed that chromosomes naturally cluster according to their overall GC richness: GC-rich chromosomes like 19 and 22 grouped together, while GC-poor chromosomes like 4 and 13 formed a separate cluster. This intrinsic genomic architecture, when combined with the known preference of MPS platforms for amplifying and sequencing fragments within a moderate GC range (typically 40–60%), explains the observed biphasic correlation. During PCR amplification in library preparation, fragments from GC-rich chromosomes may be slightly favored over extremely GC-poor ones, but both extremes are disfavored relative to mid-GC fragments. Since entire chromosomes are GC-rich or GC-poor, this results in a net overrepresentation of high-GC chromosomes and underrepresentation of low-GC chromosomes relative to a hypothetical uniform baseline.

Armed with this insight, the inventors developed a chromosome-specific regression model to capture the GC-correlation. For each chromosome j, a LOESS (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing) regression was performed on the pooled data from the 300 euploid samples, fitting the relative k-mer coverage (rᵢ,ⱼ) as a smooth function fⱼ of the chromosomal GC content (GCᵢ,ⱼ). The output of this regression, denoted as cr′ᵢ,ⱼ = fⱼ(GCᵢ,ⱼ), represents the fitted or expected relative coverage for chromosome j in sample i under the assumption of euploidy, effectively correcting for the systematic influence of GC bias. This fitted value serves as the critical theoretical baseline in all subsequent statistical tests for aneuploidy. Importantly, because the relationship is chromosome-specific, the model inherently accounts for the varying slopes and curvatures of the GC-coverage relationship across the genome, providing a far more accurate correction than global or window-based normalization methods. The robustness of this model was validated by demonstrating that the standard deviation of the residuals (observed minus fitted coverage) stabilized when the reference cohort exceeded 100 samples, confirming that 300 samples provide a sufficiently large and stable reference for clinical application.

### III. DETERMINING A FETAL GENETIC ABNORMALITY

The detection of fetal aneuploidy according to the present invention is a multi-step process that integrates GC-corrected coverage data, precise fetal DNA fraction estimation, and a sophisticated binary hypothesis testing framework. The process begins after the computation of the GC-corrected relative k-mer coverage (crᵢ,ⱼ) for each chromosome j in the test sample i, and the derivation of the corresponding fitted euploid baseline (cr′ᵢ,ⱼ) from the GC-correlation model described above.

A pivotal prerequisite for accurate aneuploidy detection is the estimation of the fetal DNA fraction (ε), as the magnitude of the coverage deviation caused by an extra chromosome is directly proportional to ε. The invention provides a novel method for estimating ε that is applicable to both male and female pregnancies. In the case of a male fetus, the presence of Y-chromosome sequences in maternal plasma provides a direct fetal-specific marker. The observed relative coverage of the Y chromosome (crᵢ,Y) is compared to two fitted baselines: cr′ᵢ,Y,f, which is the expected Y coverage in a euploid female pregnancy (effectively zero, but modeled from female reference data to account for background noise), and cr′ᵢ,Y,m, the expected Y coverage in a euploid male pregnancy derived from a reference set of 19 euploid adult males. The fetal fraction is then calculated as εᵢ,Y = (crᵢ,Y − cr′ᵢ,Y,f) / (cr′ᵢ,Y,m − cr′ᵢ,Y,f). For female fetuses, where no Y chromosome is present, the invention leverages the fact that the X chromosome in a female fetus is present in two copies, while the mother also contributes two X chromosomes. By comparing the observed X coverage (crᵢ,X) to gender-specific fitted models (cr′ᵢ,X,f for female and cr′ᵢ,X,m for male references), a fetal fraction estimate εᵢ,X can be derived, though with lower precision than in male pregnancies due to the absence of a strictly fetal-specific marker.

Once ε is estimated, the invention applies a binary hypothesis testing strategy for each autosome j. The first hypothesis test evaluates the null hypothesis H₀: “the fetus is euploid for chromosome j” against the implicit alternative. The test statistic is a t-value computed as tᵢ,ⱼ,first = (crᵢ,ⱼ − cr′ᵢ,ⱼ) / sdⱼ, where sdⱼ is the standard deviation of the residuals (cr − cr′) for chromosome j derived from the euploid reference cohort. A large absolute value of tᵢ,ⱼ,first suggests a significant deviation from euploidy. The second hypothesis test explicitly models the alternative scenario H₁: “the fetus is trisomic for chromosome j.” Under trisomy, the expected fetal contribution to chromosome j coverage is increased by a factor proportional to ε/2 (since the fetus contributes three copies instead of two, adding one extra copy out of a total of two maternal plus three fetal = five copies, but normalized in the relative coverage metric). Thus, the expected coverage under trisomy is cr′ᵢ,ⱼ(1 + εᵢ/2), and the second t-value is tᵢ,ⱼ,second = (crᵢ,ⱼ − cr′ᵢ,ⱼ(1 + εᵢ/2)) / sdⱼ. A small absolute value of tᵢ,ⱼ,second indicates good agreement with the trisomic model.

The invention then computes a logarithmic likelihood odds ratio Lᵢ,ⱼ = log[ p(tᵢ,ⱼ,first | H₀) / p(tᵢ,ⱼ,second | H₁) ], where p(t | H) is the probability density function of the t-distribution with appropriate degrees of freedom. This ratio quantifies how much more likely the observed data is under the euploid hypothesis than under the trisomic hypothesis. A positive Lᵢ,ⱼ favors euploidy, while a negative value favors trisomy. In practice, the inventors established a decision threshold of Lᵢ,ⱼ > 1 to confidently classify a chromosome as euploid, and conversely, Lᵢ,ⱼ ≤ 1 (or more stringently, based on validation data) to call trisomy. This dual-model approach is far more powerful than single-threshold z-score methods because it actively fits the expected signal under aneuploidy, rather than merely detecting any deviation from euploidy, which could be due to noise.

For sex chromosomal aneuploidies, a specialized workflow is employed. First, fetal gender is classified using a dual-criteria system: (1) a fixed threshold on Y coverage (e.g., crᵢ,Y < 0.04 for female, > 0.051 for male, with an indeterminate zone in between), and (2) a logistic regression model logit(pᵢ) = β₀ + β₁crᵢ,X + β₂crᵢ,Y, trained on reference data to output the probability pᵢ of a male fetus. Samples are classified as male if pᵢ > 0.8, female if pᵢ < 0.3, and indeterminate otherwise. For samples classified as female, a t-test is performed on the X chromosome: tᵢ,X = (crᵢ,X − cr′ᵢ,X,f) / sdX,f. A significantly low t-value (e.g., < −2.5) indicates monosomy X (45,X), while a high t-value (> 2.5) suggests trisomy X (47,XXX). For male samples, the detection of XXY or XYY is more complex because both conditions involve an extra sex chromosome but affect X coverage differently. The test assumes Y is monosomic and extrapolates the expected X coverage under euploidy as cr′ᵢ,X,f(1 − εᵢ,Y/2), reflecting the dilution of maternal X by fetal XY. The t-score tᵢ = (crᵢ,X − cr′ᵢ,X,f(1 − εᵢ,Y/2)) / sdX,f is computed; a high t-value (> 2.5) suggests an extra X chromosome (XXY) or, less directly, an extra Y (XYY). Final discrimination between XXY and XYY relies on auxiliary markers: in XXY, the fetal fraction estimated from the X chromosome (εᵢ,X) will be near zero (since the fetus has only one X, same as mother), whereas in XYY, the ratio of Y-derived to X-derived fetal fraction (R = εᵢ,Y / εᵢ,X) will be approximately 2, reflecting the two Y chromosomes versus one X in the fetus.

### IV. COMPUTER READABLE MEDIUM AND SYSTEM FOR DIAGNOSIS OF A FETAL GENETIC ABNORMALITY

The methodologies of the present invention are implemented within a specialized computational system designed for clinical diagnostic use. This system comprises one or more computing devices, each equipped with a central processing unit (CPU), volatile and non-volatile memory, input/output interfaces, and network connectivity. The system is configured to receive raw sequencing data files (e.g., in FASTQ format) generated from the massively parallel sequencing of cell-free DNA extracted from a maternal plasma sample. The data may be transferred electronically from a sequencing instrument or uploaded via a secure network interface.

Stored within the system’s memory is a bioinformatics software pipeline embodying the invention’s algorithms. Upon initiation, the pipeline executes a series of modules in a defined sequence. The first module performs high-efficiency alignment of sequencing reads to the human reference genome using the precomputed universal unique reads set as a filter, ensuring that only uniquely mappable reads are retained for analysis. The second module calculates k-mer coverage for each chromosome and normalizes it to derive relative k-mer coverage. The third module applies the GC-correlation model: for each chromosome, it retrieves the pre-stored LOESS regression function fⱼ and computes the fitted euploid coverage cr′ᵢ,ⱼ based on the chromosome’s known GC content. Concurrently, the system estimates the fetal DNA fraction ε using the gender-specific formulas described herein, leveraging Y-chromosome data for male pregnancies and X-chromosome modeling for female pregnancies.

The fourth module executes the binary hypothesis testing framework for each autosome, computing the two t-statistics and the logarithmic likelihood odds ratio, and applying the predetermined decision thresholds to classify each chromosome as euploid or trisomic. A fifth module handles sex chromosome analysis: it classifies fetal gender using the dual-threshold and logistic regression models, then applies the gender-appropriate statistical tests for sex chromosomal aneuploidies, incorporating auxiliary markers like the R-ratio for XYY detection. Throughout this process, a quality control module monitors both direct sequencing metrics (e.g., Q20 score, duplication rate) and indirect analytical metrics (e.g., total unique reads, GC consistency) against predefined acceptance criteria; if any metric falls outside acceptable limits, the system flags the sample for review or rejection before final reporting.

The final output of the system is a structured diagnostic report, which may be displayed on a user interface, printed, or transmitted electronically to a healthcare provider. The report indicates, for each chromosome of interest (13, 18, 21, X, Y), whether an aneuploidy was detected, along with supporting statistics (e.g., t-values, L-ratios, fetal fraction estimates) and quality control status. The system may also include a database for storing reference models, patient data (with appropriate privacy safeguards), and historical results for longitudinal analysis.

The invention also encompasses a non-transitory computer-readable medium, such as a hard disk drive, solid-state drive, optical disc, or cloud-based storage, encoded with program instructions that, when loaded into and executed by a computer system, cause the system to perform the methods described above. This medium enables the distribution and deployment of the NIFTY test software to clinical laboratories worldwide, ensuring standardized, high-quality analysis.

### V. EXAMPLES

The efficacy and robustness of the NIFTY test were rigorously validated in a prospective clinical study involving 903 pregnant women recruited from multiple medical centers in China between June 2009 and August 2010. All participants provided informed consent, and full karyotyping results from amniotic fluid or CVS served as the gold standard for comparison. The cohort included 866 euploid pregnancies and 37 aneuploid cases: 2 Trisomy 13, 12 Trisomy 18, 16 Trisomy 21, 4 cases of 45,X (including 1 mosaic), 2 XXY, and 1 XYY. Maternal plasma samples were processed using a standardized protocol: 5 mL of blood was collected in EDTA tubes, centrifuged twice to remove cells, and stored at −80°C until DNA extraction. Libraries were prepared from 600 µL of plasma using a modified Illumina protocol, followed by single-end sequencing on GAIIx or HiSeq 2000 platforms, yielding 2–4 million raw reads per sample. After stringent alignment to the universal unique reads set, an average of 1.7 million unique reads per sample were used for analysis.

The GC-correlation model was built using 300 randomly selected euploid samples. As predicted, chromosomes clustered by GC content in k-mer distribution analyses, and the biphasic coverage-GC relationship was confirmed, with an inflection point at 41% GC. The standard deviation of coverage residuals stabilized with >100 reference samples, validating the model’s robustness. Fetal fraction estimation showed a weak but significant increase with gestational age (correlation coefficient 0.1246), consistent with biological expectations.

In aneuploidy detection, the NIFTY test achieved perfect sensitivity (100%) for Trisomy 13 (2/2), Trisomy 21 (16/16), XXY (2/2), and XYY (1/1). For Trisomy 18, it detected all 12 cases with 99.7% specificity (1 false positive in 891 controls). For 45,X, it identified 3 of 4 cases (75% sensitivity), missing only the mosaic case with 46% normal cells, demonstrating the expected limitation in low-level mosaicism. Fetal gender was correctly identified in 99.9% of 896 non-aneuploid cases. Quality control metrics, including Q20 > 85% and duplication rate < 20%, ensured data reliability.

When compared against three prior art methods—the standard z-score (Chiu et al.), GC-corrected z-score (Chen et al.), and internal chromosome control (Lau et al.)—the NIFTY test demonstrated superior performance. It exhibited the lowest coefficient of variation (CV) across chromosomes 13, 18, and 21, translating to higher sensitivity for Trisomies 13 and 18. Crucially, it was the only method capable of reliably detecting sex chromosomal aneuploidies without gender confusion, as the other approaches either failed to model sex chromosomes appropriately or produced high false-negative rates for XXY/XYY.

These examples conclusively demonstrate that the GC-correlation methodology of the present invention provides a clinically validated, highly accurate platform for noninvasive prenatal screening of a broad spectrum of fetal chromosomal abnormalities, fulfilling a critical unmet need in reproductive medicine.
