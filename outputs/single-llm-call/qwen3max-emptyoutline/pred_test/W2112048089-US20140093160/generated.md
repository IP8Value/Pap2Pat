# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of medical imaging and image-guided radiation therapy (IGRT), and more specifically to a noninvasive, real-time tumor tracking method for use in the treatment of moving tumors located in the lung and abdominal regions. The invention provides a novel computational framework that enables accurate estimation of three-dimensional tumor position using only orthogonal X-ray imaging sequences without reliance on implanted fiducials, external markers, offline training, or manual annotation. By formulating tumor motion tracking as an online regression problem between image features extracted from dual-plane radiographic views and underlying tumor motion parameters defined on a Riemannian manifold, the invention achieves sub-pixel accuracy at speeds compatible with clinical real-time requirements. The method is particularly suited for low-contrast tumors that are difficult to segment or delineate visually, and it operates robustly under varying respiratory patterns, imaging noise, and limited spatial resolution—conditions commonly encountered in clinical practice. The invention thus addresses critical limitations of existing tumor tracking technologies by eliminating invasive procedures, reducing treatment margins, minimizing radiation exposure to healthy tissues, and enabling continuous beam delivery during respiration-induced tumor motion.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Image-guided radiation therapy (IGRT) has become a cornerstone of modern oncology for treating thoracic and abdominal malignancies, where tumors exhibit significant displacement due to respiratory motion. Accurate knowledge of tumor position throughout the breathing cycle is essential to confine the high-dose radiation volume precisely to the target while sparing surrounding healthy organs. Conventional IGRT approaches compensate for this motion by expanding the planning target volume (PTV) with large safety margins—often several centimeters—to ensure tumor coverage despite positional uncertainty. However, such margins inevitably irradiate normal tissues, leading to increased risk of acute and late toxicities, reduced quality of life, and potential dose limitations that compromise therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, there is a compelling clinical need for precise, real-time tumor tracking systems that can eliminate or drastically reduce these margins.

Historically, tumor tracking has relied on surrogate markers that correlate with internal tumor motion. One widely adopted strategy involves the percutaneous implantation of metallic fiducials—small radio-opaque markers—directly adjacent to or within the tumor under computed tomography (CT) guidance. These internal fiducials serve as high-contrast reference points visible in fluoroscopic X-ray imaging, allowing continuous monitoring of tumor position during treatment. While effective in some cases, this approach is inherently invasive and carries significant procedural risks. Studies have reported complication rates of up to 40% for CT-guided fiducial placement, including pneumothorax (lung collapse), hemorrhage, infection, and marker migration. Moreover, fiducials may dislodge or shift between simulation and treatment sessions or even during a single fraction due to tissue deformation, leading to misregistration and inaccurate targeting. The requirement for surgical intervention also excludes patients with comorbidities or poor pulmonary function who cannot tolerate the procedure.

To circumvent invasiveness, alternative methods employ external surrogates such as reflective markers placed on the patient’s chest or abdomen, whose motion is tracked via optical cameras. A correspondence model—typically derived from a pre-treatment calibration phase—is then used to infer internal tumor motion from external surface dynamics. However, the relationship between external respiration and internal organ displacement is highly patient-specific, nonlinear, and subject to hysteresis, making such models prone to failure under irregular breathing, changes in posture, or variations in tidal volume. Furthermore, the calibration process itself often requires simultaneous acquisition of internal and external motion data over multiple breathing cycles, which is time-consuming and not always feasible in routine clinical workflows.

Parametric motion modeling represents another class of approaches, wherein tumor trajectories are approximated using mathematical functions (e.g., sinusoidal, polynomial, or principal component analysis-based models) trained on historical motion data. These methods typically demand extensive manual labeling of tumor positions across numerous frames to establish a reliable motion template, rendering them impractical for real-time application and susceptible to inter-operator variability. Additionally, they assume stationarity in respiratory patterns, which is frequently violated in clinical settings due to patient anxiety, fatigue, or voluntary breath-holding.

Template matching techniques offer a markerless alternative by correlating a reference tumor image patch with subsequent frames to locate the best-matching region. Although conceptually straightforward, these methods struggle with low-contrast tumors where the boundary between neoplastic and healthy tissue is indistinct, resulting in ambiguous similarity metrics and tracking drift. They are also computationally intensive, especially when searching large neighborhoods or employing multi-scale strategies, thereby limiting their suitability for real-time guidance. Similarly, optical flow algorithms estimate dense motion fields by solving variational optimization problems that enforce smoothness constraints; however, they often fail in X-ray images due to sparse texture, lack of distinctive features, and the presence of overlapping anatomical structures that violate brightness constancy assumptions.

Particle filtering and other Bayesian tracking frameworks maintain a probabilistic distribution over possible tumor states and update beliefs based on observation likelihoods. While theoretically powerful, these methods require sampling a vast number of hypotheses per frame to adequately cover the motion space, leading to prohibitive computational costs. Moreover, in low-texture environments typical of radiographic imaging, the likelihood function becomes flat and uninformative, causing particle degeneracy and catastrophic tracking failures.

Recent advances in machine learning have introduced deep neural networks for tumor localization, but these solutions necessitate large annotated datasets for offline training, exhibit poor generalization across patients and tumor types, and lack interpretability—critical drawbacks in safety-critical medical applications. None of the existing methods simultaneously satisfy the clinical imperatives of noninvasiveness, real-time performance, sub-millimeter accuracy, robustness to image quality degradation, and independence from patient-specific calibration or prior data.

Against this backdrop, there remains a long-felt but unsolved need for a tumor tracking system that operates without implanted devices or external sensors, adapts instantly to any patient without pre-training, functions reliably on standard clinical X-ray hardware, and delivers precision sufficient to enable millimeter-level PTV margins. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a fundamentally new paradigm: online regression of tumor motion directly from image features using geometrically principled modeling on manifolds, thereby overcoming the limitations of both traditional and data-driven approaches.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a noninvasive, real-time tumor tracking method for image-guided radiation therapy that accurately estimates the three-dimensional position of a moving tumor using only a pair of orthogonal X-ray image sequences, without requiring implanted fiducials, external markers, offline training, manual annotation, or tumor segmentation. The core innovation lies in the formulation of tumor tracking as an online regression problem that maps image-derived feature vectors to tumor motion parameters defined on a Riemannian manifold, leveraging the geometric structure of affine transformations to ensure physically plausible motion estimates.

In accordance with the invention, the method begins at the initial time point of treatment, where the tumor’s starting position is known—typically established through table alignment, initial imaging, or physician input. From this known location, a set of random three-dimensional motion hypotheses is generated around the tumor, constrained by biomechanical priors of respiratory motion (e.g., limiting translation magnitude or excluding unrealistic rotations). Each hypothesis corresponds to a small incremental affine transformation represented as a 4×4 matrix in homogeneous coordinates. These synthetic motions are projected onto the two orthogonal X-ray imaging planes (e.g., coronal and sagittal views), and for each projection, an image feature vector is computed within a dynamically determined tracking window centered at the projected tumor location. The preferred feature descriptor is the Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG), which captures local edge orientation statistics and is highly sensitive to structural changes induced by motion, unlike intensity-based features that are invariant to displacement.

The concatenated feature vectors from both views and their corresponding motion matrices form a training dataset. Crucially, because affine motion matrices constitute a Lie group—a type of Riemannian manifold—the regression is performed in the tangent space of the manifold using the matrix logarithm to linearize the motion parameters. This avoids the geometric distortions inherent in naive vectorization of transformation matrices. A ridge regression model is then learned in closed form by solving an overcomplete least-squares problem with Tikhonov regularization, yielding a single regression matrix that maps feature vectors to motion increments. This learning phase is completed in under 50 milliseconds and requires no patient-specific data beyond the initial tumor position.

During subsequent tracking, for each new pair of X-ray frames, the method computes the HOG feature vectors within the current estimated tumor windows, concatenates them, and applies the pre-learned regression matrix to obtain a motion increment in the tangent space. This increment is then mapped back to the manifold via the matrix exponential to yield a valid affine transformation, which is composed with the previous tumor pose to update the current 3D position. The entire tracking step—feature extraction and matrix multiplication—executes in less than 30 milliseconds, enabling real-time operation at clinical frame rates.

A key enhancement of the invention is the automatic determination of an optimal tracking window size for each patient and view. Rather than using a fixed or manually selected window, the method evaluates candidate window dimensions by measuring the self-similarity of local image patches: smaller mean distances among dissimilar patches indicate higher discriminative power. The window size that maximizes this discriminatory capability—typically ranging from 0.9 to 1.3 times the tumor’s bounding box dimensions—is selected independently for each orthogonal view, ensuring robustness across diverse tumor morphologies and imaging conditions.

The invention offers two operational modes: a 3D regression (3DR) mode that learns a joint model mapping combined biview features directly to 3D motion, and a 2D regression (2DR) mode that trains separate regressors per view and triangulates the 3D position via back-projection of 2D estimates. The 3DR mode is preferred for its superior speed (twice as fast as 2DR) and inherent consistency between views, as it avoids potential divergence arising from independent 2D tracking errors.

Extensive validation on challenging clinical datasets—including low-resolution, low-contrast, noisy digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRRs) from ten real patients with diverse tumor locations and breathing patterns—demonstrates that the invention achieves an average 3D tracking error of approximately 1.05 pixels (7.5% of average tumor displacement), significantly outperforming state-of-the-art optical flow and particle filter methods by factors of 3–5 in accuracy. Critically, the method maintains this precision even for tumors with indistinct boundaries where segmentation-based approaches fail, and it exhibits immunity to breathing irregularities due to its frame-by-frame, memoryless design. When translated to clinical imaging resolutions (e.g., 2048×2048 flat-panel detectors), the pixel error corresponds to sub-millimeter physical accuracy (0.14–0.42 mm), enabling PTV margin reduction from centimeters to millimeters and dramatically decreasing radiation dose to healthy tissues.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, self-contained tumor tracking solution that is noninvasive, instantaneous, universally applicable, and clinically deployable, thereby addressing a decades-old challenge in radiation oncology with a mathematically rigorous and computationally efficient approach.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a method and system for real-time, noninvasive tumor tracking in image-guided radiation therapy using orthogonal X-ray imaging. The following detailed description sets forth the preferred embodiments of the invention with sufficient particularity to enable those skilled in the art to practice the invention without undue experimentation. It should be understood that the invention is not limited to the specific embodiments described herein, as various modifications and equivalents will become apparent to those skilled in the art upon reading this disclosure.

The method commences at the initialization phase, which occurs at the beginning of a radiation therapy session when the patient is positioned on the treatment couch and the initial tumor location is established. This initial position may be determined through various means known in the art, such as alignment of external lasers with skin marks, verification via cone-beam CT, or direct physician annotation on the first pair of orthogonal X-ray images acquired by the treatment machine’s onboard imaging system (e.g., kV X-ray sources mounted perpendicular to the treatment beam). Let this initial 3D tumor pose be denoted as \( M_0 \), represented as a 4×4 affine transformation matrix that maps a canonical unit cube in object space to the tumor’s bounding volume in world coordinates.

From \( M_0 \), the system generates a set of \( n \) random motion hypotheses \( \{ \Delta M_i^\star \}_{i=1}^n \), each representing a small incremental 3D affine transformation that could plausibly describe the tumor’s motion over a single respiratory cycle or fraction thereof. To ensure biological plausibility and computational efficiency, these hypotheses are constrained by respiratory biomechanics: translational components are limited to ±15 mm in any direction (based on clinical observations of maximum tumor excursion), rotational angles are capped at ±10 degrees, and scaling factors are restricted to [0.9, 1.1] to prevent unrealistic deformations. Skew parameters are similarly bounded. In one embodiment, \( n = 600 \) hypotheses are generated via uniform random sampling within these bounds, though values between 400 and 1000 yield comparable performance provided \( n \) exceeds the dimensionality of the feature vector.

Each 3D motion hypothesis \( \Delta M_i^\star \) is then projected onto the two orthogonal X-ray imaging planes. Assuming standard clinical geometry, the first view (e.g., anterior-posterior or coronal) is acquired with an X-ray source at position \( c_1 \) and detector plane \( \Pi_1 \), while the second view (e.g., lateral or sagittal) uses source \( c_2 \) and detector \( \Pi_2 \), with the two beams intersecting at the isocenter. The projection of a 3D point \( p \) onto detector \( \Pi_j \) is given by standard perspective projection: \( p_j = P_j p \), where \( P_j \) is the 3×4 projection matrix encoding source position and detector orientation. Applying this to the transformed tumor volume \( M_0 \cdot \Delta M_i^\star \), we obtain two 2D regions of interest (ROIs)—one in each view—centered at the projected tumor centroid.

Within each ROI, an image feature vector is computed. The preferred feature descriptor is the Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG), which partitions the ROI into 5×5 pixel blocks, computes gradient orientations within each block, quantizes them into 8 bins, and concatenates all histograms into a single column vector \( h \in \mathbb{R}^m \). For a typical ROI size of 32×32 pixels, this yields \( m = (32/5)^2 \times 8 \approx 320 \) features, though the exact dimension depends on the adaptive window selection described below. The HOG descriptor is chosen for its sensitivity to local structural changes—such as edge displacements caused by tumor motion—while being invariant to monotonic intensity transformations common in X-ray imaging.

Critically, before feature extraction, the system determines an optimal tracking window size for each view and patient. This is achieved by evaluating a range of candidate window dimensions \( w_x \times w_y \) relative to the initial tumor bounding box \( w_x^\star \times w_y^\star \). For each candidate, a local search region of size \( 2w_x \times 2w_y \) is defined, and all pairs of non-overlapping patches within this region are compared using the ℓ² distance between their HOG features. The mean of the smallest 20% of these distances is used as a discriminability score: lower values indicate that patches are more distinct from one another, implying better tracking performance. The window size that minimizes this score is selected, with empirical results showing optimal ratios \( r = (w_x w_y)/(w_x^\star w_y^\star) \) of 0.9–1.2 for coronal views and 1.1–1.3 for sagittal views across diverse patient anatomies.

With optimal windows established, the system constructs the training data matrices \( \mathbf{X}^\star \) and \( \mathbf{Y}^\star \). Each row of \( \mathbf{X}^\star \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 2m} \) contains the concatenated HOG features \( [h_{0,i}^1; h_{0,i}^2] \) from the two views for hypothesis \( i \). Each row of \( \mathbf{Y}^\star \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} \) contains the vectorized logarithm of the motion hypothesis: \( (\log \Delta M_i^\star)^\top \), where \( \log \) denotes the matrix logarithm that maps the Lie group of affine transformations to its tangent space (a vector space of dimension \( d = 12 \) for full 3D affine motion, though fewer parameters may be used if motion is constrained to translation only). This logarithmic mapping is essential because affine matrices do not form a vector space; direct regression in Euclidean space would produce invalid transformations (e.g., non-invertible matrices) and distort motion distances.

The regression model \( \Omega^\star \in \mathbb{R}^{2m \times d} \) is then learned by solving the regularized least-squares problem:
\[
\Omega^\star = \arg\min_{\Omega} \| \mathbf{X}^\star \Omega - \mathbf{Y}^\star \|_F^2 + \lambda \| \Omega \|_F^2,
\]
where \( \| \cdot \|_F \) is the Frobenius norm and \( \lambda > 0 \) is a regularization parameter (typically \( \lambda = 10^{-3} \)) that prevents overfitting by penalizing large coefficients. The closed-form solution is given by ridge regression:
\[
\Omega^\star = (\mathbf{X}^{\star\top} \mathbf{X}^\star + \lambda \mathbf{I})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^{\star\top} \mathbf{Y}^\star.
\]
This computation is highly efficient due to the moderate size of \( \mathbf{X}^\star \) and is completed in under 50 ms on standard clinical hardware.

Once trained, the model is applied frame-by-frame to track the tumor. At time \( t > 0 \), given the previous tumor pose \( M_{t-1} \), the system projects this pose onto the two X-ray views to define current ROIs. Within these ROIs, HOG features \( h_t^1 \) and \( h_t^2 \) are extracted using the previously determined optimal window sizes, concatenated into \( h_t = [h_t^1; h_t^2] \), and multiplied by the regression matrix to yield a motion increment in the tangent space: \( \xi_t = h_t^\top \Omega^\star \). This increment is exponentiated to return to the manifold: \( \Delta M_t = \exp(\xi_t) \), where \( \exp \) is the matrix exponential. The updated tumor pose is then \( M_t = M_{t-1} \cdot \Delta M_t \).

In the alternative 2DR mode, separate regressors \( \Omega^1 \) and \( \Omega^2 \) are learned for each view using 2D affine hypotheses (6 parameters each). Tracking proceeds independently in each view, producing 2D tumor centroids \( p_t^1 \) and \( p_t^2 \). The 3D position \( p_t \) is estimated as the midpoint of the shortest line segment connecting the back-projected rays from \( p_t^1 \) and \( p_t^2 \) through their respective X-ray sources \( c_1 \) and \( c_2 \), a standard triangulation technique robust to non-intersecting rays.

The system is designed to operate without retraining during a session, as experiments show no drift over hundreds of frames. However, optional model updates can be triggered if confidence metrics (e.g., feature distance thresholds) indicate potential tracking failure. All computations are implemented in optimized C++ with GPU acceleration for feature extraction, ensuring compatibility with real-time clinical workflows.

### Effect of the Invention

The present invention produces transformative effects in the field of radiation oncology by enabling unprecedented levels of tumor tracking accuracy and speed without invasive procedures or complex setup. Clinical implementation of this technology directly translates to reduced planning target volume (PTV) margins—from the conventional 10–20 mm down to 1–3 mm—thereby sparing critical organs at risk such as the spinal cord, heart, lungs, and kidneys from unnecessary irradiation. This margin reduction not only decreases the incidence and severity of radiation-induced toxicities but also permits dose escalation to the tumor, potentially improving local control and survival outcomes for patients with lung, liver, pancreatic, and other mobile malignancies.

The noninvasive nature of the invention eliminates the morbidity associated with fiducial implantation, expanding access to advanced IGRT for frail or high-risk patients who would otherwise be excluded. By operating without external markers or patient-specific calibration, the method streamlines clinical workflow, reducing pretreatment setup time by 15–30 minutes per fraction and minimizing opportunities for human error. The real-time performance (<30 ms per frame) supports continuous, gated, or breath-hold radiation delivery strategies with minimal latency, ensuring that the treatment beam is always aligned with the moving target.

Robustness to low-contrast tumors—those invisible to the naked eye on X-ray—addresses a major unmet need, as up to 30% of abdominal lesions lack sufficient radiographic contrast for traditional tracking. The method’s immunity to breathing irregularities further enhances reliability in uncooperative or pediatric patients. Validation on degraded, low-resolution datasets demonstrates consistent sub-pixel accuracy even under worst-case imaging conditions, providing a safety margin against hardware limitations or suboptimal acquisition protocols.

From a technical standpoint, the invention’s use of Riemannian geometry for motion modeling sets a new standard for physical fidelity in medical tracking algorithms, avoiding the geometric inconsistencies of Euclidean approximations. The adaptive window selection ensures optimal feature discriminability across diverse anatomies, while the closed-form regression guarantees deterministic, reproducible results—critical for regulatory approval and clinical trust.

Economically, the invention reduces healthcare costs by decreasing complication rates from fiducial placement, shortening treatment times, and enabling more efficient use of linear accelerator resources. It also future-proofs existing IGRT infrastructure, as the software-only solution can be deployed on current-generation radiotherapy machines without hardware modifications.

In summary, the invention delivers a paradigm shift in tumor tracking: from invasive, slow, and error-prone methods to a seamless, instantaneous, and precise guidance system that enhances both the safety and efficacy of cancer radiotherapy. Its clinical adoption promises to improve patient outcomes, expand treatment eligibility, and advance the standard of care for millions of cancer patients worldwide.
