# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents, and more particularly to novel manganese-based contrast agents comprising manganese ions intercalated within graphitic or graphene-based nanostructures. The invention provides compositions wherein paramagnetic Mn²⁺ ions are confined between layers of oxidized or reduced graphene sheets, including but not limited to oxidized micro-graphite, oxidized graphene nanoplatelets, reduced graphene nanoplatelets, and graphene nanoribbons. These materials exhibit significantly enhanced proton relaxivity—up to two orders of magnitude greater than conventional small-molecule Mn²⁺ or Gd³⁺ chelate complexes—across a broad range of magnetic field strengths (0.01–80 MHz proton Larmor frequency). The invention further encompasses methods for synthesizing such manganese-intercalated carbon nanostructures using oxidative chemical procedures involving potassium permanganate (KMnO₄), as well as their use as high-efficacy T₁-weighted MRI contrast agents with potential advantages over current clinical agents, particularly in light of safety concerns associated with gadolinium-based formulations. The disclosed compositions represent a new class of metal-ion-carbon nanostructure complexes wherein geometric confinement of paramagnetic ions within layered graphitic architectures serves as a general strategy to amplify relaxivity through modulation of inner-sphere water coordination, rotational correlation time, and electron spin dynamics.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a cornerstone of modern diagnostic radiology, providing high-resolution anatomical and functional information without ionizing radiation. However, inherent tissue contrast in MRI is often insufficient for definitive diagnosis, necessitating the use of exogenous contrast agents (CAs) to enhance image quality and diagnostic confidence. The majority of clinically approved MRI CAs operate by shortening the longitudinal (T₁) or transverse (T₂) relaxation times of water protons in target tissues, thereby increasing signal intensity (for T₁ agents) or causing signal loss (for T₂ agents). Among these, T₁ agents based on paramagnetic metal ions—primarily gadolinium(III) (Gd³⁺) and, to a lesser extent, manganese(II) (Mn²⁺)—are most widely used due to their ability to generate bright positive contrast. These agents function by accelerating the relaxation of nearby water protons through dipolar interactions between the unpaired electrons of the metal ion and the nuclear spins of water molecules.

The efficacy of a T₁ contrast agent is quantified by its relaxivity (r₁), defined as the increase in longitudinal relaxation rate (R₁ = 1/T₁) per millimolar concentration of the paramagnetic ion. Conventional Gd³⁺-based agents, such as Gd-DTPA (gadolinium-diethylenetriaminepentaacetate), exhibit r₁ values of approximately 3–5 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at clinical field strengths (1.5–3 T). Despite decades of optimization, theoretical models suggest that the relaxivity of these small-molecule chelates is fundamentally limited by rapid molecular tumbling (short rotational correlation time, τ_R), restricted inner-sphere water exchange kinetics, and suboptimal coordination geometry. Consequently, extensive research has focused on developing next-generation agents with enhanced relaxivity through macromolecular conjugation, nanoparticle encapsulation, or structural rigidification to prolong τ_R and increase the number of coordinated water molecules (q).

Over the past decade, carbon nanostructures have emerged as promising platforms for high-relaxivity contrast agents. Notably, Gd³⁺ ions encapsulated within single-walled carbon nanotubes (so-called “gadonanotubes”) have demonstrated r₁ values exceeding 100 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at low fields—a 20- to 100-fold improvement over Gd-DTPA. This dramatic enhancement has been attributed to the unique confinement of Gd³⁺ ions within the nanotube interior, which restricts molecular motion, alters hydration dynamics, and potentially modifies electronic relaxation properties. Similarly, Gd³⁺-functionalized fullerenes (gadofullerenes) and surface-conjugated carbon nanotubes have shown moderate relaxivity gains. However, these systems face challenges related to complex synthesis, batch-to-batch variability, incomplete characterization, and limited understanding of the underlying relaxation mechanisms due to their heterogeneous particulate nature.

In parallel, manganese has re-emerged as a viable alternative to gadolinium, driven by growing concerns over nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) associated with Gd³⁺ agents in renally impaired patients. Mn²⁺ is an essential biological trace element involved in enzymatic regulation and neuronal function, with a more favorable toxicity profile than Gd³⁺ when properly chelated. Early clinical Mn²⁺ agents, such as Mn-DPDP (mangafodipir), exhibited modest relaxivity (~6–7 mM⁻¹s⁻¹) and were eventually withdrawn due to formulation instability and side effects. Recent efforts have sought to improve Mn²⁺ agent performance through macromolecular scaffolds, but relaxivity enhancements have remained modest compared to the leaps observed with confined Gd³⁺ systems.

Critically, the potential of Mn²⁺-carbon nanostructure complexes as high-relaxivity MRI agents has remained entirely unexplored until now. While Gd³⁺ confinement in carbon nanotubes yields exceptional relaxivity, it is unknown whether this phenomenon extends to other paramagnetic ions or other graphitic architectures. Graphene—a two-dimensional allotrope of carbon—has attracted immense interest for biomedical applications due to its large surface area, tunable chemistry, and unique electronic properties. Oxidative exfoliation methods using KMnO₄ are routinely employed to produce graphene oxide, graphene nanoplatelets, and graphene nanoribbons from graphite or multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs). These processes inherently involve manganese-containing reagents, raising the possibility of unintentional yet structurally significant incorporation of Mn²⁺ ions into the resulting carbon frameworks.

Prior art fails to recognize or exploit this serendipitous incorporation. Conventional wisdom assumes that residual metal impurities from synthesis are undesirable contaminants that must be rigorously removed to avoid confounding biological or physical measurements. Indeed, iron catalyst residues from MWCNT synthesis are frequently cited as sources of spurious magnetism in carbon nanomaterials. However, the present inventors have discovered that trace Mn²⁺ ions introduced during standard oxidative processing become stably intercalated between graphene layers, and that this confinement—not incidental contamination—is the primary driver of extraordinary relaxivity. Moreover, the resulting materials exhibit unconventional nuclear magnetic relaxation dispersion (NMRD) profiles distinct from all known Mn²⁺ or Gd³⁺ complexes, suggesting novel relaxation mechanisms enabled by the graphitic nanoenvironment.

Existing theoretical models, such as the Solomon-Bloembergen-Morgan (SBM) equations, describe relaxivity in terms of inner-sphere (directly coordinated water), second-sphere (hydrogen-bonded water), and outer-sphere (diffusion-mediated) contributions. These models assume well-defined molecular complexes with discrete hydration numbers and correlation times. They struggle to accurately fit the NMRD data of the present Mn²⁺-graphene systems, particularly at low magnetic fields, indicating that confinement within layered carbon structures introduces new physical parameters not captured by classical theory. For instance, the effective hydration number (q) derived from fitting exceeds typical values for Mn²⁺ complexes, implying that the interlayer spacing permits coordination of up to eight water molecules—an arrangement impossible in conventional octahedral chelates.

Furthermore, prior art does not teach or suggest that the geometric architecture of the carbon host—whether tubular (nanotubes), planar (nanoplatelets), or ribbon-like (nanoribbons)—is secondary to the fundamental principle of ion confinement between graphene sheets. The present invention establishes that relaxivity enhancement is a general consequence of intercalation, irrespective of macroscopic morphology, thereby unifying disparate observations from gadonanotubes and extending them to manganese and other graphitic forms. This insight enables rational design of high-performance contrast agents using scalable, solution-based syntheses rather than intricate encapsulation techniques.

In summary, the background art lacks: (1) any recognition that Mn²⁺ ions can be intentionally or unintentionally intercalated into graphene-based materials during standard oxidative synthesis; (2) appreciation that such intercalation leads to dramatically enhanced MRI relaxivity; (3) understanding that the relaxivity enhancement stems from nanoconfinement effects rather than surface functionalization or particle size; (4) demonstration that diverse graphitic architectures (platelets, ribbons, micro-flakes) all support this phenomenon; and (5) exploitation of this discovery for the development of safer, high-efficacy Mn²⁺-based contrast agents as alternatives to Gd³⁺. The present invention addresses all these gaps, providing both novel compositions and a foundational principle for next-generation MRI contrast agent design.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides manganese-based magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agents comprising Mn²⁺ ions intercalated within layered graphitic or graphene-derived nanostructures, and methods for their preparation and use. In one aspect, the invention discloses compositions selected from the group consisting of Mn²⁺-intercalated oxidized micro-graphite, Mn²⁺-intercalated oxidized graphene nanoplatelets, Mn²⁺-intercalated reduced graphene nanoplatelets, and Mn²⁺-intercalated graphene nanoribbons, wherein the Mn²⁺ ions are confined between adjacent graphene sheets and are not present as free ions or surface-adsorbed species. These compositions exhibit longitudinal proton relaxivity (r₁) values ranging from approximately 40 to over 700 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ across magnetic fields corresponding to proton Larmor frequencies of 0.01–80 MHz, representing an 8- to 100-fold increase over conventional Mn²⁺ or Gd³⁺ chelate complexes.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for producing Mn²⁺-intercalated graphitic nanostructures, comprising: (a) subjecting a graphitic starting material selected from graphite or multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) to oxidative treatment with potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) under acidic conditions to form an oxidized intermediate; (b) optionally reducing the oxidized intermediate with a reducing agent such as hydrazine hydrate to yield a reduced product; and (c) isolating the resulting Mn²⁺-intercalated nanostructure, wherein Mn²⁺ ions derived from the reduction of KMnO₄ become trapped between graphene layers during the oxidation/exfoliation process. Critically, the method does not require post-synthesis doping or encapsulation steps; instead, Mn²⁺ incorporation occurs inherently during standard oxidative processing.

In a further aspect, the invention includes aqueous dispersions of the Mn²⁺-intercalated nanostructures stabilized by biocompatible surfactants such as Pluronic F127, suitable for intravenous administration. These dispersions are substantially free of unchelated Mn²⁺ ions, as confirmed by sodium bismuthate testing with a detection limit of 1 ppm, ensuring that observed relaxivity arises solely from intercalated species.

The invention also encompasses the use of the disclosed compositions as T₁-weighted MRI contrast agents for diagnostic imaging in mammals, including humans. Due to their high relaxivity, lower doses may be administered compared to conventional agents, potentially reducing metal load and associated toxicity risks. Moreover, because manganese is an essential biological element with established metabolic pathways, these agents offer a safer alternative to gadolinium-based products, particularly for patients with renal impairment.

Key features distinguishing the invention from prior art include: (1) the identification of nanoconfinement between graphene sheets—not particle size, shape, or surface chemistry—as the primary determinant of relaxivity enhancement; (2) the demonstration that this effect is generalizable across multiple graphitic architectures (micro-flakes, platelets, ribbons); (3) the observation of unconventional NMRD profiles characterized by steep relaxivity increases at low magnetic fields (<10 MHz), resembling those of gadonanotubes but unprecedented for manganese systems; (4) magnetic and EPR characterization confirming that Mn²⁺ is the dominant source of paramagnetism, with negligible contributions from residual iron or defect-induced magnetism; and (5) SBM modeling indicating anomalously high inner-sphere water coordination numbers (q ≈ 8) and nanosecond-scale water residence times, consistent with an expanded hydration sphere enabled by interlayer spacing.

The invention thus establishes a new paradigm for MRI contrast agent design: deliberate exploitation of two-dimensional nanoconfinement to modulate the physicochemical environment of paramagnetic ions, thereby unlocking relaxivity potentials far beyond molecular chelates. This approach is synthetically accessible, scalable, and applicable to other paramagnetic ions beyond manganese, offering a versatile platform for next-generation diagnostic agents.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to novel MRI contrast agents based on manganese ions intercalated within graphitic or graphene-derived nanostructures, and methods for their synthesis and application. The core innovation lies in the recognition that during standard oxidative exfoliation processes used to produce graphene materials—specifically those employing potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) as an oxidant—manganese ions are reduced from Mn⁷⁺ to Mn²⁺ and become stably confined between adjacent graphene sheets. This intercalation, rather than surface adsorption or residual contamination, is responsible for extraordinary enhancements in proton relaxivity, rendering these materials highly effective T₁-weighted contrast agents.

The graphitic starting materials suitable for practicing the invention include natural or synthetic graphite in micron-sized flakes (micro-graphite), as well as multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs). When micro-graphite is subjected to modified Hummers’ oxidation—a process involving concentrated sulfuric acid, sodium nitrate, and KMnO₄—the resulting product is oxidized micro-graphite, which upon sonication exfoliates into oxidized graphene nanoplatelets. Alternatively, when MWCNTs undergo longitudinal unzipping via KMnO₄ oxidation under controlled conditions, they transform into graphene nanoribbons. In both cases, the KMnO₄ serves dual roles: as an oxidizing agent that introduces oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., epoxides, hydroxyls, carboxyls) onto the carbon lattice, and as a source of manganese that is reduced in situ to Mn²⁺ during the reaction. The reduction occurs through electron transfer from the carbon framework to the permanganate ion, yielding Mn²⁺ cations that migrate into the interlayer galleries created by oxidative expansion.

The intercalation mechanism is facilitated by the increased interlayer spacing in oxidized graphitic materials, which expands from ~3.4 Å in pristine graphite to 6–10 Å or more upon oxidation. This enlarged gallery height accommodates hydrated Mn²⁺ ions, which adopt an octahedral [Mn(H₂O)₆]²⁺ configuration as confirmed by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy showing hyperfine coupling constants (~95 G) characteristic of aqua ions. Once intercalated, the Mn²⁺ ions are effectively shielded from the external environment, preventing leaching even under physiological conditions. This stability is evidenced by the absence of free Mn²⁺ in aqueous dispersions after centrifugation and surfactant stabilization, as verified by colorimetric testing with sodium bismuthate.

Optionally, the oxidized products may be chemically reduced using agents such as hydrazine hydrate, sodium borohydride, or thermal annealing to partially restore the sp² carbon network, yielding reduced graphene nanoplatelets. Reduction decreases oxygen content and interlayer spacing but does not expel the intercalated Mn²⁺ ions, which remain trapped due to strong electrostatic and van der Waals interactions with the graphene sheets. Interestingly, reduction induces changes in magnetic behavior: while oxidized forms exhibit pure paramagnetism, reduced graphene nanoplatelets display superparamagnetism at room temperature, as indicated by SQUID magnetometry showing zero-field-cooled/field-cooled (ZFC/FC) bifurcation and a blocking temperature of ~40 K. This transition likely arises from clustering of Mn²⁺ ions into nanoscale domains during reduction, enhancing magnetic dipolar interactions.

The resulting Mn²⁺-intercalated nanostructures—whether oxidized or reduced, platelet- or ribbon-shaped—share common structural motifs: (1) layered graphene sheets separated by intercalated Mn²⁺ ions and associated water molecules; (2) oxygen functional groups at sheet edges and defects that aid colloidal stability; and (3) absence of significant metal impurities other than manganese. Elemental analysis by ICP-OES confirms manganese concentrations typically in the range of 0.1–1.0 wt%, with iron content below 50 µg/g in graphene nanoribbons (derived from Fe-catalyzed MWCNTs), insufficient to contribute meaningfully to magnetism or relaxivity.

For biomedical application, the solid nanostructures are dispersed in aqueous media containing biocompatible surfactants such as Pluronic F127 (poloxamer 407), which adsorbs onto hydrophobic graphene domains via its polypropylene oxide block while presenting hydrophilic polyethylene oxide chains to the solvent. This steric stabilization prevents aggregation and ensures formation of stable, injectable colloids. Centrifugation at 5000 rpm removes large, insoluble aggregates, leaving a supernatant enriched in small, water-dispersible nanoparticles (typically <200 nm by dynamic light scattering). Crucially, these dispersions contain no detectable free Mn²⁺, confirming that relaxivity originates exclusively from intercalated species.

Proton relaxivity measurements reveal that all Mn²⁺-graphene compositions exhibit r₁ values vastly exceeding those of clinical agents. At 0.47 T (20 MHz), r₁ ranges from ~40–60 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ for oxidized/reduced nanoplatelets to ~70 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ for oxidized micro-graphite and graphene nanoribbons—approximately 10–20 times higher than Mn-DTPA (~4 mM⁻¹s⁻¹) or Gd-DTPA (~4.5 mM⁻¹s⁻¹). Transverse relaxivity (r₂) is also enhanced (19–60 fold), but the r₂/r₁ ratio remains below 5, classifying these materials as predominantly T₁ agents suitable for positive-contrast imaging. At ultra-low fields (0.01 MHz), graphene nanoribbons achieve r₁ >700 mM⁻¹s⁻¹, demonstrating field-dependent behavior ideal for emerging low-field MRI technologies.

Nuclear magnetic relaxation dispersion (NMRD) profiling across 0.01–80 MHz reveals four distinct relaxivity patterns: (1) oxidized micro-graphite shows moderate field dependence with a low-field surge; (2) oxidized nanoplatelets exhibit a bell-shaped curve peaking at 30 MHz; (3) reduced nanoplatelets display gradual increase down to 10 MHz followed by sharp rise; and (4) graphene nanoribbons show linear decline above 10 MHz and exponential growth below. These profiles differ fundamentally from molecular Mn²⁺ complexes (flat or weakly bell-shaped) and resemble gadonanotubes, suggesting shared confinement-driven mechanisms. Fitting to SBM theory yields physically plausible parameters: zero-field splitting energies (D²) of 10¹⁸–10²⁰ s⁻², electron spin correlation times (τ_S) of 1–100 ps, rotational correlation times (τ_R) of 0.1–10 ns, and inner-sphere water numbers (q) of ~8—unprecedented for Mn²⁺ but consistent with interlayer hydration spheres accommodating up to eight water molecules. Water residence times (τ_M) derived from NMRD and corroborated by ¹⁷O NMR fall in the 10–500 ns range, orders of magnitude longer than small chelates (10–100 ps), indicating slowed exchange kinetics beneficial for relaxivity.

Magnetic characterization by SQUID confirms that Mn²⁺ dominates the magnetic response. Oxidized materials show linear M-H curves indicative of paramagnetism, while reduced forms exhibit superparamagnetic hysteresis with room-temperature saturation magnetization up to 0.1 emu/g. ZFC/FC measurements reveal blocking temperatures ≤40 K, consistent with Mn²⁺ clusters <10 nm in size. EPR spectroscopy further validates the Mn²⁺ oxidation state, displaying six-line hyperfine splitting (I = 5/2 for ⁵⁵Mn) with g-values ~2.00 and linewidths of 29–31 G in solution—characteristic of high-spin d⁵ ions in octahedral symmetry. Solid-state EPR shows broader lines due to dipolar coupling, with graphene nanoribbons exhibiting additional narrow signals from carbon-centered radicals, though these do not contribute to relaxivity.

The invention’s therapeutic advantage stems from manganese’s biological compatibility. Unlike Gd³⁺, which is non-physiological and linked to NSF, Mn²⁺ is an essential cofactor for enzymes like arginase and glutamine synthetase, with homeostatic regulation via biliary excretion. Normal serum levels are ~1 nM, and toxicity only manifests after chronic overexposure (>10 mg/day for months), primarily as neurotoxicity resembling Parkinson’s disease. The low doses enabled by high relaxivity (e.g., 0.01 mmol Mn/kg vs. 0.1 mmol Gd/kg) minimize systemic exposure, while intercalation prevents rapid dissociation and accumulation in sensitive tissues like the brain.

In summary, the detailed embodiment of the invention comprises: (1) synthesis of Mn²⁺-intercalated graphitic nanostructures via KMnO₄ oxidation of graphite or MWCNTs; (2) optional reduction to tune electronic/magnetic properties; (3) formulation into stable aqueous dispersions using Pluronic surfactants; (4) administration as T₁ MRI contrast agents at reduced metal doses; and (5) exploitation of nanoconfinement to achieve relaxivity unattainable by molecular design alone. This approach is scalable, cost-effective, and adaptable to other paramagnetic ions (e.g., Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺), establishing a new materials platform for diagnostic imaging.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Synthesis of Mn²⁺-Intercalated Oxidized Graphene Nanoplatelets**

Analytical grade micro-graphite (200 mg, Sigma Aldrich) was oxidized via a modified Hummers’ method: graphite was added to a mixture of concentrated H₂SO₄ (46 mL), NaNO₃ (2 g), and stirred at 0°C before slow addition of KMnO₄ (6 g). The reaction was warmed to 35°C for 2 h, then diluted with water (92 mL) and H₂O₂ (30%, 14 mL) to terminate oxidation. The resulting oxidized micro-graphite was washed repeatedly with HCl (3%) and water until neutral pH. For exfoliation, 200 mg of dried oxidized micro-graphite was suspended in 200 mL deionized water and bath-sonicated (30 W, 1 h) to yield a stable brown dispersion. Fifty milliliters were centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 30 min), and the pellet dried to obtain oxidized graphene nanoplatelets. ICP-OES analysis confirmed Mn content of 0.42 wt% (4.2 mg/g), with no detectable Fe, Ni, or Co. XRD showed an expanded interlayer spacing of 8.2 Å, consistent with Mn²⁺ intercalation. TEM revealed few-layer platelets (2–5 sheets) with lateral dimensions of 100–500 nm.

**Example 2: Synthesis of Mn²⁺-Intercalated Reduced Graphene Nanoplatelets**

The remaining 150 mL of the sonicated dispersion from Example 1 was treated with hydrazine hydrate (1.5 mL, 37.1 mmol) and heated at 100°C for 12 h under reflux. The black precipitate was collected by filtration, washed with water (500 mL) and methanol (500 mL), and air-dried to yield reduced graphene nanoplatelets. ICP-OES showed Mn retention at 0.38 wt%, confirming intercalation stability during reduction. Raman spectroscopy indicated a D/G band intensity ratio of 1.2, reflecting defect density from residual oxygen groups. SQUID magnetometry at 300 K revealed superparamagnetic behavior with saturation magnetization of 0.08 emu/g and no coercivity, while ZFC/FC curves showed a blocking temperature of 40 K.

**Example 3: Synthesis of Mn²⁺-Intercalated Graphene Nanoribbons**

MWCNTs (150 mg, OD 10–20 nm, length 10–30 µm, Sigma Aldrich) were suspended in concentrated H₂SO₄ (30 mL) and stirred for 2 h. KMnO₄ (750 mg) was added portion-wise, and the mixture stirred at room temperature for 1 h before heating to 60°C for 1 h. The reaction was quenched with ice-water, neutralized with NaOH, and the product washed with water, ethanol, and diethyl ether. Centrifugation (8000 rpm, 20 min) yielded graphene nanoribbons as a dark solid. ICP-OES detected Mn at 0.65 wt% and Fe at 50 µg/g (0.005 wt%), consistent with residual catalyst from MWCNT synthesis. AFM showed ribbon widths of 20–50 nm and lengths up to 5 µm, with height profiles indicating 3–8 graphene layers. EPR spectroscopy displayed the characteristic six-line Mn²⁺ signal plus a narrow radical peak at g = 2.007.

**Example 4: Formulation of Aqueous Contrast Agent Dispersions**

One milligram each of oxidized micro-graphite, oxidized graphene nanoplatelets, reduced graphene nanoplatelets, and graphene nanoribbons were separately dispersed in 2 mL of 1% Pluronic F127 aqueous solution. Samples were bath-sonicated (30 W, 10 min) and centrifuged (5000 rpm, 1 h). The supernatants were tested for free Mn²⁺ by acidifying with HCl, adding NaBiO₃ in HNO₃, and observing for purple coloration (indicative of MnO₄⁻ formation). No color change occurred, confirming absence of free Mn²⁺ above 1 ppm detection limit. DLS measurements showed hydrodynamic diameters of 80–150 nm with PDI <0.2, indicating monodisperse colloids suitable for injection.

**Example 5: Relaxivity and NMRD Characterization**

Relaxivity was measured at 0.47 T (20 MHz) and 40°C using a Minispec NMR spectrometer. Serial dilutions of the dispersions from Example 4 were prepared, and T₁/T₂ determined via inversion recovery and CPMG sequences. Relaxivity (r₁) was calculated from the slope of R₁ vs. [Mn²⁺] plots, yielding values of 72, 58, 61, and 70 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ for oxidized micro-graphite, oxidized nanoplatelets, reduced nanoplatelets, and nanoribbons, respectively. NMRD profiles (0.01–80 MHz) were acquired using a fast-field-cycling relaxometer (Stelar FFC2000) at 27°C. Graphene nanoribbons showed the steepest low-field rise, reaching r₁ = 724 mM⁻¹s⁻¹ at 0.01 MHz. SBM fitting gave q = 8, τ_R = 1.2 ns, τ_M = 85 ns, and r_MnH = 2.85 Å for nanoribbons—parameters consistent with interlayer hydration.

**Example 6: In Vitro MRI Phantom Studies**

Phantoms were prepared by mixing dispersions from Example 4 with agarose (1%) in glass vials. T₁-weighted MRI was performed at 3 T (Siemens Trio) using a spin-echo sequence (TR/TE = 500/10 ms). All Mn²⁺-graphene samples produced significant signal enhancement compared to control (Pluronic only), with graphene nanoribbons showing the brightest contrast at equivalent Mn concentrations. Quantitative T₁ mapping confirmed relaxivity trends from Example 5, validating their efficacy as T₁ agents.

**Example 7: Magnetic and EPR Characterization**

SQUID magnetometry (−50 kOe to 50 kOe, 10–300 K) confirmed paramagnetism in oxidized samples and superparamagnetism in reduced forms. EPR of solid samples showed broad Mn²⁺ lines (ΔH₁/₂ = 75–80 G) and, for nanoribbons, a narrow radical signal (ΔH₁/₂ = 1.2 G). Aqueous samples displayed resolved six-line spectra with ΔH₁/₂ = 30 G, confirming octahedral [Mn(H₂O)₆]²⁺ coordination. No Mn³⁺ signals were detected even at 77 K, indicating >95% Mn²⁺ purity.

These examples demonstrate the reproducible synthesis, characterization, and superior MRI performance of Mn²⁺-intercalated graphitic nanostructures, fulfilling the invention’s objectives of high relaxivity, biocompatibility, and scalable production.
