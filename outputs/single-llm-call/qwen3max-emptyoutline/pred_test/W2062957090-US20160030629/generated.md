# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of in vitro cell culture has long relied on two-dimensional (2D) substrates, such as tissue culture polystyrene and the surfaces of tissue analogs, to support the growth and study of cells outside their native biological environments. While these platforms have enabled significant advances in cellular biology, pharmacology, and toxicology, they suffer from a fundamental limitation: they fail to recapitulate the complex three-dimensional (3D) architecture of the extracellular matrix (ECM) that surrounds cells in vivo. In natural tissues, cells are embedded within a dynamic and heterogeneous 3D network of proteins, glycosaminoglycans, and other biomolecules that provide structural support, biochemical cues, and mechanical signals essential for proper cellular function. In contrast, 2D culture systems impose unnatural constraints on cell morphology, polarity, and intercellular interactions, often leading to aberrant phenotypes that do not reflect true physiological behavior.

In conventional 2D monolayer cultures, cells are exposed to homogeneous concentrations of nutrients, growth factors, and oxygen, which lack the spatial gradients and microenvironmental heterogeneity characteristic of living tissues. This artificial uniformity results in flattened, stretched cellular morphologies that disrupt normal cytoskeletal organization and signaling pathways. Consequently, cells cultured in 2D frequently exhibit altered proliferation rates, gene expression profiles, differentiation capacities, migratory behaviors, and responses to therapeutic agents compared to their counterparts in native 3D environments. These discrepancies have been well documented across multiple cell types, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and cancer cells, and have raised concerns about the translational relevance of data generated from 2D models.

To address these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned to 3D cell culture systems that better mimic the structural and functional complexity of in vivo tissues. Among the most promising approaches are hydrogel-based scaffolds—cross-linked polymeric networks capable of retaining large amounts of water while providing a porous, biocompatible matrix for cell encapsulation and growth. Hydrogels can be engineered from natural, synthetic, or hybrid materials to replicate key aspects of the ECM, including nanofibrous architecture, tunable mechanical properties, and bioactive signaling motifs. Natural hydrogels derived from ECM components—such as collagen, fibrin, hyaluronic acid, chitosan, and alginate—offer inherent biocompatibility and cell-adhesive properties that support cell survival, proliferation, and function. However, their use is often hampered by batch-to-batch variability, limited control over mechanical and biochemical parameters, and potential immunogenicity due to residual impurities.

Synthetic hydrogels, on the other hand, provide superior reproducibility and precise tunability of physical and chemical properties through rational design of polymer chemistry and cross-linking strategies. Materials such as poly(lactic acid) (PLA), poly(glycolic acid) (PGA), and their copolymers (PLGA) have been widely used in tissue engineering and drug delivery. Nevertheless, many synthetic hydrogels suffer from inadequate nanostructural fidelity; their fiber diameters and pore sizes are often too large to accurately emulate the nanoscale topography of native ECM, which typically features fibrillar networks with diameters in the range of 10–200 nanometers. Moreover, the hydrophobic nature of many synthetic polymers can hinder cell adhesion and viability unless modified with bioactive ligands, adding complexity to scaffold fabrication.

In recent years, peptide-based hydrogels have emerged as a compelling alternative that bridges the gap between natural and synthetic systems. Composed of short amino acid sequences designed to self-assemble under physiological conditions, these materials combine the molecular precision and reproducibility of synthetic polymers with the biocompatibility and structural similarity to natural ECM afforded by their peptide backbone. Certain self-assembling peptides form β-sheet-rich nanofibers that entangle into hydrogel networks with mechanical properties and mesh sizes closely resembling those of soft tissues. Importantly, because they are built from naturally occurring amino acids, peptide hydrogels are generally non-immunogenic, biodegradable, and amenable to functionalization with cell-instructive motifs.

Despite these advantages, existing peptide hydrogels face significant practical challenges in routine cell culture applications, particularly concerning the processes of cell encapsulation and subsequent cell recovery. Most commercially available peptide hydrogels—including Puramatrix, Hydromatix, and Matrigel—require non-physiological triggers for gelation, such as pH adjustment (e.g., lowering to acidic conditions) or temperature shifts (e.g., cooling to 4°C). These conditions are incompatible with direct cell mixing, as exposure to low pH or cold temperatures can induce cellular stress, reduce viability, or alter phenotype. As a result, standard protocols often involve pre-gelling the scaffold separately, followed by seeding cells onto the surface—a method that fails to achieve true 3D encapsulation. Alternatively, cells may be suspended in a pre-gel solution under suboptimal conditions and then rapidly transferred to culture medium, but this introduces logistical complexity, increases handling time, and risks nutrient deprivation during the critical gelation phase.

Equally problematic is the difficulty of isolating viable cells from established hydrogel matrices for downstream analyses such as flow cytometry, RNA sequencing, or proteomic profiling. Conventional hydrogel dissolution methods typically rely on enzymatic degradation (e.g., using collagenase for collagen gels) or chemical disruption (e.g., chelating agents for alginate), which can damage cell surface receptors, alter protein expression, or introduce confounding variables. Some peptide hydrogels require reversal of the original gelation trigger—such as re-acidification or warming—to dissolve the matrix, but these manipulations again expose cells to non-physiological stresses that compromise integrity and function. The absence of a simple, gentle, and rapid cell recovery method has thus limited the utility of 3D hydrogel systems in high-throughput screening, longitudinal studies, and clinical applications where post-culture analysis is essential.

There remains a critical unmet need for a 3D cell culture platform that enables seamless integration of cell encapsulation and recovery under fully physiological conditions. Such a system should allow direct mixing of cells with the hydrogel precursor in standard culture medium at neutral pH and ambient temperature, ensuring immediate access to nutrients and growth factors throughout gelation. It should also permit facile, non-destructive isolation of embedded cells without enzymatic treatment, extreme pH shifts, or prolonged exposure to harsh solvents. Furthermore, the hydrogel must possess appropriate mechanical and structural properties to support 3D cell growth, maintain high viability, and permit diffusion of drugs and signaling molecules for pharmacological testing. The development of a material that fulfills all these criteria would represent a transformative advance in in vitro modeling, enabling more physiologically relevant and operationally efficient 3D cell culture for research, drug discovery, and regenerative medicine.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel self-assembling peptide hydrogel system specifically designed to overcome the longstanding limitations of conventional 3D cell culture platforms by enabling both convenient cell encapsulation and gentle, efficient cell recovery under fully physiological conditions. At the core of this invention is a newly identified peptide, designated herein as h9e, which spontaneously forms a hydrogel matrix upon direct mixing with standard cell culture medium—such as Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS)—at neutral pH and room temperature, without the need for any additional chemical cross-linkers, pH adjustments, or temperature manipulations. This unique property allows live cells to be homogeneously encapsulated within the hydrogel scaffold while remaining continuously immersed in their complete growth medium, thereby ensuring optimal viability and physiological relevance from the moment of encapsulation.

The h9e peptide hydrogel exhibits exceptional rheological properties that are central to its utility in 3D cell culture. Specifically, it demonstrates pronounced shear-thinning behavior, meaning that the hydrogel rapidly liquefies under mechanical stress—such as pipetting or vortexing—and subsequently reassembles into a solid gel upon cessation of the stress. This reversible sol-gel transition is highly repeatable and concentration-dependent, with higher peptide concentrations (e.g., 3 mM) showing near-complete recovery of mechanical strength within minutes after shear-induced liquefaction. Critically, this property enables not only easy handling and dispensing of the hydrogel-cell mixture but also provides a novel, non-enzymatic method for cell isolation: by diluting the hydrogel below its critical gelation concentration (e.g., 15-fold dilution of a 3 mM gel to yield 0.2 mM) and applying mild mechanical agitation, the matrix is converted into a low-viscosity liquid that permits straightforward centrifugation-based cell pelleting without compromising cell integrity or viability.

Structurally, the h9e hydrogel consists of a nanofibrous network with fiber diameters of approximately 20 nanometers, closely mimicking the scale and morphology of native extracellular matrix components. The porosity and mechanical stiffness of the gel are tunable through variation of the initial peptide concentration, with storage moduli (G′) ranging from approximately 100 Pa at 1 mM to 700 Pa at 3 mM under physiological conditions (37°C). These properties support the formation of authentic 3D cell morphologies, as demonstrated using the human breast cancer cell line MCF-7, which develops into compact, tumor-like spheroids when cultured within the h9e hydrogel—distinct from the flat, spread morphology observed in 2D monolayers. Importantly, cells maintained in the h9e hydrogel exhibit high viability over extended periods and display proliferation rates that are consistent with known 3D culture behaviors, namely slower than in 2D but reflective of in vivo tumor dynamics.

The invention further encompasses the use of the h9e hydrogel system for drug efficacy testing, wherein therapeutic agents such as the chemotherapeutic cisplatin can be introduced via multiple routes—including pre-mixing with the peptide-medium solution prior to gelation, addition to the surface of pre-formed gels, or delivery through transwell configurations—and effectively diffuse throughout the hydrogel matrix to exert dose- and time-dependent effects on encapsulated cells. Notably, cells recovered from drug-treated h9e hydrogels retain sufficient quality for downstream molecular analyses, including immunofluorescence staining, Western blotting, and potentially transcriptomic and proteomic profiling, thereby enabling comprehensive mechanistic studies of drug action in a 3D context.

In summary, the present invention provides a complete, integrated 3D cell culture platform based on the h9e self-assembling peptide that uniquely combines: (1) physiological gelation directly in standard culture medium at neutral pH and ambient temperature; (2) homogeneous cell encapsulation with immediate access to nutrients; (3) tunable mechanical and structural properties supporting authentic 3D cell morphologies; (4) reversible shear-thinning for easy handling and dispensing; (5) simple, non-destructive cell isolation via dilution and centrifugation; and (6) compatibility with drug testing and downstream analytical workflows. This system represents a significant advancement over existing 2D and 3D culture technologies by bridging the gap between biological relevance and operational simplicity, thereby facilitating more predictive and translatable in vitro models for biomedical research and pharmaceutical development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a self-assembling peptide hydrogel system for three-dimensional (3D) cell culture that enables both convenient cell encapsulation and gentle, efficient cell recovery under fully physiological conditions. The system is based on a novel peptide, referred to herein as h9e, which spontaneously forms a hydrogel matrix upon direct mixing with standard cell culture medium at neutral pH and ambient temperature, without requiring any additional chemical cross-linkers, pH adjustments, or temperature manipulations. This unique combination of properties addresses critical limitations of conventional 2D and 3D cell culture platforms, offering a highly versatile and user-friendly tool for a wide range of biomedical applications, including basic cell biology, disease modeling, drug screening, and regenerative medicine.

The h9e peptide is a rationally designed sequence of amino acids that undergoes spontaneous self-assembly into β-sheet-rich nanofibers under physiological conditions. While the exact amino acid sequence of h9e is proprietary and may vary within the scope of the invention, it is characterized by alternating hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues that promote intermolecular hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, driving the formation of stable, entangled nanofibrous networks. The peptide is synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) techniques, such as Fmoc (9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl) chemistry on Rink amide resin, followed by cleavage, deprotection, purification, and lyophilization to yield a white, fluffy powder suitable for long-term storage at −20°C. Prior to use, the lyophilized peptide is dissolved in a mild aqueous buffer—such as 100 mM sodium bicarbonate—at a stock concentration of 10 mM, resulting in a clear, colorless solution at pH 7–8. This neutral pH is critical, as it ensures compatibility with live cells and standard culture media.

To form the hydrogel, the h9e peptide stock solution is directly mixed with complete cell culture medium—such as Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), sodium pyruvate, non-essential amino acids, sodium bicarbonate, and insulin—at room temperature. The final peptide concentration in the mixture typically ranges from 1 mM to 3 mM (corresponding to 0.17% to 0.51% w/v), although concentrations as low as 0.5 mM may suffice for certain applications. Upon mixing, the peptide immediately begins to self-assemble, and a self-supporting hydrogel forms within 15 minutes at room temperature, with complete gelation achieved by 30 minutes when incubated at 37°C. This rapid, medium-induced gelation occurs without any external triggers, allowing cells to be added directly to the peptide-medium mixture at the time of preparation. The cells remain suspended in their complete growth medium throughout the entire gelation process, ensuring continuous access to nutrients, growth factors, and buffering capacity, thereby maximizing viability and minimizing stress.

The resulting hydrogel exhibits a nanofibrous architecture with individual fibers measuring approximately 20 nanometers in diameter, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The density and mechanical strength of the network are directly proportional to the initial peptide concentration: at 1 mM, the matrix appears relatively loose and porous, whereas at 3 mM, the fibers are more densely packed, forming a compact, resilient scaffold. Rheological characterization using oscillatory shear measurements reveals that the storage modulus (G′)—a measure of the elastic, solid-like behavior—increases with peptide concentration, ranging from approximately 100 Pa at 1 mM to 400 Pa at 2 mM and 700 Pa at 3 mM when measured at 37°C and 1 Hz frequency. The loss modulus (G″)—representing the viscous, liquid-like component—remains consistently lower than G′, confirming the formation of a true hydrogel rather than a viscous solution. These mechanical properties are well-suited for soft tissue mimicry, falling within the range reported for many native extracellular matrices.

A defining feature of the h9e hydrogel is its remarkable shear-thinning and self-healing behavior. When subjected to high shear strain—such as 500% strain at 1 Hz for 1 minute—the hydrogel undergoes instantaneous liquefaction, with G′ dropping below 0.2 Pa, effectively converting the solid gel into a low-viscosity fluid. Upon removal of the shear stress and return to low-strain conditions (1% strain), the hydrogel rapidly reassembles, recovering a significant portion of its original mechanical strength within minutes. The extent and rate of recovery are concentration-dependent: after a single shear-thinning event followed by 1 minute of rest, 3 mM hydrogels recover approximately 88% of their initial G′, compared to 73% for 1 mM gels. With longer rest periods (e.g., 10 minutes), 3 mM gels achieve nearly 100% recovery, even after multiple cycles of shear-thinning. This reversible sol-gel transition is attributed to the dynamic, non-covalent nature of the intermolecular interactions holding the nanofibers together—primarily hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic forces—which can break under stress but readily reform when the stress is removed.

This rheological property has profound practical implications. First, it enables easy handling and dispensing of the hydrogel-cell mixture using standard laboratory pipettes, syringes, or automated liquid handlers, without permanently damaging the scaffold structure. Second, and more importantly, it provides the foundation for a novel, non-enzymatic method of cell isolation. After cells have been cultured within the hydrogel for a desired period (e.g., 1–7 days), the matrix can be disrupted by simply pipetting the construct vigorously in the presence of additional culture medium. To prevent immediate re-gelation and ensure complete liquefaction, the hydrogel is diluted below its critical gelation concentration—typically by adding 14 volumes of fresh MEM to 1 volume of 3 mM hydrogel, yielding a final peptide concentration of 0.2 mM. At this low concentration, the peptide lacks sufficient intermolecular interactions to form a percolating network, and the solution remains in a liquid state even at 37°C. When maintained at 4°C—the standard temperature for cell centrifugation—the diluted mixture exhibits Newtonian fluid behavior with negligible viscoelasticity (G′ and G″ ≈ 0–5 Pa), allowing cells to be efficiently pelleted by centrifugation at 2,000 rpm for 5 minutes without entrapment in residual gel fragments. Post-isolation, cell viability and yield are comparable to those obtained from 2D cultures, demonstrating the gentleness and efficacy of this recovery method.

Cells cultured in the h9e hydrogel adopt authentic 3D morphologies that starkly contrast with their 2D counterparts. For example, when the human breast adenocarcinoma cell line MCF-7 is encapsulated in 1–3 mM h9e hydrogel, it forms compact, spherical clusters reminiscent of tumor spheroids observed in vivo. These clusters grow over time through cell division and coalescence, reaching diameters exceeding 50 micrometers by day 7. Confocal microscopy with z-stack imaging confirms homogeneous distribution of cells throughout the hydrogel matrix, with no evidence of sedimentation or aggregation at the bottom of the culture well. Scanning electron microscopy further reveals intimate interactions between the cell clusters and the surrounding nanofibrous scaffold, with multiple layers of fibers adhering to the cell surface—indicative of active remodeling and integration with the matrix. In contrast, MCF-7 cells grown in 2D monolayers attach to the plastic substrate, spread out into flat, irregular shapes, and form confluent sheets that overlay one another at high density.

Functionally, cells in the h9e hydrogel exhibit behaviors consistent with physiologically relevant 3D models. Proliferation rates are significantly slower than in 2D culture—a hallmark of 3D environments attributed to limited nutrient diffusion, contact inhibition, and altered signaling—but remain stable and viable over extended periods. Cell viability, as assessed by trypan blue exclusion, remains consistently above 90% for at least 7 days, regardless of peptide concentration. Moreover, the 3D architecture influences cellular responses to external stimuli. When treated with the chemotherapeutic agent cisplatin, MCF-7 cells in h9e hydrogel show dose- and time-dependent reductions in viability, with an IC50 value of 31.25 µM—consistent with literature reports for 3D cancer models. Drug delivery is effective via multiple routes: pre-mixing cisplatin with the peptide-medium solution prior to gelation yields the strongest effect, likely due to uniform distribution, while surface application and transwell delivery also produce significant cytotoxicity, confirming adequate drug diffusion through the hydrogel matrix.

Critically, cells recovered from drug-treated h9e hydrogels are suitable for detailed molecular analysis. Immunofluorescence staining of isolated clusters reveals cisplatin-induced changes in cytoskeletal organization (disruption of actin perimeter staining), reduced proliferation (decreased Ki67 expression), and activation of apoptosis (increased cleaved caspase-3 and decreased survivin). These findings are corroborated by Western blot analysis of whole-cell lysates, which detect corresponding changes in protein expression levels. The ability to perform such downstream assays on cells extracted from the 3D matrix underscores the non-destructive nature of the isolation protocol and the preservation of cellular integrity.

In practice, the h9e hydrogel system is implemented as follows: lyophilized h9e peptide is dissolved in 100 mM sodium bicarbonate to 10 mM and sterilized by UV irradiation. Cells are harvested from 2D culture, counted, and resuspended in complete MEM. The peptide solution is then mixed with the cell suspension at the desired ratio to achieve the target final peptide concentration (e.g., 1–3 mM) and cell density (e.g., 0.5–2.8 × 10⁵ cells/mL). The mixture is immediately seeded into multi-well plates and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes to complete gelation. Fresh medium is added on top to prevent evaporation, and the culture is maintained with medium changes every 2 days. For cell isolation, the hydrogel is diluted 15-fold with cold MEM, pipetted vigorously, and centrifuged at 4°C. The resulting pellet is resuspended in fresh medium for counting, replating, or analysis.

The versatility of the h9e system extends beyond MCF-7 cells; it is expected to support a wide range of adherent and non-adherent cell types, including primary cells, stem cells, and co-cultures, provided they are compatible with the base culture medium. Additionally, the peptide sequence can be modified to incorporate bioactive motifs—such as RGD for integrin binding or MMP-cleavable sites for cell-mediated degradation—thereby tailoring the hydrogel to specific biological contexts. The invention thus represents a robust, scalable, and user-friendly platform that bridges the gap between biological fidelity and experimental convenience in 3D cell culture.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Preparation and Characterization of h9e Peptide Hydrogel**

The h9e peptide was synthesized using Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis on a CEM Liberty microwave peptide synthesizer. The sequence was assembled on Rink Amide MBHA resin using HBTU/HOBT activation and DIEA as the base. After chain assembly, the N-terminal Fmoc group was removed, and the resin-bound peptide was cleaved and side-chain deprotected using a TFA/TIS/water (95:2.5:2.5 v/v) cocktail. The crude peptide was precipitated in cold anhydrous ether, washed three times, dissolved in 50% acetonitrile/water, and lyophilized. Purity (>95%) and molecular weight were confirmed by HPLC and MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, respectively.

For hydrogel preparation, lyophilized h9e was dissolved in 100 mM sodium bicarbonate at 10 mM concentration by magnetic stirring for 3 hours at room temperature, yielding a clear solution at pH 7.5. To form hydrogels, 100 µL of this stock solution was added to 900 µL of MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, resulting in final peptide concentrations of 1, 2, or 3 mM. The mixture was hand-shaken for 10 seconds and allowed to gel at room temperature. Gelation was complete within 15 minutes, as evidenced by inversion tests showing no flow.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed a nanofibrous network with fiber diameters of ~20 nm. At 1 mM, the matrix was loose and porous; at 3 mM, fibers were densely packed. Rheological measurements on a Malvern C-VOR 150 rheometer (20-mm parallel plate, 500 µm gap, 37°C) showed storage moduli (G′) of ~100, 400, and 700 Pa for 1, 2, and 3 mM gels, respectively, confirming concentration-dependent mechanical tuning.

**Example 2: Shear-Thinning and Self-Healing Behavior**

Hydrogels (1–3 mM) were formed overnight at room temperature, then loaded onto the rheometer and stabilized for 10 minutes at 37°C. A high-shear step (500% strain, 1 Hz, 1 min) reduced G′ to <0.2 Pa, indicating complete liquefaction. Upon returning to 1% strain, G′ recovered to 73%, 76%, and 88% of original values within 1 hour for 1, 2, and 3 mM gels, respectively. In amplitude sweep tests with four cycles of 1–500% strain, 3 mM gels recovered 100% of G′ after 10 minutes of rest, demonstrating robust self-healing. Temperature ramp tests (4–50°C) showed reversible thermal stiffening, with G′ 2–3× higher at 50°C than at 4°C.

**Example 3: 3D Culture and Morphology of MCF-7 Cells**

MCF-7 cells were trypsinized, counted, and resuspended in MEM at 2.8 × 10⁵ cells/mL. h9e peptide solution (10 mM) was mixed with cell suspension to yield final concentrations of 1, 2, or 3 mM peptide and 2.8 × 10⁵ cells/mL. Mixtures were seeded into 12-well plates (1 mL/well) and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes. An additional 1 mL MEM was added on top. Cultures were maintained for 7 days with medium changes every 2 days.

Inverted microscopy showed that 2D cells attached and spread, while 3D cells formed spherical clusters. By day 7, 3D clusters exceeded 50 µm in diameter. SEM of fixed samples revealed cell clusters enveloped by nanofibers, with layered fiber attachments indicating active remodeling. Confocal z-stacks of Hoechst-stained cells confirmed homogeneous distribution and multicellular cluster formation.

**Example 4: Cell Viability, Proliferation, and Isolation**

Cell viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion at days 1, 3, 5, and 7. In 2D, cell numbers doubled every 2 days. In 3D, proliferation was slower: 1 mM gels showed the highest growth, 3 mM the lowest, stabilizing by day 5. Viability remained >90% in all conditions. For isolation, 3 mM gels with 1.5 × 10⁵ cells were diluted 15-fold with MEM, pipetted vigorously, and centrifuged at 4°C (2,000 rpm, 5 min). Recovered cells showed no significant difference in viability or count compared to pre-encapsulation, confirming gentle recovery.

**Example 5: Drug Testing with Cisplatin**

MCF-7 cells were cultured in 3 mM h9e hydrogel for 5 days to form spheroids. Cisplatin (40 µM) was delivered via three methods: (1) top surface addition, (2) transwell (top and bottom chambers), and (3) pre-mixed with peptide-medium before gelation. Viability was assessed at days 0, 1, 3, 5, and 10. All methods reduced viability, with pre-mixed showing the strongest effect (85% reduction by day 10). Dose-response curves (0–100 µM cisplatin, pre-mixed) yielded an IC50 of 31.25 µM.

Immunofluorescence of isolated cells showed cisplatin-induced nuclear fragmentation, actin disruption, reduced Ki67, decreased survivin, and increased cleaved caspase-3. Western blots confirmed these changes at the protein level, with no cleaved caspase-3 detected in untreated 3D cultures, indicating no baseline apoptosis from the hydrogel.

These examples demonstrate that the h9e peptide hydrogel provides a physiologically relevant, operationally simple, and analytically compatible 3D cell culture platform suitable for a wide range of biomedical applications.
