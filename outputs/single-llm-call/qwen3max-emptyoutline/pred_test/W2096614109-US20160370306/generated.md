# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX) has emerged as a revolutionary technique in structural biology, enabling the determination of high-resolution three-dimensional structures of proteins that are otherwise recalcitrant to conventional X-ray crystallography. This method leverages the unique properties of hard X-ray free-electron lasers (XFELs), which generate ultra-short, intense pulses of X-ray radiation on the femtosecond timescale. The fundamental principle underlying SFX is the “diffraction-before-destruction” paradigm, wherein a diffraction pattern is recorded from a protein crystal before the intense X-ray pulse induces catastrophic radiation damage and complete disintegration of the sample. This approach circumvents the limitations imposed by radiation-induced structural degradation that plagues traditional synchrotron-based crystallography, particularly for sensitive biological macromolecules such as membrane proteins and large multi-subunit complexes.

The practical implementation of SFX requires the delivery of a continuous stream of microcrystals or nanocrystals into the path of the XFEL beam at a rate synchronized with the pulse repetition frequency of the source, which typically ranges from 1 to 120 Hz. Historically, the most common method for delivering these crystals has been the gas dynamic virtual nozzle (GDVN), which generates a high-velocity liquid jet—typically moving at 10–20 meters per second—by focusing a stream of protein crystal suspension in mother liquor with a coaxial sheath of inert gas. While effective in maintaining a steady flow of crystals across the X-ray interaction point, this method suffers from extreme inefficiency: due to the high jet velocity and the stochastic nature of crystal orientation within the stream, only approximately one in every 10,000 crystals is actually intersected by an X-ray pulse and yields a usable diffraction pattern. Consequently, this approach demands large quantities of purified protein—often in the range of 10 to 100 milligrams—to accumulate a complete dataset, a requirement that poses a significant barrier for the study of proteins that are difficult to express, purify, or crystallize in large amounts.

This limitation is particularly acute in the context of membrane proteins, which constitute a critically important class of biological targets. Membrane proteins mediate essential cellular functions including signal transduction, ion transport, and energy conversion, and they represent approximately 60% of all current pharmaceutical drug targets. Despite their biomedical significance, the structural characterization of membrane proteins has lagged far behind that of soluble proteins. As of the time of this writing, fewer than 550 unique membrane protein structures have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), compared to over 100,000 total structures. This disparity arises from the inherent challenges associated with membrane protein biochemistry: these proteins are insoluble in aqueous environments and must be extracted from lipid bilayers using detergents, forming protein–detergent micelles that are often unstable and prone to denaturation. Moreover, the crystallization of membrane proteins is notoriously difficult, frequently yielding only small, fragile crystals that are unsuitable for conventional crystallographic methods.

To address these challenges, alternative crystallization and delivery strategies have been developed. One notable advance is the use of the lipidic cubic phase (LCP) as a crystallization matrix and delivery medium. LCP is a bicontinuous lipidic mesophase formed by mixing monoacylglycerols with water, which mimics the native lipid bilayer environment and has proven highly effective for the crystallization of G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) and other membrane proteins. When used in SFX, LCP’s high viscosity enables the formation of a slow-moving, stable stream that drastically reduces protein consumption—by up to two orders of magnitude compared to GDVN—while maintaining a sufficient crystal delivery rate for data collection. However, LCP is not universally applicable. Its intrinsic curvature and low diffusion coefficients hinder the crystallization of large, multi-domain membrane protein complexes, such as photosynthetic reaction centers. Furthermore, attempts to embed pre-formed crystals—particularly those grown as protein–detergent micelles in aqueous solution—into LCP typically result in crystal dissolution, likely due to detergent partitioning into the lipid phase and subsequent destabilization of the protein complex.

In response to these limitations, researchers have explored other viscous media as potential alternatives for SFX sample delivery. Mineral oil-based greases and petroleum jelly have been tested as carrier matrices, demonstrating reduced protein consumption and compatibility with ambient-pressure data collection. However, these materials introduce significant background scattering in the form of Debye–Scherrer rings in the 3.77–5 Å resolution range, which can obscure weak diffraction signals from protein crystals, particularly at medium to low resolution. Additionally, these media have only been validated for soluble model proteins like lysozyme and have not been shown to support the delivery of large, cofactor-rich membrane protein complexes without compromising structural integrity.

Another critical consideration in the design of an SFX delivery medium is its performance under vacuum conditions, which are standard in many XFEL endstations to minimize X-ray absorption by air and reduce background noise. Many viscous carriers, including aqueous gels, are susceptible to evaporative cooling and freezing in high vacuum, leading to the formation of ice crystals that produce strong, spurious diffraction patterns that interfere with data analysis. Cryoprotectants such as glycerol or sucrose are often employed to mitigate this issue, but their addition must be carefully balanced to avoid destabilizing the protein crystals or altering the rheological properties of the delivery stream.

Against this backdrop, there remains a pressing need for a universal, low-background, low-consumption crystal delivery medium that is compatible with a wide range of protein types—including soluble proteins, membrane proteins, and large multi-subunit complexes—and that functions reliably under both vacuum and ambient-pressure conditions. Such a medium should preserve crystal integrity during embedding and delivery, form a stable and continuous extrusion stream at low flow rates, and generate minimal X-ray scattering background across all resolution ranges, particularly at low angles where large unit cell crystals produce their most informative diffraction signals. The development of such a delivery system would significantly expand the scope of SFX, enabling structural studies of rare, scarce, or difficult-to-express proteins that are currently inaccessible due to sample limitations or incompatibility with existing delivery methods.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and versatile method for delivering protein crystals—both soluble and membrane-bound—for serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX) using a biocompatible, low-scattering gel matrix composed primarily of agarose. This delivery system overcomes the major limitations of prior art methods by enabling efficient, low-consumption sample delivery while preserving crystal integrity and minimizing background X-ray scattering. The invention is based on the discovery that pre-grown protein crystals, regardless of their size, complexity, or crystallization conditions, can be homogeneously embedded into a thermally reversible agarose gel matrix that is specifically formulated to maintain crystal stability, form a stable extrusion stream, and remain non-freezing under high-vacuum conditions typical of XFEL beamlines.

The core of the invention lies in the composition and preparation of the agarose-based delivery medium. In a preferred embodiment, the medium comprises 5.6% (w/v) ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose dissolved in an aqueous solution containing 30% (v/v) glycerol and the crystallization mother liquor of the target protein. The glycerol serves a dual function: it acts as a cryoprotectant to prevent ice formation during operation in vacuum, and it enhances the viscosity and viscoelastic properties of the gel, thereby improving the stability of the extruded stream. For proteins that are incompatible with glycerol—such as certain low-ionic-strength membrane protein complexes like photosystem I—the glycerol may be replaced with an alternative cryoprotectant such as sucrose, tailored to the specific biochemical requirements of the crystal.

Crucially, the invention employs a post-crystallization embedding strategy, wherein crystals are grown using standard methods—such as vapor diffusion, dialysis, or free interface diffusion—and are subsequently mixed into the agarose matrix after gelation has commenced but before the gel becomes fully rigid. This is achieved using a syringe-based mixing protocol adapted from lipidic cubic phase (LCP) crystallography, in which a syringe containing the semi-solidified agarose is coupled with a second syringe containing a concentrated suspension of protein crystals, and the two contents are repeatedly exchanged between the syringes to achieve homogeneous distribution without mechanical damage. This approach bypasses the need to crystallize proteins directly within the agarose matrix—a process that is often impractical for large or slow-diffusing macromolecular complexes—and instead allows the use of pre-optimized crystallization conditions.

The resulting agarose-crystal mixture is then extruded through a capillary injector—such as a 50 µm inner diameter nozzle—using a pressurized inert gas sheath to stabilize the stream, at a flow rate as low as 160 nanoliters per minute. This represents a reduction in sample consumption by two orders of magnitude compared to conventional liquid jet methods, making the technique feasible for proteins available only in minute quantities. The agarose stream exhibits excellent stability under vacuum and, with appropriate formulation, can also be operated at ambient pressure in a helium environment.

A key advantage of the agarose medium is its exceptionally low X-ray scattering background. Unlike LCP, mineral oil grease, or petroleum jelly, which produce strong diffuse scattering or sharp Debye–Scherrer rings in the 3.7–5 Å range, agarose generates only a weak, broad diffuse peak centered around 3.3 Å and negligible scattering at very low resolution (<30 Å). This low background is particularly beneficial for the structure determination of large protein complexes with extended unit cells, whose diffraction signals are concentrated at low resolution. Quantitative analysis demonstrates that the background scattering from agarose is approximately 2.3 times lower than that from LCP in the critical mid-resolution range, and significantly lower than all other reported viscous media.

The invention has been successfully demonstrated with a diverse array of protein systems, including the soluble cyanobacterial light-harvesting complex phycocyanin (a 110 kDa trimer), the membrane-bound photosystem I (a 1080 kDa trimer with over 300 cofactors), and photosystem II (a 700 kDa dimer with 38 subunits). High-resolution diffraction data were collected for phycocyanin at 2.5 Å resolution, yielding a refined structure with Rwork = 18.7% and Rfree = 25.5%, confirming that the agarose embedding process does not compromise crystal order or diffraction quality. The method is compatible with a wide range of crystallization precipitants, including high concentrations of salts (e.g., 1 M NaCl, 1.25 M ammonium sulfate), polyethylene glycols (PEGs) of varying molecular weights (up to 30% PEG 8000), divalent cations (e.g., 0.2 M CaCl₂), and organic agents such as 2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol (MPD).

In summary, the invention provides a general-purpose, low-cost, and highly efficient crystal delivery platform for SFX that is broadly applicable across structural biology. It enables the structural determination of scarce, complex, or previously intractable protein systems by dramatically reducing sample requirements while enhancing data quality through minimized background scattering. The method is robust, scalable, and compatible with existing XFEL instrumentation, requiring no specialized hardware beyond standard LCP injectors. As such, it represents a significant advancement in the field of time-resolved and room-temperature structural biology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to a method and composition for the delivery of protein crystals in serial femtosecond crystallography (SFX) using an agarose-based gel matrix. This system is designed to address the critical shortcomings of existing delivery methods—namely, excessive sample consumption, crystal instability, and high background scattering—by providing a biocompatible, low-viscosity, and low-scattering medium that supports the reliable delivery of both soluble and membrane protein crystals under standard XFEL operating conditions.

The agarose delivery medium is prepared by dissolving ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose powder in a solution comprising the crystallization mother liquor of the target protein and a cryoprotectant, typically glycerol or sucrose, depending on the protein’s biochemical sensitivity. In the standard formulation, 7% (w/v) agarose is dissolved in a mixture of 30% (v/v) glycerol and 70% (v/v) crystallization buffer by heating the solution to above 85°C—preferably by submerging the container in a boiling water bath for 30 minutes—until a clear, homogeneous solution is obtained. The elevated initial agarose concentration (7%) compensates for dilution that occurs during the subsequent mixing step with the crystal suspension, which typically contains 20–25% (v/v) aqueous mother liquor, resulting in a final agarose concentration of 5.6% (w/v) in the delivery matrix. This final concentration has been empirically optimized to provide sufficient mechanical strength for stable extrusion while maintaining a viscosity that allows homogeneous crystal dispersion and compatibility with standard capillary injectors.

The choice of cryoprotectant is tailored to the specific protein system. For most soluble and membrane proteins crystallized in the presence of moderate to high ionic strength or PEG-based precipitants, glycerol is preferred due to its dual role as a viscosity enhancer and anti-freezing agent. However, for proteins that are destabilized by glycerol—such as photosystem I, which crystallizes in a low-ionic-strength buffer containing only 5 mM MES pH 6.4 and 0.02% β-dodecylmaltoside—the glycerol is replaced with 0.6 M sucrose. Sucrose provides equivalent cryoprotection without perturbing the delicate electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions that maintain the integrity of the protein complex. The agarose-sucrose formulation is prepared analogously, with 9% (w/v) agarose initially dissolved in the sucrose-containing crystallization buffer to achieve a final concentration of approximately 7% after crystal mixing, ensuring adequate stream stability.

Once the agarose solution is prepared, it is drawn into a pre-warmed syringe (e.g., a 100 µl Hamilton syringe) to prevent premature gelation. The syringe is warmed by repeatedly drawing and expelling boiling water 10–15 times, taking care not to expose the syringe body to temperatures above 80°C to preserve its mechanical integrity. Approximately 20 µl of the hot agarose solution is then loaded into the syringe and allowed to cool to room temperature (20–25°C) for about 20 minutes, during which time the agarose begins to form a soft, semi-solid gel network but remains pliable enough for mixing.

Concurrently, a concentrated suspension of pre-grown protein crystals is prepared. Crystals are typically grown using standard methods such as vapor diffusion, batch crystallization, or free interface diffusion, and are harvested once they reach the desired size (usually 1–10 µm). To increase crystal density, the suspension is subjected to gentle sedimentation or low-speed centrifugation (e.g., 500–2000 g for 1–5 minutes), followed by removal of excess supernatant. The final crystal concentration is ideally on the order of 10¹¹ crystals per milliliter, though lower densities are acceptable if compensated by longer data collection times. Approximately 5 µl of this concentrated suspension is loaded into a second syringe.

The two syringes are then connected via a syringe coupler—a device commonly used in LCP crystallography—and the contents are mixed by alternately depressing the plungers at least 40 times. This repetitive back-and-forth motion ensures thorough and gentle homogenization of the crystals within the agarose matrix without inducing shear stress or mechanical fracture. The resulting mixture contains crystals uniformly dispersed at a density of approximately 2 × 10¹⁰ crystals per milliliter, embedded within a transparent, cohesive gel that maintains hydration and chemical compatibility with the original crystallization environment.

The agarose-crystal mixture is then loaded into a standard LCP injector or similar capillary-based delivery system equipped with a 50 µm inner diameter nozzle. A coaxial sheath of inert gas (e.g., nitrogen or helium) is applied to focus and stabilize the extruding stream, which is delivered at a flow rate of 160 nl/min—orders of magnitude slower than conventional liquid jets. This low flow rate translates to a dramatic reduction in protein consumption, enabling complete SFX datasets to be collected from as little as 0.1–1 mg of protein, compared to 10–100 mg required by GDVN methods.

The system is compatible with both vacuum and ambient-pressure XFEL endstations. In vacuum, the glycerol or sucrose prevents evaporative cooling and ice formation, while in ambient-pressure helium environments, dehydration is sufficiently slow that cryoprotectants may be omitted for robust crystals. Stream stability is generally superior in vacuum due to the absence of turbulent boundary layers that can develop at the interface between the gas sheath and ambient atmosphere.

X-ray diffraction data collected using this method exhibit minimal background scattering. Agarose, being 93% water by weight, produces only weak, diffuse scattering centered at 3.3 Å, with negligible signal at low resolution (<30 Å). This contrasts sharply with LCP (50% lipid, 50% water), which generates strong low-angle scattering and a prominent 4.5 Å lipid ring, and with oil-based media, which produce sharp Debye–Scherrer rings. The low background of agarose enhances the signal-to-noise ratio, particularly for large unit cell crystals, and facilitates more accurate intensity integration and structure solution.

The method has been validated with multiple protein systems. Phycocyanin crystals delivered in agarose yielded a 2.5 Å resolution structure with excellent refinement statistics. Photosystem I and II crystals, despite their enormous size and cofactor content, produced clear diffraction patterns, confirming the method’s applicability to the most challenging membrane protein complexes. The system accommodates a wide range of crystallization conditions, including high salt, PEGs, and organic precipitants, by adjusting the agarose concentration (5.6–9%) to maintain optimal viscosity.

In conclusion, the agarose-based delivery system of the present invention represents a robust, general, and efficient platform for SFX, enabling structural studies of proteins that were previously inaccessible due to sample scarcity or incompatibility with existing delivery media.

### EXAMPLE 1

In a specific embodiment of the invention, phycocyanin (PC) crystals were successfully delivered for serial femtosecond crystallography using the agarose-based medium. Phycocyanin, a soluble light-harvesting protein complex from *Thermosynechococcus elongatus*, was purified and crystallized using free interface diffusion. The protein solution (15 mg/ml) was mixed with an equal volume of precipitant solution containing 1.0 M ammonium sulfate and 40 mM MES pH 6.4, resulting in the formation of 1–5 µm crystals within 24 hours. These crystals were filtered through a 10 µm stainless-steel mesh to remove aggregates and concentrated by gentle sedimentation.

For agarose preparation, 7% (w/v) ultralow-gelling-temperature agarose (Sigma–Aldrich, A5030) was dissolved in a solution consisting of 600 µl glycerol and 1.4 ml of a buffer containing 15% PEG 2000, 30 mM MgCl₂, and 75 mM HEPES pH 7.0. The mixture was heated in a boiling water bath for 30 minutes until fully dissolved. A 100 µl Hamilton syringe was pre-warmed by drawing and ejecting boiling water 12 times, then loaded with 20 µl of the hot agarose solution. After cooling to room temperature for 20 minutes, the syringe was connected via a coupler to a second syringe containing 5 µl of concentrated PC crystal suspension. The two contents were mixed by 45 alternating plunger strokes, yielding a homogeneous agarose-crystal mixture.

This mixture was loaded into an LCP injector and extruded through a 50 µm capillary at 160 nl/min using a nitrogen sheath gas. Data were collected at the Coherent X-ray Imaging (CXI) instrument at the Linac Coherent Light Source (LCLS) under vacuum. Over 72 minutes, 513,848 detector frames were recorded. Using Cheetah software, 41,100 hits (frames with ≥25 Bragg spots) were identified, and 14,143 were successfully indexed in space group P6₃ with unit cell parameters a = b = 153.4 Å, c = 39.6 Å. The data were merged to 2.5 Å resolution with a multiplicity of 250.6 and CC* > 0.99. Molecular replacement using PDB entry 4GY3 yielded a refined structure with Rwork = 18.7% and Rfree = 25.5%, confirming high data quality and crystal integrity. Background scattering from the agarose stream was 2.3-fold lower than LCP, with no ice rings or artifacts observed. This example demonstrates the efficacy of the agarose delivery method for high-resolution SFX of soluble protein complexes with minimal sample consumption.
