## BACKGROUND

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) represents one of the most aggressive and lethal forms of primary brain cancer, characterized by rapid proliferation, extensive infiltration into surrounding brain parenchyma, and a high propensity for recurrence despite maximal therapeutic intervention. Current clinical paradigms for GBM management rely heavily on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to delineate tumor boundaries, assess treatment response, and guide surgical resection or radiation planning. However, conventional MRI-based assessment is fundamentally limited by its inability to capture the full spatial and biological extent of disease, particularly in regions that appear radiologically normal but harbor microscopic tumor infiltration—a phenomenon known as the “tumor field effect.” This field effect manifests not only through cellular invasion beyond visible margins but also through biomechanical perturbations of the surrounding brain tissue, including mass effect, midline shift, and increased intracranial pressure, all of which contribute significantly to patient morbidity and mortality.

The clinical challenge posed by GBM is further compounded by its profound inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity, which renders histopathological grading and molecular profiling insufficient as standalone prognostic tools. While molecular markers such as O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT) promoter methylation status and isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutation status have provided incremental improvements in risk stratification, their availability is inconsistent across clinical settings, and they do not fully account for the dynamic biomechanical and structural consequences of tumor growth. Moreover, even when molecular data are available, they offer limited insight into the spatial distribution of tumor aggressiveness or the mechanical stress exerted on adjacent neural structures—factors that are increasingly recognized as critical determinants of clinical outcome.

In recent years, the field of radiomics has emerged as a promising approach to extract quantitative, subvisual features from routine medical images to characterize tumor phenotypes non-invasively. Radiomic analyses typically involve the computation of texture-, shape-, and intensity-based features from segmented tumor regions and their immediate peritumoral environments. These features have demonstrated utility in predicting survival, treatment response, and molecular subtypes in various cancers, including GBM. Similarly, deep learning models, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been applied to MRI data to automate feature extraction and improve prognostic accuracy. Despite these advances, existing radiomic and deep learning frameworks remain largely confined to the analysis of image intensities and textures within or immediately adjacent to the tumor boundary. They fail to incorporate the biomechanical consequences of tumor growth—specifically, the subtle but quantifiable deformations induced in seemingly normal brain tissue surrounding the tumor, referred to herein as the “brain around tumor” (BAT) region.

The omission of BAT deformation metrics represents a significant gap in current prognostic modeling. Biomechanical models of tumor growth have long posited that aggressive tumors exert substantial mechanical stress on surrounding tissues, leading to displacement, compression, and architectural distortion that extend well beyond the enhancing tumor margin visible on MRI. Clinical observations corroborate this: GBM is frequently associated with herniation of distal brain structures, such as the brainstem, which is a proximal cause of death in a majority of cases. Furthermore, studies have documented GBM infiltration into the brainstem in over two-thirds of patients, often without corresponding signal abnormalities on standard MRI sequences. These findings suggest that the mechanical footprint of the tumor—encoded in the deformation patterns of the BAT—may serve as a surrogate biomarker of tumor aggressiveness and overall disease burden.

Prior attempts to model tumor-induced deformations have relied primarily on deterministic mathematical simulations or atlas-based morphometric analyses. While informative, these approaches are either too abstract for direct clinical translation or lack integration with tumor-intrinsic textural features that reflect underlying biological heterogeneity. No existing method combines high-resolution, patient-specific measurements of BAT deformation with advanced radiomic descriptors of intratumoral architecture to generate a unified prognostic signature. Consequently, there remains an unmet need for a comprehensive, image-derived biomarker that captures both the morphological complexity of the tumor core and the biomechanical impact of its growth on the surrounding brain environment.

The present invention addresses this critical gap by introducing a novel integrated radiomic descriptor—termed radiomic-Deformation and Textural Heterogeneity (r-DepTH)—that synergistically combines quantitative measures of tissue deformation in the BAT region with advanced 3D textural features extracted from the tumor and peritumoral compartments. Unlike prior art, which treats tumor morphology and tissue mechanics as separate domains, r-DepTH explicitly models their interdependence, recognizing that more aggressive tumors not only exhibit greater internal heterogeneity but also induce more pronounced and asymmetric deformations in surrounding tissue. By leveraging diffeomorphic image registration to map patient-specific brain anatomy onto a healthy reference atlas, the invention enables precise quantification of displacement magnitudes at multiple distances from the tumor margin. These deformation metrics are then fused with COLLAGE (Co-occurrence of Local Anisotropic Gradient Orientations)—a gradient-based radiomic descriptor that captures higher-order statistical properties of local tissue architecture—to form a composite feature vector predictive of overall survival.

The clinical significance of this approach lies in its ability to transform routinely acquired, multi-sequence MRI scans—already standard of care for GBM patients—into rich sources of prognostic information without requiring additional imaging protocols, contrast agents, or invasive procedures. Moreover, because r-DepTH is derived entirely from image data, it is inherently scalable, objective, and amenable to integration into clinical decision-support systems. Validation on a multi-institutional cohort of 207 GBM patients demonstrates that r-DepTH significantly outperforms conventional clinical variables, standalone radiomic models, and state-of-the-art deep learning approaches in stratifying patients into high- and low-risk survival groups, with robust performance across both training and independent validation sets. These results underscore the prognostic value of incorporating biomechanical deformation as a complementary dimension of radiomic analysis, thereby advancing the field toward more holistic, physics-informed models of cancer progression.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a system, method, and computer-readable medium for generating an integrated radiomic descriptor—referred to as radiomic-Deformation and Textural Heterogeneity (r-DepTH)—for prognosticating overall survival in patients diagnosed with glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) using standard-of-care magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans. The r-DepTH descriptor is constructed by fusing two distinct classes of image-derived features: (1) biomechanical deformation features quantifying subtle tissue displacements in the brain around tumor (BAT) region, and (2) morphological textural heterogeneity features extracted from the enhancing tumor core and peritumoral edema zones. This dual-component framework enables a comprehensive characterization of the tumor field effect, capturing both the intrinsic biological complexity of the neoplasm and its extrinsic mechanical influence on the surrounding neural architecture.

The method begins with the acquisition of a multi-parametric MRI dataset for each patient, comprising at least three standard sequences: gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted (Gd-T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), and T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (T2w-FLAIR) images. These sequences are co-registered to ensure spatial correspondence across modalities, typically using a symmetric normalization (SyN) algorithm implemented in the Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs) software suite. Concurrently, manual or semi-automated segmentation is performed to delineate two key regions of interest: the enhancing tumor lesion (I_T), identified on Gd-T1w images, and the peritumoral hyperintense region (I_P), defined on T2w and FLAIR images as areas of vasogenic edema or non-enhancing infiltration. The union of I_T and I_P constitutes the visible tumor compartment, while the remainder of the brain parenchyma—excluding ventricles and non-brain tissue—is designated as the BAT region (I_B).

To quantify biomechanical deformations in I_B, a healthy adult brain template—specifically, the MNI152 T1-weighted atlas (I_Atlas)—is non-rigidly registered to the patient’s native-space MRI using a diffeomorphic transformation model. Critically, during this registration process, the tumor mask (I_mask), derived from the union of I_T and I_P, is excluded from the similarity metric computation to prevent misalignment artifacts caused by pathological intensity deviations. This is achieved through a constrained cost-function masking approach, wherein the mutual information-based optimization is restricted to non-tumorous voxels. The resulting forward transformation maps the atlas anatomy onto the patient’s brain, effectively skull-stripping the image and providing a normalized anatomical reference. More importantly, the inverse of this transformation—guaranteed to exist due to the diffeomorphic nature of the registration—yields a voxel-wise displacement field that encodes how each point in the healthy atlas must be deformed to match the patient’s anatomy. This inverse field thus represents the cumulative tissue displacement induced by the tumor’s mass effect.

From this displacement field, the magnitude of deformation at each voxel c in I_B is computed as the Euclidean norm of its three-dimensional displacement vector: D(c) = √(δt² + δu² + δv²), where (δt, δu, δv) denote the displacement components along the x, y, and z axes, respectively. To capture spatial gradients in deformation severity, the BAT region is partitioned into a series of concentric annular sub-volumes (I_Bj), each extending 5 millimeters radially outward from the tumor boundary. In the preferred embodiment, twelve such annuli are defined, spanning distances from 5 mm to 60 mm from the tumor margin, consistent with clinical target volume margins used in radiation oncology for GBM. For each annulus j ∈ {1, ..., 12}, first-order statistical descriptors—including mean, median, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis—are computed across all deformation magnitudes D(c) within that band. This yields a 60-dimensional deformation feature vector F_B = [F_B1, F_B2, ..., F_B12], where each F_Bj is a 5-element subvector representing the statistical summary of deformations at distance j.

Concurrently, textural heterogeneity within the tumor compartments is characterized using the 3D COLLAGE (Co-occurrence of Local Anisotropic Gradient Orientations) descriptor. For each voxel c in I_T and I_P, intensity gradients are computed along the three spatial dimensions to form a local gradient tensor. From this tensor, two principal orientation angles—θ(c) (azimuthal) and φ(c) (polar)—are derived, representing the dominant directions of intensity change in the local neighborhood. Two separate gray-level co-occurrence matrices (GLCMs) are then constructed: one for θ(c) and one for φ(c), each encoding the joint probability distribution of orientation pairs at a specified offset. From each GLCM, thirteen Haralick texture features—such as energy, contrast, correlation, homogeneity, and entropy—are calculated. Finally, first-order statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis) are aggregated across all voxels in I_T and I_P for each Haralick feature, resulting in two 130-dimensional vectors: F_T (from I_T) and F_P (from I_P), where 13 features × 5 statistics × 2 orientation matrices = 130.

The r-DepTH descriptor is formed by concatenating F_B, F_T, and F_P into a single 320-dimensional feature vector: F_rDepTH = [F_B; F_T; F_P]. This composite descriptor encapsulates both the extrinsic mechanical impact of the tumor (via F_B) and its intrinsic architectural complexity (via F_T and F_P). To enable survival risk stratification, F_rDepTH is input into a supervised machine learning pipeline. Specifically, a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regularized Cox proportional hazards model is trained on a cohort of GBM patients with known overall survival outcomes. LASSO performs simultaneous feature selection and coefficient estimation, identifying a sparse subset of the most prognostically relevant features while mitigating overfitting. The resulting model generates a continuous risk score (Risc) for each patient, defined as the linear combination of selected features weighted by their LASSO-derived coefficients.

An optimal threshold for dichotomizing patients into high-risk (H-R) and low-risk (L-R) groups is determined using X-tile software, which performs a grid search across all possible cutoff values to maximize the log-rank test statistic between survival curves. Kaplan-Meier analysis is then used to visualize survival differences, with statistical significance assessed via the log-rank test. Model performance is further evaluated using the concordance index (C-index), which measures the probability that, for any two randomly selected patients, the model correctly ranks their survival times, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), which quantify the relative risk of death in the H-R group compared to the L-R group.

In validation studies involving 207 retrospective GBM cases from multiple institutions, the r-DepTH descriptor demonstrated superior prognostic performance compared to alternative strategies. On an independent test set, r-DepTH achieved a C-index of 0.75, a hazard ratio of 1.94 (95% CI: 1–3), and a highly significant log-rank p-value of 0.0024, outperforming models based solely on clinical variables (age, gender, tumor volume), standalone COLLAGE features (F_T,P), deformation features alone (F_B), conventional radiomics (4376 features from CapTk), and a state-of-the-art CNN (4096 deep features). Notably, while the CNN and conventional radiomics models showed strong performance on the training set, they failed to generalize to the validation set, likely due to scanner-induced domain shifts in this multi-institutional cohort. In contrast, r-DepTH maintained robust discriminative power across datasets, underscoring the stability of its biomechanical-textural fusion approach.

Qualitative analysis revealed that high-risk patients exhibited elevated kurtosis in COLLAGE-derived energy features—indicative of extreme heterogeneity in gradient orientations—and increased skewness in deformation magnitudes at 5 mm and 10 mm from the tumor margin, reflecting asymmetric, lopsided displacement patterns consistent with aggressive mass effect. These findings align with the biological premise that more proliferative tumors exert greater and more irregular mechanical stress on surrounding tissue, leading to worse clinical outcomes.

The invention may be implemented as a software module integrated into a clinical imaging workstation or cloud-based analytics platform. Input requirements are minimal: standard DICOM-formatted MRI sequences and optional tumor segmentations (which can also be generated automatically using pre-trained segmentation networks). The output is a patient-specific risk score and survival curve, which can inform treatment intensification, clinical trial enrollment, or palliative care planning. Future extensions include incorporation of deformation directionality (phase information), integration with molecular data, and application to other solid tumors exhibiting significant mass effect, such as metastatic brain lesions or high-grade meningiomas.

In summary, the r-DepTH descriptor represents a paradigm shift in radiomic analysis by unifying morphological and biomechanical dimensions of tumor behavior into a single, clinically actionable biomarker. By leveraging the latent information encoded in tissue deformations beyond the visible tumor margin, the invention provides a more complete and physiologically grounded assessment of disease aggressiveness, ultimately enabling more precise and personalized prognosis for GBM patients.
