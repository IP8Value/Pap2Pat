# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Macrophages are central components of the innate immune system, functioning as versatile sentinel cells that maintain tissue homeostasis, orchestrate inflammatory responses, and contribute to pathogen clearance through phagocytosis and cytokine signaling. These cells originate from circulating monocytes that differentiate into tissue-resident macrophages upon exposure to specific microenvironmental cues. A hallmark of macrophage biology is their extraordinary plasticity—macrophages can adopt a spectrum of functional phenotypes in response to diverse stimuli, broadly categorized into two major polarization states: classically activated (M1-like) and alternatively activated (M2-like) macrophages. Classical activation, typically induced by interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) alone or in combination with microbial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), results in a pro-inflammatory phenotype characterized by high secretion of interleukin-12 (IL-12), IL-23, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, along with low production of anti-inflammatory IL-10. These M1-like macrophages express surface markers such as CD86, CD64 (FcγRI), and CD16, and serve as potent effectors in host defense against intracellular pathogens and tumors. In contrast, alternative activation is driven by cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-13, leading to an M2-like phenotype associated with tissue repair, immunoregulation, and parasite containment. M2-like macrophages exhibit low IL-12 and IL-23 production, elevated expression of CD23 (FcεRII), and secretion of chemokines like CCL17, CCL18, and CCL22.

Historically, macrophage polarization has been studied using gene expression profiling techniques, primarily microarray-based platforms. These approaches have provided valuable insights into transcriptional reprogramming during macrophage differentiation and activation, revealing distinct gene signatures associated with M1 and M2 states. However, microarray technology is inherently limited by its reliance on pre-defined probe sets, restricted dynamic range, background noise, and inability to detect novel transcripts, splice variants, or non-annotated genomic regions. Consequently, many aspects of macrophage biology—particularly those involving low-abundance transcripts, alternative splicing events, or previously uncharacterized genes—remain poorly understood. Moreover, the identification of robust and specific cell surface markers to reliably distinguish human M1-like from M2-like macrophages in clinical or research settings has been hampered by the incomplete resolution of traditional transcriptomic methods. While certain markers such as CD64 and CD23 have been used to infer polarization status, their expression can be context-dependent, overlapping, or insufficiently discriminatory, especially in heterogeneous tissue environments.

The advent of RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) has revolutionized transcriptome analysis by enabling comprehensive, unbiased, and quantitative assessment of the entire transcriptome at single-nucleotide resolution. Unlike microarrays, RNA-seq does not require prior knowledge of gene sequences, offers a vastly expanded dynamic range, detects low-abundance transcripts with high sensitivity, and accurately identifies alternative splicing isoforms, fusion transcripts, and novel gene structures. Despite these advantages, the application of RNA-seq to systematically dissect human macrophage polarization programs—and to leverage this information for the discovery of novel diagnostic or therapeutic biomarkers—has remained underexplored. Prior studies have largely relied on microarray data or focused on murine models, limiting the translational relevance to human disease contexts such as chronic inflammation, autoimmunity, cancer, and transplant rejection, where macrophage polarization plays a decisive role.

Furthermore, integrative analyses combining transcriptomic data with biological network modeling have suggested that macrophage polarization is governed not merely by individual marker genes but by coordinated regulatory networks involving transcription factors, signaling molecules, and effector proteins. However, the fidelity and completeness of these networks depend critically on the quality and depth of the underlying transcriptomic data. Microarray-based networks may miss key regulatory nodes due to undetected transcripts, thereby yielding incomplete or misleading biological interpretations. There exists a pressing need for a high-resolution, RNA-seq–driven characterization of human macrophage polarization that not only validates known markers but also uncovers novel differentially expressed genes, particularly those encoding cell surface proteins amenable to flow cytometric detection or therapeutic targeting. Such a resource would significantly advance both basic immunology and clinical applications, including the development of precision diagnostics for immune-mediated diseases and the design of macrophage-modulating therapies.

In summary, while the conceptual framework of M1/M2 macrophage polarization is well established, the molecular toolkit for accurately defining and distinguishing these states in humans remains inadequate. The limitations of existing technologies have obscured critical aspects of macrophage transcriptional regulation, including isoform-specific expression, gene family dynamics, and surface marker diversity. This patent addresses these gaps by providing a comprehensive, high-fidelity RNA-seq–based transcriptome atlas of human M1-like and M2-like macrophages, leading to the identification of novel, validated cell surface markers and regulatory mechanisms that were previously inaccessible through conventional methods. The invention thus establishes a new foundation for macrophage phenotyping, functional studies, and clinical translation.

## SHORT DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and system for the high-resolution characterization and discrimination of human classically activated (M1-like) and alternatively activated (M2-like) macrophages based on RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)–derived transcriptomic data. Unlike conventional microarray-based approaches, the invention leverages the superior sensitivity, dynamic range, and exon-level resolution of RNA-seq to identify novel differentially expressed genes, including previously undetected cell surface markers, alternative splicing variants, and members of gene families such as apolipoprotein L (APOL) and leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptors (LILRs). The invention further discloses specific combinations of cell surface markers—such as CD120b, TLR2, and SLAMF7 for M1-like macrophages, and CD1a, CD1b, CD93, and CD226 for M2-like macrophages—that enable robust and reliable distinction between macrophage polarization states in human samples. Additionally, the invention includes methods for detecting differential expression of splice variants, such as those of PDLIM7, which are uniquely resolvable by RNA-seq and correlate with functional polarization states. The disclosed markers and analytical frameworks are validated through orthogonal techniques including flow cytometry, quantitative PCR, and immunoblotting, confirming their utility in both research and clinical settings. The invention thus provides a transformative toolkit for macrophage phenotyping, offering enhanced accuracy over existing methods and enabling new applications in immunodiagnostics, drug development, and personalized medicine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is directed to a comprehensive methodology for the precise molecular characterization of human macrophage polarization states, specifically the classically activated (M1-like) and alternatively activated (M2-like) phenotypes, through the application of high-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) coupled with integrative bioinformatic and experimental validation. Central to the invention is the recognition that RNA-seq provides a substantially richer and more accurate representation of the macrophage transcriptome than microarray-based technologies, enabling the discovery of novel biomarkers, regulatory mechanisms, and functional distinctions that were previously obscured by technical limitations. The invention encompasses the identification of a set of differentially expressed genes, with particular emphasis on cell surface molecules, whose expression profiles are uniquely and reproducibly associated with either M1-like or M2-like macrophages. These markers are not only transcriptionally regulated in a polarization-specific manner but are also functionally expressed on the cell surface, rendering them suitable for detection by flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry, or other protein-based assays. The invention further extends to the detection of alternative splicing events and isoform switching in key regulatory genes, which represent a previously underappreciated layer of transcriptional control in macrophage polarization.

The methodology begins with the in vitro generation of human macrophages from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolated from healthy donors. CD14+ monocytes are purified using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) and differentiated into immature macrophages using granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) over a period of three days. This choice of GM-CSF over macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) is deliberate, as it yields macrophages that are more responsive to classical polarization signals and exhibit clearer distinctions in marker expression between M1 and M2 states. Following differentiation, macrophages are polarized for an additional three days under defined conditions: M1-like polarization is induced by treatment with interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), while M2-like polarization is induced by interleukin-4 (IL-4). Alternative stimuli such as LPS, TNF-α, or IL-13 may also be used, but IFN-γ and IL-4 serve as the canonical and most reproducible inducers for the respective phenotypes.

Total RNA is then extracted from the polarized macrophages and subjected to RNA-seq using the Illumina TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit. The sequencing protocol includes poly-A selection, fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, adapter ligation, and size selection to generate libraries enriched for mRNA transcripts. Paired-end 100 bp reads are generated on an Illumina HiScanSQ platform, yielding an average of 15–20 million high-quality reads per sample. The raw sequencing data are processed through a rigorous bioinformatic pipeline: reads are aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using TopHat and Bowtie, transcript abundance is quantified as reads per kilobase of exon model per million mapped reads (RPKM) using Cufflinks, and differential expression analysis is performed with Cuffdiff, applying stringent statistical thresholds (fold change ≥1.5, p-value <0.05, RPKM ≥0.3). This approach enables the detection of not only differentially expressed genes but also alternative transcription start sites, promoter usage, and coding sequence (CDS) variants.

Concurrently, a subset of samples is analyzed using microarray technology (Illumina Human HT-12 v3 BeadChips) to enable direct comparison of the two platforms. The RNA-seq data demonstrate a significantly higher dynamic range—spanning six orders of magnitude in fold-change differences compared to four for microarrays—and identify approximately twice as many differentially expressed genes, particularly those with high fold-changes (FC >4). Notably, RNA-seq reveals numerous transcripts that are entirely undetected by microarrays, including members of the APOL and LILR gene families, which are strongly upregulated in M1-like macrophages. These findings are validated at the protein level using flow cytometry for LILRB1 and western blotting for APOL3, confirming their polarization-specific expression.

A critical aspect of the invention is the systematic interrogation of the human surfaceome—the complete repertoire of cell surface proteins—to identify novel markers for macrophage polarization. By filtering the RNA-seq dataset for genes encoding transmembrane or GPI-anchored proteins, the invention identifies 475 surface molecules that are differentially expressed between M1-like and M2-like macrophages. Among these, several exhibit highly selective and robust expression patterns. For M1-like macrophages, CD120b (TNFR2), Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), and Signaling Lymphocytic Activation Molecule Family member 7 (SLAMF7) are consistently upregulated across donors and independent of the initial differentiation cytokine (GM-CSF or M-CSF). Flow cytometry confirms strong surface expression of these markers exclusively in IFN-γ–polarized cells. Conversely, M2-like macrophages show significant enrichment of CD1a, CD1b, CD93, and CD226. CD1a and CD1b, traditionally associated with dendritic cell antigen presentation, are shown here for the first time to be markedly upregulated in IL-4–polarized macrophages, suggesting a potential role in lipid antigen presentation within M2 contexts. CD93, involved in adhesion and phagocytosis, and CD226, a receptor implicated in cell migration and immune synapse formation, are similarly validated as M2-selective markers by flow cytometry.

The invention further discloses the detection of alternative splicing events that are polarization-state dependent. Using Cufflinks/Cuffdiff, the analysis identifies 20 genes with differential coding sequence usage between M1 and M2 macrophages. One exemplary gene is PDLIM7, which encodes an intracellular scaffold protein with roles in cytoskeletal organization and transcriptional regulation. RNA-seq reveals that M1-like macrophages predominantly express the PDLIM7 v1 isoform, while M2-like macrophages favor PDLIM7 v2. This isoform switch is confirmed by isoform-specific quantitative PCR and is not detectable by microarray analysis, highlighting the unique capability of RNA-seq to resolve post-transcriptional regulation in macrophage biology.

The disclosed markers and analytical methods are not limited to in vitro systems but are applicable to ex vivo macrophage populations isolated from human tissues, including tumor-associated macrophages, alveolar macrophages, or synovial macrophages in rheumatoid arthritis. The invention thus provides a universal framework for macrophage phenotyping that surpasses the limitations of traditional markers such as CD64 and CD23, which often show incomplete specificity or variable expression. By combining multiple novel markers into multi-parameter panels, the invention enables high-confidence classification of macrophage polarization states, facilitating more accurate correlation with disease outcomes, therapeutic responses, or functional assays. The invention also supports the development of diagnostic kits, antibody-based therapeutics, and cell sorting protocols tailored to specific macrophage subsets, thereby advancing both research and clinical applications in immunology and beyond.

### EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, human macrophages were generated from CD14+ monocytes isolated from the peripheral blood of healthy donors using MACS purification. Monocytes were cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) and differentiated with GM-CSF (500 U/ml) for three days. On day 3, the medium was replaced, and cells were polarized for an additional three days with either IFN-γ (200 U/ml) to induce an M1-like phenotype or IL-4 (1,000 U/ml) to induce an M2-like phenotype. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a panel of monoclonal antibodies against known macrophage markers. Results showed that both M1- and M2-like macrophages expressed high levels of CD11b and CD14, with slightly higher mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) for CD11b in M2-like cells and higher CD14 expression in M1-like cells. Classical markers CD68 and HLA-DR were uniformly expressed across all conditions, confirming successful macrophage differentiation. Critically, CD64 (FcγRI) was strongly upregulated only in M1-like macrophages, while CD23 (FcεRII) was selectively induced in M2-like macrophages, with significantly higher expression in GM-CSF–differentiated cells compared to M-CSF–differentiated counterparts. These findings validated the model system and established GM-CSF as the preferred differentiation cytokine for subsequent high-resolution transcriptome analysis.

### Example 2

Microarray-based gene expression profiling was conducted on unpolarized (M0), M1-like, and M2-like macrophages derived from seven healthy donors. Total RNA was converted to biotin-labeled cRNA and hybridized to Illumina Human HT-12 v3 BeadChips. Data were processed using the Bioconductor “limma” package with quantile normalization and filtering for genes with a coefficient of variation ≥0.5. Principal component analysis (PCA) and hierarchical clustering demonstrated clear segregation of samples by polarization state. Differential expression analysis (fold change ≥2.0, p<0.05 with Benjamini-Hochberg correction) identified 757 M1-specific and 436 M2-specific genes. Heatmap visualization confirmed expected upregulation of M1 markers (e.g., CXCL10, IL1B, GBP1, CD86, FCGR1A/B) and M2 markers (e.g., CCL22, CCL18, FCER2, F13A1). A priori knowledge-based network analysis using EGAN software revealed mutually exclusive expression patterns between M1 and M2 networks, with minimal overlap except for cell cycle-related genes. This dataset served as a benchmark for comparison with RNA-seq data, highlighting the limitations of microarray technology in detecting low-abundance or novel transcripts.

### Example 3

RNA-seq was performed on M1-like and M2-like macrophages from three independent donors using the Illumina TruSeq protocol. Libraries were sequenced to generate 100 bp paired-end reads, yielding an average of 15.0 ± 2.8 million reads for M1 and 20.4 ± 9.2 million for M2 samples. Reads were aligned to hg19 using TopHat, and transcript abundance was quantified as RPKM. Cross-platform comparison with microarray data showed a high global correlation (R² >0.9) but revealed that RNA-seq detected 11,317–11,466 expressed genes (RPKM ≥0.3), compared to 10,155–10,418 by microarray. More importantly, RNA-seq identified 1,736 genes upregulated in M1 vs. M2 (FC ≥1.5, p<0.05), versus only 834 by microarray. For M2 vs. M1, RNA-seq detected 822 genes versus 786 by microarray. Venn diagram analysis showed that RNA-seq uncovered 900 additional differentially expressed genes not detected by microarray, including DUOX1, DUOX2, TBX21, and GBP7. These findings underscored the superior sensitivity and dynamic range of RNA-seq in capturing the full complexity of macrophage polarization.

### Example 4

Exon-level resolution of RNA-seq data was used to analyze known macrophage markers. For CD68, RNA-seq showed uniform high expression across all exons in both M1 and M2 macrophages, with minimal inter-donor variability, consistent with flow cytometry and qPCR data. For CD64 (FCGR1A/B), RNA-seq revealed complete absence of all exonic reads in M2-like macrophages and high expression in M1-like cells, corroborating protein-level data. Similarly, CD23 (FCER2) showed strong exon coverage in M2 and low coverage in M1, matching flow cytometry results. This exon-resolution analysis confirmed the accuracy of RNA-seq and demonstrated its ability to validate known markers while providing deeper insight into transcript structure, including the detection of unannotated exons or splice junctions not represented on microarrays.

### Example 5

Network-based analysis was enhanced by RNA-seq data. An M1-associated network was constructed using genes with FC >4 in RNA-seq data, revealing dense clusters of immunomodulatory proteins, including the entire apolipoprotein L (APOL1–6) family and multiple leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptors (LILRA1–6, LILRB1–5). When this RNA-seq–derived network topology was overlaid with microarray expression values, only 73% of the nodes were detected, and the APOL and LILR clusters were entirely missing. Similarly, an M2 network built from RNA-seq data (FC >2.5) included central hubs such as MYC and TP53, which were absent in the microarray-based network, resulting in only 54% coverage. Protein validation confirmed high expression of APOL3 (by western blot) and LILRB1 (by flow cytometry) in M1-like macrophages, establishing these as novel M1 markers. This example demonstrated that RNA-seq not only increases the number of detected genes but also enriches biological network content, uncovering previously hidden regulatory modules.

### Example 6

Alternative splicing analysis was performed using Cufflinks/Cuffdiff on RNA-seq data. The pipeline identified 20 genes with differential coding sequence (CDS) usage between M1 and M2 macrophages. One notable example was PDLIM7, which encodes a PDZ and LIM domain-containing scaffold protein. RNA-seq coverage plots showed that while total PDLIM7 expression was slightly elevated in M1, the isoform composition differed dramatically: M1-like macrophages predominantly expressed the v1 isoform, whereas M2-like cells favored v2. Isoform-specific qPCR assays confirmed this switch, with v1 showing 3.2-fold higher expression in M1 and v2 showing 2.8-fold higher in M2 (p<0.01). Microarray probes, which targeted common exons, failed to detect this isoform-specific regulation, illustrating a key advantage of RNA-seq in resolving post-transcriptional mechanisms that may influence macrophage function through altered protein interactions or localization.

### Example 7

The human surfaceome was interrogated to identify novel cell surface markers. From the RNA-seq dataset, 475 surface-encoding genes were found differentially expressed (FC ≥1.5, p<0.05). Flow cytometry validation was performed on top candidates. M1-like macrophages showed strong surface expression of CD120b (TNFR2), TLR2, and SLAMF7, with MFI increases of 4.1-, 3.7-, and 5.2-fold over M2, respectively (p<0.001). These markers were consistently upregulated regardless of whether monocytes were initially differentiated with GM-CSF or M-CSF. In contrast, M2-like macrophages exhibited significant upregulation of CD1a, CD1b, CD93, and CD226, with MFI increases of 6.3-, 5.8-, 4.5-, and 3.9-fold over M1, respectively (p<0.001). Notably, CD1a and CD1b, typically associated with dendritic cells, were robustly induced only in M2-polarized macrophages, suggesting a previously unrecognized role in alternative activation. These markers were combined into multi-parameter flow cytometry panels, enabling high-confidence discrimination of M1 vs. M2 states with >95% accuracy in blinded samples.

### Example 8

The clinical applicability of the novel markers was demonstrated in a pilot study of macrophages isolated from human intestinal allograft biopsies. Tissue macrophages from patients experiencing acute rejection showed high expression of SLAMF7 by immunohistochemistry, consistent with an M1-like phenotype. In contrast, macrophages from stable grafts exhibited elevated CD93 and CD226, indicative of an M2-like state. These findings align with the in vitro data and suggest that the disclosed markers can be used to assess macrophage polarization in disease contexts. Furthermore, soluble CD93 levels in serum correlated with M2 macrophage infiltration, supporting its potential as a non-invasive biomarker. This example illustrates how the invention bridges in vitro discovery with clinical translation, providing tools for monitoring immune responses in transplantation, autoimmunity, and cancer.
