# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of photonic integrated circuits (PICs), and more specifically to a novel electro-optic modulator architecture implemented on a heterogeneous silicon–lithium niobate (Si-LN) platform. The invention is directed toward enabling ultra-compact, high-efficiency, and large-bandwidth optical modulation suitable for integration in complex photonic systems, including but not limited to microwave photonics, optical neural networks, and high-speed optical communication systems. The invention leverages the complementary properties of silicon (Si)—notably its high refractive index and compatibility with mature CMOS fabrication processes—and lithium niobate (LN)—particularly its strong linear electro-optic (Pockels) effect—to achieve unprecedented performance in both routing and modulation functionalities within a monolithic or heterogeneously integrated photonic circuit. The technical domain encompasses integrated optics, electro-optic device design, waveguide engineering, mode transition structures, traveling-wave electrode design, and heterogeneous material integration on wafer-scale substrates. The invention specifically addresses the long-standing trade-off between compactness, modulation efficiency, bandwidth, and crosstalk suppression in densely integrated photonic systems by introducing spatially differentiated waveguide geometries that optimize light confinement for either low-loss, tight-radius routing or high-efficiency, broadband electro-optic phase modulation, depending on the functional segment of the device. This approach enables the realization of Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI)-based modulators with sub-millimeter interaction lengths, half-wave voltage-length products (VπL) as low as 1.76 V·cm, modulation bandwidths exceeding 350 GHz, bend radii as small as 10 μm, and edge-to-edge waveguide separations down to 0.7 μm with beat lengths exceeding 1 cm—performance metrics that collectively represent a significant advancement over prior art in thin-film LN, silicon-on-insulator (SOI), and hybrid LN-SOI or SOI-LN platforms. The invention further provides a robust fabrication pathway that avoids direct etching or bonding of fragile thin-film LN, instead relying on patterning and etching of the silicon layer atop a pre-bonded Si-LN wafer, thereby enhancing process yield, scalability, and compatibility with existing semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure.

## BACKGROUND ART

The development of high-performance photonic integrated circuits (PICs) for applications such as microwave photonics, optical computing, and next-generation telecommunications has been critically constrained by the limitations of available electro-optic modulation technologies. Historically, silicon-based modulators have dominated integrated photonics due to their compatibility with CMOS fabrication. However, silicon lacks a linear electro-optic (Pockels) effect, relying instead on plasma dispersion effects in p-n or p-i-n junctions for modulation. This mechanism inherently suffers from low modulation efficiency, high optical loss, limited bandwidth (typically below 50 GHz), and nonlinear response characteristics, which are unsuitable for analog or high-fidelity digital applications. In contrast, lithium niobate (LN) has long been the material of choice for high-performance discrete modulators owing to its large Pockels coefficients, particularly r₃₃ ≈ 30.8 pm/V, which enables efficient, linear, and broadband phase modulation. Nevertheless, traditional bulk LN modulators exhibit weak optical mode confinement due to the modest refractive index contrast between LN (n ≈ 2.2) and surrounding materials like air or silica, resulting in large device footprints (centimeter-scale interaction lengths) and poor integrability with other photonic components.

The advent of thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) technology, enabled by ion-slicing and wafer bonding techniques, has revitalized interest in LN-based integrated photonics by allowing the creation of high-confinement waveguides through etching of sub-micron-thick LN layers. TFLN modulators have demonstrated remarkable improvements in modulation efficiency (VπL as low as 2 V·cm) and bandwidth (exceeding 100 GHz). However, even in TFLN platforms, achieving simultaneously high modulation efficiency, ultra-broad bandwidth, and ultra-compact routing remains challenging. Pure LN ridge waveguides, while offering strong electro-optic interaction, still require relatively large bend radii (typically >100 μm) due to radiation losses at tight bends, limiting integration density. Hybrid approaches have emerged to address this, including LN-on-SOI (LN-SOI) and SOI-on-LN (SOI-LN) configurations. In LN-SOI schemes, LN is bonded onto pre-patterned SOI wafers, and the modulator operates in a strip-loaded configuration where the optical mode is partially confined in the underlying silicon. This approach has achieved bandwidths up to 106 GHz and VπL of 6.7 V·cm, but it requires complex multi-step bonding of LN pieces, which compromises yield and scalability. Conversely, SOI-LN configurations involve fabricating silicon or silicon nitride (SiNₓ) waveguides directly on top of a continuous TFLN wafer, avoiding LN etching and enabling full-wafer processing. While this improves manufacturability, SiNₓ-LN modulators reported to date exhibit relatively large bend radii (e.g., 300 μm) and modest VπL (~2.1 V·cm), again limiting integration density and efficiency.

A fundamental challenge in all these architectures is the inherent conflict between the waveguide requirements for routing versus modulation. Efficient routing demands strong optical confinement in a high-index core (e.g., silicon) to enable small bend radii and tight waveguide spacing with minimal crosstalk. In contrast, efficient electro-optic modulation requires significant overlap between the optical mode and the electro-optic material (LN) to maximize the Pockels effect, which often necessitates weaker confinement and larger mode areas. Prior art attempts to reconcile this trade-off have typically employed uniform waveguide cross-sections throughout the device, forcing a compromise that degrades either routing compactness or modulation performance. Additionally, achieving velocity matching between the optical and microwave signals in traveling-wave modulators—a critical factor for bandwidth extension—requires careful co-design of the optical waveguide and the microwave electrode structure, which is further complicated by the differing material properties of Si and LN. Existing solutions often fail to simultaneously optimize the optical group index, microwave effective index, impedance matching, and microwave loss, thereby limiting achievable bandwidth. Moreover, mode transitions between different waveguide sections, if required, are often lossy or difficult to fabricate, especially when involving fragile TFLN layers. Thus, there exists a clear and unmet need in the art for an electro-optic modulator architecture that decouples the routing and modulation functions, enabling each to be independently optimized within a single, manufacturable device on a scalable platform, while delivering record-breaking performance in terms of compactness, efficiency, and bandwidth.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel electro-optic modulator and associated photonic integrated circuit architecture that overcomes the limitations of prior art by implementing spatially differentiated waveguide geometries on a heterogeneous silicon–lithium niobate (Si-LN) platform. Specifically, the invention introduces a Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI) modulator comprising two distinct types of waveguides: ultra-compact light-routing waveguides and high-performance light-modulating waveguides, both fabricated on a monolithic Si-LN wafer without the need for etching or bonding of the thin-film lithium niobate layer. The light-routing waveguides are designed with a relatively large silicon cross-section (e.g., 600 nm wide by 220 nm tall) to maximize optical confinement within the silicon layer, thereby enabling extremely small bend radii (as low as 10 μm) and tight edge-to-edge waveguide separations (as small as 0.7 μm) with negligible crosstalk, as evidenced by beat lengths exceeding 1 cm. In contrast, the light-modulating waveguides feature a reduced silicon cross-section (e.g., 480 nm wide by 90 nm tall), which shifts the majority of the optical mode into the underlying lithium niobate layer, thereby maximizing the overlap with the applied electric field and leveraging the strong Pockels effect of LN for highly efficient phase modulation. Critically, the invention employs a specially designed bilevel taper structure to provide low-loss mode transition between the routing and modulating waveguides, ensuring efficient coupling of the optical mode between the silicon-dominated and LN-dominated regions. This taper is fabricated solely through silicon etching, preserving the integrity of the thin-film LN and maintaining compatibility with standard CMOS processes.

The modulator further incorporates a coplanar waveguide (CPW) electrode structure in a ground-signal-ground (GSG) configuration, with the signal electrode positioned between the two arms of the MZI and ground electrodes adjacent to each arm. The dimensions of the electrodes—specifically a signal electrode width of 7 μm and a thickness of 400 nm, with a 4.5 μm gap between signal and ground—are optimized to achieve simultaneous velocity matching (between the microwave and optical group indices) and impedance matching (to 50 Ω), thereby enabling ultra-broad modulation bandwidths exceeding 350 GHz for short interaction lengths (e.g., 5 mm). The invention achieves a half-wave voltage-length product (VπL) of 1.76 V·cm, representing a significant improvement in modulation efficiency over comparable hybrid platforms. The entire device is fabricated on a wafer-scale Si-LN substrate, obtained via wafer bonding, which eliminates the need for piecewise LN bonding and enhances process robustness and scalability. The thickness of the lithium niobate layer is a critical parameter, with 700 nm identified as optimal for balancing modulation efficiency, bandwidth, and crosstalk performance. The invention thus provides a comprehensive solution that decouples the conflicting requirements of routing and modulation, enabling the realization of ultra-compact, high-efficiency, and large-bandwidth photonic integrated circuits for advanced applications in microwave photonics, optical neural networks, and beyond.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a heterogeneously integrated electro-optic modulator fabricated on a silicon–lithium niobate (Si-LN) platform, wherein the device architecture is specifically engineered to optimize distinct segments of the optical path for either ultra-compact light routing or high-efficiency electro-optic modulation. The foundational substrate for the invention is a monocrystalline Si-LN wafer, which is preferably fabricated by wafer bonding a silicon layer onto a thin-film lithium niobate (TFLN) layer that has been transferred onto a handle substrate, such as silicon dioxide or silicon. The TFLN layer, which serves as the active electro-optic medium, is oriented such that its crystal axes are aligned to maximize the Pockels effect; in the preferred embodiment, an x-cut LN wafer is utilized, wherein the extraordinary axis (z-axis) of the LN crystal is perpendicular to the wafer surface, enabling the application of an in-plane electric field to efficiently modulate the refractive index via the dominant r₃₃ electro-optic coefficient. The thickness of the TFLN layer is a critical design parameter, and experimental and simulation results indicate that a thickness of approximately 700 nm provides an optimal balance between optical mode confinement in LN for modulation, bend loss performance in the routing sections, and microwave-optical velocity matching for bandwidth enhancement. Thicker LN layers increase the optical mode overlap with the electro-optic material, thereby reducing the half-wave voltage-length product (VπL), but may exacerbate crosstalk in parallel waveguide sections and complicate velocity matching. Thinner LN layers improve routing compactness but reduce modulation efficiency. The 700 nm thickness has been found to minimize the index mismatch between the optical group index and the microwave effective index, a key factor in achieving bandwidths exceeding 350 GHz.

The modulator is configured as a Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI), which comprises an input splitter, two phase-shifting arms, and an output combiner. The input and output splitters/combiners are implemented using multimode interference (MMI) couplers, which offer advantages in terms of fabrication tolerance, broad bandwidth, and low loss compared to directional couplers. In the preferred embodiment, each MMI is designed with a width of 2.7 μm and a length of 6.6 μm, with input and output tapers of 10 μm length and 1 μm width to adiabatically couple light from the single-mode routing waveguides into the multimode region. The edge-to-edge separation between the output waveguides of the MMI is set to 0.7 μm to suppress unwanted evanescent coupling between the arms, ensuring high extinction ratio in the MZI. The simulated insertion loss of the MMI is less than 0.06 dB, demonstrating its high efficiency. Light is coupled into and out of the chip using silicon grating couplers, which are optimized for the 220 nm thick silicon layer used in the routing sections and are compatible with standard fiber coupling techniques.

The core innovation of the invention lies in the differentiation of the waveguide cross-sections along the optical path. The light-routing waveguides, which constitute the majority of the optical path—including the input/output leads, the bends connecting the MMIs to the phase shifters, and any interconnects in a larger PIC—are formed by patterning the silicon layer into strips of 600 nm width and 220 nm height. This geometry ensures that the transverse-electric (TE) polarized optical mode at the operating wavelength of 1550 nm is predominantly confined within the silicon layer, with approximately 76% of the mode energy residing in silicon. This high degree of confinement yields a high effective refractive index (n_eff ≈ 2.6) and a small mode area (≈0.16 μm²), which are the key factors enabling the waveguide to support extremely tight bends. Simulations confirm that a bend radius of 10 μm can be achieved with a bend loss of only 0.01 dB per 90-degree bend, a performance comparable to that of standard silicon-on-insulator (SOI) waveguides and significantly superior to prior art LN-based or hybrid waveguides, which typically require bend radii of 100 μm or more. Furthermore, the strong confinement allows for dense integration of parallel waveguides. When two such routing waveguides are placed with an edge-to-edge separation of 0.7 μm, the beat length—the distance over which power fully transfers from one waveguide to the other due to evanescent coupling—is calculated to be approximately 1 cm. This long beat length corresponds to very low crosstalk (<−30 dB over typical PIC lengths), making the routing waveguides suitable for complex, large-scale photonic circuits.

In stark contrast, the light-modulating waveguides, which form the phase-shifting arms of the MZI, are fabricated with a reduced silicon cross-section of 480 nm width and 90 nm height. This reduction in silicon volume deliberately weakens the optical confinement in silicon, causing the majority of the TE mode (approximately 83%) to reside in the underlying lithium niobate layer. This maximizes the overlap integral between the optical mode and the electric field generated by the electrodes, thereby enhancing the electro-optic interaction. The effective refractive index of the modulating waveguide is lower (n_eff ≈ 2.02) and the mode area is larger (≈0.76 μm²) compared to the routing waveguide, which is a necessary trade-off to achieve high modulation efficiency. The modulation efficiency is quantified by the half-wave voltage-length product (VπL), which is determined by the change in effective refractive index (Δn_eff) induced by an applied voltage. Using the Pockels effect tensor for x-cut LN and simulating the DC electric field distribution for a 1 V bias applied across the electrodes, the Δn_eff is found to vary linearly with voltage, yielding a VπL of 1.76 V·cm for an interaction length of 5 mm. This represents a significant improvement over previously reported SOI-LN modulators (VπL ≈ 2.1 V·cm) and approaches the performance of pure TFLN modulators, but with the added benefit of ultra-compact routing.

To seamlessly connect the routing and modulating waveguides, the invention employs a bilevel taper structure. This taper is formed entirely by etching the silicon layer, without any modification to the LN layer, ensuring process compatibility and robustness. The taper consists of two overlapping etched regions: a lower slab region with a constant height of 90 nm that tapers in width from 600 nm to 480 nm over a length of 44.2 μm, and an upper rib region with a height of 130 nm that tapers in width from 600 nm to a narrow tip of 120 nm over a longer length of 102.2 μm. The longitudinal offset between the start of the lower and upper tapers is 12 μm. This dual-stage design facilitates an adiabatic transformation of the optical mode from a silicon-dominated profile to an LN-dominated profile. Three-dimensional finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations demonstrate that this bilevel taper achieves a coupling efficiency of 98.6%, with the primary loss mechanism being mode mismatch at the tip of the upper taper. The taper is tolerant to fabrication variations; simulations show that etch depths ranging from 70 nm to 110 nm and tip widths up to 200 nm result in negligible degradation of coupling efficiency, providing a practical margin for manufacturing.

The electrical drive for the modulator is provided by a coplanar waveguide (CPW) electrode structure arranged in a ground-signal-ground (GSG) configuration. The signal electrode is positioned centrally between the two modulating waveguides of the MZI, while the ground electrodes are placed adjacent to the outer sides of each modulating waveguide. The gap between the signal and ground electrodes is set to 4.5 μm, a dimension that balances the need for a strong electric field in the LN layer against the constraints of microwave loss and impedance. The electrodes are fabricated from gold with a thickness of 400 nm and a signal electrode width of 7 μm. These dimensions are the result of a multi-objective optimization performed using electromagnetic simulation tools (e.g., ANSYS HFSS) to simultaneously achieve velocity matching and impedance matching. Velocity matching requires that the microwave effective index (n_m) closely matches the optical group index (n_g ≈ 2.41 at 1550 nm). Simulations show that with the chosen electrode geometry, n_m ranges from 2.4 to 2.75 across the frequency band of interest, minimizing the index mismatch. Impedance matching to the standard 50 Ω system is also achieved, with the characteristic impedance (Z₀) of the CPW varying between 50 Ω and 57 Ω. The microwave loss (α_m) is maintained at acceptable levels, ranging from 5 dB/cm to 25 dB/cm depending on frequency. The electro-optic frequency response, modeled using established transmission line theory, confirms that the modulation bandwidth is primarily limited by the velocity mismatch and microwave loss. For an interaction length of 5 mm, the 3-dB bandwidth exceeds 350 GHz, while for longer lengths such as 8 mm and 11 mm, the bandwidths are 160 GHz and 75 GHz, respectively, with corresponding half-wave voltages of approximately 2.8 V and 1.6 V. This demonstrates the ability to tailor the device for either extreme bandwidth or low drive voltage by adjusting the interaction length.

The fabrication process for the invention is highly compatible with existing semiconductor manufacturing. It begins with a commercially available Si-LN wafer, eliminating the need for complex post-processing bonding of LN. The process sequence includes: (1) definition of the routing waveguides, modulating waveguides, and bilevel tapers using electron-beam or deep-UV lithography; (2) etching of the silicon layer to the required depths using reactive ion etching (RIE), with the bilevel taper requiring a two-step etch or a grayscale lithography process; (3) deposition and patterning of the gold electrode layer using lift-off or etching; and (4) optional cladding deposition for protection. Crucially, the LN layer remains untouched throughout the process, which enhances yield and reliability. The invention thus provides a practical, scalable, and high-performance solution for the next generation of photonic integrated circuits.
