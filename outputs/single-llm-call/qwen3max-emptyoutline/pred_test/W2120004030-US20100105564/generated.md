# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of molecular diagnostics and precision oncology, particularly relating to the identification, characterization, and clinical application of gene expression signatures derived from morphologically normal breast tissue components—specifically epithelium and stroma—in both healthy individuals and patients diagnosed with breast cancer. The invention provides novel nucleic acid compositions, polypeptide binding agents, microarray platforms, and diagnostic methods that leverage the intrinsic molecular profiles of normal breast tissue to improve the accuracy of cancer subtyping, prognosis prediction, and therapeutic decision-making. More specifically, the invention discloses a set of stroma-specific and epithelium-specific genes whose expression patterns are conserved across individuals regardless of the presence of adjacent malignancy, thereby establishing a robust baseline for distinguishing tumor-intrinsic alterations from microenvironmental or systemic confounders. This foundational insight enables the development of diagnostic tools that can more reliably stratify breast cancer patients into clinically relevant subgroups, including those with basal-like and normal-like tumors that exhibit poor clinical outcomes, by comparing tumor expression profiles against the newly defined normal tissue reference signatures. The invention further encompasses kits and assay systems for detecting these signature genes in clinical specimens, as well as computational frameworks for integrating such data into treatment selection algorithms. By anchoring molecular classification in the biology of the normal breast microenvironment, the invention addresses a critical gap in current prognostic methodologies, which often fail to account for the contribution of non-neoplastic tissue to bulk tumor expression profiles, thereby enhancing the specificity and predictive power of genomic-based diagnostics in breast oncology.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Breast cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide, despite significant advances in early detection and therapeutic intervention. A persistent clinical challenge is the inability of conventional prognostic indicators—including tumor size, lymph node status, histological grade, and hormone receptor expression—to reliably predict which patients with early-stage disease will develop metastatic recurrence. Approximately 26% of patients initially diagnosed with localized breast cancer ultimately succumb to metastatic disease, underscoring the inadequacy of existing risk stratification tools. In response, high-throughput genomic technologies, particularly DNA microarrays, have been employed to identify gene expression signatures that correlate with clinical outcomes. While several such signatures have demonstrated statistical associations with prognosis, their translation into routine clinical practice has been limited by issues of reproducibility, biological interpretability, and confounding contributions from non-tumor cell populations within biopsy specimens.

A fundamental limitation of many prior genomic studies is their reliance on whole-tissue sections, which inherently contain a heterogeneous mixture of malignant epithelial cells, stromal fibroblasts, immune infiltrates, endothelial cells, and adipocytes. This cellular heterogeneity obscures the true molecular characteristics of the tumor epithelium and may introduce spurious signals derived from reactive or altered microenvironmental components. Although laser capture microdissection (LCM) has enabled the isolation of pure epithelial populations from tumor specimens, few studies have systematically examined the gene expression profiles of the surrounding stromal compartment or, critically, of morphologically normal tissues adjacent to the tumor. Histologically “normal” tissue adjacent to carcinoma has long been suspected of harboring molecular abnormalities, including loss of heterozygosity and epigenetic alterations, that may reflect field cancerization or early neoplastic transformation. However, whether these changes manifest as detectable shifts in global gene expression—and whether such shifts correlate with clinical tumor characteristics—remains unresolved.

The normal human breast is composed of a ductal-lobular epithelial system embedded within a stromal matrix that constitutes over 80% of the organ’s volume. The stroma is not merely a passive scaffold but an active participant in mammary gland development, homeostasis, and disease. Emerging evidence suggests that stromal cells can influence epithelial proliferation, differentiation, and malignant transformation through paracrine signaling, extracellular matrix remodeling, and modulation of immune responses. Consequently, accurate molecular profiling of breast cancer must account for the distinct contributions of epithelial and stromal compartments. Yet, comprehensive gene expression atlases of normal human breast epithelium and stroma—particularly from individuals with and without cancer—are lacking. Existing datasets are often derived from cultured cells, which may not reflect the in vivo state, or from small cohorts that lack statistical power to discern subtle but biologically relevant patterns.

Prior attempts to define tissue-specific gene signatures have yielded inconsistent results. For example, a serial analysis of gene expression (SAGE) study of dispersed cells from a single reduction mammoplasty identified putative epithelial and stromal markers, but the limited sample size and ex vivo processing raise questions about generalizability. Similarly, fibroblast activation signatures, such as the core serum response (CSR) profile, have been associated with poor prognosis in multiple cancers, but it is unclear whether these signatures are active in the normal breast stroma of cancer patients or represent tumor-induced reprogramming. Moreover, while breast cancer subtypes—such as luminal A, luminal B, HER2-enriched, and basal-like—have been defined based on tumor epithelial gene expression, the relationship between these subtypes and the molecular state of the surrounding normal tissue remains poorly understood. Notably, basal-like tumors, which are typically triple-negative (ER−/PR−/HER2−) and associated with poor outcomes, share features with normal basal epithelial cells, yet the extent to which their expression profiles recapitulate normal epithelial programs has not been rigorously tested using matched normal tissue references.

The absence of a validated, high-resolution molecular map of normal breast tissue has hindered the development of truly tumor-intrinsic prognostic models. Without a clear baseline of what constitutes “normal” expression in both epithelium and stroma, it is difficult to distinguish cancer-specific alterations from inter-individual variation, technical artifacts, or microenvironmental responses. Furthermore, the potential for normal tissue gene expression to serve as a predictor of tumor behavior—either directly or through its influence on tumor evolution—has not been adequately explored. There exists a critical need for a comprehensive, patient-matched dataset of normal breast epithelium and stroma that can be used to: (1) establish definitive tissue-specific gene expression signatures; (2) determine whether these signatures are altered in the context of adjacent malignancy; (3) assess the utility of normal tissue profiles in refining breast cancer molecular classification; and (4) provide a foundation for the development of next-generation diagnostic assays that minimize confounding by non-neoplastic elements. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by providing the first large-scale, LCM-based gene expression analysis of morphologically normal breast tissue from both cancer patients and healthy controls, revealing unexpected homogeneity in normal tissue profiles and enabling novel applications in cancer diagnostics and treatment selection.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive molecular characterization of morphologically normal breast epithelium and stroma, demonstrating that these tissues exhibit highly conserved gene expression profiles irrespective of the presence of adjacent invasive ductal carcinoma. Through the integration of laser capture microdissection, whole-genome microarray analysis, and rigorous bioinformatic validation, the inventors have identified a robust set of stroma-specific and epithelium-specific genes that define a “normal tissue signature.” This signature serves as a foundational reference for distinguishing tumor-intrinsic molecular features from microenvironmental or systemic influences in breast cancer specimens. Critically, the invention reveals that gene expression patterns in normal epithelium and stroma from breast cancer patients are not statistically distinct from those in healthy individuals undergoing reduction mammoplasty and do not correlate with standard clinical tumor characteristics such as estrogen receptor status, progesterone receptor status, HER2 amplification, tumor grade, lymph node involvement, or recurrence. This finding establishes that the normal breast microenvironment, at the level of global transcriptomics, remains unperturbed by the presence of adjacent malignancy, thereby providing a stable baseline for molecular diagnostics.

The invention further demonstrates that the normal tissue signature can be applied to reclassify breast cancer subtypes with enhanced biological and clinical relevance. When used to analyze a large independent cohort of breast carcinomas, the normal epithelium-specific gene set identifies a distinct cluster of tumors that are uniformly triple-negative (ER−/PR−/HER2−) and enriched for basal-like and normal-like molecular features, including expression of keratin-5, keratin-17, and gamma-glutamyl hydrolase. This cluster is associated with significantly poorer clinical outcomes compared to other subtypes, confirming the prognostic utility of the normal epithelium signature. In contrast, stroma-specific genes do not resolve this cluster, indicating that the defining features of aggressive basal-like tumors are rooted in epithelial biology rather than stromal activation. Additionally, the normal stroma signature exhibits strong similarity to gene expression profiles of desmoid-type fibromatosis (DTF) and unstimulated fibroblasts, but not to activated wound-response or core serum response (CSR) signatures, clarifying the biological state of normal breast stroma and refuting hypotheses that it is pre-activated in cancer patients.

Based on these discoveries, the invention provides a suite of diagnostic and therapeutic tools. These include: (1) nucleic acid compositions comprising the identified stroma-specific and epithelium-specific genes; (2) oligonucleotide primers and probes for the detection and quantification of these genes in clinical samples; (3) microarray platforms and hybridization protocols optimized for the assessment of the normal tissue signature; (4) polypeptide binding compositions, such as antibodies against encoded proteins like c-kit (CD117) and CD31, for immunohistochemical validation; (5) methods for diagnosing breast cancer subtypes by comparing tumor expression profiles to the normal tissue signature; and (6) methods for assigning or selecting treatment regimens based on the degree of similarity between a patient’s tumor and the normal epithelium or stroma profiles. The invention thus enables more precise molecular stratification of breast cancer patients, facilitating the identification of high-risk individuals who may benefit from aggressive or targeted therapies, while sparing low-risk patients from unnecessary interventions. By anchoring diagnostic interpretation in the biology of the normal breast, the invention overcomes key limitations of prior art approaches and represents a significant advance in the field of precision oncology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Identifying Classes and Genes for Predicting Clinical Outcome

The present invention is predicated on the discovery that morphologically normal breast tissue—comprising both epithelial and stromal compartments—exhibits a remarkably consistent gene expression profile across individuals, irrespective of the presence of adjacent invasive ductal carcinoma. This consistency enables the definition of a “normal tissue signature” that serves as a reliable reference for interpreting tumor expression data and predicting clinical outcomes. To identify this signature, the inventors employed a multi-step analytical framework involving class discovery, class distinction, and class prediction, applied to a cohort of 44 patients, including 34 with invasive ductal carcinoma and 10 healthy donors undergoing reduction mammoplasty. Laser capture microdissection (LCM) was used to isolate pure populations of epithelial and stromal cells from histologically normal regions, ensuring that the resulting expression profiles were not contaminated by tumor cells or inflammatory infiltrates.

Class discovery was performed using hierarchical clustering and multidimensional scaling on whole-genome microarray data, revealing that samples clustered strictly according to tissue type (epithelium vs. stroma) rather than patient group (cancer-adjacent vs. reduction). This observation held true even when analyzing only the 34 cancer patients, indicating that the presence of a tumor does not induce a detectable shift in the global gene expression of adjacent normal tissue. To quantify the significance of these clusters, bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations) was performed using the pvclust package, confirming that the tissue-specific clustering was highly robust (approximately unbiased p-value > 95%). Subsequent class distinction analyses, using both linear models for microarray analysis (LIMMA) and significance analysis of microarrays (SAM), identified 883 genes that were differentially expressed between epithelium and stroma in at least 50% of patient-matched samples, with stringent statistical thresholds (false discovery rate-adjusted p-value < 1e-5, fold change ≥ 2.0, average intensity > 6.0). These genes constitute the core of the normal tissue signature.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the 883-gene signature revealed biologically coherent functional categories. Epithelium-specific genes were significantly overrepresented in processes related to cell-cell junctions, basal lamina organization, epithelial differentiation, and epidermal growth factor receptor activity, consistent with the known biology of ductal epithelial cells. Stroma-specific genes were enriched for extracellular matrix structural constituents, collagen binding, angiogenesis, and wound response pathways, reflecting the supportive and vascularized nature of the stromal compartment. Importantly, immunohistochemical validation using antibodies against c-kit (epithelium-specific) and CD31 (stroma-specific) confirmed the spatial localization predicted by the microarray data, demonstrating the technical and biological fidelity of the signature.

To assess the utility of the normal tissue signature in predicting clinical outcomes, the inventors applied class prediction algorithms, specifically prediction around medoids (PAM), to construct tissue-specific predictors for a range of clinical variables, including estrogen receptor (ER) status, progesterone receptor (PR) status, HER2 status, tumor grade, lymph node involvement, menopausal status, age, tumor size, and recurrence. Cross-validation was performed at every step of predictor construction, including the initial gene selection phase, to avoid overfitting. None of the resulting predictors achieved low prediction error; the average mean prediction error across all variables was approximately 50%, indicating that gene expression in normal tissue is not predictive of the clinical characteristics of the adjacent tumor. This finding was corroborated by principal component analysis (PCA), which showed that the primary sources of variation in the data were attributable to differences between tissue types (3.98% of total variation) and inter-individual variation (3.58%), with no significant association to clinical variables after Bonferroni correction.

However, when the normal tissue signature was applied to an independent dataset of 295 breast carcinomas, it successfully identified a clinically relevant subgroup of tumors. Hierarchical clustering using the 349 mapped genes from the normal signature resolved the cohort into two major clusters: a large cluster (257 samples) enriched for stroma-specific genes and a smaller cluster (38 samples) enriched for epithelium-specific genes. The latter cluster was uniformly triple-negative (ER−/PR−/HER2−) and expressed hallmark genes of basal-like and normal-like breast cancer subtypes, such as keratin-5, keratin-17, and gamma-glutamyl hydrolase. Survival analysis revealed that patients in this cluster had significantly worse outcomes (p = 0.000489), confirming the prognostic value of the normal epithelium signature. Notably, this cluster was not resolved when using only stroma-specific genes, underscoring the epithelial origin of the aggressive phenotype. Thus, while normal tissue gene expression itself does not predict tumor characteristics, it provides a powerful lens through which to interpret tumor expression data and identify high-risk subtypes.

### Cancers

The present invention is primarily directed toward the diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment of breast cancer, with specific emphasis on invasive ductal carcinoma, the most common histological subtype. However, the principles and compositions disclosed herein may also be applicable to other epithelial malignancies that arise in stroma-rich microenvironments, such as ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancers. The core insight—that normal tissue gene expression profiles are stable and unperturbed by adjacent malignancy—suggests that similar reference signatures could be developed for other organs to improve the accuracy of molecular diagnostics. In the context of breast cancer, the invention addresses a critical limitation of current genomic classifiers, which often conflate tumor-intrinsic signals with contributions from the surrounding stroma. By providing a clear delineation of epithelial versus stromal gene expression, the invention enables the deconvolution of bulk tumor profiles into their constituent components, thereby enhancing the specificity of subtype assignment.

The invention is particularly relevant for the identification and management of basal-like breast cancer, a subtype characterized by triple-negative receptor status (ER−/PR−/HER2−), high histological grade, and poor prognosis. Basal-like tumors are thought to originate from progenitor cells of the terminal ductal-lobular unit and share molecular features with normal basal epithelial cells. The present invention confirms this relationship by demonstrating that basal-like tumors are enriched for genes expressed in normal breast epithelium, as defined by the LCM-derived signature. This finding has important implications for therapy, as basal-like tumors do not respond to endocrine or HER2-targeted treatments and are often treated with chemotherapy alone. The ability to identify these tumors with high confidence using the normal epithelium signature could facilitate enrollment in clinical trials of novel agents, such as PARP inhibitors or immune checkpoint blockers, which show promise in triple-negative disease.

In addition to basal-like cancer, the invention also informs the classification of other breast cancer subtypes, including luminal A, luminal B, and HER2-enriched. The larger cluster of tumors identified by the normal tissue signature—enriched for stroma-specific genes—includes both ER-positive (luminal) and HER2-positive cases, suggesting that stromal activation is a common feature across multiple subtypes. However, the invention clarifies that this stromal signal reflects the normal stromal composition of the tumor rather than an activated wound-response program, as the normal stroma signature shows minimal overlap with core serum response (CSR) genes. This distinction is crucial because CSR activation has been linked to poor prognosis, whereas the presence of normal stroma may simply reflect tumor architecture. Thus, the invention helps to disentangle prognostic signals that are truly tumor-intrinsic from those that are artifactual or microenvironmental.

### Nucleic Acid Compositions

The present invention provides novel nucleic acid compositions comprising the genes identified as differentially expressed between normal breast epithelium and stroma. These compositions include both the full-length coding sequences and functional fragments thereof, such as exons, introns, promoters, enhancers, and untranslated regions (UTRs), that are useful for diagnostic or research purposes. The nucleic acid compositions are derived from the 883-gene normal tissue signature, which includes 321 epithelium-specific genes and 562 stroma-specific genes, as validated by LCM, microarray analysis, and immunohistochemistry. Representative epithelium-specific genes include KIT (encoding c-kit/CD117), EPCAM, KRT5, KRT17, and GGH, while representative stroma-specific genes include PECAM1 (encoding CD31), VWF, COL1A1, FN1, and ACTA2. These genes are listed in full in the accompanying sequence listing and are incorporated herein by reference.

The nucleic acid compositions may be provided in isolated or purified form, free from naturally occurring contaminants such as genomic DNA, RNA, or proteins. They may be synthesized chemically, amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), or cloned into vectors for recombinant expression. The compositions may also be labeled with detectable moieties, such as fluorescent dyes (e.g., Cy3, Cy5), radioactive isotopes, or enzymatic tags, to facilitate their use in hybridization assays, microarrays, or in situ hybridization. In one embodiment, the nucleic acid compositions are immobilized on a solid support, such as a glass slide, bead, or microfluidic chip, to create a diagnostic array for the simultaneous detection of multiple signature genes. In another embodiment, the compositions are formulated as probes for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) or digital droplet PCR (ddPCR), enabling sensitive and specific quantification of gene expression in clinical samples.

The invention also encompasses nucleic acid compositions that are complementary to the signature genes, such as antisense oligonucleotides, siRNAs, or shRNAs, which may be used for functional studies or therapeutic modulation of gene expression. Additionally, the compositions include allelic variants, splice variants, and orthologs of the signature genes that retain the same tissue-specific expression pattern. For example, a nucleic acid composition encoding a truncated isoform of KIT that is still preferentially expressed in epithelium would be considered within the scope of the invention. The compositions are designed to be compatible with a wide range of molecular biology platforms, ensuring their utility in both research and clinical settings.

### SDPP Genes and Nucleic Acids

The term “SDPP genes” as used herein refers to the subset of the normal tissue signature that encodes Secreted, Dispersed, Plasma membrane-associated, or Pericellular Proteins—collectively termed SDPP proteins—that are amenable to detection by polypeptide binding agents such as antibodies. These genes are of particular interest because their protein products are accessible for immunohistochemical or immunoassay-based detection in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) or frozen tissue sections, making them ideal candidates for clinical diagnostics. The SDPP gene list includes both epithelium-specific and stroma-specific members, such as KIT (epithelium), PECAM1 (stroma), VWF (stroma), EPCAM (epithelium), and CDH1 (epithelium).

The nucleic acids corresponding to SDPP genes are provided in isolated form and may be used to generate recombinant proteins for antibody production, to design hybridization probes, or to serve as standards in quantitative assays. The invention includes full-length cDNA sequences of SDPP genes, as well as truncated versions encoding specific domains (e.g., the extracellular domain of KIT) that are sufficient for antibody recognition. These nucleic acids may be operably linked to promoters and other regulatory elements in expression vectors for use in cell-based assays or protein production. In one embodiment, the SDPP nucleic acids are incorporated into multiplex PCR panels that allow for the simultaneous amplification and detection of multiple SDPP genes from a single RNA sample, streamlining the diagnostic workflow.

The SDPP genes are selected based on their high differential expression between epithelium and stroma, their localization to accessible cellular compartments, and their validation by immunohistochemistry. For example, KIT mRNA is highly expressed in normal epithelium but absent in stroma, and this pattern is mirrored at the protein level by c-kit immunostaining. Similarly, PECAM1 (CD31) is stroma-specific and localizes to endothelial cells, where it is readily detectable by IHC. The inclusion of such genes in the SDPP category ensures that the nucleic acid compositions disclosed herein are directly translatable into protein-based diagnostic tools, bridging the gap between genomic discovery and clinical application.

### Nucleic Acids, Primers and Probes

The present invention provides a comprehensive set of oligonucleotide primers and probes designed for the specific detection and quantification of the normal tissue signature genes. These reagents are optimized for use in a variety of nucleic acid amplification and detection platforms, including quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR), digital droplet PCR (ddPCR), in situ hybridization (ISH), and microarray hybridization. Each primer and probe set is designed to target a unique region of the corresponding gene, ensuring high specificity and minimal cross-reactivity with other transcripts. The design parameters include amplicon length (typically 70–150 bp for qRT-PCR), melting temperature (Tm ≈ 60°C for primers, 68–70°C for probes), GC content (40–60%), and avoidance of secondary structures or repetitive elements.

For qRT-PCR applications, the invention provides forward and reverse primers flanking a short amplicon, along with a TaqMan probe labeled with a fluorophore (e.g., FAM) and a quencher (e.g., TAMRA). The probe is designed to hybridize within the amplicon, allowing for real-time monitoring of amplification. In ddPCR, the same primer-probe sets are used, but the reaction is partitioned into thousands of nanoliter-sized droplets, enabling absolute quantification without the need for a standard curve. For ISH, the invention provides longer probes (50–100 nt) labeled with digoxigenin or fluorescein, which can be detected by chromogenic or fluorescent microscopy to visualize the spatial distribution of signature genes in tissue sections.

The primer and probe sequences are listed in full in the accompanying sequence listing and are selected to cover the entire spectrum of the normal tissue signature, with emphasis on SDPP genes for ease of validation. In addition to gene-specific reagents, the invention also provides control primers and probes for housekeeping genes (e.g., GAPDH, ACTB, HPRT1) to normalize for RNA input and quality. The reagents are provided in lyophilized or liquid form, optionally pre-aliquoted into plates or cartridges for high-throughput use. The invention further encompasses kits containing all necessary reagents for performing a complete diagnostic assay, including RNA extraction buffers, reverse transcription enzymes, PCR master mix, and positive/negative controls.

### Polypeptide Binding Compositions

The present invention provides polypeptide binding compositions, primarily antibodies and antigen-binding fragments thereof, that specifically recognize the protein products of the normal tissue signature genes. These compositions are essential for validating the microarray findings at the protein level and for developing immunohistochemical (IHC) or immunoassay-based diagnostic tests. The antibodies are raised against recombinant proteins, synthetic peptides, or native antigens corresponding to the SDPP genes, and are selected for high affinity, specificity, and compatibility with FFPE or frozen tissue sections.

Exemplary polypeptide binding compositions include monoclonal and polyclonal antibodies against c-kit (CD117), CD31 (PECAM1), von Willebrand factor (VWF), epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM), and E-cadherin (CDH1). The anti-c-kit antibody, for instance, is shown to stain normal breast epithelium but not stroma, confirming the epithelium-specific expression of KIT. Similarly, anti-CD31 and anti-VWF antibodies selectively label endothelial cells within the stromal compartment, validating the stroma-specificity of PECAM1 and VWF. These antibodies are conjugated to detectable labels such as horseradish peroxidase (HRP), alkaline phosphatase (AP), or fluorescent dyes (e.g., Alexa Fluor 488, Cy3) for use in IHC, immunofluorescence, or flow cytometry.

The invention also encompasses engineered antibody formats, such as single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), Fab fragments, and bispecific antibodies, which may offer improved tissue penetration or multiplexing capabilities. In addition to antibodies, the polypeptide binding compositions include non-immunoglobulin scaffolds, such as affibodies, DARPins, or aptamers, that bind to the same epitopes with comparable affinity. These compositions are provided in purified form, optionally formulated with stabilizers, preservatives, or blocking agents to enhance shelf life and performance. The invention further includes kits containing the polypeptide binding compositions, along with detection reagents (e.g., DAB substrate for HRP), mounting media, and protocol instructions for standardized IHC staining.

### Microarrays

The present invention provides specialized microarray platforms designed for the interrogation of the normal tissue signature in clinical and research samples. These microarrays consist of a solid support (e.g., glass slide) onto which nucleic acid probes corresponding to the 883-gene signature are immobilized in a spatially defined pattern. The probes are typically 60-mer oligonucleotides synthesized in situ using photolithographic or inkjet printing technology, ensuring high specificity and sensitivity. The microarray layout includes multiple replicate spots for each gene to assess technical variability, as well as control probes for housekeeping genes, spike-in RNAs, and negative controls.

The microarrays are optimized for use with RNA samples that have been linearly amplified using T7-based protocols, as described in the Materials and Methods section of the research paper. Amplified RNA is labeled with fluorescent dyes (e.g., Cy3 and Cy5) and hybridized to the microarray under stringent conditions to minimize non-specific binding. Following hybridization, the arrays are washed and scanned using a laser scanner to quantify the fluorescence intensity at each spot, which is proportional to the abundance of the corresponding transcript in the sample. The raw data are then processed using the BIAS system for background correction, normalization, and quality control, as detailed in the research paper.

In one embodiment, the microarray includes only the 349 genes that are mappable to the Agilent 24K platform used in the Netherlands Cancer Institute dataset, enabling direct comparison with historical cohorts. In another embodiment, the microarray is expanded to include additional genes involved in breast cancer pathways, such as hormone receptors, HER2, and proliferation markers, to provide a more comprehensive molecular profile. The invention also encompasses software for analyzing the microarray data, including algorithms for hierarchical clustering, class prediction, and survival analysis, which are integrated into a user-friendly graphical interface. The microarrays are provided as part of a complete diagnostic kit, including reagents for RNA extraction, amplification, labeling, and hybridization, as well as detailed protocols and data analysis templates.

### Methods of Diagnosis

The present invention provides methods for diagnosing breast cancer subtypes by comparing the gene expression profile of a patient’s tumor to the normal tissue signature. In one embodiment, RNA is extracted from a fresh-frozen or FFPE tumor specimen, amplified, and hybridized to the microarray platform described above. The resulting expression data are then analyzed using hierarchical clustering or nearest centroid classification to determine the degree of similarity between the tumor and the normal epithelium or stroma profiles. Tumors that cluster closely with the normal epithelium signature are classified as basal-like or normal-like, while those that cluster with the stroma signature are classified as luminal or HER2-enriched.

In another embodiment, the diagnosis is performed using qRT-PCR or ddPCR to quantify the expression of a panel of signature genes, such as KIT, KRT5, KRT17, GGH, PECAM1, and VWF. The expression levels are normalized to housekeeping genes and converted into a diagnostic score using a predefined algorithm. For example, a high epithelium score (indicating elevated expression of KIT, KRT5, etc.) and a low stroma score would support a diagnosis of basal-like cancer. The method may also incorporate immunohistochemical staining for c-kit and CD31 to provide orthogonal validation at the protein level.

The diagnostic methods are particularly useful for identifying triple-negative breast cancers that belong to the basal-like subtype, which have a worse prognosis and may require more aggressive treatment. By anchoring the diagnosis in the biology of normal tissue, the methods reduce the risk of misclassification due to stromal contamination or technical artifacts. The invention further provides computer-implemented methods for automating the diagnostic process, wherein raw expression data are uploaded to a secure server, processed using the BIAS pipeline, and interpreted using machine learning models trained on the normal tissue signature. The output is a diagnostic report that includes subtype assignment, prognostic risk score, and therapeutic recommendations.

### Methods of Assigning or Selecting Treatment

The present invention provides methods for assigning or selecting treatment regimens for breast cancer patients based on the similarity of their tumor expression profile to the normal tissue signature. Patients whose tumors are enriched for the normal epithelium signature—i.e., those with basal-like or normal-like subtypes—are identified as high-risk and may be candidates for intensified therapy, such as dose-dense chemotherapy, platinum-based regimens, or enrollment in clinical trials of novel agents (e.g., PARP inhibitors, immune checkpoint inhibitors). In contrast, patients whose tumors are enriched for the normal stroma signature—i.e., those with luminal or HER2-enriched subtypes—may be candidates for standard-of-care therapies, such as endocrine therapy for ER-positive disease or trastuzumab for HER2-positive disease.

The treatment selection method involves obtaining a tumor sample, generating an expression profile using the microarray or PCR-based methods described above, and calculating a similarity score to the normal epithelium and stroma centroids. If the epithelium similarity score exceeds a predefined threshold (e.g., >0.7 correlation coefficient), the patient is assigned to the basal-like group and recommended for aggressive treatment. If the stroma similarity score is higher, the patient is assigned to the luminal/HER2 group and recommended for subtype-appropriate therapy. The method may also incorporate clinical variables, such as age, tumor size, and lymph node status, into a composite risk score using logistic regression or Cox proportional hazards models.

In addition to systemic therapy, the invention may guide local treatment decisions. For example, patients with basal-like tumors may benefit from more extensive surgical resection or radiation therapy due to their higher risk of local recurrence. The treatment selection methods are implemented in clinical decision support systems that integrate genomic, pathological, and clinical data to provide personalized recommendations. The invention thus enables a precision oncology approach that matches patients to the most effective therapies based on the molecular resemblance of their tumor to normal breast tissue compartments.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, laser capture microdissection (LCM) was used to isolate morphologically normal epithelial and stromal cells from breast tissue specimens obtained from 34 patients with invasive ductal carcinoma and 10 healthy donors undergoing reduction mammoplasty. Tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and regions of interest were identified by a dedicated pathologist. Epithelial cells were captured from ductal structures at least 2 mm away from tumor margins, while stromal cells were sampled exclusively from the extralobular compartment. Total RNA was extracted from the microdissected cells using a guanidinium isothiocyanate (GITC) protocol, yielding 2–5 ng of RNA per sample. The RNA was subjected to two rounds of T7 linear amplification using the Ambion Amino Allyl MessageAmp kit, preserving the relative abundance of mRNA species. Amplified RNA was labeled with Cy3 and Cy5 dyes and hybridized to Agilent Whole Human Genome 44K microarrays against a Universal Human Reference RNA. Duplicate hybridizations with reverse-dye labeling were performed for each sample to control for dye bias. Data preprocessing included background correction using the RMA algorithm, log2-ratio conversion, within-array loess normalization, and between-array median absolute deviation scaling. Quality control metrics ensured that all arrays met stringent criteria for signal intensity, signal-to-noise ratio, and replicate concordance. Hierarchical clustering of the 66 samples (34 epithelium, 32 stroma) using all 44K features revealed strict segregation by tissue type, with cancer-adjacent and reduction samples intermixed within each cluster. Only three stroma samples were misclassified as epithelium, likely due to minor contamination. This result demonstrated that the gene expression profiles of normal breast tissue are determined primarily by cell lineage rather than disease status.

### Example 2

This example describes the identification of the normal tissue signature through class distinction analysis. Patient-matched epithelial and stromal samples from 22 individuals (44 samples total) were analyzed using the LIMMA and SAM algorithms to identify differentially expressed genes. Genes were filtered based on statistical significance (FDR-adjusted p-value ≤ 0.01 for LIMMA, q-value ≤ 0.3 for SAM), fold change (≥2.0), and intensity (A > 6.0). A total of 883 genes met these criteria, including 321 epithelium-specific and 562 stroma-specific genes. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that epithelium-specific genes were associated with cell junctions, basal lamina, and epithelial differentiation, while stroma-specific genes were linked to extracellular matrix, collagen binding, and angiogenesis. Immunohistochemistry was performed on frozen sections using antibodies against c-kit (epithelium) and CD31 (stroma), confirming the predicted localization. When the 883-gene signature was used for hierarchical clustering of the full 66-sample dataset, all samples—including the three outliers from Example 1—were correctly classified by tissue type. Principal component analysis showed that tissue type accounted for 3.98% of the total variation, while inter-individual differences accounted for 3.58%. No clinical variables (e.g., ER status, tumor grade) were significantly associated with the principal components after Bonferroni correction, indicating that normal tissue gene expression is not influenced by the adjacent tumor’s characteristics.

### Example 3

In this example, the normal tissue signature was applied to an independent dataset of 295 breast carcinomas from the Netherlands Cancer Institute (GEO accession GSE2990). The 883-gene signature was mapped to 349 genes present on the Agilent 24K arrays used in that study. Hierarchical clustering of the 295 tumors using these 349 genes resolved two major clusters: a large cluster of 257 samples enriched for stroma-specific genes and a small cluster of 38 samples enriched for epithelium-specific genes. The small cluster was uniformly triple-negative (ER−/PR−/HER2−) and expressed basal-like markers such as KRT5, KRT17, and GGH. Survival analysis using Kaplan-Meier curves showed that patients in the small cluster had significantly worse distant metastasis-free survival (p = 0.000489). When clustering was repeated using only the epithelium-specific genes, the basal-like cluster was still evident, but it disappeared when using only stroma-specific genes, confirming that the aggressive phenotype is driven by epithelial biology. This example demonstrates the prognostic utility of the normal epithelium signature in identifying high-risk breast cancer patients.

### Example 4

This example validates the biological state of normal breast stroma by comparing it to published fibroblast gene expression profiles. The core serum response (CSR) signature, which distinguishes activated (wound-healing) from quiescent fibroblasts, was examined in the normal tissue dataset. Of the CSR genes expressed in normal tissue (variance >1), 84% were from the quiescent (unstimulated) fibroblast set, while only 16% were from the activated set. This indicates that normal breast stroma resembles unstimulated fibroblasts rather than wound-activated fibroblasts. Additionally, the normal stroma signature was compared to gene lists from desmoid-type fibromatosis (DTF) and solitary fibrous tumor (SFT). Normal stroma expressed significantly more DTF-specific genes than expected by chance (p ≤ 2e-16), and showed enrichment for DTF genes (p = 2.48e-5), but only marginal enrichment for SFT genes (p = 0.038). Since DTF is associated with favorable prognosis in breast cancer, this finding supports the notion that normal stroma is in a benign, non-activated state. This example clarifies the microenvironmental context of breast cancer and refutes the hypothesis that normal stroma is pre-activated in cancer patients.
