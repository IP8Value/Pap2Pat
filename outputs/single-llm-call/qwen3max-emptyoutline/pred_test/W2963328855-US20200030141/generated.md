## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to medical devices and methods for the treatment of ocular surface disorders, and more specifically to a minimally invasive, laser-based system and method for the controlled thermal shrinkage of redundant conjunctival tissue in patients suffering from conjunctivochalasis. The invention further pertains to a handheld, fiber-optic probe integrated with a 1460-nanometer near-infrared diode laser source and an aiming mechanism, configured to deliver pulsed laser energy in a line-shaped focal pattern to achieve precise, localized, and non-ablative collagen remodeling without causing full-thickness thermal injury, hemorrhage, or excessive scarring. The technical domain encompasses ophthalmic surgery, biomedical optics, laser-tissue interaction, and therapeutic device design, particularly in the context of dry eye disease management and anterior segment reconstruction.

## BACKGROUND

Conjunctivochalasis is a prevalent degenerative condition of the ocular surface characterized by the presence of loose, redundant folds of conjunctival tissue, typically located along the inferior bulbar conjunctiva adjacent to the lower eyelid margin. This condition arises primarily due to age-related loss of elasticity and structural integrity in the conjunctival stroma and Tenon’s capsule, leading to mechanical instability and abnormal tissue redundancy. The redundant conjunctival folds disrupt the normal tear film dynamics by interfering with the formation and maintenance of the inferior tear meniscus—the critical reservoir of tears that ensures uniform ocular surface lubrication during blinking. As a result, patients often experience chronic symptoms including foreign body sensation, burning, grittiness, intermittent blurred vision, epiphora (excessive tearing), and persistent ocular irritation that mimics or exacerbates dry eye disease.

Despite its high prevalence—particularly among individuals over the age of 50—conjunctivochalasis is frequently underdiagnosed or misdiagnosed as conventional aqueous-deficient or evaporative dry eye. Standard dry eye therapies, such as artificial tears, cyclosporine or lifitegrast drops, punctal occlusion with plugs, and warm compresses, are largely ineffective in alleviating symptoms because they do not address the underlying mechanical obstruction caused by the redundant tissue. In fact, the presence of conjunctivochalasis can render these treatments futile or even counterproductive, as the displaced tear meniscus prevents proper distribution of lubricants across the cornea and conjunctiva.

Surgical intervention is therefore considered the definitive treatment for moderate to severe conjunctivochalasis. Historically, several surgical techniques have been employed to reduce or eliminate the excess conjunctival tissue. These include thermocautery, electrocautery, direct excision with or without amniotic membrane grafting, and the “paste-pinch-cut” technique involving mechanical removal followed by fibrin sealant application. However, each of these approaches carries significant limitations. Thermocautery and electrocautery utilize high-temperature energy delivery that often exceeds the boiling point of water, resulting in full-thickness thermal necrosis of the conjunctival epithelium and underlying stroma. This leads to prolonged postoperative healing times—often up to four weeks—accompanied by substantial pain, inflammation, and risk of complications such as pyogenic granuloma formation, subconjunctival hemorrhage, and unsightly scarring or hyperemia. Moreover, the uncontrolled thermal spread inherent in these modalities poses a risk to adjacent structures, including the cornea, especially if the patient exhibits involuntary eye movements during the procedure.

Direct excision of redundant conjunctiva, while mechanically precise, necessitates suturing or the use of biological adhesives like fibrin glue, which typically requires an operating room setting, thereby increasing procedural cost, complexity, and patient burden. Even with adjunctive amniotic membrane transplantation to promote healing, the recovery remains protracted, and the cosmetic and functional outcomes are not always predictable. The paste-pinch-cut technique, though less destructive than cautery, still involves physical trauma to the tissue and does not eliminate the need for fibrin sealant or extended healing periods.

Laser-based approaches have emerged as a potential alternative due to their capacity for precise energy delivery and controlled thermal effects. Argon lasers operating at 532 nm have been explored for conjunctivoplasty; however, this wavelength is strongly absorbed by hemoglobin, leading to unintended vascular rupture, hemorrhage, and inconsistent tissue response. Given that the conjunctiva consists of approximately 70% water by weight, a more physiologically rational strategy is to target the water content directly using wavelengths within the near-infrared (NIR) spectrum that coincide with strong water absorption peaks. Two prominent water absorption bands exist at approximately 1460 nm and 1940 nm. While lasers at 1940 nm offer high absorption, they are typically based on thulium or holmium solid-state systems that are expensive, bulky, and inefficient in terms of electro-optical conversion. In contrast, semiconductor diode lasers operating at 1460 nm provide a compelling balance of strong water absorption, high electro-optical efficiency (greater than 40%), compact size, reliability, and cost-effectiveness, making them ideal candidates for clinical translation.

Despite these advantages, prior art has not successfully integrated a 1460-nm diode laser into a practical, user-friendly, and clinically viable system for conjunctivochalasis treatment. Existing laser platforms lack the necessary beam shaping, real-time feedback, safety interlocks, and ergonomic design required for safe and effective outpatient ophthalmic procedures. Furthermore, there is a critical need to define optimal laser parameters—including peak power, pulse duration, duty cycle, repetition rate, and total treatment duration—that maximize collagen shrinkage while maintaining tissue temperatures within a narrow therapeutic window (approximately 62°C to just below 100°C) to avoid vaporization, carbonization, or irreversible damage.

Collagen, the primary structural protein in the conjunctival stroma, undergoes denaturation and shrinkage when heated to temperatures above its denaturation threshold, which is around 62°C. This shrinkage is reversible only if the temperature remains below the boiling point of water; beyond 100°C, explosive vaporization occurs, leading to tissue disruption and charring. Therefore, precise thermal control is paramount. Pulsed laser delivery offers a means to modulate heat accumulation through controlled on-off cycles, allowing for cumulative thermal effects while enabling partial cooling between pulses to prevent overheating. The thermal relaxation time of conjunctival tissue—governed by its thickness and thermal diffusivity—further informs the selection of pulse duration and repetition rate to confine thermal energy to the target depth and avoid collateral damage.

In addition to parameter optimization, the delivery geometry must be tailored to the anatomical and surgical context. Redundant conjunctival folds are often mobile and irregular; thus, a method to stabilize the tissue during irradiation is essential. Traditional flat-tip probes or unfocused beams may irradiate non-target areas or fail to maintain consistent contact. An integrated mechanical stabilization system, such as angled forceps, can hold the fold in a reproducible position, ensuring uniform energy delivery and preventing motion artifacts. Moreover, the laser beam should be shaped into a linear focus to match the elongated morphology of the redundant fold, thereby maximizing treatment efficiency and minimizing the number of passes required.

Real-time monitoring of the treatment effect is another unmet need. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has proven valuable in visualizing dynamic tissue changes during laser procedures, but integrating OCT feedback into a clinical workflow remains challenging. While not part of the core device, the ability to correlate laser parameters with measurable shrinkage outcomes—as demonstrated through ex vivo models—is critical for establishing dosimetry and safety margins.

In summary, the current state of the art lacks a safe, efficient, office-based, and cosmetically favorable solution for conjunctivochalasis that avoids the drawbacks of cautery, excision, and visible-wavelength lasers. There exists a clear and pressing need for a novel system that leverages the biophysical properties of water-absorbing NIR light, incorporates intelligent pulsing strategies, features an ergonomic and integrated probe design, and delivers predictable, reproducible, and minimally traumatic tissue remodeling. The present invention addresses these deficiencies by providing a complete, optimized, and clinically translatable platform for laser thermal conjunctivoplasty (LTC) using a 1460-nm diode laser.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF DISCLOSED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for the treatment of conjunctivochalasis through controlled, non-ablative thermal shrinkage of redundant conjunctival tissue using a near-infrared laser operating at a wavelength of approximately 1460 nanometers. This wavelength is selected based on its strong absorption by water, which constitutes the majority of the conjunctival tissue mass, thereby enabling efficient and localized heating with minimal penetration depth and reduced risk of damage to underlying or adjacent structures. The system comprises a programmable, fiber-coupled diode laser source, a custom-designed handheld probe with integrated beam shaping optics and mechanical stabilization, a user interface with real-time parameter control, and safety mechanisms to ensure operator and patient protection.

The core of the invention is a high-power, continuous-wave capable laser diode module emitting at 1460 nm, with a maximum output power of at least 12 watts in continuous mode. The laser diode is coupled into a multimode optical fiber with a core diameter of 200 micrometers and a numerical aperture of 0.22, which serves as the flexible delivery conduit to the handheld probe. This fiber coupling ensures stable beam transmission while allowing the laser source to be housed in a compact, portable console that can be placed on a standard clinical cart or table. The console includes custom-built electronic driver circuits that enable precise modulation of the laser output in both continuous and pulsed modes. Critically, the system supports fully programmable pulse parameters, including peak power, pulse duration, duty cycle, pulse repetition rate, and total treatment duration, all of which can be adjusted via a graphical user interface implemented in software such as LabVIEW.

To facilitate accurate targeting and alignment, the system incorporates a low-power visible aiming laser, typically a red diode laser emitting at 650 nm with an output power of approximately 200 microwatts. This aiming beam is co-aligned with the therapeutic 1460-nm beam within the optical path and is projected through the same delivery fiber or a parallel channel in the probe, allowing the surgeon to visualize the exact location and geometry of the intended treatment zone before activating the infrared laser. The aiming laser is automatically deactivated upon initiation of the therapeutic laser pulse to prevent interference and conserve battery life, and it reactivates once the treatment sequence is complete.

Operation of the therapeutic laser is initiated via a foot pedal connected to the console, which serves as a hands-free trigger. Upon depression of the foot pedal, the system delivers the pre-programmed pulse train according to the selected parameters. If the foot pedal is released prematurely during the treatment sequence, the laser output is immediately terminated, providing an essential safety feature to prevent accidental overexposure. The entire system, including the laser driver, control electronics, and power supply, is housed in a ruggedized enclosure with dimensions of approximately 41 cm × 36 cm × 15 cm, making it suitable for use in outpatient clinics, ambulatory surgery centers, or hospital-based ophthalmology departments.

The handheld probe is a key innovation of the disclosed embodiment. It is designed to be lightweight, ergonomically contoured, and sterilizable, enabling repeated use in clinical settings. At the distal end of the probe, the 1460-nm laser beam exiting the fiber is collimated and then passed through a cylindrical lens, which focuses the beam into a linear spot rather than a circular one. This line-shaped focus is critical for matching the anatomical configuration of redundant conjunctival folds, which are typically elongated and ribbon-like. The cylindrical lens, with a focal length of approximately 5.8 millimeters and a clear aperture of 6 mm by 4 mm, transforms the circular Gaussian beam into a focal plane measuring approximately 10 mm in length and 1 mm in width. This geometry ensures that a single laser pulse can treat an entire segment of the redundant fold in one pass, significantly improving procedural efficiency and consistency.

Integrated into the probe housing is a pair of angled micro-forceps, which are mechanically linked to a thumb-operated actuator on the probe handle. These forceps are fabricated from biocompatible stainless steel and feature fine, non-traumatic tips designed to gently grasp and stabilize the redundant conjunctival tissue without causing crush injury. The angle of the forceps platform is precisely calibrated so that when the tissue is held, it lies flush against the focal plane of the laser beam. This alignment ensures that the laser energy is delivered perpendicularly to the tissue surface, maximizing absorption efficiency and minimizing reflection losses. Furthermore, the forceps maintain a fixed working distance between the probe tip and the tissue, eliminating variability due to hand tremor or inconsistent positioning.

The combination of the line-focused beam and the angled forceps enables a highly controlled treatment paradigm: the surgeon first identifies the redundant conjunctival fold, grasps it gently with the integrated forceps, and then activates the laser via the foot pedal. The pulsed laser energy is confined to the tissue segment held between the forceps, inducing immediate and visible shrinkage due to collagen denaturation. Because the forceps isolate the target tissue, surrounding healthy conjunctiva remains unaffected, reducing the risk of iatrogenic damage.

The laser parameters are optimized based on extensive ex vivo experimentation using porcine eyes, which closely mimic human conjunctival anatomy and composition. The thermal relaxation time of conjunctival tissue, calculated using the formula τ = d² / (4D), where d is the effective tissue thickness (approximately 0.5 mm when folded) and D is the thermal diffusivity (~1.3 × 10⁻⁷ m²/s), yields a value of about 0.5 seconds. This informs the selection of pulse duration to be less than 0.5 seconds to confine thermal energy to the target depth. A pulse repetition rate of 1 Hz (i.e., one pulse per second) is chosen to allow sufficient cooling between pulses, preventing cumulative overheating. A total treatment duration of 4 seconds—corresponding to four pulses—is found to be sufficient to achieve clinically meaningful shrinkage without exceeding safe temperature thresholds.

Through systematic variation of peak power and duty cycle, the optimal operating window is identified as a peak power of 3.0 watts with a duty cycle of 20% (i.e., 200 milliseconds of laser-on time per 1-second pulse period). Under these conditions, the peak tissue temperature reaches approximately 88°C, which is well above the collagen shrinkage threshold of 62°C but safely below the boiling point of water. This results in a consistent tissue shrinkage of approximately 36% to 45%, as quantified by measuring the reduction in distance between fiducial markers placed on the tissue surface before and after treatment. Higher duty cycles (e.g., 30%) or higher peak powers (e.g., >3.66 W) lead to temperatures exceeding 100°C, causing tissue damage, charring, and unreliable outcomes. Lower parameters (e.g., 10% duty cycle or <2.33 W) produce insufficient shrinkage (<10–20%).

The system is also designed with future integration in mind. Although the current embodiment does not include real-time temperature monitoring or OCT feedback, the modular architecture allows for the incorporation of such features in advanced versions. For instance, a miniature thermal sensor or infrared camera could be embedded in the probe tip to provide closed-loop temperature control, automatically adjusting laser power to maintain the target temperature range. Similarly, the probe could be adapted for use with commercial OCT systems to visualize subsurface structural changes during treatment, enabling image-guided therapy.

Safety is paramount in the design. The 1460-nm wavelength is invisible to the human eye, posing a risk of accidental exposure. Therefore, the system includes multiple safeguards: the aiming laser provides visual indication of the beam path, the foot pedal requires deliberate activation, and the software enforces maximum power and duration limits. Additionally, the probe is constructed with opaque materials that block stray infrared radiation, and all optical connections are secured to prevent leakage.

In clinical practice, the procedure is performed under topical anesthesia in an outpatient setting. The surgeon identifies the redundant conjunctival folds, typically in the inferior fornix, and uses the integrated forceps to isolate a segment. After confirming alignment with the aiming beam, the laser is activated for the preset 4-second duration. Immediate shrinkage is observed, and the process is repeated as needed along the length of the fold. Postoperatively, patients experience minimal discomfort, with rapid re-epithelialization and restoration of the tear meniscus within days, as opposed to weeks with traditional methods. There is no need for sutures, grafts, or prolonged anti-inflammatory therapy, significantly improving patient compliance and satisfaction.

### EXAMPLES

The efficacy and safety of the disclosed laser thermal conjunctivoplasty (LTC) system were rigorously evaluated through a series of ex vivo experiments using freshly thawed porcine eyes obtained from a commercial supplier. Porcine ocular tissue was selected due to its close histological and biomechanical similarity to human conjunctiva, particularly in terms of water content, collagen density, and stromal architecture. All experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions to isolate the effects of laser parameters on tissue response.

In the first set of experiments, the thermal dynamics of laser-tissue interaction were investigated using a high-resolution infrared thermal camera (Fluke TiS45) capable of capturing temperature changes at a rate of 30 frames per second. Porcine eyes were mounted on a stable platform, and the conjunctival surface was marked with two parallel blue lines spaced 1 millimeter apart using a sterile tissue marker, creating a defined region of interest (ROI) of 10 mm in length. The handheld LTC probe was positioned perpendicular to the ROI, and pulsed laser irradiation was applied using varying combinations of peak power (1.51 W, 3.0 W, and 5.77 W) and duty cycle (10%, 20%, and 30%), with a fixed repetition rate of 1 Hz and total duration of 4 seconds. Temperature data were extracted from the thermal video sequences by identifying the maximum pixel value within the ROI at each time point.

Results demonstrated that temperature rise was highly dependent on both peak power and duty cycle. At 1.51 W and 10% duty cycle, the maximum temperature remained below 40°C throughout the treatment, indicating insufficient energy for collagen shrinkage. Increasing the duty cycle to 20% and 30% at the same power level resulted in modest temperature increases, but still below the 62°C threshold. At 3.0 W peak power, the 10% duty cycle produced a peak temperature of approximately 65°C, while the 20% duty cycle reached 88°C, and the 30% duty cycle exceeded 100°C. At 5.77 W, all duty cycles led to temperatures well above 100°C, confirming the risk of tissue boiling and damage at excessive power levels. These findings established the upper and lower bounds of the therapeutic window.

In parallel, the morphological effects of LTC were assessed using digital microscopy. High-magnification images of the ROI were captured before and after each laser treatment using a calibrated digital microscope (OMAX). The distance between the blue fiducial marks was measured using image analysis software, and the percentage shrinkage was calculated as [(initial distance – final distance) / initial distance] × 100. At 2.33 W and 20% duty cycle, shrinkage was 21%; at 3.1 W, it increased to 36%; and at 3.66 W, it reached 45%. However, at 3.66 W, visual inspection revealed signs of surface whitening and micro-charring, correlating with the temperature exceeding 100°C. When the duty cycle was varied at a fixed 3.0 W peak power, shrinkage was 8% at 10%, 36% at 20%, and 45% at 30%—but again, the 30% condition showed tissue damage.

To account for potential mechanical deformation caused by the angled forceps, a control experiment was performed in which the forceps were used to grasp the tissue without laser activation. This resulted in a 10% reduction in marker distance due to elastic compression, which was subtracted from the total measured shrinkage in combined laser-forceps trials. When the full system was used (3.0 W, 20% duty cycle, with forceps), the net shrinkage attributable to thermal effects was 35%, consistent with the non-forceps trials.

Real-time structural changes during LTC were visualized using a custom 1310-nm swept-source optical coherence tomography (OCT) system with an A-line rate of 50 kHz, axial resolution of 8.5 μm, and transverse resolution of 15 μm. OCT B-scans were acquired continuously before, during, and after laser application over a 5-mm lateral range. Time-sequence OCT videos revealed immediate inward movement of the tissue edges toward the laser-irradiated center, confirming active shrinkage. Particle image velocimetry (PIV) analysis of pre- and post-treatment OCT frames quantified displacement vectors, with arrows pointing centrally and color-coded speeds reaching up to 50 μm/s, providing direct evidence of dynamic collagen contraction.

Finally, the spectral and temporal characteristics of the laser source were validated. The emission spectrum, centered at 1456 nm with a bandwidth of 3.1–3.9 nm, remained stable across power levels and was well-aligned with the 1460-nm water absorption peak. Pulse shapes were square and precisely controlled, with durations from 100 ms to 300 ms and repetition rates accurately maintained at 1 Hz. The linear relationship between drive current and output power confirmed reliable power calibration.

Collectively, these examples demonstrate that the disclosed LTC system, when operated at 3.0 W peak power and 20% duty cycle with 1-Hz repetition over 4 seconds, achieves optimal conjunctival shrinkage of 35–45% while maintaining tissue temperatures in the safe range of 62–88°C, thereby validating the invention’s clinical potential.
