# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language processing, and more specifically to systems and methods for spoken language understanding in dialogue-based applications. In particular, the invention provides a neural generative model for slot filling that operates on unaligned dialogue data—i.e., utterances annotated only at the sentence level with semantic slot-value pairs, without explicit word-level alignment—and is capable of accurately predicting slot values even when those values are out-of-vocabulary (OOV) with respect to a predefined lexical inventory. The invention leverages a hybrid sequence-to-sequence architecture that integrates a standard attentional decoder with a pointer network mechanism, enabling the system to dynamically choose between generating tokens from a fixed vocabulary and copying tokens directly from the input utterance. This dual-mode prediction capability allows the model to robustly handle both enumerable slots (e.g., “price range = moderate”) and non-enumerable slots (e.g., “restaurant name = La Trattoria”), including cases where the slot value has never been observed during training. The invention is particularly advantageous in real-world spoken dialogue systems, such as virtual assistants, customer service bots, and task-oriented conversational agents, where user utterances frequently contain novel or domain-specific entities that cannot be exhaustively enumerated in advance. By eliminating the need for manual annotation at the word level and avoiding reliance on handcrafted alignment rules or domain-specific dictionaries, the invention significantly reduces development time and enhances portability across new domains and languages.

## BACKGROUND

Spoken language understanding (SLU) is a critical component of modern dialogue systems, responsible for converting raw user utterances into structured semantic representations that can be processed by downstream dialogue management modules. A central subtask within SLU is slot filling, which involves identifying and extracting specific pieces of information—referred to as slot-value pairs—from an utterance. For example, given the user query “I’d like a moderately priced Italian restaurant,” a slot-filling system should output semantic frames such as {pricerange: moderate, food: Italian}. Traditionally, slot filling has been formulated as a sequence labeling problem, where each word in the input utterance is assigned a label indicating its role in the semantic frame, commonly using tagging schemes such as BIO (Begin, Inside, Outside). Early approaches employed statistical models like conditional random fields (CRFs), which achieved strong performance when trained on datasets with precise word-level annotations, such as the Air Travel Information System (ATIS) corpus. Subsequently, neural architectures—particularly recurrent neural networks (RNNs) combined with attention mechanisms—have further improved performance by capturing long-range dependencies and contextual nuances in spoken language.

However, these conventional sequence labeling models suffer from a fundamental limitation: they require training data annotated at the word level, which is expensive, time-consuming, and often impractical to obtain at scale. In many real-world scenarios, only sentence-level annotations are available—i.e., the system knows that an utterance conveys certain slot-value pairs, but not which specific words correspond to those values. For instance, a dataset might indicate that the utterance “Find me a cheap Thai place” contains the slots {pricerange: cheap, food: Thai}, without specifying that “cheap” maps to pricerange and “Thai” to food. To bridge this gap, some prior systems have resorted to heuristic rule-based alignment procedures that attempt to match slot values to substrings in the utterance using manually curated word aliases or pattern-matching heuristics. Yet such approaches are brittle: they perform poorly in the presence of speech recognition errors, syntactic variation, or paraphrasing, and they require significant engineering effort to adapt to new domains. Empirical studies have shown that even under ideal conditions, rule-based alignment recovers correct word-to-slot mappings in only about 73% of cases, and models trained on such noisy pseudo-labels exhibit substantially degraded performance compared to those trained on gold-standard alignments.

An alternative line of research has reframed slot filling as a classification problem, where the entire utterance is mapped directly to one or more slot-value pairs without intermediate word-level labeling. While this avoids the alignment problem, it introduces new challenges. First, many slot types—such as restaurant names, street addresses, or dish names—are open-vocabulary, meaning their possible values are vast, unbounded, or entirely unknown at training time. Classifiers that rely on a fixed output space cannot generalize to such OOV (out-of-vocabulary) values, leading to systematic failures in real-world deployments. Second, even for enumerable slots, data sparsity becomes a critical issue when training data is limited, as the model may never observe certain valid slot-value combinations during training. These limitations severely restrict the applicability of classification-based approaches in dynamic, open-domain dialogue systems.

To address these shortcomings, recent work has explored generative modeling paradigms, particularly sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) architectures, which treat slot filling as a transduction task: mapping an input utterance to a sequence of slot values. While promising, standard Seq2Seq models still face the OOV problem because they generate outputs solely from a fixed target vocabulary constructed from the training data. When a slot value in the test utterance does not appear in this vocabulary—e.g., a new restaurant name—the model either fails to predict it or substitutes it with an incorrect but in-vocabulary token. This issue is especially acute in spoken dialogue, where user queries routinely introduce novel entities.

A breakthrough in handling OOV tokens came with the introduction of the pointer network (Ptr-Net), a variant of the Seq2Seq model that, instead of generating tokens from a vocabulary, learns to “point” to positions in the input sequence and copy tokens directly from there. Originally developed for combinatorial optimization problems, Ptr-Nets have since been successfully adapted to natural language tasks such as abstractive summarization, question answering, and reading comprehension, where the ability to copy rare or unseen words from the source text is essential. By allowing the model to selectively copy input tokens, Ptr-Nets effectively expand the output vocabulary to include any word present in the current utterance, thereby circumventing the OOV bottleneck.

Despite these advances, no existing system has effectively combined the strengths of generative modeling and pointer mechanisms specifically for slot filling on unaligned dialogue data. Prior attempts either rely on rigid classification frameworks ill-suited for open-vocabulary slots or use pure generation or pure copying strategies that lack flexibility. There remains a need for a unified, end-to-end trainable model that can seamlessly switch between generating known slot values from a vocabulary and copying novel values directly from the input, all while operating without word-level supervision. Such a system would eliminate dependence on manual alignment rules, generalize robustly to unseen entities, and enable rapid deployment across diverse dialogue domains with minimal human intervention. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel hybrid neural architecture that integrates attentional Seq2Seq decoding with dynamic pointer-based copying, trained exclusively on sentence-level annotations, thereby providing a scalable, accurate, and domain-agnostic solution to the slot filling problem in spoken language understanding.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a neural generative system and method for slot value prediction in spoken language understanding that operates effectively on dialogue data annotated only at the sentence level, without requiring word-level alignment between utterances and their corresponding slot-value pairs. The core innovation lies in a hybrid sequence-to-sequence architecture that combines a standard attentional decoder with a pointer network mechanism, enabling the model to dynamically decide—at each decoding step—whether to generate a slot value token from a fixed vocabulary or to copy a token directly from the input utterance. This dual-mode prediction strategy allows the system to accurately handle both enumerable slots (e.g., price ranges, meal types) whose values are known in advance and non-enumerable slots (e.g., restaurant names, street addresses) that may contain out-of-vocabulary (OOV) terms not observed during training. The model is trained end-to-end using only sentence-level semantic annotations, eliminating the need for costly manual word-level labeling or error-prone heuristic alignment rules. The encoder employs a bidirectional gated recurrent unit (GRU) to produce contextualized representations of the input utterance, while the decoder uses a unidirectional GRU augmented with an attention mechanism to compute context-aware hidden states. At each decoding time step, the system computes two probability distributions: one over the fixed slot vocabulary (via the generative component) and another over the positions of the input sequence (via the pointer component). A learned gating mechanism, implemented as a sigmoid function conditioned on the decoder input, decoder state, and attention context vector, determines the optimal interpolation weight between these two distributions to produce the final prediction over an extended vocabulary comprising both the fixed vocabulary and all tokens in the current input. This architecture enables robust performance even under high OOV conditions, as demonstrated by empirical evaluations on benchmark dialogue datasets. Furthermore, the invention is integrated into a complete spoken language understanding pipeline, where predicted slot values are paired with dialog act and slot type classifiers—implemented via convolutional neural networks—to produce full semantic frames. Experimental results show that the proposed system achieves state-of-the-art performance on the Dialog State Tracking Challenge 2 (DSTC2) dataset for both slot value prediction and overall SLU, outperforming prior art that relies on support vector machines, joint CNN-LSTM classifiers, or basic Seq2Seq models. Notably, the system maintains superior accuracy even when trained on reduced datasets with artificially inflated OOV rates, demonstrating exceptional robustness and data efficiency. The invention requires no manually designed features, domain-specific dictionaries, or post-processing rules, making it highly portable across new dialogue domains and languages. By unifying generation and copying in a single differentiable framework trained on weakly supervised data, the present invention provides a scalable, accurate, and practical solution to the slot filling problem in real-world spoken dialogue systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a neural generative model for slot value prediction that operates on unaligned dialogue data—i.e., utterances annotated only with sentence-level slot-value pairs, without explicit word-to-slot alignments—and is specifically engineered to overcome the out-of-vocabulary (OOV) problem that plagues conventional approaches. The system architecture is built upon a hybrid sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) framework that synergistically combines a standard attentional decoder with a pointer network (Ptr-Net) mechanism, enabling dynamic switching between vocabulary-based generation and input-based copying during decoding. This design allows the model to accurately predict slot values regardless of whether they belong to a closed set of enumerable categories (e.g., “price range = cheap”) or represent open-vocabulary entities (e.g., “restaurant = Bistro Garden”) that may never have been seen during training. The entire system is trained end-to-end using only sentence-level annotations, obviating the need for labor-intensive word-level labeling or brittle heuristic alignment procedures.

The input to the system is a natural language utterance represented as a sequence of words \( X = \{x_1, x_2, ..., x_T\} \), where \( T \) is the length of the utterance. Each word \( x_i \) is first embedded into a dense vector representation using a trainable embedding matrix, resulting in a sequence of embedding vectors \( \{e_1, e_2, ..., e_T\} \). These embeddings are then processed by a bidirectional gated recurrent unit (GRU) encoder, which captures contextual information from both past and future tokens. Specifically, the forward GRU processes the sequence from left to right, producing hidden states \( \overrightarrow{h}_1, \overrightarrow{h}_2, ..., \overrightarrow{h}_T \), while the backward GRU processes it from right to left, yielding hidden states \( \overleftarrow{h}_1, \overleftarrow{h}_2, ..., \overleftarrow{h}_T \). The final encoder hidden state for each position \( i \) is obtained by concatenating the forward and backward states: \( h_i = [\overrightarrow{h}_i; \overleftarrow{h}_i] \). This bidirectional encoding ensures that each token representation incorporates rich contextual cues from the entire utterance, which is crucial for disambiguating slot values in complex or ambiguous phrasings.

The decoder is implemented as a unidirectional GRU that generates the output sequence of slot value tokens one at a time. At each decoding time step \( t \), the decoder receives as input the embedding of the previously predicted token (or a start-of-sequence token for \( t=1 \)) and updates its hidden state \( s_t \) based on this input and its previous state \( s_{t-1} \). To incorporate information from the input utterance, the decoder employs an additive attention mechanism, similar to that proposed by Bahdanau et al. (2014). The attention scores \( e_{t,i} \) between the current decoder state \( s_t \) and each encoder hidden state \( h_i \) are computed as \( e_{t,i} = v_a^\top \tanh(W_a s_t + U_a h_i) \), where \( W_a \), \( U_a \), and \( v_a \) are trainable parameters. These scores are normalized via softmax to produce attention weights \( a_{t,i} = \exp(e_{t,i}) / \sum_{j=1}^T \exp(e_{t,j}) \), which represent the relevance of each input token to the current decoding step. The context vector \( c_t \) is then computed as the weighted sum of encoder hidden states: \( c_t = \sum_{i=1}^T a_{t,i} h_i \).

The core innovation of the invention resides in the hybrid output layer, which fuses predictions from two complementary sources: a generative component and a pointer component. The generative component produces a probability distribution \( P_{\text{gen}}(w) \) over a fixed slot vocabulary \( V \), which includes only the values of enumerable slots (e.g., “cheap”, “expensive”, “Italian”, “Chinese”) observed in the training data. This distribution is computed by passing the concatenation of the decoder input embedding \( d_t \), decoder state \( s_t \), and context vector \( c_t \) through a linear projection followed by softmax: \( P_{\text{gen}}(w) = \text{softmax}(W_o [d_t; s_t; c_t])_w \), where \( W_o \) is a trainable weight matrix. Crucially, the slot vocabulary \( V \) excludes values of non-enumerable slots (e.g., proper nouns like restaurant names), as these are assumed to be unpredictable from a fixed set in real-world applications.

Simultaneously, the pointer component produces a probability distribution \( P_{\text{ptr}}(i) \) over the positions \( \{1, 2, ..., T\} \) of the input utterance, effectively allowing the model to “copy” tokens directly from the input. This distribution is derived directly from the attention weights: \( P_{\text{ptr}}(i) = a_{t,i} \). Thus, if a slot value appears verbatim in the input—such as “La Trattoria” in the utterance “Book a table at La Trattoria”—the pointer mechanism can select it with high probability, even if it is OOV with respect to \( V \).

To combine these two distributions into a single prediction over an extended vocabulary \( V_{\text{ext}} = V \cup \{x_1, x_2, ..., x_T\} \), the model employs a learned gating mechanism that computes a scalar mixing weight \( p_t \in [0,1] \) at each time step. This weight is determined by a sigmoid function applied to a linear combination of the decoder input, state, and context vector:  
\[
p_t = \sigma(w_d^\top d_t + w_s^\top s_t + w_c^\top c_t),
\]  
where \( w_d \), \( w_s \), and \( w_c \) are trainable weight vectors and \( \sigma \) denotes the sigmoid activation. The final probability of outputting a token \( w \) is then given by:  
\[
P(w) = p_t \cdot P_{\text{gen}}(w) + (1 - p_t) \cdot \sum_{i: x_i = w} P_{\text{ptr}}(i).
\]  
This formulation ensures that if \( w \) is in the fixed vocabulary \( V \), it receives contributions from both generation and copying (if it also appears in the input); if \( w \) is OOV but present in the input, it receives probability mass only from the pointer component; and if \( w \) is neither in \( V \) nor in the input, its probability is zero. During training, the model is optimized to maximize the likelihood of the ground-truth slot value sequence using standard cross-entropy loss, with gradients backpropagated through both the generative and pointer pathways.

The system is trained exclusively on sentence-level annotations, meaning that for each utterance, the training target is simply the sequence of slot values (e.g., [“moderate”, “Italian”]) without any indication of which input words correspond to which values. This weak supervision is sufficient because the attention and pointer mechanisms implicitly learn to align output tokens to relevant input spans through end-to-end optimization. No external alignment rules, dictionaries, or feature engineering are required.

In a complete spoken language understanding (SLU) pipeline, the slot value predictor is complemented by two additional modules: a dialog act classifier and a slot type classifier. The dialog act classifier, implemented as a convolutional neural network (CNN), takes the entire utterance as input and predicts one or more dialog acts (e.g., “inform”, “request”) associated with the utterance. The slot type classifier, also a CNN, takes each predicted slot value along with the original utterance as input and assigns it to a predefined slot type (e.g., mapping the value “Italian” to the slot type “food”). These classifiers operate on fixed, low-cardinality output spaces and thus achieve high accuracy even with simple architectures. Importantly, the entire SLU system—including slot value prediction, dialog act classification, and slot type assignment—is trained independently for each component, without joint modeling or reliance on dialogue context, yet still achieves state-of-the-art performance due to the robustness of the core slot value predictor.

Empirical validation was conducted on the Dialog State Tracking Challenge 2 (DSTC2) dataset, a benchmark collection of multi-turn restaurant search dialogues containing 11,677 training, 3,934 development, and 9,890 test utterances, each annotated with dialog acts and slot-value pairs. The model used 128-dimensional GRU hidden states, 100-dimensional word embeddings, and no dropout. In slot value prediction alone, the hybrid model achieved an F1 score of 85.2%, outperforming a CNN baseline (79.6%) and a basic Seq2Seq model without pointer mechanism (83.1%). More significantly, under artificially induced high-OOV conditions—created by subsampling training examples containing non-enumerable slots—the hybrid model demonstrated remarkable stability, suffering only a 5.4% F1 drop when training data was reduced to 5% of its original size, compared to 10.3% and 9.2% drops for CNN and basic Seq2Seq, respectively. In full SLU evaluation, the system incorporating the hybrid slot predictor achieved an F1 score of 82.7%, surpassing prior state-of-the-art methods based on SVMs (76.4%) and joint CNN-LSTM classifiers (79.8%). Case studies confirmed that the pointer mechanism enabled correct prediction of rare or OOV values (e.g., “asian oriental” for food type), whereas baselines either hallucinated in-vocabulary substitutes or failed to predict anything.

The invention thus provides a fully neural, end-to-end trainable solution for slot filling that is robust to OOV entities, requires no word-level supervision, and eliminates dependency on manual rules or domain-specific resources. Its modular design allows seamless integration into larger dialogue systems, and its data efficiency makes it particularly suitable for low-resource or rapidly evolving domains. By unifying generation and copying in a single probabilistic framework, the invention represents a significant advancement in the scalability and reliability of spoken language understanding for real-world applications.
