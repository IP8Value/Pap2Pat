# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of electrochemical energy storage systems, and more particularly to a method and system for modeling the dynamic electrical behavior of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries for use in battery management systems (BMS). The invention provides a computationally efficient, low-order equivalent circuit model that accurately captures the unique electrochemical characteristics of Li-S cells—including their dual-plateau voltage profile, strong temperature dependence, state-of-charge (SoC)-dependent open-circuit voltage (OCV), memory effects arising from polysulfide shuttle dynamics, and current-rate sensitivity—while remaining suitable for real-time implementation on embedded control hardware with limited computational resources. The model is specifically designed to support critical BMS functions such as SoC estimation, power capability prediction, and health monitoring in electric vehicles (EVs) and other applications requiring reliable and safe operation of Li-S battery packs. Unlike conventional equivalent circuit models developed for lithium-ion (Li-ion) chemistries, which assume relatively stable and predictable electrochemical behavior, the present invention incorporates novel parameterization techniques, behavioral reparameterization, and linearized state-space representations that explicitly account for the transient and history-dependent phenomena inherent to Li-S chemistry. The invention further includes a robust system identification framework based on prediction error minimization (PEM) applied to mixed-current pulse test data across multiple temperatures, enabling accurate extraction of model parameters that reflect both steady-state and dynamic responses under realistic operating conditions. This technical field sits at the intersection of electrochemistry, control theory, system identification, and embedded systems engineering, addressing a critical gap in the commercialization pathway for next-generation Li-S batteries by providing the foundational modeling infrastructure necessary for intelligent battery control and diagnostics.

## BACKGROUND

The transition toward sustainable transportation has intensified global demand for high-energy-density, cost-effective, and safe electrochemical energy storage solutions. Lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery technology has emerged as a promising candidate to replace conventional lithium-ion (Li-ion) systems due to its theoretical specific energy of approximately 2600 Wh/kg—more than double that of current Li-ion cells—and the abundance, low cost, and environmental benignity of sulfur. However, despite these advantages, widespread adoption of Li-S batteries in electric vehicles (EVs) and other demanding applications has been hindered by several intrinsic challenges related to their complex electrochemical mechanisms. Unlike Li-ion batteries, which rely on intercalation reactions that yield relatively smooth and reproducible voltage profiles, Li-S cells operate through multi-step conversion reactions involving soluble lithium polysulfide intermediates (Li₂S₈, Li₂S₆, Li₂S₄, etc.) that undergo redox transformations between elemental sulfur (S₈) at the cathode and lithium metal at the anode. This reaction pathway results in a characteristic discharge curve with two distinct voltage plateaus: a high plateau around 2.35 V associated with the reduction of S₈ to long-chain polysulfides, and a low plateau near 2.1 V corresponding to the further reduction to short-chain sulfides (Li₂S₂, Li₂S). Critically, the low plateau exhibits an extremely flat voltage response, rendering traditional open-circuit voltage (OCV)-based state-of-charge (SoC) estimation ineffective, as the OCV remains nearly constant over a wide range of SoC values and may even revert to approximately 2.1 V after rest regardless of the actual charge state.

Compounding this challenge is the polysulfide shuttle effect, wherein soluble high-order polysulfides diffuse from the cathode to the anode, where they are reduced to insoluble lower-order species that can redeposit on electrode surfaces or precipitate in the electrolyte. This parasitic process causes significant self-discharge during rest periods, reduces coulombic efficiency, accelerates capacity fade, and introduces strong history dependence into the cell’s electrical behavior—meaning that the present voltage response depends not only on the instantaneous current and SoC but also on prior cycling conditions, rest durations, and temperature exposure. Furthermore, the usable capacity and power capability of Li-S cells are highly sensitive to operational parameters such as discharge rate, temperature, and cycling protocol. High currents can induce rapid formation of insulating Li₂S layers that passivate the cathode, drastically reducing accessible capacity, while low temperatures exacerbate electrolyte viscosity increases and slow reaction kinetics, particularly at the transition between the two voltage plateaus where peak internal resistance is observed. These phenomena render standard Li-ion modeling approaches inadequate for Li-S systems.

In the domain of battery modeling, equivalent circuit network (ECN) models—such as the widely used Thevenin model comprising a voltage source, series resistance, and one or more resistor-capacitor (RC) parallel branches—have become the de facto standard for real-time BMS applications due to their low computational complexity and ability to reproduce transient voltage responses using only terminal current and voltage measurements. For Li-ion batteries, such models are typically parameterized using pulse discharge tests, with parameters expressed as functions of SoC, temperature, and sometimes cycle number. However, direct application of these methods to Li-S cells fails to capture essential physics: the OCV is not a single-valued function of SoC due to hysteresis and relaxation effects; internal resistance exhibits non-monotonic variation across the discharge curve with pronounced peaks; and model parameters show significant dependence on current magnitude and thermal history. Prior attempts to model Li-S cells using ECNs have largely focused on electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) at fixed SoC, making them unsuitable for dynamic cycling scenarios. Although recent studies have proposed operational ECN models with two or three RC pairs for specific Li-S pouch cells, these lack generalizability, do not systematically address temperature dependence, and often neglect the impact of current rate on parameter values.

Moreover, conventional ECN parameterization assumes that component values (e.g., R₀, Rₚ, Cₚ) remain constant over short time intervals during system identification, an assumption invalidated in Li-S cells by rapid SoC-induced shifts in OCV during high-current pulses. This leads to biased parameter estimates when using standard linear system identification techniques. Additionally, the physical interpretation of ECN elements in Li-S systems is ambiguous—the “capacitor” may represent double-layer effects, diffusion processes, or even pseudo-capacitive contributions from polysulfide redox couples—making it difficult to impose meaningful constraints during parameter fitting. There remains a critical need for a modeling framework that decouples behavioral observables (e.g., steady-state voltage drop, dynamic bandwidth, fraction of delayed response) from underlying circuit topology, enabling intuitive constraint application and robust identification even in the presence of SoC drift during testing. Finally, existing models rarely incorporate interpolation schemes that allow seamless operation across a continuous temperature range without resorting to high-dimensional lookup tables, which are impractical for embedded BMS implementations. Thus, the background art lacks a comprehensive, validated, and computationally tractable modeling solution tailored to the unique operational demands of Li-S batteries in real-world applications such as electric mobility.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and robust framework for modeling the dynamic electrical behavior of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries, specifically designed for integration into battery management systems (BMS) for electric vehicles and other high-performance applications. The core innovation lies in a behaviorally reparameterized, linearized equivalent circuit model that accurately captures the distinctive electrochemical characteristics of Li-S cells—including their dual-plateau voltage profile, strong temperature dependence, SoC-dependent open-circuit voltage (OCV) hysteresis, and current-rate sensitivity—while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for real-time embedded implementation. Unlike conventional approaches that treat equivalent circuit components (resistors, capacitors) as primary parameters, the invention introduces a set of intuitive behavioral parameters: total steady-state resistance (R_int), dynamic fraction (r_p), and dynamic bandwidth (U_p), which directly correspond to observable cell responses such as settled voltage drop under constant current, proportion of delayed versus instantaneous voltage response, and time constant of transient behavior, respectively. This reparameterization facilitates the imposition of physically meaningful constraints during system identification and improves numerical stability.

A key aspect of the invention is the derivation of a linearized state-space representation of the nonlinear SoC-dependent Thevenin model, which explicitly accounts for changes in OCV and internal states during high-current pulses—a phenomenon commonly neglected in prior art but critically important for accurate Li-S modeling. This linearization introduces additional terms in the state and output matrices that capture the coupling between SoC evolution and voltage dynamics, thereby correcting for biases that would otherwise arise from assuming frozen parameters during identification windows. The model is parameterized using a rigorous system identification methodology based on Prediction Error Minimization (PEM), applied to a novel mixed-current pulse test protocol conducted across multiple temperatures (20°C to 50°C). This protocol employs pulses of varying amplitudes (e.g., 290 mA, 1450 mA, 2900 mA) with sufficient rest periods to isolate current-dependent effects without confounding aging influences, enabling the extraction of parameters that reflect realistic operational conditions.

The identified parameters are then represented as smooth, differentiable functions of SoC using piecewise polynomial approximations, with separate polynomials fitted to the high and low voltage plateaus for OCV and internal resistance to accommodate their distinct behaviors, seamlessly blended via a partial sinusoidal transition function. Other parameters, exhibiting less pronounced plateau dependence, are modeled with single polynomials. To enable operation across a continuous temperature range, the coefficients of these polynomials are stored in compact one-dimensional lookup tables and interpolated linearly between discrete calibration temperatures, avoiding the complexity of two-dimensional surface fits while preserving model differentiability—essential for advanced state estimation techniques such as Kalman filtering. The resulting model demonstrates high accuracy (e.g., root mean square error of 32 mV) when validated against realistic drive-cycle profiles (e.g., NEDC) under variable thermal conditions, outperforming simplified Li-ion-derived models in capturing Li-S-specific phenomena. The invention thus provides a complete, validated, and deployable modeling solution that bridges the gap between fundamental Li-S electrochemistry and practical BMS requirements, enabling reliable SoC estimation, power prediction, and health monitoring for next-generation battery systems.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

### Generating the Cell Model Module—Equivalent Circuit Example

The foundation of the present invention is a modified Thevenin equivalent circuit model specifically adapted to represent the dynamic voltage response of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery cells under operational conditions. This model serves as the core computational module within a battery management system (BMS) and is structured to balance physical interpretability with mathematical tractability for real-time implementation. The basic topology consists of an open-circuit voltage (OCV) source, denoted U_OC(X), which is a function of the state-of-charge (SoC) X; a series ohmic resistance R_0(X); and a single parallel resistor-capacitor (RC) branch comprising a polarization resistance R_p(X) and a capacitance C_p(X). The terminal voltage V_t is given by the sum of the OCV, the voltage drop across R_0 due to load current I_L, and the voltage across the RC branch, U_p. While this structure resembles conventional Li-ion ECN models, its parameterization and underlying assumptions are fundamentally re-engineered to address the unique challenges of Li-S chemistry.

Crucially, all model parameters—U_OC, R_0, R_p, and C_p—are treated as explicit functions of SoC, reflecting the strong state dependence observed in Li-S cells. This contrasts with many prior approaches that assume quasi-static parameters over short time horizons, an assumption invalidated by the rapid SoC changes during high-current pulses and the non-monotonic, plateau-dependent behavior of Li-S parameters. The SoC itself evolves according to the standard Coulomb counting equation: dX/dt = -I_L / Q_cap, where Q_cap is the nominal capacity of the cell in ampere-hours (Ah), converted to coulombs for consistency in units. The dynamics of the RC branch voltage U_p are governed by the differential equation: dU_p/dt = -(1/(R_p C_p)) U_p + (1/C_p) I_L. Together, these equations form a nonlinear state-space system with two states: X (SoC) and U_p (polarization voltage).

However, direct use of this nonlinear model in system identification poses significant challenges. During a current pulse, the SoC changes appreciably, causing U_OC to drift. If identification algorithms assume U_OC is constant over the pulse duration—as is typical in linear PEM frameworks—the resulting parameter estimates become biased. To overcome this, the invention introduces a linearized small-signal representation around a defined operating point. The operating point is characterized by a nominal SoC X̄, a nominal polarization voltage Ū_p, and a nominal current Ī_L chosen such that the derivative of the terminal voltage is zero (i.e., steady-state condition for the linearized model, though not necessarily for the full nonlinear system). Perturbations from this operating point are defined as δX = X - X̄, δU_p = U_p - Ū_p, δI_L = I_L - Ī_L, and δV_t = V_t - V̄_t.

Linearizing the nonlinear state equations yields a linear time-invariant (LTI) system in terms of these perturbations:
d(δX)/dt = -δI_L / Q_cap  
d(δU_p)/dt = a_21 δX - (1/τ_p) δU_p + (1/C_p) δI_L  
δV_t = c_11 δX + δU_p + R_0 δI_L  

Here, τ_p = R_p C_p is the time constant of the RC branch, c_11 = dU_OC/dX|_{X=X̄} represents the local slope of the OCV-SoC curve at the operating point, and a_21 is a coupling term that arises from the SoC dependence of R_p and C_p (though in practice, a_21 is often negligible compared to c_11 and may be omitted for simplicity). The inclusion of the c_11 δX term in the output equation is a critical innovation: it explicitly accounts for the change in OCV due to SoC drift during the identification window, thereby correcting for a major source of error in conventional approaches. This linearized model retains the essential dynamics of the original nonlinear system while being amenable to standard linear system identification techniques such as Prediction Error Minimization (PEM).

To enhance usability and enforce physical plausibility, the model is further reparameterized in terms of behavioral variables rather than raw circuit components. Specifically, the total steady-state resistance R_int(X) = R_0(X) + R_p(X) is introduced, representing the asymptotic voltage drop per unit current after all transients have decayed. The dynamic fraction r_p(X) = R_p(X) / R_int(X) quantifies the proportion of the total resistance that contributes to the delayed (capacitive) response, ranging from 0 (purely resistive) to 1 (purely capacitive). The dynamic bandwidth U_p(X) = 1 / τ_p(X) = 1 / (R_p(X) C_p(X)) defines the inverse time constant, governing how quickly the system responds to changes in current. This behavioral parameterization offers several advantages: first, constraints on R_int, r_p, and U_p are more intuitive (e.g., R_int > 0, 0 ≤ r_p ≤ 1, U_p > 0) and directly relate to measurable cell behaviors; second, it avoids numerical issues associated with reciprocal parameters (e.g., estimating C_p directly can be ill-conditioned if R_p is small); third, it simplifies the mapping back to conventional ECN parameters via R_p = r_p R_int, R_0 = (1 - r_p) R_int, and C_p = 1 / (U_p R_p) = 1 / (U_p r_p R_int).

This reparameterized, linearized model forms the basis for the cell model module. It is implemented in software as a set of differential and algebraic equations that can be integrated forward in time using standard numerical methods (e.g., Euler or Runge-Kutta) within a BMS microcontroller. The inputs are the measured load current I_L(t) and ambient temperature T (treated as a slowly varying parameter), and the outputs include the predicted terminal voltage V_t(t), as well as internal states useful for diagnostics (e.g., U_p for assessing polarization buildup). The model’s structure ensures compatibility with observer-based state estimation algorithms such as the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) or Unscented Kalman Filter (UKF), which require differentiable system models to propagate uncertainty and correct state estimates using voltage measurements. By embedding the physics of Li-S behavior into a mathematically sound and computationally lean framework, this cell model module enables accurate real-time prediction of battery performance under diverse driving and environmental conditions.

### Generating the Memory Effect Model

A defining characteristic of lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries that distinguishes them from conventional lithium-ion systems is the presence of significant memory effects stemming primarily from the polysulfide shuttle mechanism and the precipitation/dissolution dynamics of lithium sulfide species. These effects manifest as history-dependent voltage responses, where the terminal voltage at a given state-of-charge (SoC) and current is influenced not only by the immediate past but also by longer-term cycling patterns, rest durations, and thermal exposure. Traditional equivalent circuit models, which assume Markovian dynamics (i.e., future behavior depends only on the present state), fail to capture these phenomena, leading to persistent errors in voltage prediction and state estimation. The present invention addresses this limitation by incorporating an implicit memory effect model through its novel parameterization strategy and system identification protocol, rather than introducing explicit additional state variables that would increase computational burden.

The memory effects in Li-S cells arise from several interrelated processes. First, during discharge, soluble long-chain polysulfides (Li₂S₈, Li₂S₆) generated in the high-voltage plateau diffuse into the electrolyte and may migrate to the lithium anode, where they are reduced to insoluble short-chain sulfides (Li₂S₂, Li₂S). These deposits form an insulating layer on the anode surface, increasing interfacial resistance and altering reaction kinetics. Upon subsequent charging or resting, partial re-dissolution of this layer occurs, but the extent depends on the concentration of remaining polysulfides in the electrolyte—a quantity that itself depends on prior discharge depth, current rate, and rest time. Second, in the low-voltage plateau, the precipitation of Li₂S creates a porous but resistive film on the cathode, whose morphology and conductivity evolve with cycling history, affecting both capacity utilization and impedance. Third, the open-circuit voltage (OCV) exhibits relaxation behavior: after a current pulse, the voltage does not immediately settle to a value determined solely by SoC but continues to drift over minutes to hours as polysulfide concentrations equilibrate and solid phases reorganize. This means that the OCV measured after a short rest may differ significantly from that after a long rest, even at identical SoC.

Rather than attempting to model these complex physicochemical processes explicitly—which would require high-fidelity electrochemical models incompatible with BMS constraints—the invention leverages the fact that memory effects are partially encoded in the SoC dependence of model parameters when identification is performed under representative conditions. Specifically, the mixed-current pulse test protocol used for parameter identification (described in detail below) includes rest periods of fixed duration (e.g., 10 minutes) between pulses. As a result, the identified parameters R_int(X), r_p(X), and U_p(X) inherently reflect the average state of the cell after such a rest interval, effectively embedding a "memory horizon" corresponding to the rest time. For instance, the OCV function U_OC(X) derived from this protocol represents the relaxed voltage after 10 minutes of rest, not the instantaneous post-pulse voltage. Similarly, the internal resistance R_int(X) captures the impedance state following partial re-equilibration of polysulfide concentrations.

This approach constitutes an implicit memory model: by calibrating the behavioral parameters against data that includes standardized rest periods, the model automatically accounts for the dominant relaxation processes occurring within that timeframe. When deployed in a BMS, the model will therefore predict voltages consistent with the expected post-rest behavior, aligning with typical EV usage patterns where rest periods (e.g., traffic stops) last several minutes. Moreover, the linearized state-space formulation includes the c_11 term (dU_OC/dX), which accommodates gradual OCV shifts during pulses, indirectly capturing short-term memory effects related to SoC drift. For longer-term memory (e.g., cycle-to-cycle degradation), the invention provides a framework for updating model parameters based on health indicators, though this is considered an extension beyond the base embodiment.

Critically, the piecewise polynomial representation of parameters—particularly the separate fitting of U_OC and R_int over the high and low plateaus—allows the model to reproduce the distinct memory signatures of each region. In the high plateau, where polysulfide shuttle is active, the OCV shows greater sensitivity to rest duration and prior current history; the model captures this through a steeper or more variable U_OC(X) function in this region. In the low plateau, where precipitation dominates, the resistance R_int(X) exhibits a sharp rise near end-of-discharge that depends on cumulative discharge depth; the polynomial fit reflects this trend. The smooth sinusoidal blending between plateau-specific functions ensures differentiability while preserving regional characteristics. Thus, without adding explicit memory states, the invention achieves a pragmatic and effective representation of Li-S memory effects through careful experimental design, behavioral parameterization, and functional approximation, striking an optimal balance between accuracy and computational feasibility for BMS deployment.

### Parameterisation by Prediction Error Minimisation (PEM)

The accurate and robust parameterization of the lithium-sulfur (Li-S) battery model described herein is achieved through a systematic application of Prediction Error Minimization (PEM), a well-established system identification technique that provides statistically optimal parameter estimates under mild assumptions about measurement noise. PEM operates by minimizing a cost function defined as the weighted norm of the prediction errors—the differences between measured terminal voltage data and the voltage predicted by the model over a sequence of input-output observations. This approach is particularly well-suited to the present invention because it naturally accommodates the linearized state-space representation of the cell model and allows for the incorporation of prior knowledge through parameter constraints.

The PEM framework begins with the linearized discrete-time model derived from the continuous-time equations presented earlier. Using a sampling interval Δt, the state vector z_k = [δX_k, δU_p,k]^T evolves according to z_{k+1} = A z_k + b δI_{L,k}, and the output is δV_{t,k} = c^T z_k + d δI_{L,k}, where A, b, c, and d are matrices/vectors containing the behavioral parameters R_int, r_p, U_p, and c_11 (the OCV slope). For each current pulse in the identification dataset, a local operating point (X̄, Ū_p, Ī_L) is defined, typically as the average SoC and current over the pulse window. The measured current I_{L,k} and voltage V_{t,k} are then detrended by subtracting the nominal values to obtain δI_{L,k} and δV_{t,k}. The prediction error at time k is ε_k(θ) = V_{t,k} - V̂_{t,k}(θ), where θ = [R_int, r_p, U_p, c_11]^T is the parameter vector to be estimated, and V̂_{t,k}(θ) is the model prediction computed by simulating the linearized system with input δI_{L,k} and initial state z_0 = 0 (assuming the system starts at the operating point).

The cost function to be minimized is the sum of squared prediction errors over the identification window: V_N(θ) = (1/N) Σ_{k=1}^N ε_k(θ)^2, where N is the number of samples in the window. In practice, the window is extended to include 300 seconds before and after each current pulse to capture pre-pulse baseline and post-pulse relaxation dynamics, ensuring that transient effects are fully observed. The minimization problem min_θ V_N(θ) is solved using numerical optimization algorithms such as Levenberg-Marquardt or trust-region reflective methods, which iteratively adjust θ to reduce the cost function. Crucially, the behavioral parameterization enables the imposition of hard constraints during optimization: R_int > 0, 0 ≤ r_p ≤ 1, U_p > 0, and c_11 within expected bounds based on preliminary OCV measurements. These constraints prevent non-physical solutions and improve convergence.

The identification is performed independently for each current pulse across the entire discharge cycle, yielding a set of parameter estimates {θ_i} associated with the average SoC X_i of each pulse. This pulse-wise approach isolates the effects of SoC from those of current rate and temperature, as each pulse is short enough that SoC changes are modest, yet long enough to excite the RC dynamics. To investigate current dependence, pulses of multiple amplitudes (e.g., 0.1C, 0.5C, 1.0C rates) are interleaved in the test protocol, allowing comparison of θ_i across rates at similar SoC. Similarly, tests are repeated at discrete temperatures (20°C, 30°C, 50°C), generating temperature-specific parameter trajectories.

Following identification, the discrete parameter estimates {θ_i(X_i, T)} are smoothed and represented as continuous functions of SoC using least-squares polynomial fitting. For parameters exhibiting distinct behaviors in the high and low voltage plateaus (notably U_OC and R_int), separate polynomials are fitted to data points in each plateau region, then blended using a partial sinusoidal transition function g_{m,c}(X) that ensures C¹ continuity (continuous first derivative). The transition point c_T (e.g., c_20 = 0.68 at 20°C) is determined as the SoC where the two plateau polynomials intersect or where the resistance peak occurs. Parameters with weaker plateau dependence (r_p, U_p) are fitted with single polynomials. The polynomial coefficients p_j(T) for each temperature T are stored in lookup tables. For operation at intermediate temperatures, linear interpolation of these coefficients between calibration points generates temperature-adaptive parameter functions, preserving model differentiability for Kalman filter applications.

This PEM-based parameterization workflow delivers a model that is both data-driven and physically informed. By leveraging the linearized model’s ability to account for SoC drift during pulses, it avoids the bias inherent in conventional frozen-parameter approaches. The behavioral reparameterization ensures numerical robustness and interpretability, while the piecewise polynomial representation with temperature interpolation provides a compact, accurate, and computationally efficient mapping from SoC and temperature to model parameters. Validation against independent drive-cycle data confirms that this methodology yields a model capable of predicting Li-S cell voltage with high fidelity under realistic operating conditions, fulfilling the stringent requirements of automotive BMS applications.
