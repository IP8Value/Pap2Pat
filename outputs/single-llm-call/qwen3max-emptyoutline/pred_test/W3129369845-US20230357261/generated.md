## BACKGROUND

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a devastating inflammatory bowel disease that predominantly affects premature infants, particularly those born before 32 weeks of gestation and weighing less than 1500 grams at birth. Characterized by acute intestinal inflammation, mucosal necrosis, bacterial translocation, and systemic sepsis, NEC remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality in neonatal intensive care units worldwide, with an overall mortality rate approaching 30% and significantly higher among extremely low birth weight infants. Despite decades of clinical research and advances in neonatal care, no effective pharmacologic therapy currently exists for the prevention or treatment of NEC, and management remains largely supportive, often culminating in emergency surgical resection of necrotic bowel segments when medical therapy fails. The etiology of NEC is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay between intestinal immaturity, dysregulated host immune responses, microbial colonization, and enteral feeding practices. A central pathogenic mechanism involves exaggerated signaling through toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), which is expressed at markedly higher levels in the premature intestinal epithelium compared to term infants. Upon activation by luminal bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), TLR4 triggers pro-inflammatory cytokine production, impairs mucosal repair, induces enterocyte apoptosis, and disrupts barrier integrity—collectively promoting the cascade of events that culminate in full-thickness intestinal necrosis.

Current preventive strategies have focused almost exclusively on postnatal interventions, including the use of human breast milk instead of formula, probiotic supplementation, and cautious advancement of enteral feeds. While breastfeeding is associated with a significantly reduced risk of NEC, many premature infants are unable to receive sufficient volumes of maternal milk due to lactation challenges, necessitating the use of donor milk or formula—both of which carry elevated NEC risk compared to mother’s own milk. Moreover, even among exclusively breastfed preterm infants, cases of NEC still occur, indicating that additional protective mechanisms may be deficient or overwhelmed in susceptible individuals. This underscores a critical unmet need for novel therapeutic approaches that target the underlying molecular pathways driving NEC pathogenesis.

Recent evidence suggests that the origins of NEC may extend beyond the postnatal period and involve in utero developmental programming of the fetal gut. Epidemiological studies have shown that infants exposed to chorioamnionitis—an intrauterine infection characterized by maternal inflammation—exhibit increased incidence and severity of NEC, implying that the prenatal environment can influence postnatal disease susceptibility. Furthermore, maternal dietary patterns during pregnancy, such as adherence to a Mediterranean diet rich in cruciferous vegetables, have been correlated with reduced rates of preterm birth and potentially lower NEC risk, although the biological mechanisms remain unclear. These observations raise the possibility that maternal–fetal signaling pathways exist that can modulate neonatal intestinal immunity and resilience to inflammatory injury.

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) is a ligand-activated transcription factor historically studied for its role in xenobiotic metabolism but now recognized as a critical regulator of mucosal immunity, epithelial homeostasis, and host–microbe interactions. AHR is activated by a diverse array of endogenous, dietary, and microbial ligands, including indole derivatives produced by commensal bacteria from dietary tryptophan, as well as phytochemicals such as indole-3-carbinol (I3C) found abundantly in cruciferous vegetables like broccoli, cabbage, and kale. In the adult intestine, AHR signaling has been shown to promote the maintenance of group 3 innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s), regulatory T cells, and intraepithelial lymphocytes (IELs), and to enhance barrier function through the induction of antimicrobial peptides and tight junction proteins. However, the role of AHR in the developing neonatal gut—and particularly in the context of NEC—has remained largely unexplored.

Prior art does not disclose or suggest that AHR signaling is deficient in the premature infant intestine or that maternal administration of AHR ligands during pregnancy can confer protection against NEC in offspring. Nor does the prior art teach that AHR activation in the neonatal intestinal epithelium directly suppresses TLR4 expression and signaling—a key pathogenic driver of NEC—through mechanisms involving microRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation. Furthermore, while certain FDA-approved drugs have been incidentally noted to possess AHR agonist activity, none have been identified, optimized, or clinically evaluated for the specific purpose of preventing or treating NEC via prenatal or postnatal AHR activation. The present invention addresses these critical gaps by establishing a novel mechanistic link between maternal–fetal AHR signaling and NEC pathogenesis, and by providing specific AHR agonists—including dietary compounds and repurposed pharmaceuticals—that can be administered either during pregnancy or after birth to prevent or mitigate this life-threatening condition.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and compositions for the prevention and treatment of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and other inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract in infants through the activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR). The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that AHR signaling is significantly reduced in the intestinal epithelium of infants with NEC across multiple species, including humans, mice, and piglets, and that this deficiency contributes causally to disease pathogenesis rather than merely representing a consequence of inflammation. The inventors have further demonstrated that AHR activation—whether achieved through maternal dietary supplementation during pregnancy, direct administration to the neonate, or inclusion in infant nutritional formulas—confers robust protection against experimental NEC by specifically suppressing toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling and expression in the intestinal epithelium. This suppression occurs through AHR-dependent upregulation of microRNAs known to negatively regulate TLR4, including miR-146b, let-7i, and miR-223, thereby interrupting a key inflammatory cascade that drives mucosal injury in NEC.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method of preventing or treating NEC in an infant comprising administering to the infant an effective amount of an AHR agonist. In another aspect, the invention provides a method of preventing NEC in an infant comprising administering to the mother during pregnancy or lactation an effective amount of an AHR agonist, wherein the AHR agonist is transferred to the fetus or neonate and activates AHR in the infant’s intestinal epithelium, thereby reducing TLR4 signaling and conferring protection against NEC. The AHR agonist may be a naturally occurring dietary compound such as indole-3-carbinol (I3C), or a synthetic or repurposed pharmaceutical agent such as the benzimidazole derivative designated herein as “A18,” which has been identified through high-throughput screening as a potent and clinically relevant AHR activator capable of reducing TLR4 signaling in human intestinal tissue ex vivo and preventing NEC in murine models.

In a further aspect, the invention provides an infant nutritional formula comprising an AHR agonist, wherein the formula is designed for enteral administration to premature or at-risk infants to activate AHR in the intestinal epithelium and reduce the risk of developing NEC. The formula may be a standard bovine-based or amino acid-based formula supplemented with I3C, A18, or another AHR ligand, or it may be a human milk-based fortifier enriched with AHR-activating compounds. The invention also encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising an AHR agonist in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, formulated for oral, enteral, or intravenous administration to pregnant women, lactating mothers, or neonates.

Additionally, the invention includes methods of identifying subjects at increased risk for NEC based on reduced AHR expression or activity in intestinal biopsies or stool samples, and methods of monitoring therapeutic response by assessing AHR pathway activation (e.g., CYP1A1 expression) or downstream suppression of TLR4 signaling (e.g., reduced TNF-α or IL-6 levels). The protective effects of AHR activation are shown to be independent of interleukin-22 (IL-22), intestinal barrier integrity, and intraepithelial lymphocytes—mechanisms previously implicated in AHR-mediated mucosal protection in adult models—thereby revealing a novel, epithelium-intrinsic anti-inflammatory pathway unique to the neonatal gut.

Collectively, the invention establishes a paradigm-shifting approach to NEC prevention by targeting a previously unrecognized in utero window of opportunity for intervention, leveraging maternal–fetal signaling to program neonatal intestinal resilience, and repurposing safe, orally bioavailable AHR agonists for clinical translation. The disclosed methods and compositions offer significant advantages over existing strategies by addressing the root molecular cause of NEC rather than merely mitigating secondary consequences, and by enabling both prenatal prophylaxis in cases of threatened preterm labor and postnatal therapy in high-risk neonates.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### A. Compounds

The present invention encompasses the use of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonists for the prevention and treatment of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and related inflammatory gastrointestinal disorders in infants. AHR agonists suitable for use in the invention include naturally occurring dietary ligands, microbial metabolites, synthetic small molecules, and repurposed pharmaceutical agents that bind to and activate AHR, leading to its nuclear translocation, dimerization with the AHR nuclear translocator (ARNT), and transcriptional regulation of target genes such as CYP1A1, CYP1B1, and AHRR. The invention specifically contemplates AHR agonists that are orally bioavailable, stable in the gastrointestinal tract, non-toxic at therapeutic doses, and capable of crossing the placental barrier or being secreted into breast milk to achieve effective concentrations in the fetal or neonatal intestine.

One class of AHR agonists according to the invention comprises indole derivatives, particularly those derived from the breakdown of glucobrassicin in cruciferous vegetables. Indole-3-carbinol (I3C), a natural compound formed upon the enzymatic hydrolysis of glucobrassicin in foods such as broccoli, Brussels sprouts, and cabbage, is a prototypical AHR ligand that has been shown in the present studies to effectively activate AHR in the neonatal intestinal epithelium and protect against experimental NEC. I3C is converted in the acidic environment of the stomach to a variety of condensation products, including 3,3'-diindolylmethane (DIM), which also exhibits AHR agonist activity. For use in the invention, I3C may be administered as a purified compound, as a standardized botanical extract, or as part of a maternal diet enriched in cruciferous vegetables. The effective dose of I3C for NEC prevention in murine models ranges from 25 to 50 mg per kg body weight per day, with 25 mg/kg/day selected as the optimal balance between efficacy and safety; extrapolation to human dosing would consider factors such as metabolic rate, placental transfer efficiency, and established safety profiles in pregnancy.

Another class of AHR agonists disclosed herein includes synthetic or semi-synthetic small molecules identified through high-throughput screening of clinical compound libraries. The lead compound, designated “A18,” is a benzimidazole derivative with the chemical formula C16H14F3N3O2S and a molecular weight of 369 Da. A18 was identified from a library of FDA-approved drugs using an AHR-luciferase reporter assay in intestinal epithelial cells and subsequently validated for its ability to induce CYP1A1 expression in vitro and in vivo, suppress TLR4 signaling in mouse and human intestinal tissues, and prevent experimental NEC in neonatal mice. Structural analysis indicates that the benzimidazole core of A18 serves as the primary pharmacophore for AHR binding, consistent with the known preference of AHR for planar, hydrophobic heterocyclic structures. A18 is orally bioavailable, achieves effective concentrations in the neonatal gut following maternal or direct administration, and demonstrates a favorable safety profile at doses up to 300 mg/kg/day in murine models. Given its status as a derivative of proton pump inhibitors (e.g., omeprazole), which are widely used in pregnancy and neonates, A18 represents a promising candidate for rapid clinical repurposing.

Additional AHR agonists encompassed by the invention include microbial tryptophan metabolites such as indole, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), indole-3-aldehyde (IAld), and kynurenine, which are produced by commensal bacteria including Lactobacillus spp. and Bifidobacterium spp. from dietary tryptophan. These metabolites are present in human breast milk and may contribute to its protective effects against NEC through AHR activation. The invention thus includes methods of enriching infant formulas or maternal diets with probiotic strains that produce AHR-activating tryptophan metabolites, or with prebiotic fibers that promote the growth of such bacteria. Other contemplated AHR ligands include flavonoids (e.g., quercetin, resveratrol), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (e.g., β-naphthoflavone), and endogenous ligands such as FICZ (6-formylindolo[3,2-b]carbazole), although the latter may be less suitable for chronic administration due to potential toxicity.

All AHR agonists used in the invention are selected based on their ability to: (1) bind AHR with sufficient affinity to induce conformational change and nuclear translocation; (2) activate transcription of canonical AHR target genes in intestinal epithelial cells; (3) suppress TLR4 expression and signaling in a dose-dependent manner; (4) demonstrate efficacy in preventing or treating experimental NEC in preclinical models; and (5) exhibit an acceptable safety and pharmacokinetic profile for use in pregnant women, lactating mothers, or neonates. Compounds may be used individually or in combination to achieve synergistic AHR activation or to target multiple pathways involved in NEC pathogenesis.

### B. Methods of Treatment

The present invention provides methods for the prevention and treatment of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and other inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract in infants through the administration of aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonists. These methods are predicated on the discovery that AHR signaling is deficient in the intestinal epithelium of infants with NEC and that restoration of AHR activity—either prenatally via maternal administration or postnatally via direct delivery to the neonate—confers significant protection against disease development and progression. The methods are applicable to premature infants at high risk for NEC, including those born before 32 weeks of gestation, with birth weights less than 1500 grams, or with additional risk factors such as prolonged antibiotic exposure, formula feeding, or history of intrauterine growth restriction.

In one embodiment, the method comprises administering to an infant at risk for or diagnosed with NEC an effective amount of an AHR agonist, wherein the AHR agonist activates AHR in the intestinal epithelium, leading to downregulation of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) expression and signaling, reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokine production (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), and attenuation of mucosal injury. The AHR agonist may be administered enterally via oral gavage, mixed into infant formula or breast milk, or delivered parenterally in severe cases where enteral access is contraindicated. The timing of administration may be prophylactic (initiated at birth or upon admission to the NICU in high-risk infants) or therapeutic (initiated upon early signs of feeding intolerance, abdominal distension, or radiographic evidence of pneumatosis intestinalis). Dosing regimens are tailored to the specific AHR agonist used, with exemplary doses including 25–50 mg/kg/day of indole-3-carbinol (I3C) or 100–300 mg/kg/day of the compound A18, adjusted based on clinical response and tolerability.

In another embodiment, the method comprises administering to a pregnant woman at risk for preterm delivery an effective amount of an AHR agonist during the third trimester of pregnancy, wherein the AHR agonist crosses the placental barrier and activates AHR in the fetal intestinal epithelium, thereby programming the developing gut for enhanced resilience to postnatal inflammatory insults. This prenatal intervention strategy is particularly advantageous in cases of threatened preterm labor, where there is a narrow window of opportunity to administer protective agents before delivery. Maternal administration may be achieved through oral supplementation with I3C, A18, or a diet enriched in AHR-activating foods (e.g., cruciferous vegetables), with dosing initiated as early as 24 weeks of gestation and continued until delivery. The efficacy of this approach is supported by murine studies demonstrating that maternal I3C or A18 administration induces CYP1A1 expression in fetal intestines at embryonic day 17.5 and significantly reduces NEC severity in offspring subjected to experimental disease induction.

In a further embodiment, the method comprises administering to a lactating mother an effective amount of an AHR agonist, wherein the compound is secreted into breast milk and delivered to the nursing infant, activating AHR in the neonatal gut and providing ongoing protection against NEC. This approach leverages the natural route of maternal–neonatal transfer and is especially beneficial for mothers who are able to provide some volume of breast milk but require supplementation with formula. The AHR agonist may be administered to the mother orally during the postpartum period, with concentrations in breast milk monitored to ensure therapeutic levels in the infant.

The methods of treatment according to the invention may be combined with standard supportive care for NEC, including bowel rest, gastric decompression, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and fluid resuscitation. They may also be integrated with other preventive strategies such as probiotic supplementation, standardized feeding protocols, and avoidance of unnecessary antibiotics. Importantly, the AHR-targeted therapies disclosed herein address the root molecular pathology of NEC—dysregulated TLR4 signaling—rather than merely managing symptoms, offering the potential to significantly reduce the incidence of severe NEC, the need for surgical intervention, and associated mortality and long-term morbidity such as short bowel syndrome and neurodevelopmental impairment.

### C. Infant Nutritional Formula Comprising Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor (AHR) Agonists for the Prevention and Treatment of Inflammatory Disorders

The present invention provides an infant nutritional formula specifically designed for the prevention and treatment of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and other inflammatory gastrointestinal disorders in premature and high-risk infants, wherein the formula is supplemented with one or more aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonists. Standard infant formulas—whether bovine milk-based, soy-based, or amino acid-based—are known risk factors for NEC due to their lack of bioactive components present in human breast milk that support intestinal maturation and immune regulation. The inventive formula overcomes this limitation by incorporating AHR-activating compounds that mimic or enhance the protective effects of breast milk through targeted modulation of neonatal intestinal immunity.

The base formula may be any commercially available or custom-prepared nutritional product suitable for enteral feeding in premature infants, including but not limited to Similac Special Care, Enfamil Premature, or Neosure. To this base, an effective amount of an AHR agonist is added, such that upon ingestion, the compound reaches the distal small intestine—the primary site of NEC pathology—and activates AHR in the intestinal epithelium. Exemplary AHR agonists for inclusion in the formula include indole-3-carbinol (I3C), 3,3'-diindolylmethane (DIM), the synthetic compound A18, or microbial tryptophan metabolites such as indole-3-aldehyde. The concentration of the AHR agonist is optimized to achieve therapeutic efficacy without altering the taste, stability, or nutritional composition of the formula; for instance, I3C may be included at a final concentration of 0.1–1.0 mg/mL, corresponding to a daily intake of 25–50 mg/kg for a typical feeding volume.

In one embodiment, the formula is a human milk-based fortifier, wherein pooled donor human milk is concentrated and supplemented with protein, minerals, and vitamins to meet the nutritional needs of very low birth weight infants, and further enriched with an AHR agonist to boost its inherent protective properties. Human milk naturally contains AHR ligands derived from the mother’s diet and gut microbiota, but concentrations may be suboptimal in mothers of premature infants due to stress, illness, or dietary restrictions. Fortification with additional AHR agonists ensures consistent and reliable delivery of protective signals to the infant gut.

In another embodiment, the formula is designed as a ready-to-feed liquid or powder that can be reconstituted with water or breast milk, allowing for flexible administration in hospital and home settings. The AHR agonist may be microencapsulated or formulated with stabilizers (e.g., cyclodextrins, lipids) to protect it from degradation in the upper gastrointestinal tract and ensure release in the ileum. Quality control measures include testing for AHR agonist content, sterility, osmolality, and absence of contaminants, in compliance with FDA regulations for infant formula.

Clinical use of the inventive formula involves initiating feedings as per standard NICU protocols, with close monitoring of tolerance, growth, and signs of NEC. Infants receiving the AHR-supplemented formula are expected to exhibit reduced incidence of feeding intolerance, lower levels of inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., fecal calprotectin, serum IL-6), and decreased rates of confirmed NEC compared to those receiving standard formula. The formula may be used as a sole source of nutrition or as a supplement to mother’s own milk, providing a practical and scalable solution to bridge the gap between ideal (exclusive breastfeeding) and real-world feeding practices in the NICU.

### D. Maternal Administration of an Aryl Hydrocarbon Receptor (AHR) Agonist

The present invention provides a method of preventing necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) in offspring through the maternal administration of an aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonist during pregnancy or lactation. This approach capitalizes on the natural transfer of bioactive compounds from mother to fetus or neonate via the placenta or breast milk, respectively, to deliver protective AHR signaling to the developing intestinal epithelium during critical windows of vulnerability. The method is particularly suited for pregnancies at high risk of preterm delivery, where antenatal intervention can prime the fetal gut for enhanced resilience to postnatal inflammatory challenges.

During pregnancy, the AHR agonist is administered orally to the mother, typically beginning in the late second or early third trimester (e.g., 24–28 weeks of gestation) and continuing until delivery. The compound crosses the placental barrier via passive diffusion or active transport mechanisms, reaching the fetal circulation and accumulating in the developing intestine. Murine studies confirm that maternal administration of indole-3-carbinol (I3C) or the compound A18 induces expression of the AHR target gene Cyp1a1 in fetal intestines as early as embryonic day 17.5, demonstrating functional activation of the pathway in utero. This prenatal AHR signaling programs the intestinal epithelium to exhibit reduced TLR4 expression and blunted inflammatory responses to bacterial ligands after birth, thereby lowering the threshold for NEC development.

During lactation, the AHR agonist is administered to the mother postpartum, where it is secreted into breast milk and ingested by the nursing infant. Human and murine data show that breast milk contains maternally derived AHR ligands that activate the receptor in the neonatal gut, contributing to its well-established protective effects against NEC. Supplementation of the mother’s diet or direct oral dosing with additional AHR agonists (e.g., I3C capsules, A18 tablets) enhances the concentration of these protective factors in milk, ensuring adequate delivery to the infant even when milk volumes are limited. This strategy is especially valuable for mothers of extremely premature infants who may struggle with lactation but can still provide some breast milk as a vehicle for AHR agonist delivery.

The AHR agonists suitable for maternal administration include dietary compounds (e.g., I3C from cruciferous vegetable extracts), pharmaceutical agents (e.g., A18, omeprazole), or combinations thereof. Dosing is calibrated to achieve therapeutic levels in the fetus or neonate while maintaining maternal safety; for example, I3C may be given at 25–50 mg/kg/day to the mother, translating to effective but non-toxic exposure in the offspring. Safety considerations include avoiding compounds with known teratogenicity or adverse effects on fetal development, and selecting agents with established use in pregnancy (e.g., proton pump inhibitors like omeprazole, from which A18 is derived).

This maternal administration strategy offers several advantages over direct neonatal treatment: it leverages natural biological pathways of nutrient and signal transfer, avoids the need for invasive procedures in fragile newborns, and provides continuous protection during the highest-risk period immediately after birth. It also aligns with public health recommendations to optimize maternal health and nutrition as a means of improving neonatal outcomes, representing a paradigm shift from reactive postnatal care to proactive prenatal prevention of NEC.

### E. Pharmaceutical Compositions and Administration

The present invention provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising an aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonist in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, formulated for administration to pregnant women, lactating mothers, or neonates for the prevention or treatment of necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC). These compositions are designed to deliver effective concentrations of the AHR agonist to the target site—the fetal or neonatal intestinal epithelium—while ensuring stability, safety, and ease of use in clinical settings.

For maternal administration during pregnancy, the composition may be formulated as an oral tablet, capsule, or liquid suspension containing I3C, A18, or another AHR agonist, along with excipients such as microcrystalline cellulose, lactose, magnesium stearate, and film-coating agents to enhance stability and palatability. Enteric coatings may be employed to protect acid-labile compounds like I3C from degradation in the stomach, ensuring release in the small intestine for optimal absorption. Dosage forms are designed for once- or twice-daily dosing, with strengths calibrated to deliver 25–100 mg of I3C or 100–300 mg of A18 per dose, based on maternal weight and gestational age.

For neonatal administration, the composition may be formulated as an oral solution, powder for reconstitution, or enteral suspension compatible with gavage feeding tubes commonly used in the NICU. The AHR agonist is dissolved or suspended in a vehicle such as sterile water, saline, or a small volume of infant formula, with optional stabilizers (e.g., polysorbate 80, glycerin) to maintain solubility and prevent adsorption to tubing. Concentrations are adjusted to allow precise dosing based on infant weight, with typical formulations providing 1–5 mg/mL of I3C or 10–30 mg/mL of A18. For critically ill infants unable to tolerate enteral feeds, parenteral formulations (e.g., intravenous solutions) may be developed using solubilizing agents like cyclodextrins, though enteral delivery is preferred to maximize local gut exposure.

Pharmaceutical compositions for inclusion in infant formula or breast milk fortifiers comprise the AHR agonist blended with macronutrients (proteins, fats, carbohydrates), micronutrients (vitamins, minerals), and emulsifiers to ensure homogeneity and stability during storage and feeding. Lyophilized powders may be prepared for long-term shelf life, with reconstitution instructions provided for bedside preparation.

All compositions are manufactured under Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) and tested for identity, purity, potency, and sterility as appropriate. Stability studies are conducted under accelerated and real-time conditions to establish expiration dates and storage requirements (e.g., refrigeration for liquid formulations). Pharmacokinetic studies in animal models and, eventually, human subjects guide dosing regimens to achieve sustained AHR activation in the target tissue without systemic toxicity.

Administration protocols are tailored to the clinical scenario: prophylactic dosing begins at birth or upon NICU admission for high-risk infants, or during the third trimester for mothers at risk of preterm delivery; therapeutic dosing is initiated upon early signs of NEC and continued until resolution of symptoms. Monitoring includes assessment of AHR pathway activation (e.g., CYP1A1 mRNA in stool or blood) and inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP, IL-6) to guide duration of therapy.

### F. Definitions

As used herein, the term “aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonist” refers to any compound that binds to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor and induces a conformational change that promotes its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, dimerization with ARNT, and transcriptional activation of target genes such as CYP1A1, CYP1B1, and AHRR. AHR agonists include natural ligands (e.g., indole-3-carbinol, microbial tryptophan metabolites), synthetic small molecules (e.g., A18, β-naphthoflavone), and pharmaceutical agents with incidental AHR-activating properties (e.g., omeprazole).

The term “necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC)” refers to an inflammatory bowel disease of premature infants characterized by intestinal mucosal or transmural necrosis, typically affecting the terminal ileum and proximal colon, and diagnosed based on clinical signs (e.g., feeding intolerance, abdominal distension), radiographic findings (e.g., pneumatosis intestinalis), and/or histopathological evidence of mucosal injury.

The term “infant” includes neonates (from birth to 28 days of life) and premature infants (born before 37 weeks of gestation), particularly those at high risk for NEC due to low birth weight (<1500 g), extreme prematurity (<28 weeks), or other comorbidities.

The term “maternal administration” refers to the delivery of a compound to a pregnant or lactating woman, with the intent that the compound or its active metabolites are transferred to the fetus via the placenta or to the neonate via breast milk.

The term “effective amount” means a quantity of an AHR agonist sufficient to activate AHR in the intestinal epithelium, suppress TLR4 signaling, and reduce the incidence or severity of NEC, without causing unacceptable toxicity. Effective amounts are determined empirically and may vary based on the specific compound, route of administration, and patient characteristics.

The term “infant nutritional formula” refers to any commercially prepared or custom-mixed liquid or powder designed to provide complete or supplemental nutrition to infants via enteral feeding, including bovine milk-based, soy-based, amino acid-based, and human milk-based products.

The term “inflammatory disorder” encompasses conditions involving dysregulated immune activation in the gastrointestinal tract, including but not limited to NEC, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and antibiotic-associated colitis, particularly when associated with elevated TLR4 signaling or AHR deficiency.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Maternal Administration of the AHR Ligand Indole-3-Carbinol (I3C) During Pregnancy Prevents Necrotizing Enterocolitis in Offspring

To evaluate whether maternal activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) during pregnancy could protect offspring against necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), pregnant C57BL/6 mice were fed a control diet or a diet supplemented with indole-3-carbinol (I3C) at 25 mg per kg body weight per day throughout gestation and lactation. At embryonic day 17.5 (E17.5), fetal intestines were harvested and analyzed for expression of Cyp1a1, a canonical AHR target gene. Quantitative RT-PCR revealed a significant induction of Cyp1a1 mRNA in the ileum of fetuses from I3C-fed dams compared to controls (p = 0.0317), confirming that maternal I3C administration leads to functional AHR activation in the fetal gut. Similarly, intestinal tissues from lactating dams and suckling pups at postnatal day 1 (P1) and P11 showed elevated Cyp1a1 expression, demonstrating sustained AHR pathway activation across the perinatal period.

To assess the functional impact of maternal I3C on NEC susceptibility, pups from I3C-fed or control dams were subjected to a well-validated experimental NEC model involving formula feeding, bacterial supplementation, and hypoxia exposure from P7 to P11. Histological evaluation of terminal ileum sections revealed markedly reduced mucosal injury in pups from I3C-fed mothers, including preservation of villus architecture, reduced epithelial sloughing, and minimal inflammatory infiltrate, compared to severe damage in control pups. Blinded NEC severity scoring confirmed a significant reduction in disease severity (p = 0.0031). Consistent with these findings, intestinal expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines Il6 and Tnf-α was significantly lower in the I3C group (p = 0.0329 and p = 0.0274, respectively).

To determine whether maternal-derived AHR ligands were present in fetal/neonatal compartments, amniotic fluid, breast milk, and maternal serum were collected from I3C-fed or control dams and applied to IEC-6 enterocytes. Only fluids from I3C-fed dams induced Cyp1a1 expression in recipient cells, confirming the presence of bioactive AHR ligands. Furthermore, neonatal pups from I3C-fed dams exhibited blunted intestinal inflammatory responses to intraperitoneal LPS challenge, with significantly reduced Il6 and Tnf-α induction compared to controls (p = 0.0192 and p = 0.0004, respectively), indicating that maternal I3C administration attenuates TLR4 signaling capacity in the offspring gut. These results establish that maternal dietary supplementation with an AHR ligand during pregnancy can prevent NEC in offspring through transplacental and/or transmammary delivery of protective signals.

### Example 2

AHR Expression on the Newborn Intestinal Epithelium Is Required for Protection Against NEC

To investigate the cellular site and mechanism of AHR-mediated protection against NEC, AHR expression was assessed in intestinal tissues from human infants, mice, and piglets with and without NEC. Immunofluorescence staining and qRT-PCR demonstrated significantly reduced AHR protein and mRNA levels in the ileum of NEC patients compared to surgical controls (p < 0.0001 for protein, p = 0.0019 for mRNA). Similar reductions were observed in murine and porcine experimental NEC models, suggesting a conserved pathogenic role for AHR deficiency across species.

To determine whether AHR loss is causal in NEC pathogenesis, global Ahr knockout (Ahr-/-), intestinal epithelial cell-specific knockout (AhrΔIEC), and myeloid-specific knockout (AhrΔlys) mice were subjected to experimental NEC. Ahr-/- and AhrΔIEC mice developed significantly more severe disease than wild-type controls, as evidenced by higher histologic injury scores (p = 0.0074 for both) and elevated Il6 and Tnf-α expression (p < 0.01 for all comparisons). In contrast, AhrΔlys mice exhibited NEC severity comparable to wild-type, indicating that AHR signaling in leukocytes is dispensable for protection. These findings demonstrate that AHR expression specifically on the intestinal epithelium is necessary and sufficient to limit NEC development.

To test whether postnatal AHR activation could rescue NEC susceptibility, wild-type and knockout pups were fed formula supplemented with I3C (25 mg/kg/day) during NEC induction. I3C significantly reduced NEC severity and cytokine expression in wild-type and AhrΔlys mice but provided minimal protection in Ahr-/- and AhrΔIEC mice, confirming that epithelial AHR is the critical mediator of I3C’s protective effects. Dose-response studies identified 25–50 mg/kg/day as the optimal I3C range for AHR activation and NEC prevention. Collectively, these results establish that AHR functions autonomously in the intestinal epithelium to protect against NEC, independent of immune cell populations.

### Example 3

Identification and Validation of the AHR Ligand “A18” for Prevention of NEC

To discover clinically translatable AHR agonists for NEC prevention, a high-throughput screen of an FDA-approved drug library was performed using an AHR-luciferase reporter IEC-6 cell line. The lead hit, designated “A18” (C16H14F3N3O2S, MW 369 Da), induced luciferase activity in a dose-dependent manner (EC50 ~5 μM). A18 also upregulated Cyp1a1 mRNA in wild-type but not Ahr-/- IEC-6 cells, confirming AHR specificity.

In vivo, oral administration of A18 (300 mg/kg/day) to neonatal mice induced intestinal Cyp1a1 expression and significantly suppressed LPS-induced Il6, Tnf-α, and Tlr4 mRNA levels. In the experimental NEC model, A18-treated wild-type pups exhibited reduced histologic injury, lower NEC scores (p = 0.0003), and decreased cytokine expression compared to vehicle controls; no protection was seen in Ahr-/- mice, verifying on-target activity.

Critically, A18 retained efficacy in human tissue: ex vivo treatment of resected ileal explants from NEC patients induced CYP1A1 and suppressed LPS-driven TNF-α and TLR4 expression (p < 0.01 for all). Finally, maternal administration of A18 during pregnancy induced Cyp1a1 in pup intestines and significantly reduced NEC severity in offspring (p = 0.0023), mirroring the effects of I3C.

These findings validate A18 as a potent, orally bioavailable AHR agonist that prevents NEC through epithelial TLR4 suppression and is effective in both murine and human systems, supporting its potential for clinical repurposing in high-risk pregnancies or neonates.
