# DESCRIPTION

## EXAMPLE 1

### Secretion ELISA for Detecting the Effector Protein SptP when Bound to its Cognate Chaperone SicP

The present invention provides a highly specific and sensitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) method for detecting the effector protein SptP of *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhimurium in complex with its cognate chaperone SicP. This assay, herein referred to as the Secretion ELISA, is designed to monitor the secretion competence of the type III secretion system (T3SS) encoded within pathogenicity island 1 (SPI-1) by specifically capturing and quantifying the SptP-SicP complex from bacterial culture supernatants. The method exploits the unique biochemical properties of this chaperone-effector pair, which remains stably associated in the bacterial cytoplasm until the T3SS is fully assembled and primed for effector translocation. Upon functional maturation of the needle complex, the SptP-SicP complex is recognized by the secretion apparatus and partially or fully dissociated during translocation; however, a detectable fraction of the complex may remain intact or transiently associated in the extracellular milieu under specific experimental conditions, particularly during early stages of secretion induction or in strains with mutations that subtly impair translocation efficiency without abolishing secretion entirely. The Secretion ELISA is thus a powerful tool for assessing the functional integrity of the SPI-1 T3SS, enabling rapid screening of bacterial mutants, evaluation of environmental conditions affecting virulence gene expression, and characterization of pharmacological inhibitors targeting T3SS assembly or function.

In the Secretion ELISA, bacterial cultures are grown under SPI-1-inducing conditions, typically involving high osmolarity, late-logarithmic growth phase, and oxygen availability, to maximize expression and assembly of the T3SS needle complex. Following induction, bacterial cells are removed by centrifugation, and the clarified culture supernatant is collected. This supernatant contains secreted proteins, including effectors such as SptP, translocators like SipB and SipC, and structural components of the needle filament under certain conditions. To specifically detect the SptP-SicP complex, a microtiter plate is first coated with a monoclonal or polyclonal antibody that recognizes an epitope on SicP that is not occluded upon binding to SptP. This capture antibody is selected based on epitope mapping and binding assays to ensure it retains high affinity for SicP whether free or in complex with SptP. After blocking non-specific binding sites on the plate, the culture supernatant is added and incubated to allow the SicP-containing complexes to bind to the immobilized anti-SicP antibody. Unbound proteins are then washed away under mild detergent conditions that preserve the SptP-SicP interaction but remove non-specifically adherent material.

Detection of the captured complex is achieved using a secondary antibody directed against SptP. This detection antibody is conjugated to an enzyme such as horseradish peroxidase (HRP) or alkaline phosphatase (AP), enabling colorimetric, chemiluminescent, or fluorescent readout upon addition of the appropriate substrate. Critically, the detection antibody must recognize an epitope on SptP that remains accessible when SptP is bound to SicP. Epitope accessibility is confirmed through co-immunoprecipitation and Western blotting experiments using purified recombinant SptP-SicP complexes. The signal intensity generated in the assay is directly proportional to the amount of SptP-SicP complex present in the supernatant, which in turn reflects the functional state of the SPI-1 T3SS. In wild-type *S*. Typhimurium under optimal inducing conditions, a robust signal is observed. In contrast, strains harboring deletions in key structural genes of the needle complex—such as Δ*invG*, Δ*prgH*, or Δ*prgK*—show markedly reduced or absent signal, consistent with their inability to assemble a functional secretion apparatus. Similarly, mutations in regulatory genes such as *hilA* or *invF*, which control expression of SPI-1 genes, result in minimal secretion and thus low ELISA signal.

The specificity of the Secretion ELISA is further validated by demonstrating that the signal is abolished when either SptP or SicP is genetically deleted. In a Δ*sptP* strain, no signal is detected despite normal SicP expression and secretion of other effectors, confirming that the assay depends on the presence of SptP. Conversely, in a Δ*sicP* strain, SptP is unstable and degraded in the cytoplasm due to lack of chaperone protection, leading to negligible SptP in the supernatant and thus no detectable signal. Importantly, complementation of these deletion strains with plasmid-encoded *sptP* or *sicP*, respectively, restores the ELISA signal, confirming the genetic dependence of the assay on both components. Additionally, the assay distinguishes between secretion-competent and secretion-defective states: for example, a strain expressing a PrgH variant truncated at the C-terminus (e.g., PrgHΔ4) may retain partial secretion capacity, resulting in an intermediate ELISA signal that correlates with the degree of needle complex instability observed in structural studies.

The Secretion ELISA offers several advantages over traditional methods such as Western blotting of culture supernatants. It is quantitative, high-throughput, and requires significantly less sample volume and processing time. Moreover, by specifically targeting the chaperone-bound form of the effector, it provides a more physiologically relevant readout of secretion competence than assays that detect total secreted SptP, which may include degradation products or misfolded aggregates. The assay can be adapted to detect other chaperone-effector pairs in the SPI-1 system (e.g., SigE-SopA, InvB-SipA) by substituting the appropriate capture and detection antibodies, thereby enabling multiplexed assessment of T3SS functionality. Furthermore, the Secretion ELISA can be employed in drug discovery screens to identify small molecules that inhibit T3SS assembly or effector recognition, as such compounds would dose-dependently reduce the ELISA signal without affecting bacterial growth. The method is also applicable to clinical isolates of *Salmonella* to assess virulence potential or to environmental samples for pathogen detection when combined with enrichment and selective culture techniques.

In summary, the Secretion ELISA represents a novel diagnostic and research tool grounded in the molecular architecture and functional dynamics of the *S*. Typhimurium SPI-1 T3SS. By leveraging the stable interaction between SptP and SicP as a biomarker for secretion competence, this assay provides a direct, specific, and quantifiable measure of needle complex functionality, filling a critical gap in the current methodologies for studying bacterial type III secretion systems.

## EXAMPLE 2

### Structure ELISA to Detect the Attachment of PrgI to the PgH-Containing Base Element

The present invention further discloses a Structure ELISA, a specialized immunoassay designed to detect the physical attachment of the needle filament protein PrgI to the base substructure of the *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhimurium SPI-1 type III secretion system (T3SS) needle complex, specifically to the PrgH-containing inner rings. This assay serves as a direct biochemical readout of needle complex assembly progression, distinguishing between incomplete base structures and fully assembled complexes competent for effector secretion. The Structure ELISA is predicated on the sequential assembly pathway of the needle complex, wherein the multi-ring base—composed of PrgH, PrgK, and InvG—is assembled first and functions as a dedicated secretion machine for the inner rod (PrgJ) and needle (PrgI) subunits. Only upon complete polymerization of the PrgI needle onto the base does the secretion apparatus undergo a conformational switch to secrete effector proteins. Thus, the presence of PrgI stably associated with the base is a hallmark of a mature, functional needle complex.

In the Structure ELISA, intact needle complexes are isolated from bacterial membranes using gentle detergent solubilization (e.g., 0.1% LDAO) followed by purification via sucrose density gradient centrifugation to ensure homogeneity and exclude unassembled subunits or degradation products. The purified needle complexes are then immobilized onto a microtiter plate, either by passive adsorption or, preferably, by capture using an antibody specific to a base component that is not involved in PrgI binding. In a preferred embodiment, the plate is coated with a monoclonal antibody directed against an epitope in the cytoplasmic domain of PrgH, which is distal to the PrgI attachment site and thus does not interfere with needle-base association. After blocking, the immobilized complexes are probed with a detection antibody specific for PrgI. This antibody is conjugated to an enzyme reporter (e.g., HRP) to enable quantitative signal generation. A positive signal indicates that PrgI is physically attached to the PrgH-containing base, confirming successful needle assembly.

The specificity and utility of the Structure ELISA are demonstrated through comparative analysis of wild-type and mutant strains. In wild-type *S*. Typhimurium, a strong signal is observed, reflecting the presence of fully assembled needle complexes. In contrast, a Δ*prgI* mutant yields no signal, as expected. More informatively, a Δ*invG* mutant—which assembles a base composed of PrgH and PrgK but lacks the outer rings and neck region—still supports PrgI polymerization, as evidenced by electron microscopy showing needle-like extensions on the inner rings. Consequently, the Structure ELISA produces a positive signal with Δ*invG* complexes, confirming that InvG is not required for PrgI attachment to the base. However, mutations that disrupt the inner ring structure—such as deletions in *prgH* or *prgK*—abolish the signal, as no stable base is formed to support needle assembly. Notably, the PrgHΔ4 truncation mutant, which exhibits partial instability in the needle complex, shows a reduced but detectable signal, correlating with its ability to form needle complexes that are prone to disassembly under stress (e.g., high pH). This graded response makes the Structure ELISA sensitive to subtle defects in base-needle connectivity.

The assay is further validated by treating purified needle complexes with conditions known to dissociate the needle filament, such as alkaline pH (e.g., pH 10.4). After such treatment, the Structure ELISA signal is lost, confirming that the detected PrgI is indeed attached to the base and not merely co-purifying as free polymer. Reconstitution experiments, wherein purified PrgI is added back to base-only complexes (e.g., from a Δ*prgI* strain complemented in trans), can also be used to demonstrate specificity: only when PrgI is allowed to polymerize under permissive conditions does the ELISA signal reappear.

The Structure ELISA provides a critical advantage over indirect methods such as monitoring PrgI secretion into culture supernatants, which cannot distinguish between secreted monomers, unassembled polymers, and base-attached needles. By directly probing the structural integrity of the assembled complex, this assay offers unambiguous evidence of needle-base attachment. It is particularly valuable for characterizing point mutations or domain deletions in PrgH, PrgK, or PrgI that may permit base assembly and PrgI secretion but impair stable needle anchoring. For instance, mutations in the C-terminal domain of PrgH—which interacts with InvG and potentially with the inner rod—may not affect PrgI polymerization per se but could destabilize the entire complex, leading to reduced ELISA signal despite normal PrgI levels in supernatants.

Moreover, the Structure ELISA can be adapted to study other T3SSs by substituting homologous components (e.g., MxiH for PrgI in *Shigella*, YscF in *Yersinia*) and corresponding antibodies. It also serves as a platform for screening inhibitors that block needle assembly—a promising anti-virulence strategy—as such compounds would prevent PrgI attachment and yield a negative ELISA signal even in the presence of all structural genes. In vaccine development, the assay can verify the structural fidelity of recombinant needle complexes used as immunogens. Overall, the Structure ELISA is a precise, reliable, and versatile tool for dissecting the molecular architecture and assembly dynamics of bacterial nanomachines.

## EXAMPLE 3

### Immunodetection of the Translocators SipB and SipC (Western Blot)

The present invention also encompasses a refined Western blot methodology for the specific immunodetection of the translocator proteins SipB and SipC, which are essential components of the *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhimurium SPI-1 type III secretion system (T3SS). These hydrophobic proteins form the translocon pore in the host cell membrane, enabling the delivery of effector proteins into the eukaryotic cytosol. Their detection in bacterial culture supernatants serves as a key indicator of T3SS functionality, as translocator secretion precedes and is required for efficient effector translocation. However, due to their inherent hydrophobicity, tendency to aggregate, and susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, standard Western blot protocols often yield inconsistent or weak signals for SipB and SipC. The disclosed method optimizes sample preparation, electrophoresis, and immunodetection conditions to achieve high sensitivity, reproducibility, and specificity.

In this method, bacterial cultures are induced for SPI-1 expression under defined conditions (e.g., LB broth with 0.3 M NaCl, pH 7.0, late-log phase). Culture supernatants are collected by centrifugation and filtration to remove cells and debris. To prevent aggregation and maintain solubility, secreted proteins are immediately mixed with a modified Laemmli sample buffer containing a high concentration of SDS (4–6%) and a reducing agent (e.g., 100 mM DTT), and heated at 95°C for 10 minutes. This denaturation step is critical for disrupting hydrophobic interactions and ensuring complete solubilization of SipB and SipC. Samples are then loaded onto Tris-Tricine SDS-PAGE gels, which provide superior resolution for low-molecular-weight proteins compared to standard Tris-Glycine gels; SipB (~60 kDa) and SipC (~42 kDa) are well-separated under these conditions.

Following electrophoresis, proteins are transferred to a low-fluorescence PVDF membrane using a semi-dry or wet transfer system optimized for hydrophobic proteins (e.g., extended transfer time, inclusion of methanol in the transfer buffer). The membrane is blocked with a solution containing 5% non-fat dry milk or BSA in TBST to minimize non-specific binding. Primary antibodies—high-affinity polyclonal or monoclonal antibodies raised against purified recombinant SipB and SipC—are applied at empirically determined dilutions (e.g., 1:1000 to 1:5000) and incubated overnight at 4°C to enhance specificity. After washing, HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies are used for detection with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrates, and signals are captured using a digital imager with linear dynamic range to allow quantitative comparison.

The method’s validity is confirmed by analyzing isogenic mutant strains: wild-type *S*. Typhimurium shows strong bands for both SipB and SipC; Δ*invG*, Δ*prgH*, or Δ*prgK* mutants show no detectable translocators, consistent with their secretion deficiency; and complemented strains restore the signal. Importantly, the assay distinguishes between secretion and stability: a Δ*sicA* mutant (lacking the chaperone for SipB/SipC) shows reduced or absent bands due to cytoplasmic degradation, even though the secretion apparatus is intact. This highlights the necessity of chaperone-mediated stabilization for translocator detection.

This optimized Western blot protocol is indispensable for functional characterization of T3SS mutants, evaluation of secretion kinetics, and validation of secretion assays such as the Secretion ELISA described herein. It provides a direct, visual confirmation of translocator presence, complementing quantitative ELISA data with qualitative information on protein integrity and size.
