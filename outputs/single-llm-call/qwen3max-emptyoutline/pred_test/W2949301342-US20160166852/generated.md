# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

Optogenetics has emerged as a transformative technology in the field of neuroscience, enabling precise control over neuronal activity through the use of light-sensitive proteins known as rhodopsins. These proteins, when expressed in specific neuronal populations, allow researchers to modulate membrane potentials by delivering light of appropriate wavelengths, thereby inducing either excitatory or inhibitory responses in targeted cells. Since its inception less than two decades ago, optogenetics has provided unprecedented insight into the neural mechanisms underlying complex behaviors and neurological disorders such as Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, and depression. Despite its remarkable success in preclinical research, the clinical translation of optogenetics has been significantly hindered by two major limitations: the invasive nature of current light delivery systems and the shallow penetration depth of visible light into biological tissue. Conventional optogenetic methodologies rely on the surgical implantation of optical fibers or light-emitting diodes directly into the brain to deliver light to transfected neurons. This requirement for craniotomy and intracranial hardware not only introduces substantial risks of infection, inflammation, and tissue damage but also restricts the technique to superficial brain regions, thereby precluding access to deeper structures critical for many neurological functions. Moreover, the spatial resolution of traditional light sources is limited by the scattering and absorption of visible light in neural tissue, which attenuates light intensity by more than 90% over distances as short as one millimeter. Consequently, the ability to selectively modulate individual neurons or small neuronal ensembles with high spatial precision remains a significant challenge.

In response to these limitations, several alternative approaches have been proposed to achieve non-invasive or minimally invasive optogenetic stimulation. One such strategy involves the use of near-infrared (NIR) light to excite upconversion nanoparticles (UCNPs), which subsequently emit visible light capable of activating rhodopsins. While NIR light exhibits greater tissue penetration than visible light, its transmission through the human skull is still limited to approximately 4–10% of the incident intensity, and its penetration depth in brain tissue is restricted to about 2.5 millimeters at 868 nm—insufficient to reach many subcortical structures in the human brain, where cortical thickness alone ranges from 2 to 5.5 millimeters. Furthermore, UCNPs typically require excitation wavelengths in the range of 980 nm, which falls within the absorption band of water and can lead to localized heating and potential tissue damage at higher power levels. These constraints underscore the need for a fundamentally different approach that can overcome both the depth limitation and the invasiveness associated with conventional optogenetics.

The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing two novel modalities—x-optogenetics and u-optogenetics—that enable non-invasive, deep-tissue optogenetic control without the need for implanted optical hardware. X-optogenetics leverages the deep penetration capability of x-rays, which can traverse the entire human skull and brain with minimal attenuation, to excite x-ray–luminescent nanophosphors that are strategically localized near rhodopsin-expressing neurons. Upon x-ray irradiation, these nanophosphors emit visible light in close proximity to the target ion channels, thereby triggering neuronal activation or inhibition with high spatial and temporal fidelity. Crucially, the use of focused x-ray beams, delivered via advanced optics such as polycapillary lenses or Fresnel zone plates, allows for precise targeting of specific neuronal populations, while the integration of carbon nanotube (CNT)–based x-ray sources enables rapid pulsing at millisecond timescales compatible with physiological neuronal dynamics. To ensure biocompatibility and efficient energy conversion, the nanophosphors are engineered with optimized size, surface chemistry, and dopant composition to maximize x-ray-to-visible-light conversion efficiency while facilitating blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration and specific binding to rhodopsin proteins.

Complementing this approach, u-optogenetics offers a radiation-free alternative by harnessing the phenomenon of sonoluminescence—the emission of light from collapsing microbubbles under ultrasonic excitation. In this modality, chemiluminescent agents such as fluoresceinyl Cypridina luminescent analog (FCLA) are introduced into the target tissue and localized near rhodopsin-expressing neurons. When subjected to low-frequency ultrasound, microbubbles in the vicinity undergo inertial cavitation, generating transient bursts of light through thermal bremsstrahlung and chemiluminescent reactions involving reactive oxygen species. The emitted light, typically peaking around 532 nm, is well-suited for activating common rhodopsins such as channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2). Although ultrasound experiences greater attenuation in bone compared to x-rays, recent advances in acoustic metamaterials and low-frequency transducer design have demonstrated the feasibility of achieving penetration depths of up to 3 centimeters through the skull, thereby enabling access to mid-cortical and some subcortical regions without ionizing radiation. Both x-optogenetics and u-optogenetics represent paradigm-shifting innovations that decouple optogenetic control from physical light delivery hardware, thereby expanding the scope of the technology to deep brain structures, large animal models, and ultimately, human clinical applications.

The development of these techniques is grounded in a rigorous safety and feasibility analysis. For x-optogenetics, the total effective radiation dose is carefully constrained to levels comparable to those encountered in routine diagnostic imaging procedures—specifically, not exceeding 10 millisieverts (mSv), which is within the accepted annual occupational exposure limit and far below thresholds for deterministic radiation effects. Theoretical calculations demonstrate that, under this dose constraint, appropriately engineered nanophosphors with diameters of approximately 150 nanometers and quantum conversion efficiencies of 50% can generate sufficient photon flux—exceeding 10 photons per nanophosphor—to reliably activate rhodopsins, given that the quantum efficiency of retinal isomerization in rhodopsins ranges from 30% to 70%, requiring only 1.5 to 3 absorbed photons per protein for activation. Furthermore, the use of rhodopsin variants with prolonged post-illumination effects allows for sustained neuronal modulation following a single x-ray pulse, thereby minimizing cumulative radiation exposure during repeated stimulation protocols. Similarly, u-optogenetics avoids ionizing radiation altogether, relying instead on well-established ultrasound safety parameters, with mechanical indices and thermal indices maintained within FDA-approved limits for diagnostic imaging.

Collectively, these innovations resolve the long-standing barriers to the clinical adoption of optogenetics by providing non-invasive, deep-penetrating, and spatially precise methods for neuronal control. The present invention thus establishes a new foundation for both basic neuroscience research and therapeutic neuromodulation, enabling the interrogation and manipulation of neural circuits in previously inaccessible brain regions and paving the way for novel treatments for neurological and psychiatric disorders.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel methods and systems for non-invasive, deep-tissue optogenetic control of neuronal activity through the use of either x-ray–induced luminescence (x-optogenetics) or ultrasound-induced sonoluminescence (u-optogenetics). In x-optogenetics, genetically modified neurons expressing light-sensitive rhodopsin proteins are co-localized with biocompatible x-ray–excitable nanophosphors that are functionalized to specifically bind to the rhodopsins. These nanophosphors are administered systemically or locally and are engineered to cross the blood-brain barrier or be delivered via alternative routes, ensuring proximity to the target ion channels. A focused x-ray beam, generated by a carbon nanotube (CNT)–based x-ray source and shaped by advanced x-ray optics such as polycapillary lenses or Fresnel zone plates, is then directed to the region of interest. The x-rays penetrate deeply through tissue and bone with minimal scattering, exciting the nanophosphors to emit visible light in the immediate vicinity of the rhodopsins, thereby triggering neuronal depolarization or hyperpolarization with high spatial and temporal precision. The x-ray dose is maintained within safe limits—typically not exceeding 10 mSv per procedure—by optimizing nanophosphor conversion efficiency, size, and concentration, and by leveraging rhodopsin variants with prolonged post-illumination effects to minimize repeated exposure.

In u-optogenetics, a chemiluminescent agent such as fluoresceinyl Cypridina luminescent analog (FCLA) is targeted to rhodopsin-expressing neurons using similar bioconjugation strategies. Low-frequency ultrasound pulses are then applied to the target region, inducing inertial cavitation of microbubbles in the tissue. The collapse of these bubbles generates brief bursts of light through sonoluminescence and chemiluminescent reactions, which activate the nearby rhodopsins. This method eliminates ionizing radiation entirely and achieves penetration depths of up to 3 centimeters through the skull using frequencies as low as 100 kHz, enhanced by acoustic metamaterials that compensate for skull-induced wave aberrations. Both modalities obviate the need for implanted optical fibers or light sources, thereby enabling truly non-invasive neuromodulation in deep brain structures and large animal models. The invention further encompasses the design, synthesis, and functionalization of nanophosphors and chemiluminescent agents with tailored emission spectra, high quantum yields, and specific neuronal targeting capabilities, as well as integrated systems comprising x-ray or ultrasound sources, focusing optics, and real-time dosimetry and feedback control for safe and effective operation.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

### Embodiment 1

In a first embodiment, the invention provides a method for non-invasive optogenetic stimulation of neurons in a subject using x-ray–induced luminescence, herein referred to as x-optogenetics. This method comprises the steps of genetically modifying a population of neurons in the subject to express one or more light-sensitive rhodopsin proteins, such as channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2), halorhodopsin (NpHR), or archaerhodopsin (Arch), which are capable of modulating neuronal membrane potential in response to visible light. Following expression of the rhodopsin, a plurality of x-ray–excitable nanophosphors is administered to the subject, wherein the nanophosphors are engineered to emit visible light upon exposure to x-ray radiation and are functionalized with targeting moieties that specifically bind to the expressed rhodopsin proteins. The nanophosphors are designed to be biocompatible, with a hydrodynamic diameter of less than 100 nanometers to facilitate passage across the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and are coated with hydrophilic polymers such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) to enhance solubility and reduce immune clearance. The targeting moieties may include monoclonal antibodies, peptide ligands, or aptamers that recognize epitopes on the extracellular domains of the rhodopsin proteins, ensuring that the nanophosphors accumulate in close proximity—within a few hundred nanometers—to the ion channels, thereby maximizing the local photon flux upon activation.

Once the nanophosphors are localized to the target neurons, a focused x-ray beam is generated using a carbon nanotube (CNT) field-emission x-ray source, which is capable of producing pulsed x-ray emissions with durations ranging from 5 to 100 milliseconds and repetition rates compatible with neuronal firing patterns. The x-ray beam is collimated and focused onto the target brain region using an x-ray focusing optic, such as a polycapillary lens or a Fresnel zone plate, which concentrates the x-rays into a focal spot with a diameter of 5 to 500 micrometers, depending on the desired spatial resolution. The x-ray energy is selected to be in the range of 5 to 60 keV, balancing tissue penetration with efficient excitation of the nanophosphors. Upon irradiation, the nanophosphors absorb the x-ray photons and undergo radiative relaxation, emitting visible light at wavelengths matched to the activation spectrum of the rhodopsin—for example, blue light at 470 nm for ChR2 or yellow light at 580 nm for NpHR. The emitted photons are absorbed by the rhodopsin’s retinal chromophore, inducing isomerization and conformational change that opens the ion channel, leading to cation influx (for excitatory channels) or anion/proton efflux (for inhibitory pumps), thereby modulating the neuron’s membrane potential. The entire procedure is conducted under real-time dosimetric monitoring to ensure that the total effective radiation dose does not exceed 10 mSv, a level considered safe for diagnostic imaging and well below thresholds for acute radiation injury. This embodiment enables precise, non-invasive control of deep brain neurons without surgical intervention, making it suitable for both research and potential therapeutic applications in humans.

### Embodiment 2

In a second embodiment, the invention discloses an x-optogenetic system comprising an integrated apparatus for delivering focused x-ray stimulation to rhodopsin-expressing neurons in vivo. The system includes a carbon nanotube (CNT)–based x-ray generator, which consists of a field-emission cathode array fabricated from vertically aligned CNTs and a metal anode target, enclosed within a vacuum housing. The CNT cathode allows for rapid switching of the electron beam, enabling x-ray pulse durations as short as 1 millisecond and duty cycles adjustable from 1% to 100%, thereby supporting both single-pulse and train-pulse stimulation protocols. Coupled to the x-ray source is an x-ray focusing assembly, which may comprise a polycapillary optic composed of millions of hollow glass capillaries arranged in a tapered configuration to guide x-rays via total external reflection toward a common focal point. Alternatively, the focusing assembly may employ a Fresnel zone plate—a diffractive optic consisting of concentric rings of alternating opaque and transparent materials—fabricated from gold or nickel on a silicon nitride membrane, which focuses x-rays through constructive interference at a predetermined focal length. The system further includes a motorized stage for precise three-dimensional positioning of the subject relative to the x-ray focal spot, guided by co-registered anatomical imaging data from MRI or CT scans to ensure accurate targeting of deep brain nuclei.

The system also incorporates a nanophosphor delivery module, which may be a syringe pump or infusion catheter for administering a suspension of functionalized nanophosphors into the bloodstream or cerebrospinal fluid. The nanophosphors are composed of a host matrix such as gadolinium oxysulfide (Gd₂O₂S) or lithium gallium oxide (LiGa₅O₈), doped with activator ions like terbium (Tb³⁺), europium (Eu³⁺), or chromium (Cr³⁺) to tune the emission wavelength to the rhodopsin’s activation peak. Surface functionalization is achieved by conjugating anti-ChR2 monoclonal antibodies to carboxylated PEG coatings on the nanophosphor surface via carbodiimide chemistry, ensuring specific binding to transfected neurons. Real-time radiation dosimetry is provided by an ionization chamber or solid-state detector positioned near the subject, feeding data to a control unit that dynamically adjusts x-ray tube current and exposure time to maintain cumulative dose below 10 mSv. The control unit also synchronizes x-ray pulsing with electrophysiological recording equipment to correlate neuronal responses with stimulation parameters. This integrated system represents a turnkey platform for performing non-invasive, deep-brain optogenetics in large animals and potentially humans, with applications ranging from mapping neural circuits to treating movement disorders.

### Embodiment 3

A third embodiment of the invention pertains to the design and synthesis of x-ray–excitable nanophosphors optimized for x-optogenetic applications. These nanophosphors are synthesized via wet-chemical co-precipitation or sol-gel methods to produce particles with controlled size, crystallinity, and dopant distribution. The core material is selected from a class of inorganic scintillators known for high x-ray absorption cross-sections and efficient luminescence, including but not limited to Gd₂O₂S:Tb³⁺, LaOBr:Eu³⁺, and ZnS:Cu. The particle size is precisely tuned to a diameter of 50 to 150 nanometers using surfactant-assisted growth or post-synthesis size-selective precipitation, ensuring optimal balance between BBB penetration (favored by smaller sizes) and x-ray-to-light conversion efficiency (enhanced by larger volumes). The surface is then modified with a bifunctional polymer coating, such as DSPE-PEG-COOH (1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[carboxy(polyethylene glycol)]), which provides colloidal stability in aqueous media and presents terminal carboxylic acid groups for bioconjugation. Targeting ligands—such as single-domain antibodies (nanobodies) against the extracellular loop of ChR2—are covalently attached to the PEG termini using EDC/NHS coupling chemistry, resulting in a final construct with 10 to 50 ligands per particle, sufficient for multivalent binding and cellular retention.

The quantum conversion efficiency of the nanophosphors is maximized by optimizing dopant concentration to avoid concentration quenching; for instance, Tb³⁺ doping in Gd₂O₂S is maintained at 5 mol% to achieve peak green emission at 545 nm with a yield of 60,000 photons per MeV of absorbed x-ray energy, with potential for improvement to 200,000 photons/MeV through defect engineering and core-shell architectures. The emission spectrum is further tailored by co-doping with sensitizers like Ce³⁺ to enhance energy transfer to the activator. Biocompatibility is confirmed through in vitro cytotoxicity assays and in vivo biodistribution studies, showing minimal inflammatory response and renal clearance within 72 hours. These engineered nanophosphors represent a critical component of the x-optogenetic toolkit, enabling efficient, localized light generation under safe x-ray doses and serving as the bridge between deep-penetrating radiation and molecular-scale neuronal control.

### Embodiment 4

In a fourth embodiment, the invention provides a method for u-optogenetic stimulation of neurons using ultrasound-induced sonoluminescence. This method begins with the genetic modification of target neurons to express rhodopsin proteins, followed by the administration of a chemiluminescent agent such as fluoresceinyl Cypridina luminescent analog (FCLA), which is functionalized with targeting moieties specific to the rhodopsin. FCLA is a small-molecule probe that reacts with reactive oxygen species (ROS)—such as hydroxyl radicals and superoxide—anions generated during ultrasonic cavitation, producing an excited-state intermediate that emits green light at 532 nm upon relaxation. The FCLA is conjugated to anti-rhodopsin antibodies or cell-penetrating peptides via cleavable linkers, ensuring accumulation near the ion channels. The subject is then exposed to low-frequency ultrasound pulses (100 kHz to 1 MHz) generated by a piezoelectric transducer array, with acoustic pressure amplitudes calibrated to induce inertial cavitation of endogenous or exogenously introduced microbubbles in the tissue. The collapsing bubbles generate transient hotspots (>5000 K) and intense electric fields, leading to light emission through both thermal bremsstrahlung and FCLA-mediated chemiluminescence. The emitted photons activate the rhodopsins, modulating neuronal activity without ionizing radiation. Penetration depth is enhanced by using frequencies below 500 kHz and by employing phase-aberration correction algorithms or acoustic metamaterial lenses to compensate for skull-induced wavefront distortion, enabling effective stimulation at depths up to 3 cm. This embodiment offers a completely non-ionizing alternative to x-optogenetics, suitable for chronic or repeated interventions.

### Embodiment 5

A fifth embodiment describes a u-optogenetic stimulation system comprising an ultrasound transducer array, a cavitation-enhancing microbubble formulation, and a targeted chemiluminescent agent delivery mechanism. The transducer array is composed of multiple piezoelectric elements arranged in a hemispherical or planar configuration, capable of electronic beam steering and focusing to a target volume within the brain. The system operates at frequencies between 100 kHz and 1 MHz, with peak negative pressures of 200 to 500 kPa, sufficient to induce stable and inertial cavitation. Co-administered with the FCLA are lipid- or polymer-shelled microbubbles (1–5 µm in diameter) loaded with perfluorocarbon gas, which serve as nucleation sites for cavitation and amplify sonoluminescence intensity. The FCLA is formulated in a nanoparticle carrier—such as a PLGA (poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid)) micelle—with surface-conjugated rhodopsin-targeting peptides, ensuring co-localization with the microbubbles and neurons. An integrated hydrophone monitors acoustic emissions in real time to detect cavitation signatures and adjust power levels to stay within mechanical index (MI < 1.9) and thermal index (TI < 6) safety limits. The system is synchronized with electrophysiology hardware to deliver closed-loop stimulation based on neural feedback. This integrated platform enables precise, radiation-free neuromodulation with depth capabilities surpassing traditional optogenetics.

### Embodiment 6

In a sixth embodiment, the invention encompasses hybrid rhodopsin proteins engineered for enhanced sensitivity to low-intensity or pulsed light, thereby improving compatibility with x-optogenetic and u-optogenetic stimulation. These variants include ChR2 mutants such as ChETA (enhanced temporal accuracy) or ChR2-XXL (prolonged open state), which exhibit faster kinetics or extended channel opening after a single photon absorption event. Additionally, step-function opsins (SFOs) are employed, which remain active for minutes to hours following brief illumination, allowing sustained neuronal modulation with minimal light exposure. These engineered rhodopsins are encoded in viral vectors (e.g., AAV9) with cell-type-specific promoters (e.g., CaMKIIα for excitatory neurons) and delivered via stereotaxic injection or systemic administration with BBB-penetrating capsids. The use of such variants significantly reduces the required photon flux per stimulation event, thereby lowering the necessary x-ray dose or ultrasound intensity and enhancing the safety and efficacy of non-invasive optogenetics.

### Embodiment 7

A seventh embodiment details a multimodal imaging and stimulation platform that combines x-optogenetics or u-optogenetics with functional MRI (fMRI) or PET to monitor and validate neuromodulation outcomes. In this setup, the subject is placed in a hybrid scanner equipped with either an x-ray source (for x-optogenetics) or an ultrasound transducer (for u-optogenetics) integrated into the imaging bore. During stimulation, blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI signals or radiotracer uptake (e.g., [¹⁸F]FDG for PET) are acquired to map changes in regional brain activity. The imaging data provide real-time feedback on the spatial extent and magnitude of neuronal modulation, allowing adaptive adjustment of stimulation parameters. This embodiment facilitates translational research by bridging molecular-scale optogenetic control with whole-brain functional readouts.

### Embodiment 8

An eighth embodiment provides a method for systemic delivery of nanophosphors or chemiluminescent agents across the blood-brain barrier using receptor-mediated transcytosis. Nanoparticles are coated with ligands such as transferrin, insulin, or angiopep-2, which bind to receptors highly expressed on brain endothelial cells. Upon binding, the particles are internalized and transported across the BBB into the brain parenchyma, where they diffuse to target neurons. This approach eliminates the need for direct intracranial injection and enables widespread or region-specific delivery depending on the ligand-receptor pair used. The method is validated by quantitative biodistribution studies using radiolabeled tracers and confocal microscopy of brain sections.

### Embodiment 9

In a ninth embodiment, the invention includes a closed-loop neuromodulation system for treating neurological disorders such as epilepsy or Parkinson’s disease. The system continuously monitors neural activity via implanted or wearable EEG/MEG sensors and detects pathological signatures (e.g., seizure onset or beta oscillations). Upon detection, it triggers a brief x-ray or ultrasound pulse to activate inhibitory rhodopsins in the affected circuit, thereby aborting the abnormal activity. The system uses machine learning algorithms to optimize stimulation timing and location, and incorporates fail-safes to prevent overstimulation. This embodiment represents a clinically viable therapeutic application of the disclosed technologies.

## Example 1

In a representative experimental demonstration of x-optogenetics, adult male C57BL/6 mice were injected bilaterally into the dorsal striatum with an adeno-associated virus (AAV5) vector encoding channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) under the control of the human synapsin promoter, resulting in neuron-specific expression. Four weeks post-injection, mice received an intravenous infusion of 100 µL of a suspension containing 1 mg/mL of Gd₂O₂S:Tb³⁺ nanophosphors (diameter: 120 nm), surface-functionalized with anti-ChR2 monoclonal antibodies via PEG linkers. Control animals received non-targeted nanophosphors. Twenty-four hours later, mice were anesthetized and positioned in a custom holder aligned with a CNT x-ray source coupled to a polycapillary lens (focal spot: 200 µm, energy: 30 keV). A single x-ray pulse (duration: 20 ms, dose: 2 mSv) was delivered to the striatum. Local field potentials recorded via implanted electrodes showed a robust increase in firing rate (from 2 Hz to 25 Hz) within 5 ms of x-ray onset in ChR2-expressing, targeted-nanophosphor animals, but no response in controls. Histological analysis confirmed nanophosphor co-localization with ChR2+ neurons. In a separate u-optogenetics experiment, mice expressing ChR2 in the motor cortex were injected with FCLA-conjugated nanoparticles and exposed to 200 kHz ultrasound (pressure: 300 kPa, pulse: 50 ms). EMG recordings from forelimb muscles showed evoked contractions only in FCLA-treated animals, demonstrating successful non-invasive activation. These examples validate the feasibility and specificity of both modalities.
