# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Radio frequency identification (RFID) technology has emerged as a transformative tool in numerous industrial, commercial, and biomedical domains due to its capacity for wireless data transmission, passive operation, and robustness under extreme environmental conditions. Unlike conventional optical identification systems such as barcodes, RFID tags do not require line-of-sight communication and can function reliably in environments involving solvents, high or low pressures, extreme temperatures, and aqueous or biological media. These attributes have enabled the integration of RFID into diverse applications ranging from supply chain logistics and automated toll collection to implantable medical devices and ingestible digital therapeutics. In recent years, the miniaturization of RFID components has progressed significantly, yielding microchips with dimensions as small as 10 to 600 micrometers, thereby opening new avenues for their incorporation into biological systems at the cellular and tissue levels. Despite these advances, the application of RFID technology within complex three-dimensional biological constructs—particularly those derived from human stem cells—has remained largely unexplored, primarily due to concerns regarding biocompatibility, structural integrity, and functional interference with native tissue physiology.

The advent of human organoids—three-dimensional, self-organizing tissue structures derived from pluripotent or adult stem cells that recapitulate key architectural and functional features of human organs—has revolutionized in vitro modeling of human development, disease, and drug response. Organoids derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) offer unprecedented fidelity to human pathophysiology, enabling the study of genetic disorders, infectious diseases, and personalized therapeutic responses in a controlled laboratory setting. However, the scalability and utility of organoid-based platforms for high-throughput phenotypic screening are currently constrained by the absence of reliable, non-invasive methods to track, identify, and retrieve individual organoids within heterogeneous populations. Conventional approaches rely on labor-intensive, manual handling and labeling, which are not only prone to error but also fundamentally incompatible with large-scale comparative studies involving hundreds or thousands of donor-derived lines. This limitation is particularly acute in the context of “cellomics”—a proposed paradigm that seeks to couple population-scale stem cell biobanks with systematic phenotypic characterization to uncover the genetic and molecular basis of human variability. While transcriptomic and genomic analyses have provided valuable insights into expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) and disease-associated variants, they often fail to capture higher-order functional phenotypes that manifest only at the tissue or organoid level, such as metabolic activity, barrier function, or secretory capacity. Thus, there exists a critical need for technologies that enable the unambiguous identification and longitudinal monitoring of individual organoids without compromising their structural or functional integrity.

Prior attempts to integrate electronic components into biological tissues have faced significant challenges. Early efforts involving the intracellular delivery of micro-RFID devices were limited to immortalized cell lines such as phagocytic or melanoma cells, which lack the physiological relevance and structural complexity of primary human tissues. Moreover, these approaches often resulted in cytotoxicity, impaired cell viability, or disruption of normal cellular processes, thereby precluding their application in more sophisticated models like organoids. The unique architecture of organoids—characterized by polarized epithelial layers, basement membrane formation, and lumenogenesis through self-assembly—presents both an opportunity and a challenge for device integration. On one hand, the dynamic reorganization of cells during organoid morphogenesis may facilitate the passive internalization of micro-devices; on the other hand, any mechanical or chemical perturbation during this process could disrupt the delicate balance of signaling pathways required for proper tissue development. Consequently, successful integration demands not only a biocompatible and miniaturized RFID platform but also a methodological framework that aligns with the natural developmental trajectory of organoids.

Furthermore, the practical deployment of such a system in research and clinical settings necessitates compatibility with standard laboratory workflows, including cryopreservation, sterilization, histological processing, and long-term culture. Traditional RFID tags, while durable in industrial contexts, have not been systematically evaluated for resilience under the stringent conditions of biological experimentation—such as exposure to liquid nitrogen, autoclaving, organic solvents, and fluctuating pH levels. Without demonstrated robustness across these parameters, the utility of RFID-integrated organoids would be severely limited. Additionally, the absence of integrated readout systems capable of simultaneously capturing phenotypic data (e.g., fluorescence intensity, morphological features) and digital identifiers (e.g., Electronic Product Code, EPC) has hindered the realization of true high-throughput, multiplexed organoid screening. Existing imaging platforms typically require manual annotation or post-hoc correlation with external databases, introducing bottlenecks and potential sources of misidentification. Therefore, the development of a unified detection apparatus that couples real-time RFID interrogation with quantitative phenotyping represents a crucial technological advancement.

In summary, while the convergence of organoid biology and microelectronics holds immense promise for advancing precision medicine and drug discovery, the field lacks a validated, scalable, and functionally neutral strategy for embedding digital identifiers into living human tissues. The present invention addresses this gap by leveraging the self-assembly properties of iPSC-derived organoids to seamlessly incorporate ultracompact RFID microchips—herein referred to as O-Chips—into their luminal compartments without impairing native structure or function. This approach not only enables the unambiguous tracking of individual organoids across time and experimental conditions but also facilitates the development of pooled, high-throughput screening paradigms that dramatically reduce cost, labor, and inter-experimental variability. By establishing the biocompatibility, durability, and functional neutrality of O-Chip integration, and by demonstrating its utility in a forward cellomics workflow for identifying genetic drivers of disease phenotypes, the invention provides a foundational platform for the next generation of in vitro human tissue models.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and robust method for the integration of ultracompact radio frequency identification (RFID) microchips—referred to herein as O-Chips—into human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived organoids, thereby enabling the digital identification, tracking, and high-throughput phenotypic screening of individual organoids without compromising their native structural or functional integrity. The invention is based on the discovery that the self-assembly and lumenogenesis processes inherent to organoid formation can be harnessed to passively and efficiently internalize O-Chips into the central cavity of developing organoids, resulting in RFID-integrated organoids (RiOs) that maintain full biological fidelity. Each O-Chip, measuring approximately 460 × 480 micrometers and featuring a 512-bit memory area, is capable of wireless communication with external readers at distances up to 1–2 millimeters, allowing for non-invasive interrogation of stored digital identifiers such as Electronic Product Codes (EPCs). Critically, the integration process does not require invasive microinjection, electroporation, or chemical modification, thereby preserving the viability and differentiation potential of the constituent cells.

The invention further demonstrates that RiOs exhibit equivalent morphological, molecular, and functional characteristics compared to non-integrated control organoids, including the expression of lineage-specific markers (e.g., albumin, alpha-fetoprotein, HNF4α), secretion of hepatic proteins, bile acid transport, lipid accumulation, and iron metabolism. Moreover, the O-Chips embedded within RiOs retain full functionality following exposure to a wide range of environmental stressors commonly encountered in biological research, including cryopreservation at temperatures as low as −196°C (liquid nitrogen), autoclaving (both dry and moist heat sterilization), immersion in organic solvents and aqueous solutions during histological processing, and prolonged culture under dynamic pH conditions. This exceptional durability ensures that RiOs can be stored, shipped, sectioned, and analyzed using standard laboratory protocols without loss of digital identity or biological function.

A key aspect of the invention is the development of a high-throughput detection system that enables the simultaneous acquisition of phenotypic data and digital identification from flowing RiOs. This system comprises a microfluidic flow path, a precision syringe pump for controlled organoid transport, a fluorescence imaging module, and an RFID reader probe, all integrated into a single platform. As RiOs pass through the detection zone, the system captures both a fluorescence image (e.g., for lipid accumulation using BODIPY dye) and the EPC from the embedded O-Chip, thereby directly correlating phenotype with donor identity in real time. This capability is particularly transformative for pooled screening applications, wherein multiple donor-derived RiOs are co-cultured and subjected to a common stimulus, eliminating well-to-well variability and drastically reducing reagent and labor costs.

As a proof of concept, the invention demonstrates a “forward cellomics” screening approach using a pool of seven iPSC-derived liver RiOs, including two from patients with Wolman disease—a monogenic disorder of lysosomal acid lipase deficiency characterized by severe hepatic steatosis. Following induction of lipid accumulation with free fatty acids, the pooled RiOs were analyzed using the integrated detection system, which identified the two Wolman-derived RiOs based on their significantly elevated BODIPY fluorescence intensity and confirmed their identity via wireless EPC readout. This experiment validates the ability of the RiO platform to recapitulate human genetic disease phenotypes and to enable genotype-phenotype mapping in a high-throughput, pooled format.

In summary, the present invention provides a comprehensive solution for the digitalization of human organoids, combining a biocompatible integration method, a durable and miniaturized RFID platform, and an integrated phenotyping-detection system. This technology enables scalable, cost-effective, and reproducible organoid-based research for applications in drug discovery, toxicology, personalized medicine, and the systematic study of human genetic variation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below unless otherwise indicated by the context.

“Organoid” refers to a three-dimensional, self-organizing multicellular structure derived in vitro from stem cells—such as induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) or adult stem cells—that recapitulates key structural, functional, and genetic aspects of a specific organ or tissue. Organoids exhibit polarization, lumen formation, and cell-type diversity characteristic of the native organ from which they are modeled, and they are capable of performing organ-specific functions such as protein secretion, metabolite transport, or barrier formation.

“Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)” means a somatic cell that has been reprogrammed to a pluripotent state through the introduction of defined transcription factors (e.g., OCT4, SOX2, KLF4, c-MYC), thereby acquiring the capacity to differentiate into any cell type of the three germ layers, including endoderm, mesoderm, and ectoderm.

“O-Chip” denotes an ultracompact, passive radio frequency identification (RFID) microchip specifically designed for integration into biological organoids. The O-Chip is typically between 10 and 600 micrometers in its largest dimension, with a preferred embodiment measuring 460 × 480 micrometers. It contains a coiled antenna for wireless energy harvesting and data transmission, and a non-volatile memory area of at least 512 bits for storing a unique digital identifier, such as an Electronic Product Code (EPC).

“RFID-integrated organoid (RiO)” refers to an organoid that has incorporated an O-Chip within its internal structure, preferably within the lumen formed during self-assembly, such that the O-Chip is fully enclosed by organoid tissue and remains functionally active for wireless communication with external RFID readers.

“Self-assembly” describes the autonomous process by which dissociated stem cell-derived progenitors reaggregate and organize into polarized, three-dimensional structures with defined apical-basal polarity and lumen formation, driven by intrinsic developmental programs and extracellular matrix cues, without external scaffolding or mechanical intervention.

“Electronic Product Code (EPC)” is a standardized digital identifier stored in the memory of an RFID tag that uniquely identifies a physical object. In the context of the present invention, each O-Chip is pre-programmed with a unique EPC that corresponds to the donor identity, cell line origin, or experimental batch of the organoid into which it is integrated.

“Forward cellomics” is a screening methodology wherein a heterogeneous pool of donor-derived organoids is subjected to a phenotypic assay (e.g., drug treatment, metabolic challenge), and individuals exhibiting extreme or notable phenotypes are subsequently identified by retrieving their embedded digital identifiers (e.g., via RFID readout), thereby enabling reverse mapping from phenotype to genotype.

“Cryopreservation” refers to the process of preserving biological materials at ultralow temperatures, typically using liquid nitrogen (−196°C) or controlled-rate freezing, to maintain viability and functionality for future use. In the context of RiOs, cryopreservation includes both slow freezing and vitrification protocols.

“Phenotypic screening” encompasses any assay that measures a functional, morphological, or biochemical output of an organoid, such as protein secretion, ion transport, lipid accumulation, gene expression, or response to pharmacological agents, as opposed to purely genomic or transcriptomic analyses.

“Wireless detection” means the non-contact interrogation of an O-Chip using radiofrequency signals emitted by an external reader, resulting in the retrieval of stored data (e.g., EPC) without physical connection or disruption of the organoid.

“Lumen” refers to the internal cavity formed during organoid development, which is lined by polarized epithelial cells and mimics the functional luminal space of native organs such as the gut, liver ducts, or kidney tubules.

### Examples

The present invention is exemplified through the successful integration of O-Chips into human iPSC-derived liver organoids, the validation of their structural and functional equivalence to non-integrated controls, the demonstration of O-Chip durability under diverse laboratory conditions, and the implementation of a high-throughput phenotyping platform for pooled genetic screening.

In a first example, human iPSCs were differentiated into posterior foregut spheroids through sequential exposure to Activin A and FGF4/CHIR99021 over six days. At day 6, the foregut spheroids were dissociated into single cells or small clusters and reseeded into 96-well plates containing commercially available O-Chips (460 × 480 μm, 512-bit memory). The cells were allowed to reaggregate around the O-Chips over 24–48 hours, after which a laminin-rich basement membrane matrix was added to induce polarization and lumen formation. Within three days, fully formed RFID-integrated organoids (RiOs) were observed, with each organoid completely encapsulating a single O-Chip within its central lumen. This process was reproducible across eight independent donor-derived iPSC lines, with an integration efficiency exceeding 95% (92 out of 96 organoids successfully incorporated an O-Chip).

In a second example, the structural and functional integrity of RiOs was assessed in comparison to standard liver organoids (control LOs). Morphological analysis revealed no discernible differences between RiOs and control LOs in size, shape, or tissue organization. Quantitative PCR analysis showed equivalent expression levels of liver-specific markers, including albumin (ALB), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and alpha-1-antitrypsin (AAT). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of culture supernatants confirmed that RiOs secreted ALB at levels comparable to control LOs (152.9 ng/mL vs. 150.0 ng/mL, a 1.02-fold difference). Immunofluorescent staining demonstrated the presence of ALB-positive hepatocyte-like cells and nuclear localization of HNF4α, a master regulator of hepatic differentiation, within RiOs. Functional assays further confirmed that RiOs retained key hepatic capabilities: they actively transported fluorescent bile acid analogs (CLF and rhodamine 123) into their lumens via BSEP and MDR1 transporters, and accumulated neutral lipids in response to fatty acid supplementation, as visualized by BODIPY 493/503 staining. Iron accumulation was also demonstrated using FeRhoNox 540/575 dye following ammonium iron sulfate treatment. Collectively, these results establish that O-Chip integration does not impair the native biology of liver organoids.

In a third example, the environmental durability of O-Chips was rigorously tested. O-Chips embedded in RiOs remained functional after exposure to temperatures ranging from 4°C to −196°C (liquid nitrogen), confirming compatibility with standard cryopreservation protocols. Both slow freezing and vitrification methods preserved RiO morphology and O-Chip readability. Autoclaving at 121°C for 15 minutes (moist heat) and 160°C for 2 hours (dry heat) did not compromise O-Chip integrity or signal transmission. Furthermore, O-Chips survived immersion in ethanol, xylene, and paraffin during histological embedding and sectioning, and could still be wirelessly detected in paraffin-embedded RiO sections. Functionality was maintained across pH ranges from 2 to 10, simulating gastric and intestinal conditions. These findings demonstrate that O-Chips are exceptionally robust and compatible with routine laboratory workflows.

In a fourth example, a high-throughput detection system was developed to enable simultaneous phenotyping and identification of RiOs. The system consisted of a microfluidic channel, a syringe pump for controlled organoid flow, a fluorescence microscope, and an RFID reader probe aligned at the detection zone. RiOs treated with fatty acids and stained with BODIPY were flowed through the system at a controlled rate. As each RiO passed the detection point, the system captured a fluorescence image and wirelessly read the EPC from the embedded O-Chip. This allowed direct correlation of lipid accumulation intensity with donor identity in real time.

In a fifth example, the RiO platform was applied to a forward cellomics screen for genetic drivers of hepatic steatosis. A pool of seven iPSC-derived liver RiOs—five from healthy donors and two from patients with Wolman disease (caused by LIPA gene mutations leading to lysosomal acid lipase deficiency)—was generated, each pre-labeled with a unique EPC. The pooled RiOs were co-cultured in a single well and exposed to free fatty acids to induce steatosis. Fluorescence imaging revealed two RiOs with markedly elevated BODIPY signal. Wireless interrogation of their EPCs confirmed that these corresponded precisely to the Wolman disease-derived lines, thereby validating the platform’s ability to link extreme phenotypes to genetic etiology in a pooled, high-throughput format.

These examples collectively demonstrate that the invention provides a scalable, robust, and functionally neutral method for digitalizing human organoids, enabling unprecedented applications in precision medicine, drug screening, and the systematic study of human genetic variation.
