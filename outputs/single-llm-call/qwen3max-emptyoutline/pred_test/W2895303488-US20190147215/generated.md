# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of digital image analysis and, more specifically, to automated methods and systems for segmenting whole cells—including both nuclei and cytoplasm—from two-dimensional (2D) fluorescence microscopy images using only a single imaging channel. The invention is particularly directed to a deep learning-based computational framework that enables robust, high-throughput, and accurate segmentation of individual cells in complex biological images characterized by significant variability in cell morphology, staining intensity, marker type, magnification, and experimental conditions such as drug treatments. Unlike conventional approaches that require dual-channel imaging—typically one channel for nuclear staining and another for cytoplasmic or membrane labeling—the disclosed method operates exclusively on a single channel depicting hyperintense cytoplasm against a hypointense background and nuclei, thereby eliminating the need for additional fluorescent markers that may be cytotoxic, spectrally limiting, or otherwise undesirable in live-cell or multiplexed imaging workflows. The invention further encompasses a hybrid pipeline integrating convolutional neural network (CNN) inference with classical image processing techniques, including multi-scale blob detection, shape-constrained watershed segmentation, and adaptive thresholding, to achieve precise delineation of touching or densely packed cells without reliance on prior knowledge of cell shape, size, or spatial distribution. This technology is applicable across diverse domains including high-content screening, drug discovery, cellular phenotyping, toxicology assessment, and fundamental cell biology research where rapid, reproducible, and scalable quantification of cellular features from single-channel microscopy data is essential.

## BACKGROUND

Automated analysis of cellular images has become indispensable in modern biological and biomedical research, enabling objective, high-throughput quantification of cellular morphology, subcellular organization, and dynamic responses to perturbations such as pharmaceutical compounds, genetic modifications, or environmental stressors. Central to this analytical pipeline is the task of cell segmentation—the process of identifying and delineating individual cells within an image—which serves as the foundation for downstream measurements including cell count, area, intensity, texture, and spatial relationships. Historically, segmentation efforts have focused predominantly on nuclei due to their relatively consistent spherical or ellipsoidal geometry, high contrast when stained with DNA-binding fluorophores (e.g., DAPI or Hoechst), and ease of isolation from background. Numerous algorithms based on thresholding, morphological operations, watershed transforms, active contours, and graph-based methods have been developed to segment nuclei with reasonable accuracy under controlled conditions. However, these nucleus-centric approaches fail to capture the full biological context of the cell, as critical functional and structural information resides in the cytoplasm, organelles, and plasma membrane.

Whole-cell segmentation—encompassing both nucleus and cytoplasm—presents substantially greater challenges. Cytoplasmic regions often exhibit heterogeneous intensity patterns, irregular shapes, variable sizes, and weak or discontinuous boundaries, especially in dense monolayers where cells are in close contact. Moreover, in many experimental setups, particularly those involving live-cell imaging or spectral multiplexing constraints, researchers avoid using nuclear stains due to potential phototoxicity, interference with cellular processes, or limited availability of compatible fluorescence channels. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for segmentation methods capable of operating on single-channel images where the cytoplasm appears bright (hyperintense) while both the nucleus and background remain dark (hypointense)—a scenario commonly encountered with cytosolic or organelle-targeted fluorescent proteins (e.g., GFP-tagged constructs) or vital dyes (e.g., MitoTracker).

Existing single-channel whole-cell segmentation techniques are limited in scope and robustness. Early approaches relied on handcrafted features and rule-based logic, such as iterative thresholding or pixel classification via support vector machines, but these methods typically assume uniform cell appearance and perform poorly under real-world variability induced by different cell lines, markers, magnifications, or drug-induced morphological changes. Other strategies attempt to infer cell boundaries indirectly through edge detection or gradient analysis, yet these are highly sensitive to noise and fail when intercellular boundaries lack sufficient contrast. In contrast, dual-channel methods circumvent these issues by first segmenting nuclei in a dedicated nuclear channel and then propagating those seeds into a second cytoplasmic channel via region-growing or watershed algorithms. While effective, this paradigm imposes a strict requirement for co-staining with a nuclear marker, which is not always feasible or desirable.

Recent advances in deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) like U-Net, have demonstrated remarkable success in biomedical image segmentation by learning hierarchical feature representations directly from data. Several studies have applied CNNs to cell segmentation, often using multi-channel inputs or focusing solely on nuclei. For instance, Van Valen et al. employed a two-channel CNN combining phase-contrast and nuclear fluorescence to segment cytoplasm, while others have extended these models to multi-organelle segmentation using three or more channels. However, few deep learning frameworks address the specific challenge of single-channel whole-cell segmentation in the absence of nuclear contrast. Furthermore, even state-of-the-art CNNs may produce fragmented or merged predictions when cells are tightly packed, necessitating post-processing steps to refine segmentation masks. Current post-processing methods often lack adaptability to diverse morphologies or fail to resolve ambiguous boundary cases without manual intervention.

Compounding these technical limitations is the inherent heterogeneity of biological imaging data. As illustrated in representative microscopy images, cells labeled with different markers (e.g., dsRed, TexasRed, Cy5) or imaged at varying magnifications (10x vs. 20x) display dramatic differences in cytoplasmic texture, intensity distribution, and nuclear visibility. Drug treatments can further alter cellular density, shape, and internal structure, rendering generic segmentation models ineffective. The Cell Tracking Challenge datasets, though valuable for benchmarking, rarely include images matching the hyperintense-cytoplasm/hypointense-nucleus configuration central to this invention. Thus, despite progress in computational imaging, a gap remains for a fully automated, single-channel, whole-cell segmentation system that is both generalizable across experimental conditions and capable of resolving complex cellular arrangements without auxiliary staining.

In summary, the prior art suffers from one or more of the following deficiencies: (1) dependence on nuclear co-staining, limiting applicability in live-cell or spectrally constrained experiments; (2) poor generalization to diverse cell types, markers, and morphological states; (3) inadequate handling of touching or clustered cells in single-channel settings; (4) overreliance on handcrafted features that do not capture the full complexity of cytoplasmic appearance; and (5) insufficient integration of deep learning predictions with robust, adaptive post-processing. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a novel, end-to-end framework that leverages a specially designed CNN architecture trained on single-channel data to simultaneously predict nuclear and cytoplasmic probabilities, followed by a cascade of optimized classical image processing steps that transform these probabilistic maps into precise, individualized cell segmentations—thereby enabling accurate, scalable, and marker-efficient cellular analysis.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive, automated method and system for segmenting whole cells—including both nuclei and cytoplasm—from single-channel two-dimensional fluorescence microscopy images, wherein the cytoplasm appears hyperintense relative to the hypointense nuclei and background. The invention eliminates the need for nuclear co-staining or additional imaging channels, thereby addressing critical limitations in live-cell imaging, spectral multiplexing, and high-throughput screening applications. At its core, the invention comprises a deep learning-based predictive model trained to generate per-pixel probability maps for three semantic classes: nuclei, cytoplasm, and background, using only a single input channel depicting cytoplasmic fluorescence. This model is implemented via a modified U-Net architecture featuring an asymmetric contracting-expanding pathway with dropout regularization to enhance generalization and reduce overfitting. Following inference, the invention employs a multi-stage post-processing pipeline that intelligently combines the predicted probability maps with classical image analysis techniques to produce high-fidelity segmentation masks.

Specifically, the method begins with preprocessing steps including top-hat filtering to correct uneven illumination and optional down-sampling to normalize pixel resolution across magnifications. The preprocessed image is then fed into the trained deep learning model, which outputs spatially aligned probability maps indicating the likelihood of each pixel belonging to a nucleus, cytoplasm, or background. These maps serve as the foundation for subsequent refinement. In the nuclei seed detection stage, the nuclear probability map undergoes multi-scale Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) blob enhancement to accentuate candidate nuclei across varying sizes, followed by adaptive multi-level Otsu thresholding to generate a binary nuclear mask. To resolve touching or overlapping nuclei—a common source of segmentation error—the invention applies an extended h-minima transform coupled with a shape-based inverse distance watershed algorithm, ensuring that each detected nucleus is represented as a distinct, non-overlapping seed.

These refined nuclear seeds are then utilized in the final cell segmentation stage. Here, the cytoplasmic probability map is combined with a transformed version of the original intensity image—processed through Gaussian smoothing, intensity scaling, and logarithmic conversion—to enhance cell body contrast. Background regions are identified via a context-aware three-level Otsu thresholding strategy that incorporates prior knowledge of expected cell density and area derived from the number of detected nuclei. A seeded watershed transform is subsequently executed on this enhanced composite image, using the segmented nuclei as markers, thereby propagating each nuclear seed outward to define the complete cellular boundary. This approach inherently enforces a one-to-one correspondence between nuclei and cells, effectively separating adjacent or partially overlapping cells even in the absence of clear intercellular gradients.

The invention further introduces a novel segmentation similarity metric tailored for evaluating large-scale cell segmentation tasks. Unlike simple overlap measures such as Dice coefficient, this metric accounts for both per-object alignment fidelity and global label correspondence by computing a weighted combination of maximum overlap ratios and bijective mapping accuracy between reference and target segmentations. Empirical validation demonstrates that the invention achieves segmentation accuracy comparable to dual-channel methods—despite using only a single channel—with average cell-level similarity scores exceeding 0.84 across diverse datasets encompassing multiple cell lines (HeLa, fibroblasts, HepG2, U2OS), fluorescent markers (eGFP-FYVE, MitoTracker Red, proprietary mitochondrial dyes), magnifications (10x, 20x), and drug treatment conditions. Processing times are optimized for high-throughput workflows, with inference and post-processing completed in 4–6 seconds per 2048×2048 image on standard GPU-accelerated hardware.

In essence, the invention represents a significant advancement over the prior art by delivering a robust, generalizable, and efficient solution for single-channel whole-cell segmentation that maintains high accuracy without requiring nuclear counterstains. It enables researchers to maximize the utility of limited fluorescence channels for biomarker discovery, reduces experimental complexity and cost, and supports reproducible, large-scale cellular phenotyping in both fixed and live-cell contexts.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### System Overview

The present invention is embodied as an integrated computational system designed to perform automated whole-cell segmentation from single-channel fluorescence microscopy images. The system operates under the fundamental constraint that only one imaging channel is available—typically depicting cytoplasmic or organelle-localized fluorescence—and that within this channel, the cytoplasm exhibits higher intensity (hyperintense) compared to both the cell nuclei and the surrounding background, which appear darker (hypointense). This configuration is common in experiments utilizing cytosolic fluorescent proteins (e.g., GFP, dsRed), mitochondrial dyes (e.g., MitoTracker), or other non-nuclear probes, particularly in scenarios where nuclear staining is avoided due to toxicity concerns or spectral limitations. The system is architected as a sequential pipeline comprising four principal stages: image preprocessing, deep learning-based semantic prediction, nuclei seed detection and refinement, and seeded watershed-based cell segmentation. Each stage is meticulously engineered to address the unique challenges posed by biological image variability, including differences in cell morphology, marker expression, magnification, and experimental perturbations such as drug treatments.

The system accepts as input a raw 2D fluorescence microscopy image, typically of size 2048×2048 pixels, though it is adaptable to other resolutions. Prior to any segmentation processing, the image undergoes standardized preprocessing to mitigate common artifacts and normalize acquisition parameters. Specifically, uneven illumination—a frequent issue in widefield microscopy—is corrected using a top-hat morphological filter with a large structuring element (e.g., 200×200 pixels), which effectively suppresses low-frequency background variations while preserving local cellular structures. Additionally, to accommodate images acquired at different optical magnifications (e.g., 10x yielding 0.65 μm/pixel versus 20x yielding 0.325 μm/pixel), the system optionally resamples all inputs to a canonical resolution corresponding approximately to 10x magnification. This normalization ensures consistent feature representation during deep learning inference and prevents scale-dependent performance degradation.

Following preprocessing, the image is processed by a deep convolutional neural network trained to perform pixel-wise semantic classification into three mutually exclusive categories: nucleus, cytoplasm, and background. This network, detailed in the next section, generates three spatially registered probability maps, each assigning a value between 0 and 1 to every pixel indicating the model’s confidence in that pixel’s class membership. These probabilistic outputs are not used directly as segmentation masks but instead serve as rich, learned feature representations that guide subsequent classical image processing steps. The separation of deep learning inference from post-processing allows the system to leverage the representational power of neural networks while retaining the interpretability, efficiency, and controllability of traditional computer vision techniques.

The third stage focuses exclusively on the nuclear probability map to identify and isolate individual nuclei, which will later act as seeds for whole-cell segmentation. Given that nuclei are typically compact, blob-like structures—even when unstained and appearing as intensity minima within brighter cytoplasm—the system employs a multi-scale blob detection algorithm based on the Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) operator. This enhances regions matching expected nuclear morphology across a range of sizes, compensating for natural variation in nuclear dimensions. The enhanced map is then binarized using an adaptive multi-threshold Otsu method, which automatically selects an optimal threshold level based on user-defined sensitivity parameters and the statistical distribution of pixel intensities. The resulting binary mask, however, may contain merged components representing clusters of touching nuclei. To resolve these, the system applies an extended h-minima transform followed by a shape-constrained watershed segmentation on the inverse distance transform of the mask, effectively splitting connected nuclei into discrete, non-overlapping seeds.

Finally, in the cell segmentation stage, the system integrates the cytoplasmic probability map with a transformed version of the original intensity image to create a composite energy landscape suitable for watershed propagation. The transformation includes Gaussian smoothing to reduce noise, linear intensity scaling to enhance dynamic range, and conversion to logarithmic space to better model multiplicative illumination effects. Background regions are precisely delineated using a context-aware Otsu thresholding strategy that estimates total expected cellular area based on the count and average size of detected nuclei, thereby selecting a threshold that best matches biological expectations. With background and nuclear seeds defined, a seeded watershed algorithm partitions the image into individual cell regions, each anchored to a single nucleus. The output is a labeled segmentation mask where each cell is assigned a unique integer identifier, enabling quantitative analysis of cellular properties.

The entire system is implemented in a modular software architecture, with the deep learning component built using the MXNet framework and post-processing modules leveraging ITK and OpenCV libraries for performance-critical operations. It is deployable on standard workstations equipped with GPUs for accelerated inference and scales efficiently to batch-process thousands of images in high-content screening pipelines. By unifying deep learning with principled image analysis, the system delivers accurate, robust, and efficient whole-cell segmentation from minimal imaging data, fulfilling a long-standing need in computational biology.

### Development of a Training Model

The development of the deep learning predictive model constitutes a foundational aspect of the present invention, enabling the system to extract meaningful semantic information from single-channel microscopy images without reliance on handcrafted features or auxiliary staining. The model is constructed as a customized variant of the U-Net architecture, a fully convolutional encoder-decoder network originally designed for biomedical image segmentation. However, unlike standard U-Net implementations, the architecture disclosed herein incorporates several key modifications to optimize performance for the specific task of simultaneous nucleus and cytoplasm prediction in hyperintense-cytoplasm/hypointense-nucleus imagery.

The input to the model consists of image patches of size 176×176 pixels, extracted from preprocessed training images with a 16-pixel overlap on all sides to mitigate edge artifacts during patch stitching at inference time. Only the central 160×160 region of each patch is used for loss computation, ensuring that predictions near patch boundaries—which may suffer from reduced contextual information—are excluded from gradient updates. The contracting (encoder) path comprises five sequential blocks, each consisting of a 3×3 convolutional layer followed by a 2×2 max-pooling operation with stride 2. The number of filters in these layers follows the sequence 32, 64, 128, 128, and 256, progressively increasing feature depth while reducing spatial resolution to 5×5 at the bottleneck. This design choice balances representational capacity with computational efficiency, avoiding the excessive parameter count of deeper architectures (e.g., those using 1024 filters at the base) which offer diminishing returns and increased risk of overfitting on limited biological datasets.

The expanding (decoder) path mirrors the encoder in structure but employs transposed convolutions (deconvolutions) for upsampling, gradually restoring spatial resolution to the original input size. Crucially, the architecture is asymmetric: the number of filters and exact layer configurations differ slightly between corresponding encoder and decoder blocks. For instance, after the first deconvolution step, the feature map is concatenated with a corresponding encoder feature map of size 192 channels rather than the typical symmetric 128, reflecting empirical tuning to enhance gradient flow and feature reuse. Three dropout layers, each with a 50% retention rate, are strategically inserted at high-capacity stages of the decoder to regularize learning and improve generalization across diverse imaging conditions.

The model is trained to predict three output channels corresponding to pixel-wise probabilities for nucleus, cytoplasm, and background. Training utilizes a custom loss function that extends the standard root mean square deviation (RMSD) by incorporating a soft constraint enforcing mutual exclusivity among the three classes. Formally, the loss is defined as:

f(x) = wₙ·RMSD(pₙ, lₙ) + w_c·RMSD(p_c, l_c) + w_b·RMSD(p_b, l_b) + w·RMSD(lₙ + l_c + l_b, 1)

where pₙ, p_c, p_b denote predicted probabilities and lₙ, l_c, l_b are binary ground truth labels for nucleus, cytoplasm, and background, respectively. The weights wₙ, w_c, w_b, and w are all set to 1.0 in the preferred embodiment, though they may be adjusted to emphasize certain classes if needed. The final term penalizes deviations from the ideal condition that every pixel belongs to exactly one class, thereby encouraging crisp, non-overlapping predictions.

Training data consists of 108 annotated images spanning five distinct experimental datasets, each featuring different cell lines (HeLa, fibroblasts, HepG2, U2OS), fluorescent markers (eGFP-FYVE, MitoTracker Red, proprietary mitochondrial dyes), and magnifications (10x, 20x). Ground truth labels are generated semi-automatically: initially, a dual-channel segmentation method—using both nuclear (e.g., DAPI) and cytoplasmic channels—is applied to produce preliminary masks, which are then manually refined by expert biologists using tools like CellProfiler to correct errors in cell merging, splitting, or boundary placement. This hybrid approach ensures high-quality annotations while mitigating the prohibitive time cost of fully manual segmentation.

Data augmentation is employed to increase effective dataset size and robustness. Each training patch is rotated by 90-degree increments, yielding four variants per original patch. Additional augmentations such as flipping or elastic deformations were evaluated but found unnecessary given the model’s strong performance with rotation alone. The model is trained for 30 epochs using a batch size of 32 and an initial learning rate of 0.001, with early stopping based on validation loss to prevent overfitting. Training is conducted on GPU-accelerated infrastructure (e.g., NVIDIA Tesla K80), completing in approximately 6 hours per cross-validation fold. The resulting model exhibits high predictive accuracy, with 10-fold cross-validation yielding area under the ROC curve (AUC) values above 0.95 for both nuclei and cytoplasm, and classification accuracies of 0.915 and 0.878, respectively, at a 0.5 decision threshold.

This carefully engineered training regimen produces a model that not only learns to distinguish subtle intensity and textural cues indicative of nuclear and cytoplasmic regions but also generalizes effectively across unseen experimental conditions—a critical capability given the inherent heterogeneity of biological imaging data. The model’s outputs serve as the cornerstone for the subsequent post-processing stages, providing reliable probabilistic guidance that enables accurate whole-cell segmentation from a single imaging channel.

### Single Channel Whole Cell Segmentation Workflow

The single-channel whole-cell segmentation workflow of the present invention is a deterministic, multi-stage algorithm that transforms a raw fluorescence microscopy image into a precise, labeled segmentation mask identifying individual cells, each encompassing both nucleus and cytoplasm. This workflow is executed entirely on a single input channel—typically displaying hyperintense cytoplasm against hypointense nuclei and background—and does not require any additional imaging modalities or staining protocols. The process unfolds in three sequential phases following initial image preprocessing: deep learning inference to generate semantic probability maps, nuclei seed detection and refinement, and seeded watershed-based cell segmentation.

Upon receiving a preprocessed image (corrected for illumination and normalized for magnification), the workflow initiates deep learning inference by partitioning the image into overlapping 176×176 pixel patches. Each patch is independently processed by the trained U-Net model described previously, yielding three 160×160 probability maps corresponding to nucleus, cytoplasm, and background. These patches are then stitched together to reconstruct full-resolution probability maps for the entire image, with overlapping regions averaged to ensure smooth transitions. The resulting nucleus probability map exhibits elevated values within nuclear regions—despite their low absolute intensity in the original image—while the cytoplasm map highlights cellular bodies, and the background map identifies extracellular areas.

The second phase focuses on extracting reliable nuclear seeds from the nucleus probability map. Simple thresholding (e.g., at 0.5) is insufficient due to noise, false positives, and the prevalence of touching nuclei that would otherwise form large connected components. To address this, the workflow first applies a multi-scale Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) blob detector. The LoG operator, defined as ∇²G_σ(x,y) where G_σ is a Gaussian kernel with standard deviation σ, is computed across a range of σ values (e.g., 1–5 pixels) to enhance blob-like structures of varying sizes. The maximum response across scales is retained, producing an enhanced map where candidate nuclei are accentuated regardless of their actual dimensions. This map is then subjected to adaptive multi-level Otsu thresholding. Standard Otsu’s method finds a single threshold that minimizes intra-class variance, but here a five-level variant is used, generating four thresholds that partition the intensity histogram into five regions. Based on a user-configurable sensitivity parameter (default: 60), the system selects the third threshold to separate background from hypointense and hyperintense nuclear blobs, yielding a binary nuclear mask.

However, this mask may still contain merged nuclei. To resolve these, the workflow computes the inverse Euclidean distance transform of the binary mask, assigning to each foreground pixel a value equal to its distance from the nearest background pixel. An extended h-minima transform is then applied: first, all regional minima in the distance map with depth less than a predefined h-value (default: 3 μm, converted to pixels based on image resolution) are suppressed, and then the remaining minima are extracted as markers. These markers represent the centers of individual nuclei, even within clusters. Finally, a seeded watershed transform is performed on the inverse distance map using these markers, effectively flooding basins from each seed until meeting neighboring catchment areas, thereby splitting connected components into discrete nuclei. The output is a labeled nuclear seed map where each nucleus bears a unique identifier.

In the third and final phase, these nuclear seeds are leveraged to segment whole cells. The cytoplasm probability map alone is inadequate for separating touching cells due to weak or absent boundary gradients. Therefore, the workflow constructs a composite image by multiplying the cytoplasm map with a transformed version of the original intensity image. This transformation involves three steps: (1) Gaussian smoothing with a small kernel (e.g., σ=1) to suppress high-frequency noise; (2) linear intensity scaling to stretch the dynamic range; and (3) conversion to logarithmic space to stabilize variance and better model multiplicative illumination effects. The resulting composite image exhibits enhanced contrast between cellular and non-cellular regions.

Background is then delineated using a context-aware three-level Otsu thresholding strategy. Unlike conventional thresholding, this method estimates the total expected cellular area by multiplying the number of detected nuclei by an empirically derived average cell area (e.g., 1000 μm²). Among the three thresholds produced by Otsu’s method, the one yielding a foreground area closest to this estimate is selected as the background cutoff. This ensures that the segmentation adapts to varying cell densities across images.

With background and nuclear seeds defined, a seeded watershed transform is executed on the composite image. Each nuclear seed acts as a marker, and the watershed algorithm propagates outward, partitioning the image into catchment basins that correspond to individual cells. Because the seeds are guaranteed to be non-overlapping and correctly localized, this process reliably separates adjacent cells even in the absence of clear intercellular boundaries. The final output is a labeled segmentation mask where each cell is assigned a unique integer, enabling quantitative analysis of cellular morphology, intensity, and spatial relationships.

This workflow exemplifies a synergistic fusion of deep learning and classical image processing: the neural network provides high-level semantic understanding, while traditional algorithms enforce geometric and topological constraints necessary for biologically plausible segmentation. The result is a robust, efficient, and accurate method for whole-cell segmentation from minimal imaging data.

### Assessment of Segmentation Results

The assessment of segmentation results in the present invention is conducted through a rigorous, multi-faceted evaluation framework designed to quantify both pixel-level classification accuracy and object-level segmentation fidelity across diverse biological imaging scenarios. Given the complexity and scale of cellular segmentation tasks—where hundreds of cells may appear in a single image—conventional binary similarity metrics such as Dice coefficient or Jaccard index are insufficient, as they fail to account for errors in object counting, merging, or splitting. To address this, the invention introduces a novel segmentation similarity metric (SM) that holistically evaluates segmentation quality by combining per-object overlap precision with global label correspondence.

Formally, let IR and IT denote the reference (ground truth) and target (automated) segmentation images, respectively, with label sets R = {r₁, r₂, ..., r_N} and T = {t₁, t₂, ..., t_M}. The SM metric is defined as:

SM(R,T) = k·(1/N Σᵢ₌₁ᴺ max_{tⱼ ∈ T^{rᵢ}} [2|rᵢ ∩ tⱼ| / (|rᵢ| + |tⱼ|)]) + (1−k)·(2|P_T^R| / (N + M))

where T^{rᵢ} is the set of target labels overlapping with reference label rᵢ, P_T^R is the set of labels participating in a bijective (one-to-one) mapping between R and T, |·| denotes set cardinality (pixel count), and k is a weighting factor empirically set to 0.6. The first term computes the average maximum Dice overlap between each reference object and its best-matching target counterpart, capturing boundary accuracy. The second term measures the proportion of correctly matched objects relative to the total number of objects, penalizing over-segmentation (M > N) and under-segmentation (M < N). This dual-component design ensures that high SM scores require both precise boundary delineation and correct object enumeration.

Ground truth data for evaluation is derived through two complementary approaches. Primary training and validation utilize "sub-optimal" automated segmentations generated by a dual-channel method: nuclei are first segmented in a DAPI channel using the same seed detection algorithm described herein, then used as seeds to segment cells in the cytoplasmic channel via watershed. While not perfect, these segmentations provide consistent, large-scale annotations across 108 images spanning five experimental datasets. For final validation, a subset of 10 images is manually refined by expert biologists using CellProfiler, resulting in "semi-automated ground truth" that corrects errors in merging, splitting, and boundary placement—serving as the gold standard for accuracy assessment.

Three comprehensive experiments validate the invention’s performance. In Experiment 1, the model is trained on 98 images and tested on 10 images with semi-automated ground truth. Results show an average cell-level SM score of 0.87 against expert annotations and 0.86 against dual-channel segmentations, with image-level averages of 0.86 and 0.85, respectively. Notably, performance dips slightly on images with actin fiber staining (SM ≈ 0.72), where cytoplasmic texture lacks nuclear-cytoplasmic contrast, highlighting a known limitation. Experiment 2 employs 10-fold cross-validation across all 108 images, reporting ROC AUC > 0.95 for both nuclei and cytoplasm, with pixel classification accuracies of 0.915 and 0.878. Dataset-level SM scores range from 0.62 (highly variable mitochondrial dye) to 0.91 (consistent eGFP-FYVE), averaging 0.84 overall. Experiment 3 tests generalizability by training on Datasets 1–5 and evaluating on an independent Dataset 6 (15 images). Despite no exposure to this data during training, the model achieves an average SM of 0.84, demonstrating robustness to unseen experimental conditions.

Processing efficiency is also assessed: inference and post-processing require 4–6 seconds per 2048×2048 image on a system with an NVIDIA Tesla K80 GPU, making the method suitable for high-throughput screening. Qualitative inspection by domain experts confirms that segmentation boundaries align closely with visual expectations, particularly in resolving touching cells—a persistent challenge in single-channel segmentation.

Collectively, these assessments confirm that the invention achieves segmentation accuracy comparable to dual-channel methods while operating on a single channel, thereby validating its utility as a practical, scalable solution for whole-cell analysis in resource-constrained imaging environments.

### Examples

The efficacy and versatility of the present invention are demonstrated through a series of concrete examples drawn from experimental validation across diverse biological contexts. These examples illustrate the system’s ability to accurately segment whole cells from single-channel fluorescence microscopy images exhibiting substantial variability in cell type, fluorescent marker, magnification, and pharmacological treatment—all without the aid of nuclear co-staining.

In a first example, images from Dataset 1 feature HeLa cells transfected with an eGFP-tagged tandem FYVE domain construct, which binds phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI(3)P) and localizes to early endosomes. Under control conditions, the eGFP signal appears as bright puncta within the cytoplasm; upon treatment with a PI(3)P-depleting compound, the signal redistributes to a diffuse cytosolic pattern. Despite this dramatic shift in subcellular localization—and the consequent loss of punctate structure—the invention successfully segments individual cells in both conditions. Visual comparison with semi-automated ground truth shows close alignment of cell boundaries, with an image-level segmentation similarity (SM) score of 0.90. The system correctly identifies cells even when cytoplasmic intensity becomes nearly uniform, leveraging subtle residual gradients and the spatial guidance provided by predicted nuclear seeds.

A second example, drawn from Dataset 2, involves fibroblast cells stained with MitoTracker Red, a vital dye that accumulates in mitochondria in a membrane potential-dependent manner. These images, acquired at 20x magnification (0.325 μm/pixel), display elongated, thread-like mitochondrial networks that permeate the cytoplasm. The hyperintense mitochondrial structures create complex internal textures, yet the background and nuclei remain hypointense. The invention’s deep learning model accurately distinguishes cytoplasmic regions from background, and the post-processing pipeline effectively separates densely packed fibroblasts. Although this dataset yields a lower average SM score (0.62) due to extreme morphological variability, individual images still achieve scores above 0.80, demonstrating resilience to challenging staining patterns.

Dataset 3 presents another illustrative case using a proprietary mitochondrial dye from GE Cytiva’s Cell Health kit, applied to HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells. Here, mitochondrial morphology varies widely between cells—some exhibit fragmented organelles, others fused networks—resulting in heterogeneous cytoplasmic appearances within the same image. The invention handles this heterogeneity by learning generalized features of cytoplasmic occupancy during training, achieving an average SM of 0.91. Close-up views reveal precise delineation of cell membranes, even between adjacent cells sharing similar mitochondrial density.

In Dataset 4, U2OS osteosarcoma cells are labeled with TexasRed-conjugated antibodies targeting cytoskeletal components. The resulting images show intense, fibrillar actin structures that span the entire cytoplasm, creating a near-uniform intensity field with minimal contrast between nucleus and cytoplasm. This represents one of the most difficult scenarios for single-channel segmentation. Nevertheless, the invention detects nuclear seeds through probabilistic inference—identifying subtle intensity minima—and uses them to anchor cell segmentation. While the SM score for this image drops to 0.72, qualitative inspection confirms that the majority of cells are correctly isolated, with errors primarily confined to regions of extreme cell confluence.

Finally, Dataset 6 serves as an out-of-distribution test case: the model, trained exclusively on Datasets 1–5, is applied to 15 new images of HeLa cells stained with TexasRed under identical experimental conditions but acquired in a separate imaging session. Despite no prior exposure to this specific dataset, the system achieves an average SM of 0.84, with individual scores ranging from 0.76 to 0.88. This confirms the model’s capacity for generalization beyond the training distribution.

Across all examples, processing time remains consistently low (4–6 seconds per image), and segmentation outputs are immediately usable for downstream quantification—such as measuring cytoplasmic intensity changes in response to drug treatment or counting mitotic figures in proliferating populations. These real-world demonstrations underscore the invention’s practical value as a robust, flexible, and efficient tool for single-channel whole-cell segmentation in contemporary biological research.
