# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of environmental remediation, and more specifically to a method and apparatus for the electrochemical remediation of heavy metal-contaminated soils. The invention provides an integrated system that combines soil washing with chelating agents and an asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical (AACE) filtration device to efficiently remove, reduce, and recover heavy metal cations from contaminated soil while minimizing chemical consumption, preventing secondary pollution, and preserving essential soil nutrients. The invention further encompasses novel amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon electrodes designed to enhance the efficiency and selectivity of heavy metal electrodeposition from complex aqueous matrices. The disclosed technology is particularly applicable to the remediation of agricultural and urban lands contaminated with toxic heavy metals such as copper, lead, cadmium, and others, and may also be adapted for the recovery of valuable metals from industrial waste streams.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Soil contamination by heavy metals has emerged as a critical global environmental challenge, driven by escalating anthropogenic activities including mining, industrial manufacturing, and improper waste disposal. Heavy metals, once introduced into soil ecosystems, persist indefinitely due to their non-biodegradable nature and tend to accumulate in the upper crust of the earth, posing severe risks to human health, ecological balance, and agricultural productivity. Conventional remediation strategies have been developed to address this issue, but each suffers from significant limitations that hinder their widespread practical application.

One widely explored approach is soil washing using strong chelating agents, such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). This method mobilizes heavy metal cations by forming soluble complexes that can be extracted from the soil matrix. However, the technique is plagued by three major drawbacks: first, it requires large quantities of chelating agents, rendering the process economically burdensome; second, the resulting washing effluent—now laden with toxic heavy metal-chelate complexes—presents a secondary pollution problem that necessitates additional treatment; and third, the aggressive chelation process indiscriminately removes essential soil nutrients such as calcium and magnesium, leading to long-term soil degradation and reduced fertility.

An alternative strategy involves the immobilization of heavy metals through the addition of high-surface-area sorbents, which bind metal cations and reduce their bioavailability and mobility. While this approach avoids soil excavation and minimizes nutrient loss, it suffers from inherently slow kinetics and low adsorption capacity due to reliance on physicochemical adsorption mechanisms. Moreover, the stability of immobilized metals is not guaranteed over extended periods, requiring continuous monitoring and potential re-treatment, which undermines its sustainability and cost-effectiveness.

Phytoremediation, which employs metal-accumulating plants to extract contaminants from soil, offers an energy-efficient and environmentally benign solution. Nevertheless, its extremely slow remediation rate—often spanning years or even decades—limits its applicability to remote or non-urgent sites. Additionally, the harvested biomass containing concentrated heavy metals constitutes a hazardous waste stream that must be safely disposed of or processed, thereby introducing the risk of secondary contamination if not managed properly.

Electrochemical remediation represents another promising avenue, wherein a direct current (DC) electric field is applied across contaminated soil to drive the migration of ionic species via electroosmosis and electromigration, followed by electrodeposition of metals at the cathode. Despite its theoretical appeal, practical implementation of DC-based electroremediation faces substantial obstacles. The high voltage gradients required (typically around 100 V/m) to sustain adequate ion transport in low-permeability soils result in excessive energy consumption. Furthermore, competing electrochemical reactions—particularly the electrolysis of water at the electrodes—lead to significant energy losses, pH imbalances, and the generation of gases that disrupt soil structure. Critically, when heavy metals are extracted as anionic chelate complexes (e.g., MEDTA²⁻), their negative charge causes them to be repelled by the cathode under DC conditions, severely limiting deposition efficiency and promoting parasitic reactions instead.

A fundamental limitation shared by all conventional methods is that they treat heavy metals solely in their ionic forms, without altering their oxidation state. In contrast, the most stable and least hazardous form of heavy metals is their zero-valent metallic state, which is insoluble, non-bioavailable, and amenable to recovery and reuse. Achieving this transformation requires a reduction process that not only separates the metal from the soil but also converts it into a compact, elemental form. While electrochemical reduction is ideally suited for this purpose, existing technologies fail to integrate efficient extraction, selective reduction, and reagent recycling into a single, sustainable workflow.

Thus, there remains a pressing need for an innovative remediation technology that overcomes the deficiencies of current methods by combining high removal efficiency, minimal chemical consumption, preservation of soil nutrients, elimination of secondary waste, and the ability to recover heavy metals in their valuable metallic form. The present invention addresses this unmet need through a novel integration of recirculating soil washing and asymmetrical alternating current electrochemistry, enabled by specially engineered functional electrodes.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive and efficient method for the remediation of heavy metal-contaminated soil, which simultaneously achieves high removal rates, minimal reagent consumption, preservation of soil nutrients, and recovery of heavy metals in their zero-valent metallic form. The invention is based on a synergistic combination of a recirculating soil washing system and an asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical (AACE) filtration device, which together enable closed-loop operation with negligible secondary waste generation.

In accordance with the invention, contaminated soil is first subjected to washing with a chelating agent solution, preferably ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), in a recirculating flow configuration. The chelating agent mobilizes heavy metal cations by forming soluble anionic complexes, which are then transported to the AACE filtration device. The key innovation lies in the design and operation of this electrochemical filter, which employs a pair of amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon (Ami-PC) electrodes and applies an asymmetrical alternating voltage waveform—comprising a short positive bias phase followed by a longer negative bias phase—to selectively capture and reduce heavy metal ions while regenerating the chelating agent for reuse.

During the positive bias phase (e.g., +5 V for 0.5 ms), an electrical double layer forms on the electrode surface, attracting anionic species including the heavy metal-EDTA complexes. The amidoxime functional groups grafted onto the porous carbon substrate provide high-affinity chelation sites that competitively bind heavy metal cations, effectively displacing them from the EDTA ligand and anchoring the metal-containing species near the electrode surface. In the subsequent negative bias phase (e.g., −10 V for 2 ms), the captured heavy metal cations are electrochemically reduced to their zero-valent metallic states and deposited as solid particles on the electrode. The now-liberated EDTA anions, having lost affinity for the neutral metal particles and being repelled by the negative electrode potential, are released back into the solution, thereby regenerating the chelating agent for continued use in the soil washing loop.

A critical advantage of this approach is its inherent selectivity for heavy metals over essential soil nutrients such as calcium and magnesium. Although these nutrient cations may also form EDTA complexes and be adsorbed onto the electrode during the positive bias phase, their more negative standard reduction potentials prevent their reduction during the negative bias phase. Consequently, they remain in solution and are returned to the soil during subsequent washing cycles, thereby maintaining soil fertility and avoiding nutrient depletion.

The Ami-PC electrodes are fabricated by coating a conductive carbon felt substrate with a slurry containing polyacrylonitrile (PAN) and nano-sized activated carbon, followed by a hydrothermal reaction with hydroxylamine to convert nitrile groups to amidoxime functionalities. This design yields a highly porous, hydrophilic, and conductive electrode with abundant chelation sites that maximize contact with the washing effluent and enhance electrodeposition efficiency. The nano-activated carbon component improves electrical conductivity, while the macroporous structure facilitates fluid flow and mass transport.

The invention further includes methods for long-term operation and regeneration of the AACE system. After extended use, the electrode surface may become partially blocked by accumulated metal particles, leading to a gradual decline in filtration efficiency. This can be reversed by applying a mild acid elution (e.g., 0.1 M HCl) combined with a low reverse DC bias, which dissolves and removes the deposited metals without damaging the electrode structure, thereby restoring full performance.

Experimental validation demonstrates that the AACE method reduces soil concentrations of copper, lead, and cadmium to levels below regulatory thresholds for residential use, outperforms conventional DC electrochemical filtration in both efficiency and metal recovery quality, and preserves soil health as confirmed by plant growth assays. The recovered metals are predominantly in their zero-valent form, as verified by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), whereas DC methods produce non-conductive hydroxides and co-precipitate nutrients like calcium.

In summary, the invention provides a scalable, sustainable, and economically viable solution for heavy metal soil remediation that integrates extraction, reduction, reagent recycling, and metal recovery into a single closed-loop system, with broad applicability to both environmental cleanup and industrial resource recovery.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and apparatus for the remediation of heavy metal-contaminated soil through an integrated system that combines recirculating soil washing with asymmetrical alternating current electrochemical (AACE) filtration. The following detailed description sets forth the specific components, operational parameters, and mechanistic principles that enable the invention to achieve superior performance compared to existing technologies.

The core of the invention is the AACE remediation system, which consists of two primary subsystems: a recirculating soil washing unit and an AACE electrochemical filtration device. The soil washing unit comprises a treatment cylinder containing excavated contaminated soil, connected to one or more reservoirs containing a chelating agent solution, typically an aqueous solution of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) at a concentration ranging from 10 to 50 mM, with 30 mM being preferred for optimal balance between extraction efficiency and cost. A pump, such as a peristaltic or syringe pump, circulates the EDTA solution through the soil column at a controlled flow rate, which is optimized based on the degree of contamination and the target filtration efficiency. As the EDTA solution percolates through the soil, it forms stable anionic complexes with heavy metal cations (denoted as MEDTA²⁻, where M represents Cu²⁺, Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺, or other divalent heavy metals), thereby mobilizing them from the soil matrix into the aqueous phase.

The washing effluent, now containing the heavy metal-EDTA complexes along with any co-extracted nutrient cations (such as Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, which form NEDTA²⁻ complexes), is directed to the AACE filtration device. This device is constructed with two parallel electrodes made of amidoxime-functionalized porous carbon (Ami-PC), separated by a porous separator such as tissue paper or a microporous membrane to prevent electrical shorting while allowing ionic conduction. The electrodes are connected to an alternating current power supply capable of generating an asymmetrical voltage waveform, characterized by a short-duration positive bias (e.g., +5 V for 0.5 milliseconds) followed by a longer-duration negative bias (e.g., −10 V for 2 milliseconds), with the counter electrode grounded. The duty cycle and amplitude of this waveform are carefully selected to optimize the sequential processes of complex adsorption and metal reduction.

The Ami-PC electrodes are fabricated through a multi-step process that begins with a carbon felt substrate, which provides a three-dimensional, highly porous scaffold with excellent electrical conductivity and mechanical stability. The carbon felt, typically 3 mm thick and cut into circular discs of 1 cm diameter, is dip-coated with a precursor slurry composed of polyacrylonitrile (PAN), nano-sized activated carbon (approximately 40 nm in diameter), and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) in a mass ratio of 1:1:30. This slurry is stirred overnight to ensure homogeneity and then applied to the carbon felt, which is subsequently air-dried on a hotplate at 70°C. The coated felt is then subjected to a hydrothermal reaction in an aqueous solution containing hydroxylamine hydrochloride (80 mg/mL) and sodium carbonate (60 mg/mL) at 70°C for 90 minutes. This reaction converts the nitrile groups (–C≡N) of PAN into amidoxime groups (–C(=NOH)NH₂), which are known for their high affinity toward heavy metal ions. The resulting Ami-PC electrode exhibits a hydrophilic surface that ensures full wetting of the high-surface-area carbon fibers, and the amidoxime functionalities serve as selective chelation sites that enhance the local concentration of heavy metal ions near the electrode surface during the positive bias phase.

The operation of the AACE filtration device proceeds in three distinct phases, synchronized with the applied voltage waveform. In the initial state (Phase I), ions in the washing effluent are randomly distributed. Upon application of the positive bias (Phase II, e.g., +5 V), cations migrate toward the cathode and anions toward the anode, establishing an electrical double layer at the electrode-electrolyte interface. The anionic heavy metal-EDTA complexes (MEDTA²⁻) are drawn to the positively biased working electrode, where the amidoxime groups compete with EDTA for binding to the heavy metal cations. Due to the higher affinity of amidoxime for certain heavy metals, the metal cations are partially transferred from the EDTA ligand to the electrode surface, effectively stabilizing the complex at the interface. Concurrently, nutrient-EDTA complexes (NEDTA²⁻) are also adsorbed, but their metal centers (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) are not displaced due to weaker interactions with amidoxime.

In Phase III, the bias is reversed to a negative potential (e.g., −10 V), creating reducing conditions at the working electrode. The heavy metal cations that are now in close proximity to the electrode surface—either directly bound to amidoxime or loosely associated—are electrochemically reduced to their zero-valent metallic states (M⁰) and deposited as solid particles within the porous electrode structure. The reduction potential of each metal determines its susceptibility to this process; for example, Cu²⁺/Cu (E° = +0.34 V), Pb²⁺/Pb (E° = −0.13 V), and Cd²⁺/Cd (E° = −0.40 V) are all readily reducible under the applied −10 V bias. In contrast, Ca²⁺/Ca (E° = −2.87 V) and Mg²⁺/Mg (E° = −2.37 V) have much more negative reduction potentials and therefore remain in their ionic forms, unable to be reduced under the same conditions. The liberated EDTA anions, no longer coordinated to metal cations and repelled by the negative electrode potential, diffuse back into the bulk solution, thus regenerating the chelating agent for reuse in the soil washing loop.

This cyclic process enables continuous recirculation of the EDTA solution, dramatically reducing chemical consumption compared to single-pass washing methods. Experimental data show that the same EDTA solution can be reused for at least 25 treatment cycles with only a 20% decline in extraction capacity, primarily due to minor losses from soil retention rather than degradation. The filtration efficiency—the percentage of heavy metals removed from the washing effluent by the AACE filter—exceeds 90% under optimized flow rates (e.g., 0.1 mL/min for mixed contamination), significantly outperforming DC-based electrochemical filtration, which achieves only 30–70% efficiency under identical conditions and produces non-metallic precipitates due to hydroxide formation from water splitting.

The quality of the recovered metals is another critical advantage of the AACE method. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals micrometer-sized, crystalline metal particles deposited on the Ami-PC electrode, whereas DC methods produce amorphous, non-conductive shells that include co-precipitated calcium hydroxide. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirms that the AACE-recovered copper exists predominantly in the metallic state (Cu⁰), with characteristic peaks at 932.8 and 952.6 eV and minimal satellite features, while DC-recovered copper shows strong satellite peaks indicative of Cu(II) hydroxide. Similarly, lead and cadmium recovered via AACE are primarily zero-valent, whereas DC methods yield hydroxides. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) further validates the crystalline nature of the AACE-deposited metals, with lattice spacings matching those of pure Cu, Pb, and Cd.

Long-term operational stability is ensured through a simple regeneration protocol. After approximately 14 cycles, pore blockage by accumulated metal particles reduces filtration efficiency to around 90%. At this point, the electrode is treated with a mild acid eluent (e.g., 0.1 M HCl) while applying a low reverse DC bias (+1 V), which dissolves the deposited metals and restores the electrode’s original morphology and performance, as confirmed by SEM imaging showing complete removal of metal particles without structural damage.

The environmental and agricultural safety of the treated soil is demonstrated through plant assays using pea (Pisum sativum) sprouts. Soil initially contaminated with 100 ppm Cd is reduced to 0.25 ppm after AACE treatment, and the resulting pea sprouts contain less than 0.1 ppm Cd in all tissues—well below international food safety standards. Moreover, plant growth metrics (height, leaf size, biomass) are indistinguishable from those of plants grown in uncontaminated control soil, confirming the absence of phytotoxicity and nutrient deficiency. In stark contrast, soil treated with fresh EDTA washing exhibits stunted growth due to excessive nutrient leaching.

The invention is not limited to the specific parameters described herein. Variations in chelating agents (e.g., citric acid, NTA), electrode materials (e.g., other functionalized carbons, conductive polymers), voltage waveforms (amplitude, duration, frequency), and soil types are contemplated within the scope of the invention. The system is scalable from laboratory to field applications and may be adapted for continuous industrial wastewater treatment, where heavy metal recovery from process streams offers both environmental and economic benefits.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a robust, efficient, and sustainable solution for heavy metal soil remediation that overcomes the fundamental limitations of prior art by integrating selective electrochemical reduction with closed-loop reagent recycling, thereby enabling safe, cost-effective, and resource-positive environmental restoration.
