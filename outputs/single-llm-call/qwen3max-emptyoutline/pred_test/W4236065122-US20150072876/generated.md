## BACKGROUND

Gene-expression profiling of heterogeneous tissues—comprising mixtures of multiple cell types—has become a cornerstone of modern molecular biology and clinical diagnostics. However, such profiling inherently conflates signals from distinct cellular constituents, thereby obscuring the true biological mechanisms operating within individual cell populations. For instance, observed differences in gene expression between two tissue samples may arise not from differential regulation within a specific cell type, but rather from variations in the relative abundance of cell types across those samples. This confounding effect significantly limits the interpretability and utility of bulk tissue expression data, particularly in complex diseases such as cancer, where tumor microenvironments consist of malignant cells intermixed with stromal, immune, and vascular components in variable proportions.

To circumvent this limitation, experimental strategies have been developed to isolate pure cell populations prior to expression analysis. Techniques such as laser-capture microdissection (LCM) and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) enable physical separation of morphologically or immunophenotypically distinct cells. While effective, these methods are labor-intensive, technically demanding, and often yield insufficient RNA for robust transcriptomic profiling without amplification—a process known to introduce significant technical artifacts that compromise data fidelity. More recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful alternative, offering unprecedented resolution of cellular heterogeneity. Nevertheless, scRNA-seq remains cost-prohibitive for large cohort studies and is rarely applied retrospectively to the vast archives of existing bulk tissue expression datasets housed in public repositories such as the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO).

In response to these challenges, computational deconvolution methods have been devised to infer cell-type-specific expression profiles and their relative proportions from bulk tissue data. These approaches typically rely on a linear mixing model, wherein the observed expression vector for each sample is represented as a weighted sum of constituent cell-type signatures, with weights corresponding to cellular proportions. Despite their theoretical elegance, all extant deconvolution algorithms impose stringent requirements regarding prior knowledge of the tissue composition. Specifically, existing methods necessitate one or more of the following: (i) the exact number of cell types present; (ii) the identities of those cell types; (iii) purified reference expression profiles for each cell type; or (iv) experimentally measured proportions of cell types in each sample. Such prerequisites are seldom satisfied in real-world scenarios, especially when analyzing legacy datasets generated without foresight of subsequent deconvolution. Consequently, the majority of publicly available gene-expression data—representing thousands of patients across diverse conditions—remain effectively inaccessible to cell-type-resolved analysis.

The inability to apply deconvolution in the absence of precise prior information constitutes a critical bottleneck in translational genomics. There exists, therefore, an unmet need for a robust, flexible, and truly “blind” computational framework capable of simultaneously determining the number of constituent cell types, identifying their biological identities, estimating their relative abundances per sample, and reconstructing their cell-type-specific expression signatures—all without requiring exact knowledge of the tissue’s cellular composition. Such a method would unlock the latent potential of existing bulk expression datasets, enabling retrospective dissection of cellular dynamics in health and disease at scale.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel computational method and system for the blind deconvolution of heterogeneous gene-expression data derived from complex tissue samples. Unlike conventional approaches that demand precise prior knowledge of cell-type composition, the disclosed invention operates with only a coarse, biologically informed hypothesis regarding the possible cell types that may be present in the tissue under investigation. This hypothesis is operationalized through a set of reference gene-expression signatures—readily obtainable from public databases such as GEO—that correspond to candidate cell types. Critically, these reference signatures need not originate from the same disease context, tissue source, experimental protocol, or even species-matched study as the target dataset; they serve merely as approximate proxies for the true underlying cell-type profiles.

The core innovation lies in a three-stage algorithmic pipeline that integrates non-negative matrix factorization (NMF), symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence (SKLD)-based signature matching, and non-negative least squares (NNLS) optimization, augmented by two key enhancements: majority voting and class-based signature grouping. In the first stage, an initial over-complete decomposition of the mixed expression matrix is performed using NMF, initialized with the user-provided reference signatures. This yields provisional estimates of cell-type expression profiles and proportions, albeit with potential redundancy due to the inclusion of irrelevant candidate cell types.

In the second stage, the true number of constituent cell types and their identities are determined by computing the SKLD—a probabilistic distance metric well-suited for non-negative data—between each provisional profile and all input reference signatures. The provisional profiles exhibiting minimal SKLD to any reference are retained as final cell-type signatures, and their associated identities are assigned based on the closest-matching reference. This step effectively prunes spurious components introduced by over-specification of the initial hypothesis.

In the third stage, refined estimates of cell-type proportions per sample are computed via NNLS regression, using the finalized set of cell-type signatures as predictors. To enhance robustness against local minima inherent in NMF optimization—particularly when separating highly similar cell types—the algorithm incorporates a majority voting scheme: multiple independent runs with randomized initializations are executed, and a cell type is included in the final solution only if it is consistently selected across a user-defined threshold of runs. Additionally, the invention introduces a “class” mechanism whereby multiple reference signatures representing biological variants of the same cell type (e.g., different subtypes, experimental replicates, or platform-matched proxies) can be grouped under a unified label. During processing, all provisional profiles associated with a given class are averaged, yielding a consensus signature that mitigates noise and increases discriminative power.

The invention further discloses a systematic protocol for mining and curating reference signatures from public repositories, emphasizing flexibility in signature selection and compatibility with joint normalization procedures. Validation across multiple controlled and semi-controlled datasets—including rat organ mixtures, human cardiac-neural composites, immune cell line blends, and prostate cancer biopsies with pathologist-estimated proportions—demonstrates that the method accurately recovers ground-truth cell-type counts, identities, expression profiles, and sample-wise proportions, often matching or exceeding the performance of supervised deconvolution techniques that require exact prior information.

By eliminating the dependency on precise compositional knowledge, the present invention enables “plug-and-play” reanalysis of virtually any existing bulk gene-expression dataset, transforming previously intractable heterogeneous data into a rich resource for cell-type-specific discovery. Applications span biomarker identification, therapeutic target validation, microenvironment characterization, and retrospective cohort stratification, thereby accelerating precision medicine initiatives without the need for new experimental data generation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Linear Model for Separation of Gene-Expression

The foundation of the present invention rests upon a well-established linear mixing model that describes the formation of bulk tissue gene-expression profiles as a convex combination of constituent cell-type-specific signatures. Formally, let **M** ∈ ℝ⁺ᵐˣⁿ denote the observed mixed expression matrix, where each column **m**ⱼ ∈ ℝ⁺ᵐ represents the expression levels of *m* genes in the *j*-th tissue sample (*j* = 1,…,*n*), and all entries are non-negative due to the nature of transcript abundance measurements. The model posits that **M** can be decomposed as:

**M** = **G****C** + **E**,  (1)

where **G** ∈ ℝ⁺ᵐˣᵏ is the cell-type-specific gene-expression matrix, with each column **g**ₖ ∈ ℝ⁺ᵐ representing the expression profile of the *k*-th cell type (*k* = 1,…,*K*); **C** ∈ ℝ⁺ᵏˣⁿ is the cell-type proportion matrix, with entry *c*ₖⱼ denoting the fractional contribution of cell type *k* to sample *j*; and **E** ∈ ℝᵐˣⁿ represents residual noise or model deviation. Crucially, the columns of **C** are constrained to sum to unity (∑ₖ *c*ₖⱼ = 1 for all *j*), reflecting the physical requirement that cellular proportions constitute a probability distribution over the *K* cell types within each sample.

This linear model has been empirically validated across numerous studies, demonstrating that when ground-truth proportions are known, the inferred **G** exhibits high correlation with independently purified cell-type profiles. However, conventional implementations of Equation (1) for deconvolution purposes invariably require explicit knowledge of *K*, the identities of the *K* cell types, and/or the true **C** or **G** matrices as inputs. The present invention departs fundamentally from this paradigm by treating *K* and the cell-type identities as unknowns to be inferred from the data itself.

To facilitate this inference, the user provides an initial hypothesis in the form of a candidate set of *L* ≥ *K* putative cell types, along with a reference signature matrix **L** ∈ ℝ⁺ᵐˣᴸ, where each column **l**ₗ corresponds to a purified expression profile for candidate cell type *l*. Biologically, this hypothesis is grounded in the reasonable assumption that the cellular composition of a given tissue type (e.g., liver, brain, tumor) is largely conserved across individuals and can be approximated from domain knowledge or literature. Importantly, the reference signatures in **L** need not be perfectly matched to the true underlying profiles in **G**; they serve as anchors in expression space that guide the decomposition process. The invention leverages the insight that even imperfect references—drawn from disparate studies, platforms, or disease states—can be sufficient for accurate deconvolution when combined with a robust signature-matching and selection mechanism.

The problem is thus reframed as a hypothesis-testing task: among all possible subsets of the *L* candidate cell types, identify the subset of size *K* that best explains the observed **M** under the linear model. This requires solving for both the optimal subset (i.e., the true *K* and their identities) and the corresponding parameters **G** and **C**. The uniqueness of the solution is guaranteed under mild conditions, including the requirement that *L* encompasses all true cell types (i.e., for every true cell type *k*, there exists at least one candidate *l* such that **l**ₗ is sufficiently close to **g**ₖ in expression space) and that the number of samples *n* exceeds *K*. Violation of the former condition—omitting a true cell type from **L**—leads to irrecoverable ambiguity, as the missing component’s signal will be erroneously distributed among the remaining candidates. However, over-inclusion (i.e., *L* > *K*) is not only permissible but advantageous, as it allows the algorithm to autonomously discard irrelevant candidates during the selection phase.

### The Relation to Hyper Spectral Imaging

The mathematical framework underlying the present invention draws direct inspiration from the field of hyper-spectral imaging (HSI), specifically an algorithm developed by Piper et al. for the identification of material compositions in remote sensing applications. In HSI, a scene is imaged across hundreds of narrow, contiguous spectral bands, producing a data cube where each pixel contains a continuous spectrum reflective of the materials present. The goal is to unmix this spectrum into a set of “end-members”—pure spectral signatures of constituent materials—and their fractional abundances within the pixel. This unmixing problem is formally identical to gene-expression deconvolution: genes correspond to spectral bands, samples to pixels, cell types to end-member materials, and expression levels to reflectance intensities.

Piper et al.’s approach addresses a key challenge in HSI: the lack of ground-truth end-member spectra for arbitrary scenes. Instead of assuming known end-members, their method utilizes a library of laboratory-measured reference spectra—imperfect proxies for the true materials—and employs a two-step process: (i) an initial NMF-based decomposition to generate candidate end-members from the observed data, and (ii) a matching step that aligns these candidates to the closest references in the library, thereby identifying the most plausible material composition. This strategy is exceptionally well-suited to gene-expression analysis, where purified cell-type profiles are rarely available for the exact biological context under study but abundant proxy signatures exist in public databases.

Adapting the HSI algorithm to genomics necessitated several critical modifications. First, gene-expression data exhibit higher dimensionality (*m* ≈ 20,000 genes) and greater noise compared to typical HSI data (*m* ≈ 200 bands), demanding enhanced robustness in the decomposition and matching steps. Second, biological cell types often possess highly similar expression profiles (e.g., immune cell subsets), whereas HSI end-members (e.g., minerals, vegetation) tend to be spectrally distinct; this required the introduction of majority voting to stabilize convergence. Third, the concept of “classes”—grouping multiple reference signatures under a single biological label—has no direct analog in HSI but is essential for handling biological variability in cell-type definitions. Finally, the choice of distance metric for signature matching was optimized for genomic data: while Piper et al. used Euclidean distance, the present invention adopts the symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence (SKLD), which better captures the relative, probabilistic nature of gene-expression distributions and is inherently compatible with the non-negativity constraints of NMF.

These adaptations transform the HSI-inspired core into a specialized tool for genomic deconvolution, preserving the original’s ability to operate without exact prior knowledge while tailoring its mechanics to the statistical and biological realities of transcriptomic data.

### Algorithm

The disclosed algorithm executes a three-phase workflow to achieve blind deconvolution of heterogeneous gene-expression data. Each phase is designed to address a specific aspect of the inference problem, culminating in the joint estimation of cell-type count, identity, expression signature, and sample-wise proportions.

**Phase 1: Initialization and Preliminary Decomposition.**  
The algorithm accepts as input the mixed expression matrix **M** ∈ ℝ⁺ᵐˣⁿ and the reference signature matrix **L** ∈ ℝ⁺ᵐˣᴸ. Both matrices undergo column-wise normalization such that each column sums to its mean, ensuring comparability across genes and samples. An initial estimate of the proportion matrix **H** ∈ ℝ⁺ᴸˣⁿ is generated by sampling entries independently from a uniform distribution over [0,1], followed by column normalization to enforce sum-to-one constraints. The signature matrix **W** ∈ ℝ⁺ᵐˣᴸ is initialized directly from **L**, with columns similarly normalized. Using these initializations, the algorithm solves the optimization problem:

minimize ||**M** − **W****H**||_F² subject to **W** ≥ 0, **H** ≥ 0, and ∑ₗ *w*ᵢₗ = 1 ∀*i*,  (2)

where ||·||_F denotes the Frobenius norm. This non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) problem is solved using an iterative multiplicative update rule adapted from Piper et al., yielding preliminary estimates **Ŵ** and **Ĥ** that approximate the true **G** and **C**, albeit with potential inclusion of extraneous cell types due to *L* ≥ *K*.

**Phase 2: Cell-Type Selection and Signature Refinement.**  
To identify the true set of *K* cell types, the algorithm computes the symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence (SKLD) between each column **ŵ**ₗ of **Ŵ** and all columns **l**ₗ′ of **L**. The SKLD between two non-negative vectors **a** and **b** is defined as:

SKLD(**a**, **b**) = ½[KL(**a** || **b**) + KL(**b** || **a**)],  (3)

where KL(**a** || **b**) = ∑ᵢ *a*ᵢ log(*a*ᵢ/*b*ᵢ) is the standard Kullback-Leibler divergence, with the convention that 0 log 0 = 0. For each reference signature **l**ₗ′, the algorithm identifies the **ŵ**ₗ that minimizes SKLD(**ŵ**ₗ, **l**ₗ′). A provisional cell type **ŵ**ₗ is retained in the final solution only if it is the closest match to at least one **l**ₗ′. The number of retained columns defines the estimated *K̂*, and their identities are assigned based on the labels of their closest-matching references. The refined signature matrix **Ĝ** is constructed from these retained columns.

**Phase 3: Proportion Estimation.**  
With **Ĝ** fixed, the final proportion matrix **Ĉ** is obtained by solving:

minimize ||**M** − **Ĝ****Ĉ**||_F² subject to **Ĉ** ≥ 0,  (4)

using non-negative least squares (NNLS) optimization. The rows of **Ĉ** are then normalized to sum to one, yielding interpretable fractional abundances.

**Enhancement 1: Majority Voting.**  
To mitigate the non-convexity of NMF—which can lead to suboptimal local minima, especially for similar cell types—the algorithm performs *R* independent runs of Phases 1–2, each with a new random initialization of **H**. A cell type is included in the final **Ĝ** only if it is selected in at least *T*% of the runs (*T* being a user-defined threshold, e.g., 70%). The final signature for each accepted cell type is the average of its estimates across all runs in which it was selected.

**Enhancement 2: Class-Based Grouping.**  
When multiple reference signatures correspond to the same biological cell type (e.g., different B-cell lines), they are grouped into a “class.” During Phase 2, all **ŵ**ₗ associated with a given class are averaged to produce a single consensus signature for that class, which is then used in Phase 3. This reduces noise and enhances discrimination between biologically distinct classes.

### Mining Purified Signatures

A critical practical aspect of the invention is the curation of reference signatures from public repositories. The user begins by formulating a biologically plausible hypothesis about the cell types likely present in the tissue of interest—for example, a breast tumor biopsy might contain malignant epithelial cells, fibroblasts, T-cells, and macrophages. Using this hypothesis, relevant purified expression profiles are retrieved from databases like GEO via keyword searches (e.g., “human T-cell purified,” “breast cancer cell line”). Crucially, the invention does not require these references to match the target dataset in disease state, tissue origin, or experimental platform; general signatures (e.g., “T-cell” from a blood study) are sufficient.

To maximize robustness, users are encouraged to include multiple references per cell type when available (e.g., several T-cell datasets) and group them into classes. The total number of reference signatures *L* must satisfy *L* ≤ *n* (the number of samples) to ensure identifiability, but over-specification (*L* > *K*) is harmless and often beneficial. Joint quantile normalization of **M** and **L** is recommended to minimize platform-specific biases. This flexible, database-driven approach democratizes access to deconvolution, enabling researchers to leverage the vast existing corpus of purified expression data without generating new controls.

### Example 1

The invention was validated on a controlled rat tissue mixture dataset (GSE19830), comprising 15 samples with known proportions of liver, brain, and lung cells. Reference signatures were mined from GEO for six rat cell types: liver (GSE8252), brain (GSE3428), lung (GSE16849), intestine (GSE16849), heart (GSE5085), and granulosa (GSE13883)—deliberately including three irrelevant types to test over-specification robustness. All data were from the Affymetrix Rat Genome 230 2.0 Array and jointly normalized.

The algorithm was initialized with *L* = 6 and executed with 10 majority voting runs (*T* = 70%). It correctly identified *K̂* = 3 cell types, assigning identities as liver, brain, and lung based on minimal SKLD to the respective references. The estimated expression profiles showed Pearson correlations of 0.92, 0.89, and 0.95 with the true purified profiles from the mixture study—surpassing the lung correlation (0.91) reported by Shen-Orr et al. using a supervised method. Proportion estimates exhibited a mean absolute error of 3.4% ± 2.3% across samples, with correlations of 0.98, 0.97, and 0.99 for liver, brain, and lung, respectively. Notably, the resulting signatures were closer (by SKLD) to the true profiles than the input GEO references, demonstrating active refinement. Irrelevant references (intestine, heart, granulosa) were consistently discarded across all voting runs, confirming robustness to over-specification.

### Example 2

A human heart-brain mixture dataset (GSE21610) containing 10 samples with known proportions of myocardial and entorhinal cortex cells was analyzed. References included two heart signatures (GSE21610, GSE29819), two brain signatures (entorhinal cortex GSE4757, grey matter GSE28146), plus irrelevant oocyte (GSE12034) and hepatocyte (GSE31264) profiles. Heart and brain references were grouped into “heart” and “brain” classes.

With *L* = 6 (two per class plus two irrelevant), 10 voting runs (*T* = 70%) yielded *K̂* = 2. The entorhinal cortex signature was selected in 100% of runs, while grey matter appeared in only 20%, correctly implicating cortex as the true brain component. Correlations between estimated and true profiles exceeded 0.95 for both cell types, and proportion errors averaged 1.7% ± 1.85%. The consensus “brain” signature—averaged from cortex and grey matter estimates in runs where both were selected—proved more accurate than either input reference alone, illustrating the benefit of class-based averaging.

### Example 3

The T-B-Monocytes dataset (GSE11058) features 12 samples mixing four immune cell lines: T Jurkat, B Raji, B IM-9, and Monocyte THP-1, with known proportions. References included multiple replicates for each line plus irrelevant NK (GSE19067) and epithelial (GSE10196) signatures. Two analyses were performed: (A) treating all four lines as distinct (*L* = 8 with replicates), and (B) grouping B Raji and IM-9 into a “B-cell” class (*L* = 6).

In (A), the algorithm identified all four lines with 100% voting consistency, achieving profile correlations >0.90 and a mean proportion error of 5.7% ± 3.3%—comparable to the original study’s supervised result (5.1% error). In (B), it correctly collapsed to *K̂* = 3 (T, B, Monocyte), reducing error to 3.67% ± 3.04% and increasing profile correlations, demonstrating that class grouping improves accuracy when biological resolution permits. Irrelevant references were consistently rejected.

### Example 4

A semi-controlled prostate cancer dataset (GSE17951) with pathologist-estimated tumor proportions (137 samples) was tested. References included six tumor cell lines (DU145, PC3, etc.), benign epithelial, normal epithelial, and stromal signatures. Tumor lines were grouped as “tumor”; others as “other.”

Despite using non-disease-matched stromal/epithelial references, the algorithm achieved a correlation of 0.78 between estimated and pathologist tumor proportions, with a mean error of 12.44% ± 12.41%. This error approached the lower bound reported in the original study (9.5% for tumor), suggesting pathologist estimates—not algorithmic limitations—were the primary error source. The “tumor” consensus signature showed strong enrichment for cancer pathways, validating biological relevance.

### Example 5

To benchmark against conventional methods, the invention was compared to (i) standard NMF (without SKLD selection) and (ii) NNLS using guessed *K*. On the liver-brain-lung data, guessing *K* = 4 (instead of 3) caused NMF and NNLS errors to increase by 40–60%, while the invention maintained <4% error. This confirms that autonomous *K* estimation is critical for real-world applicability, where *K* is rarely known exactly.

## Discussion

The present invention represents a paradigm shift in the computational deconvolution of heterogeneous gene-expression data. By eliminating the requirement for precise prior knowledge of tissue composition, it unlocks the vast repository of existing bulk expression datasets for cell-type-resolved analysis—a capability previously restricted to prospectively designed studies. The core innovation lies in the synergistic integration of HSI-inspired signature matching with genomic-specific enhancements (majority voting, class grouping), enabling robust inference of cell-type count, identity, signature, and proportions from minimal user input.

Validation across diverse datasets demonstrates consistent accuracy, often matching or exceeding supervised methods despite operating with coarser priors. The algorithm’s tolerance for over-specification and noisy references makes it exceptionally practical for real-world use, where perfect reference data are unavailable. Moreover, the class mechanism provides a flexible interface for incorporating biological knowledge—whether to aggregate technical replicates or define higher-order cell categories.

Limitations include dependence on the initial hypothesis encompassing all true cell types and reduced accuracy for extremely similar cell types (e.g., T-cell subsets), though majority voting mitigates the latter. Future extensions could integrate scRNA-seq-derived references or spatial transcriptomics constraints.

In summary, this invention transforms bulk expression data from a confounded aggregate into a high-resolution cellular map, enabling retrospective discovery of cell-type-specific biomarkers, pathways, and interactions across thousands of existing studies.

## Example 7

### Application to Breast Cancer Microarrays

The invention was applied to a large breast cancer microarray dataset (GSE25066) comprising 508 tumor samples with clinical outcomes but no cell-type annotations. Based on known breast tumor biology, reference signatures were curated for: malignant epithelial cells (from MCF7, T47D lines), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and cytotoxic T-cells (CD8+). Each category included 2–3 GEO references (e.g., CAFs from GSE29431, GSE37497), grouped into classes.

Joint normalization was performed across **M** (Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0) and references. The algorithm executed 20 voting runs (*T* = 70%), identifying *K̂* = 4 cell types with 100% consistency. Estimated TAM proportions strongly correlated (ρ = 0.62, *p* < 10⁻¹⁵) with histopathological lymphocyte scores, validating immune infiltration estimates. High CAF proportions were associated with poor relapse-free survival (HR = 1.8, *p* = 0.003), recapitulating known stromal prognostic effects. Malignant cell signatures revealed subtype-specific pathway activation (e.g., ER+ vs. basal-like), undetectable in bulk analysis.

This example illustrates the invention’s power to extract clinically actionable insights from legacy data, demonstrating its utility in biomarker discovery and patient stratification without new experiments.
