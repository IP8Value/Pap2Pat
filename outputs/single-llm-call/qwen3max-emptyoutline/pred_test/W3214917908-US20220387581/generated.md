# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) represents a significant and persistent threat to global swine production, causing severe enteric disease characterized by acute watery diarrhea, vomiting, dehydration, and high mortality—particularly in neonatal piglets less than seven days of age. First identified in Europe during the early 1970s, PEDV re-emerged with devastating impact in Asia around 2010 and was introduced into the United States in 2013, rapidly spreading across North America and resulting in substantial economic losses estimated in the hundreds of millions of dollars annually. The causative agent, PEDV, is a positive-sense, single-stranded, enveloped RNA virus belonging to the family Coronaviridae, genus Alphacoronavirus, with a genome approximately 28 kilobases in length. The virus primarily targets mature enterocytes lining the villi of the small intestine, leading to villous atrophy, malabsorption, and life-threatening secretory diarrhea. Due to the immaturity of the neonatal immune system and the absence of passive immunity in naïve herds, newborn piglets exhibit extreme susceptibility, with mortality rates approaching 100% in outbreaks involving virulent strains.

Current control strategies rely heavily on biosecurity measures and vaccination; however, commercially available vaccines in North America—including those conditionally licensed from Harrisvaccines and Zoetis—have demonstrated only marginal efficacy in field conditions. These products are typically based on either inactivated whole virus or RNA platforms administered parenterally (e.g., intramuscularly), which predominantly stimulate systemic immunity but fail to induce robust mucosal immune responses at the primary site of viral entry: the intestinal epithelium. Given that PEDV initiates infection at the mucosal surface of the gut, effective protection ideally requires the induction of both systemic and mucosal immunity, particularly secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) in the lactogenic compartment, which can be transferred to nursing piglets via colostrum and milk. This passive transfer of lactogenic immunity is widely recognized as the principal mechanism by which maternal vaccination confers protection to suckling offspring against enteric pathogens such as PEDV.

The spike (S) glycoprotein of PEDV has been established as the dominant target for neutralizing antibodies due to its critical role in viral attachment and fusion with host cell receptors. Structurally, the S protein is cleaved into two functional subunits: S1, which mediates receptor binding, and S2, which facilitates membrane fusion. Within the S1 domain lies a conserved region known as the core neutralizing epitope (COE), spanning amino acids 499–638, which harbors multiple conformational and linear epitopes capable of eliciting potent virus-neutralizing antibodies. Consequently, numerous vaccine candidates have focused on recombinant expression of the S1 subunit, the COE fragment, or full-length S protein in various heterologous systems, including bacterial, yeast, insect, and mammalian cells. However, these approaches often suffer from low yields, improper post-translational modifications (notably glycosylation), instability, and high production costs—barriers that impede large-scale deployment in livestock settings.

Alternative strategies have explored oral delivery of PEDV antigens using live vectors such as probiotic bacteria (e.g., Lactobacillus spp.) engineered to express S or nucleocapsid (N) proteins. While some studies report induction of mucosal and systemic immune responses following oral administration, concerns regarding genetic stability, regulatory approval, scalability, and consistent antigen dosing remain unresolved. Plant-based molecular farming has emerged as a promising platform for producing recombinant veterinary vaccines, offering advantages such as eukaryotic protein processing, cost-effective biomass production, ambient-temperature stability, and suitability for oral delivery without purification. Among plant hosts, maize (Zea mays) has proven particularly advantageous due to its high biomass yield, well-established agronomic practices, and natural bioencapsulation of recombinant proteins within seed storage organelles, which shields antigens from degradation in the acidic and enzymatic environment of the gastrointestinal tract.

Previous work has demonstrated successful expression of the PEDV S1 protein in transgenic maize, achieving high accumulation levels targeted specifically to the embryo (germ) fraction, thereby enabling concentration through standard grain milling techniques. This maize-produced S1 antigen exhibited remarkable thermostability, retaining immunoreactivity after prolonged storage at ambient temperatures—a critical feature for distribution in resource-limited agricultural regions. In a pilot study, orally administered maize-S1 fed to weaned pigs (27 days old) induced serum neutralizing antibodies following PEDV challenge, though protection could not be assessed in neonates due to the age-dependent severity of disease. Building upon this foundation, the present invention addresses a critical unmet need: the development of a practical, low-cost, orally deliverable maternal vaccine that elicits lactogenic immunity capable of protecting highly vulnerable nursing piglets against lethal PEDV challenge.

Notably, prior attempts to express PEDV antigens in plants have largely focused on injectable formulations requiring downstream purification—defeating the purpose of a low-cost oral platform. Only one report describes expression of the COE domain in maize, but the antigen was purified and injected into mice, thus failing to leverage the inherent advantages of oral delivery or evaluate protection in a relevant swine model. Furthermore, while maize-based oral vaccines have shown success against other porcine coronaviruses—such as transmissible gastroenteritis virus (TGEV), where feeding transgenic corn expressing TGEV S protein to sows resulted in elevated neutralizing antibodies in milk and protection of piglets—no such demonstration existed for PEDV until the work underlying this invention. Crucially, the inventors discovered an unexpected dose-response relationship: a low oral dose of maize-expressed S1 conferred superior protection compared to a higher dose, suggesting complex immunomodulatory effects potentially involving oral tolerance or cytokine regulation. Additionally, the identification of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) in sow milk as a novel negative correlate of protection—where lower levels were associated with higher piglet survival—provides new mechanistic insights into PEDV pathogenesis and vaccine efficacy.

Thus, despite decades of research, there remains a pressing need for a PEDV vaccine that is (1) orally administrable to eliminate labor-intensive injections, (2) stable under ambient storage conditions to reduce cold-chain dependency, (3) producible at scale using existing agricultural infrastructure, (4) capable of inducing robust lactogenic immunity in sows, and (5) demonstrably efficacious in protecting neonatal piglets—the population most at risk. The present invention fulfills these criteria through a maize-based oral vaccine platform delivering the PEDV S1 antigen, validated in a controlled challenge study showing significant improvement in piglet survival when sows are vaccinated during gestation. This approach represents a paradigm shift from conventional parenteral vaccines toward a practical, sustainable, and economically viable solution for global swine health.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel oral vaccine composition for the prevention of porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) infection in swine, comprising transgenic maize grain expressing a recombinant spike (S) protein subunit of PEDV, specifically the S1 domain, wherein said maize grain is administered orally to pregnant sows to induce lactogenic immunity that confers passive protection to nursing piglets against lethal PEDV challenge. The invention further encompasses methods of producing said vaccine, methods of vaccinating sows, and methods of protecting neonatal piglets through maternal immunization. Critically, the recombinant S1 antigen is expressed in the embryo (germ) fraction of maize kernels under the control of a seed-specific promoter, enabling high-level accumulation and natural bioencapsulation within the grain matrix, which preserves antigen integrity during passage through the gastrointestinal tract and facilitates uptake by gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT). The vaccine is formulated as whole or milled maize grain containing a defined concentration of S1 antigen, eliminating the need for purification, adjuvants, or cold-chain storage, thereby offering a low-cost, scalable, and logistically simple intervention suitable for widespread use in commercial swine operations.

In a preferred embodiment, the maize grain contains between 5 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg of recombinant S1 protein, with an optimal dose identified as approximately 10 mg of S1 antigen per kilogram of feed. Administration occurs during gestation in three discrete vaccination windows—corresponding to days 57, 85, and 110 of pregnancy—with each window consisting of a three-day feeding period during which sows receive 1 kg of transgenic maize per day. This regimen was empirically determined to maximize the induction of neutralizing antibodies in sow milk while minimizing potential immunosuppressive effects observed at higher antigen doses. The invention is distinguished from prior art by its demonstration that oral delivery of unpurified, plant-produced S1 antigen directly in feed elicits protective lactogenic immunity, as evidenced by significantly improved survival rates (53%) in piglets born to vaccinated sows compared to non-vaccinated controls (10%) following experimental PEDV challenge.

Moreover, the invention identifies novel immunological correlates of protection: specifically, the presence of neutralizing antibodies (NABs) in sow milk and reduced concentrations of granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) in milk are statistically correlated with increased piglet survivability. These biomarkers provide valuable tools for evaluating vaccine efficacy without requiring live-virus challenge studies and offer new insights into the immunopathogenesis of PEDV. The downregulation of GM-CSF—a pro-inflammatory cytokine implicated in excessive immune activation in other coronavirus infections such as SARS-CoV-2—suggests that the S1 antigen may exert immunomodulatory effects beyond mere antibody induction, potentially mitigating harmful inflammatory responses that contribute to disease severity in neonates.

The maize-based platform leverages established agricultural practices for propagation, harvest, and storage, with the recombinant protein remaining stable for years at ambient temperatures, thus enabling stockpiling and rapid deployment during outbreaks. Because maize is a standard component of swine feed, the vaccine integrates seamlessly into existing feeding protocols without requiring specialized handling or equipment. This contrasts sharply with injectable vaccines, which demand skilled labor, sterile conditions, individual animal restraint, and refrigerated transport—all of which increase costs and limit accessibility, particularly in developing regions. The invention therefore represents a transformative advance in veterinary vaccinology, combining scientific innovation with practical feasibility to address a disease of major economic and animal welfare significance.

## DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to an oral vaccine for porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) comprising transgenic Zea mays (maize) grain that expresses a recombinant spike (S) protein subunit of PEDV, specifically the S1 domain, and methods of using said vaccine to protect nursing piglets through maternal immunization. The recombinant S1 protein is encoded by a nucleic acid sequence derived from a virulent strain of PEDV, such as the USA/NC/49469/2013 isolate, and is operably linked to a seed-specific promoter to ensure targeted expression in the embryo (germ) of the maize kernel. This subcellular targeting strategy enhances antigen accumulation and facilitates concentration of the S1 protein through standard dry-milling processes that separate the germ fraction from the endosperm and pericarp. The resulting transgenic maize grain serves as both the production vehicle and the delivery matrix for the vaccine, obviating the need for protein purification, formulation with adjuvants, or maintenance of a cold chain.

The maize plants used in the invention are generated through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation or biolistic delivery of a plant expression cassette containing the PEDV S1 coding sequence fused in-frame to a signal peptide for endoplasmic reticulum (ER) retention, such as the KDEL or HDEL tetrapeptide motif, which promotes proper folding and stability of the glycoprotein within the ER lumen. The expression cassette further includes a strong constitutive or seed-specific promoter—examples of which include the maize 27 kDa gamma-zein promoter, the globulin-1 promoter, or the legumin B4 promoter—to drive high-level transcription exclusively during seed development. A selectable marker gene, such as phosphinothricin acetyltransferase (bar) or neomycin phosphotransferase II (nptII), is included for the identification and propagation of transgenic events. Following transformation, T0 plants are regenerated and self-pollinated to produce homozygous T2 or T3 lines exhibiting stable, high-level S1 expression, with representative lines such as PDC18 demonstrating accumulation of S1 protein at levels exceeding 0.1% of total soluble protein in the germ fraction.

For vaccine preparation, transgenic maize kernels are harvested at physiological maturity, dried to a moisture content below 12%, and subjected to mechanical fractionation using industrial milling equipment. In a typical process, whole kernels are first passed through a pin mill (e.g., CIM-8-MIS, Munson) to break the pericarp, followed by roller milling (e.g., DP650-14, Roskamp) to separate the germ from the endosperm. The germ-enriched fraction is then isolated using a vibratory separator (e.g., LS18S55, SWECO) and ground to a fine meal using a hammer mill (e.g., Glen Mill) such that more than 80% of particles pass through a 20-mesh sieve (850 µm). The resulting corn meal is packaged in sealed, labeled containers and stored at ambient temperature, where the S1 antigen remains immunologically intact for extended periods due to the protective lipid and protein matrix of the maize germ.

The vaccine is administered orally to pregnant sows during gestation according to a defined protocol designed to coincide with key stages of fetal immune development and mammary gland differentiation. Specifically, sows receive the transgenic maize during three vaccination windows: days 57 ± 1, 85 ± 1, and 110 ± 1 of gestation, with each window comprising three consecutive days of feeding. On each vaccination day, sows are fasted for four hours prior to administration to enhance gastric emptying and intestinal exposure, then fed a measured quantity of transgenic corn meal mixed into their standard ration. In the low-dose regimen, sows consume 1 kg of corn meal per day, delivering approximately 10 mg of S1 antigen daily based on quantitative Western blot analysis calibrated against a purified recombinant COE standard. A high-dose regimen delivers 1.5 kg/day (50 mg S1/kg), though empirical data indicate that the low dose provides superior protection, likely due to avoidance of oral tolerance mechanisms that may be triggered by excessive antigen exposure.

Upon ingestion, the maize grain matrix protects the S1 antigen from denaturation in the stomach, allowing it to reach the small intestine where it is released through enzymatic and microbial digestion of the plant cell wall. The particulate nature of the maize germ facilitates uptake by M cells overlying Peyer’s patches in the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), initiating a mucosal immune response characterized by the activation of dendritic cells, T helper cells, and B cells. This leads to the class-switching of B cells to IgA production and their homing to the mammary gland via the common mucosal immune system, resulting in the secretion of PEDV-specific sIgA and neutralizing IgG into colostrum and milk. Following farrowing, nursing piglets ingest these antibodies, which neutralize PEDV in the intestinal lumen and prevent infection of enterocytes, thereby reducing viral replication, villous atrophy, and clinical disease.

The efficacy of the vaccine was rigorously evaluated in a controlled challenge study conducted under biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) containment. Sixteen PEDV-naïve Large White × Yorkshire sows were randomly assigned to four treatment groups: (1) intramuscular injection with a commercial killed-virus vaccine (INJ), (2) non-vaccinated controls (CON), (3) low-dose oral vaccine (LOV), and (4) high-dose oral vaccine (HOV). All sows were confirmed PEDV-negative by RT-PCR of fecal samples and serum neutralization assay prior to enrollment. Vaccination protocols were implemented as described, and litters were challenged orally with 10 mL of PEDV suspension (10⁴ TCID₅₀/mL) between days 3–5 of lactation. Piglets were monitored daily for 11 days post-challenge for clinical signs including diarrhea, dehydration, and lethargy, with a composite disease severity index calculated per litter. Survival was assessed on day 11, and biological samples (serum, colostrum, milk) were collected for immunological analysis.

Results demonstrated that piglets from LOV sows exhibited significantly higher survival rates (53%) compared to CON (10%) and HOV (7%) groups, with the INJ group showing intermediate protection (37%). Neutralizing antibody titers were elevated in serum and milk of LOV sows relative to controls, while colostral titers were highest in the INJ group—consistent with the differential trafficking of IgG (systemic) versus IgA (mucosal) isotypes. Notably, cytokine profiling revealed a significant reduction in GM-CSF levels in both serum and milk of LOV and INJ sows compared to CON, and statistical modeling identified milk GM-CSF concentration and milk NAB presence as independent predictors of piglet survival. Specifically, each standard deviation increase in milk GM-CSF was associated with a marked decrease in survival odds, whereas detectable milk NABs multiplied survival odds by up to 65-fold. These findings underscore the dual role of the vaccine in enhancing protective humoral immunity while suppressing detrimental inflammatory pathways.

The invention thus provides a complete, integrated solution for PEDV control: a genetically defined maize line producing a stabilized S1 antigen, a scalable production and processing method, a validated oral dosing regimen, and immunological correlates that enable rational vaccine optimization. By harnessing the natural properties of maize as a bioreactor and delivery vehicle, the invention overcomes the limitations of conventional vaccine platforms and offers a sustainable, farmer-friendly tool for safeguarding swine health worldwide.

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the practice of the invention but are not intended to limit its scope. All procedures involving animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC #VRI-S-21-1381) and conducted in accordance with USDA and NIH guidelines.

**Example 1: Generation and Characterization of Transgenic Maize Expressing PEDV S1 Protein**

A synthetic gene encoding the S1 subunit (amino acids 1–735) of the PEDV USA/NC/49469/2013 strain was codon-optimized for maize expression and fused at the C-terminus to the KDEL ER retention signal. This construct was cloned downstream of the maize 27 kDa gamma-zein promoter and upstream of the nopalin synthase terminator in a binary vector containing the bar selectable marker. Immature maize embryos (Hi-II genotype) were transformed via Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain AGL1 carrying the expression cassette. Regenerated T0 plants were screened by PCR for transgene integration and grown to maturity in a greenhouse. T1 progeny were analyzed by Western blot using a monoclonal antibody against the PEDV COE domain (Medgene Labs, SD6-29). One event, designated PDC18, showed consistent S1 expression at ~0.15% of total soluble protein in the germ fraction. Homozygous T3 seeds from PDC18 were propagated in a contained field plot, and grain was harvested at 25% moisture, dried to <12% moisture, and stored at 25°C for six months prior to use. Western blot confirmed antigen stability throughout storage.

**Example 2: Vaccine Preparation and Dosing**

Transgenic PDC18 kernels and non-transgenic control kernels were processed as follows: kernels were milled using a CIM-8-MIS pin mill (Munson) to fracture the pericarp, then passed through a DP650-14 roller mill (Roskamp) set to separate germ from endosperm. The germ-rich fraction was isolated on an LS18S55 vibratory separator (SWECO) and ground in a Glen Mill hammer mill to a particle size where >80% passed a 20-mesh screen. S1 concentration was quantified by densitometry of Western blots comparing corn meal extracts to a purified recombinant COE standard curve, yielding values of 10.2 ± 1.3 mg S1/kg for the low-dose batch and 51.7 ± 3.8 mg/kg for the high-dose batch. Corn meal was packaged in 1 kg vacuum-sealed bags and stored at room temperature.

Sixteen PEDV-naïve sows (Large White × Yorkshire, parity 2–4) were housed in HEPA-filtered isolation rooms with 12:12 light/dark cycles. After confirmation of PEDV negativity by fecal RT-PCR (Idexx PEDV/PDCoV Multiplex RNA Mix) and serum neutralization assay, sows were artificially inseminated and randomly assigned to four groups (n=4): INJ (intramuscular Zoetis vaccine on days 57, 85, 110 of gestation), CON (non-vaccinated), LOV (1 kg transgenic corn/day on days 57–59, 85–87, 110–112), HOV (1.5 kg transgenic corn/day on same days). On vaccination days, sows were fasted 4 h pre-feeding, received corn at 08:00, and resumed normal diet after 1 h. Sows were moved to farrowing crates on day 110. Average litter sizes were 10.75 (INJ), 10.25 (CON), 12.75 (LOV), and 10.5 (HOV).

**Example 3: Challenge Study and Immunological Analysis**

Between days 3–5 of lactation, each piglet received 10 mL of PEDV suspension (ISU batch #PEDV USA/NC/49469/2013, 10⁴ TCID₅₀/mL) via intragastric tube. Clinical scores for diarrhea (0–2), dehydration (0–2), and general health (0–3) were recorded daily for 11 days. Disease severity index = (sum of scores)/litter size. On day 11, survival rate = (live piglets/challenged piglets) × 100. Colostrum was collected within 6 h of farrowing; serum and milk were collected on day 1 of lactation and day 6 post-challenge, respectively.

Neutralizing antibody titers were determined at South Dakota State University Diagnostics Lab using a focus reduction neutralization test (FRNT). Vero-76 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated with serial 1:2 dilutions of serum/milk/colostrum plus 100 FFU of PEDV. After overnight incubation with trypsin-supplemented MEM, cells were fixed in 80% acetone and stained with FITC-conjugated anti-PEDV mAb SD6-29. Titers were reported as the highest dilution achieving ≥90% focus inhibition.

Cytokine levels (GM-CSF, IFN-γ, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-1ra, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IL-18, TNF-α) were measured in duplicate by multiplex ELISA (Eve Technologies). Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD correction (p≤0.05). Piglet survival was modeled using logistic regression with sow as random effect and piglet weight at challenge as covariate.

Results showed LOV piglets had 53% survival vs. 10% in CON (p<0.001). Milk NAB titers were significantly higher in LOV vs. CON and HOV (p<0.05). GM-CSF was reduced in LOV and INJ serum and milk vs. CON (p≤0.05). Correlation analysis revealed milk NAB presence (OR=3.71–65.89) and low milk GM-CSF (OR=0.05–0.72 per SD increase) were significant predictors of survival (p≤0.05). These data confirm that oral delivery of maize-produced S1 at a low dose induces protective lactogenic immunity and modulates inflammatory responses to improve neonatal outcomes.

## Example 2

In a follow-up study designed to optimize the vaccination schedule and assess cross-protection against heterologous PEDV strains, twenty-four PEDV-naïve sows were enrolled and divided into six treatment groups (n=4 per group): (1) LOV-3D (three 3-day feedings as in Example 1), (2) LOV-1D (single 1-day feeding on day 85), (3) LOV-5D (five consecutive days on days 83–87), (4) LOV + adjuvant (co-administration of 10 mg S1/kg with 1 mg/kg CpG oligodeoxynucleotide), (5) non-transgenic maize control, and (6) commercial injectable vaccine (positive control). All sows were challenged with a contemporary PEDV variant (USA/IA/2020, GenBank MN985325) at 10⁴.⁵ TCID₅₀/mL on day 4 of lactation. Piglet survival was monitored for 14 days.

Preliminary results indicated that the LOV-3D regimen maintained superior efficacy (58% survival), while LOV-1D provided minimal protection (15%), confirming the necessity of repeated antigen exposure. The LOV-5D group showed reduced survival (22%), reinforcing the existence of an optimal dosing window beyond which immune regulation may occur. Surprisingly, addition of CpG adjuvant did not enhance protection (45% survival) and was associated with transient fever in sows, suggesting that the maize matrix itself provides sufficient immunostimulation. Cross-neutralization assays using milk from LOV-3D sows showed 80% inhibition of the heterologous strain, indicating broad reactivity likely mediated by conserved epitopes within the S1 domain. These findings support the robustness of the original protocol and its applicability against evolving PEDV field isolates.

## Example 3

To evaluate long-term stability and field deployability, transgenic maize grain (PDC18 line) was stored under varied environmental conditions for up to 24 months. Samples were held at 4°C, 25°C, and 37°C with 60% relative humidity, and S1 antigen integrity was assessed quarterly by Western blot and ELISA. After 24 months, antigen recovery remained above 90% at 4°C and 25°C, and 75% at 37°C, demonstrating exceptional thermostability. In a simulated field trial, 500 kg of transgenic grain was shipped without refrigeration from Iowa to a commercial swine farm in North Carolina during summer months (ambient temperatures 28–35°C). Upon arrival, S1 concentration was verified, and the grain was incorporated into gestating sow rations at 1 kg/head/day during the standard three-window protocol. Litters were naturally exposed to endemic PEDV circulating on the farm. Over a six-month observation period, farms using the maize vaccine reported a 62% reduction in pre-weaning mortality compared to historical controls, with no adverse effects on sow productivity or feed intake. Serological monitoring confirmed sustained milk NAB titers throughout lactation, validating the real-world effectiveness and logistical advantages of the invention.
