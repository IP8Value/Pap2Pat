# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of molecular biology and genetic engineering, specifically in the design, construction, and application of engineered transcription factors for the targeted regulation of gene expression in eukaryotic cells. More particularly, the invention relates to synthetic transcription activator-like (TAL) effector proteins that have been reprogrammed to recognize specific DNA sequences in human genomic DNA and to modulate transcription of endogenous or exogenous target genes. The invention further encompasses a novel, modular, and highly efficient cloning methodology—termed “Golden TAL Technology”—for the rapid assembly of custom TAL effector proteins with user-defined DNA-binding specificities. This technology enables the precise control of gene expression in a wide range of human cell types, thereby providing a powerful platform for research, therapeutic development, and biotechnological applications. The engineered TAL proteins described herein are derived from bacterial TAL effectors originally secreted by plant-pathogenic Xanthomonas species but have been functionally adapted for use in human cells through strategic modifications, including the replacement of the native C-terminal activation domain with heterologous transcriptional activation domains such as that of the herpes simplex virus (HSV) VP16 protein. The invention thus bridges the gap between prokaryotic virulence factors and eukaryotic gene regulation systems, demonstrating that bacterial DNA-binding proteins can be repurposed as programmable tools for human genome manipulation without requiring host-specific co-factors. The technical scope of the invention includes not only the functional TAL-based transcriptional activators themselves but also the methods for their construction, the vectors encoding them, and their use in activating specific endogenous human genes—including but not limited to PUMA, IFNα1, and IFNβ1—thereby enabling controlled modulation of cellular processes such as apoptosis and innate immune responses.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Transcription activator-like (TAL) effectors are a class of virulence proteins secreted by phytopathogenic bacteria of the genus Xanthomonas during infection of susceptible plant hosts. These proteins are translocated into the nucleus of plant cells via the type III secretion system, where they bind to specific promoter sequences of host genes and activate transcription, thereby subverting plant defense mechanisms or promoting nutrient acquisition for the pathogen. The hallmark of TAL effectors is a central domain composed of tandem repeats, typically ranging from 1.5 to 33.5 units, each repeat being approximately 34 amino acids in length. Within this repeat array, the DNA-binding specificity is determined by two hypervariable amino acid residues at positions 12 and 13 of each repeat, known as the repeat-variable diresidue (RVD). Each RVD recognizes a single nucleotide in the target DNA sequence in a modular and largely context-independent manner, with well-established codes such as HD for cytosine, NI for adenine, NG for thymine, and NN for guanine or adenine. The linear order of RVDs in the TAL protein directly corresponds to the sequence of the target DNA, which is preceded by a conserved 5′ thymine residue. This simple and predictable code has made TAL effectors highly attractive as platforms for engineering custom DNA-binding proteins.

Prior to the discovery of TAL effectors’ programmable DNA recognition, zinc finger (ZF) proteins represented the primary technology for designing sequence-specific DNA-binding domains. ZF arrays, however, suffer from significant limitations, including context-dependent binding affinities, difficulty in predicting optimal finger combinations, and laborious assembly procedures. In contrast, the modular architecture of TAL effectors allows for straightforward design: by arranging repeats with defined RVDs in a desired order, one can generate a protein that binds any user-specified DNA sequence of sufficient length (typically ≥10.5 repeats for functional activity). This feature has spurred intense interest in adapting TAL effectors for applications beyond plant pathology, particularly in mammalian systems.

Initial efforts to harness TAL effectors for genome engineering focused on creating TAL nucleases (TALENs)—fusion proteins combining the TAL DNA-binding domain with the catalytic domain of the FokI endonuclease—to induce site-specific double-strand breaks and thereby facilitate gene editing. However, the potential of TAL effectors as transcriptional regulators remained underexplored until recently. Early studies demonstrated that native TAL effectors such as AvrBs3 and Hax3 could activate reporter genes in plant cells when their cognate binding sites were placed upstream of minimal promoters. These proteins contain a C-terminal region that functions as a transcriptional activation domain (AD), exhibiting structural and functional similarities to acidic ADs found in eukaryotic transcription factors. Deletion of this domain abolishes most, but not all, transcriptional activity, suggesting that while the AD is essential for robust activation, the DNA-binding event itself may contribute minimally to transcriptional output.

Despite these advances, several challenges impeded the broader adoption of TAL effectors in non-plant systems. First, the high sequence similarity among TAL repeats renders conventional cloning strategies—such as PCR amplification and restriction enzyme-based ligation—prone to recombination, misassembly, and errors. Second, it was unclear whether TAL effectors, which evolved to function in plant nuclei, could operate effectively in human cells, given potential differences in chromatin structure, transcription machinery, and nuclear import mechanisms. Third, even if functional, the native TAL AD might be suboptimal in human cells, limiting transcriptional output.

Recent reports provided preliminary evidence that artificial TAL proteins could activate human genes when fused to strong heterologous ADs like VP16. However, these studies relied on PCR-based assembly methods that introduced codon variability between repeats to reduce homology, potentially affecting protein expression or stability. Moreover, systematic comparisons of native versus engineered TAL proteins across multiple human cell lines, and assessments of their ability to regulate endogenous genes—not just reporters—were lacking. There was also no standardized, scalable, and error-resistant platform for rapidly constructing TAL proteins with arbitrary repeat compositions.

Thus, a significant need existed for a robust, modular, and versatile system to engineer TAL-based transcriptional activators tailored for human applications, coupled with empirical validation of their functionality in diverse cellular contexts. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by introducing a novel cloning strategy—Golden TAL Technology—that enables the seamless, PCR-free, and high-fidelity assembly of TAL proteins with designed repeat architectures, and by demonstrating that both native and engineered TAL effectors can function as potent and specific transcriptional activators in human cells, including the ability to upregulate clinically relevant endogenous genes such as those involved in apoptosis and antiviral immunity.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides engineered transcription activator-like (TAL) effector proteins capable of specifically binding to predetermined DNA sequences in human cells and modulating the transcription of associated genes. The invention further discloses a modular and highly efficient cloning methodology—designated “Golden TAL Technology”—for the rapid, accurate, and flexible assembly of custom TAL effector proteins with user-defined DNA-binding specificities. In one aspect, the invention demonstrates that native TAL effectors derived from Xanthomonas bacteria, such as AvrBs3 and Hax3, retain transcriptional activation activity in human cells despite their evolutionary origin in plant pathogens, thereby establishing the cross-kingdom functionality of these proteins. In another aspect, the invention shows that the transcriptional activity of TAL effectors in human cells can be significantly enhanced by replacing the native C-terminal activation domain with a heterologous activation domain, particularly the potent VP16 activation domain from herpes simplex virus. The resulting engineered TAL-VP16 fusion proteins exhibit strong, specific, and reproducible activation of both reporter genes and endogenous human genes, including PUMA (p53 upregulated modulator of apoptosis), IFNα1 (interferon alpha 1), and IFNβ1 (interferon beta 1), across multiple human cell lines such as HEK293T-Rex, HeLa, and Huh7. The Golden TAL Technology overcomes the limitations of prior cloning methods by utilizing a hierarchical, restriction enzyme-based assembly system that employs type IIs restriction enzymes (BpiI and BsaI) to enable scarless, directional, and simultaneous ligation of multiple DNA fragments in a single reaction. This approach eliminates the need for PCR amplification of repetitive sequences, thereby minimizing errors and ensuring high fidelity in the construction of TAL repeat arrays. The technology utilizes a library of standardized plasmid modules, each encoding a single TAL repeat with a specific RVD (e.g., NI, HD, NN, NG) and flanked by unique overhangs that dictate its position within the final array. Through a two-step assembly process, users can construct TAL proteins containing between 1 and 24 repeats in a matter of days, with the flexibility to incorporate various N-terminal tags (e.g., GFP), C-terminal domains (e.g., VP16, GAL4, or native TAL termini), and expression vectors. The invention thus provides a complete and scalable platform for the design, construction, and deployment of programmable transcriptional activators for research, diagnostic, and therapeutic applications in human biology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is based on the unexpected discovery that transcription activator-like (TAL) effector proteins, originally characterized as virulence factors in plant-pathogenic Xanthomonas bacteria, can function as sequence-specific transcriptional activators in human cells. This cross-kingdom functionality arises from the intrinsic modularity and programmability of the TAL DNA-binding domain, which operates independently of host-specific co-factors. The core of the invention comprises engineered TAL effector proteins that have been optimized for use in human systems through strategic modifications, particularly the substitution of the native C-terminal activation domain with a heterologous, potent activation domain such as VP16 from herpes simplex virus. These engineered proteins, referred to herein as HuTALs (human TAL effectors), are designed to bind specific promoter sequences in the human genome and drive transcription of target genes with high specificity and tunable strength.

The DNA-binding domain of a TAL effector consists of a variable number of tandem repeats, each approximately 34 amino acids long, with the exception of a truncated “half-repeat” at the C-terminal end of the array. The specificity of each repeat for a single nucleotide in the target DNA is dictated by two hypervariable amino acids at positions 12 and 13, known as the repeat-variable diresidue (RVD). The RVD code is well established: for example, HD recognizes cytosine, NI recognizes adenine, NG recognizes thymine, and NN recognizes guanine (with some cross-reactivity to adenine). The linear sequence of RVDs in the TAL protein directly corresponds to the nucleotide sequence of the target DNA, which must be preceded by a 5′ thymine residue. This one-to-one correspondence between RVD order and DNA sequence enables the rational design of TAL proteins to bind virtually any user-defined DNA sequence of sufficient length—typically at least 10.5 repeats for functional activity, though shorter arrays may retain partial function.

To exploit this programmability for human applications, the present invention provides a comprehensive toolkit for constructing custom TAL proteins. Central to this toolkit is the “Golden TAL Technology,” a modular cloning strategy inspired by Golden Gate assembly, which leverages the properties of type IIs restriction enzymes to enable high-fidelity, multi-fragment DNA assembly in a single reaction. Type IIs enzymes, such as BpiI and BsaI, cleave DNA outside of their recognition sites, allowing the creation of user-defined overhangs that are independent of the enzyme’s recognition sequence. In Golden TAL Technology, individual TAL repeat modules are cloned into plasmid vectors flanked by BpiI sites engineered to produce specific four-base overhangs that determine the positional identity of the repeat within the final array. For instance, a repeat intended to occupy the first position in the array will have overhangs compatible only with the vector backbone and the second-position repeat, and so on. This ensures directional and ordered assembly without the risk of misligation or recombination that plagues traditional cloning methods when dealing with highly repetitive sequences.

The system is organized into two hierarchical assembly steps. In the first step, one to six individual repeat modules are assembled into an intermediate “repeat assembly” vector using a BpiI-mediated cut-ligation reaction. The intermediate vector contains a lacZ cassette flanked by BpiI sites, enabling blue-white screening for successful recombinants. In the second step, the completed repeat assembly is combined with additional modules—including an N-terminal module (e.g., encoding a nuclear localization signal and/or a fluorescent tag such as GFP), a C-terminal module (e.g., encoding VP16, GAL4, or the native TAL C-terminus), and a destination expression vector—using a BsaI-mediated cut-ligation reaction. The BsaI sites are designed to produce overhangs that ensure correct orientation and seamless fusion of all components. The entire process is PCR-free, relying instead on pre-validated plasmid subclones of individual repeats, which eliminates PCR-induced mutations and maintains near-identical codon usage across repeats to avoid translational biases.

Using this platform, the inventors constructed a series of TAL proteins targeting the promoters of human genes of interest, including PUMA, IFNα1, and IFNβ1. These HuTALs were equipped with a C-terminal VP16 activation domain to maximize transcriptional output in human cells. Upon transfection into human cell lines such as HEK293T-Rex, HeLa, and Huh7, the HuTALs localized to the nucleus, confirming the functionality of their nuclear localization signals in the heterologous host. When tested on reporter constructs containing the cognate TAL binding sites upstream of a minimal cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter driving luciferase expression, the HuTALs induced luciferase activity 22- to 34-fold above background, demonstrating high specificity and potency. More importantly, when expressed in human cells, the HuTALs successfully upregulated endogenous mRNA levels of their target genes: PUMA by 1.5-fold, IFNα1 by 3.1-fold, and IFNβ1 by 3.5-fold. Although the induction of endogenous genes was less pronounced than that of reporter genes—likely due to chromatin compaction, epigenetic silencing, or competition with endogenous transcription factors—the results unequivocally establish the feasibility of using engineered TAL effectors to modulate native human gene expression.

Control experiments confirmed that transcriptional activation was strictly dependent on the match between the TAL RVD array and the target DNA sequence. Mismatched combinations showed no significant activation, and deletion of the activation domain (whether native or VP16) abolished most, but not all, activity—revealing a low level of residual transcriptional activation that may stem from the DNA-binding event itself or weak interactions with basal transcription machinery. Notably, the native TAL activation domain was functional in human cells, albeit at lower levels than VP16, indicating that the core mechanism of TAL-mediated transcription is conserved across eukaryotes. The superior performance of VP16 in human cells underscores the advantage of tailoring the activation domain to the host organism.

The invention thus provides a complete and versatile system for programmable gene regulation in human cells, comprising: (1) a set of standardized plasmid modules encoding individual TAL repeats with defined RVDs; (2) a suite of assembly vectors for constructing TAL proteins with 1 to 24 repeats; (3) expression vectors for delivering TAL proteins with customizable N- and C-terminal domains; and (4) validated protocols for transfection, localization, and functional assessment in multiple human cell lines. This system enables researchers to design and deploy TAL-based transcriptional activators for a wide range of applications, from basic studies of gene function to the development of gene therapies for diseases involving dysregulated gene expression.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the functionality of native TAL effectors and their derivatives was evaluated in plant cells using a β-glucuronidase (GUS) reporter assay. The TAL effectors AvrBs3 and Hax3, along with modified versions lacking their native activation domains (ΔAD) or fused to heterologous activation domains (VP16 or GAL4), were expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium-mediated transient transformation. Each TAL construct included an N-terminal green fluorescent protein (GFP) tag to confirm nuclear localization, which was verified by fluorescence microscopy. Reporter constructs were generated by inserting the cognate DNA binding sites for AvrBs3 (AvrBs3-box) or Hax3 (Hax3-box) upstream of a minimal promoter driving the uidA (GUS) gene. Co-infiltration of Agrobacterium strains carrying TAL expression constructs and matching reporter constructs resulted in strong GUS activity for the native AvrBs3 and Hax3 proteins, confirming their transcriptional activation capability in planta. Deletion of the activation domain reduced GUS activity to approximately 15% of the wild-type level, indicating that the native C-terminal domain is the primary driver of transcriptional activation. Replacement of the deleted domain with VP16 or GAL4 activation domains partially restored activity, but levels remained substantially lower than those of the native proteins, suggesting that the native TAL activation domain is optimally adapted for function in plant cells. These results established the baseline activity of TAL effectors in their native host and provided a comparative framework for assessing their performance in heterologous systems.

### Example 2

This example demonstrates the transcriptional activation capability of TAL effectors in human cells. Reporter constructs were created by placing the AvrBs3-box or Hax3-box upstream of a minimal human cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter driving a luciferase (luc) reporter gene. These constructs were co-transfected into HEK293T-Rex human embryonic kidney cells along with expression plasmids for native AvrBs3, Hax3, or their derivatives (ΔAD, VP16-fused, or GAL4-fused). All TAL proteins included an N-terminal GFP tag and localized to the nucleus, as confirmed by fluorescence microscopy and Western blotting. Luciferase assays revealed that native AvrBs3 and Hax3 specifically activated their cognate reporter constructs, albeit at modest levels. Deletion of the activation domain drastically reduced activity, while fusion to the VP16 activation domain resulted in a 2–3-fold increase in luciferase activity compared to the native proteins and a 50–80-fold increase over negative controls. In contrast, the GAL4 activation domain provided little to no enhancement. These findings indicate that TAL effectors can function in human cells and that their activity can be significantly potentiated by substituting the native activation domain with a human-optimized domain such as VP16. The experiment was replicated in Huh7 hepatocarcinoma and HeLa cervical cancer cells, yielding similar results and confirming the broad applicability of TAL-based transcriptional activators across diverse human cell types.

### Example 3

This example details the development and implementation of the Golden TAL Technology, a modular cloning strategy for the rapid assembly of custom TAL effector proteins. Individual TAL repeat modules, each encoding a specific RVD (NI, HD, NN, or NG) and flanked by BpiI restriction sites, were cloned into a pUC57-derived vector. The BpiI sites were engineered to produce unique four-base overhangs that dictate the positional order of repeats in the final array. A library of 24 single-repeat plasmids was constructed, covering all common RVDs. In the first assembly step, 1–6 repeat modules were combined into an intermediate assembly vector containing a lacZ cassette flanked by BpiI sites, enabling blue-white screening. In the second step, the repeat assembly was ligated with N-terminal (GFP-NLS), C-terminal (VP16), and backbone modules using BsaI-mediated cut-ligation. The entire process was performed in a single-tube reaction at 37°C for 1 hour, followed by enzyme inactivation. Using this system, synthetic TAL proteins with 12–20 repeats were constructed in under 48 hours with >90% accuracy, as verified by sequencing. The method eliminated PCR-induced errors and maintained consistent codon usage, ensuring high protein expression and stability. Golden TAL Technology thus provides a robust, scalable, and user-friendly platform for generating TAL proteins with arbitrary DNA-binding specificities.

### Example 4

In this example, synthetic TAL proteins (HuTALs) were designed to target the promoters of endogenous human genes—PUMA, IFNα1, and IFNβ1—and were equipped with a C-terminal VP16 activation domain. The target DNA sequences were identified within the proximal promoter regions of each gene, and corresponding RVD arrays were assembled using Golden TAL Technology. The resulting HuTAL expression plasmids were transfected into HEK293T-Rex cells, and nuclear localization was confirmed by GFP fluorescence. As a preliminary test, reporter constructs containing the HuTAL binding sites upstream of a minimal CMV promoter-luciferase cassette were co-transfected with HuTAL plasmids, resulting in 22- to 34-fold induction of luciferase activity, demonstrating high specificity and potency. Subsequently, HuTALs were expressed in the absence of reporter constructs to assess their ability to modulate endogenous gene expression. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis showed that PUMA mRNA levels increased by 1.5-fold, IFNα1 by 3.1-fold, and IFNβ1 by 3.5-fold relative to control transfections. Western blotting confirmed comparable expression levels of all HuTALs. These results validate the capacity of engineered TAL effectors to upregulate clinically relevant endogenous human genes, paving the way for therapeutic applications in areas such as cancer (via PUMA-mediated apoptosis) and antiviral immunity (via interferon induction).

### Example 5

This example investigates the influence of promoter context on TAL-mediated transcriptional activation. A reporter construct was generated by replacing the minimal CMV promoter in the luciferase vector with the native 675-bp promoter sequence of the IFNβ1 gene, including its natural regulatory elements. This construct was co-transfected into HEK293T-Rex cells with the IFNβ1-targeting HuTAL. Luciferase activity increased by 3.2-fold, a magnitude comparable to the 3.5-fold induction observed for the endogenous IFNβ1 gene. In contrast, the same HuTAL induced a 34-fold increase when tested on a minimal CMV promoter-reporter. This comparison demonstrates that the chromatin environment, presence of repressive elements, or competition with endogenous transcription factors in native promoters can significantly dampen TAL-mediated activation. The results explain why endogenous gene induction is typically weaker than reporter gene activation and highlight the importance of promoter architecture in determining the efficacy of TAL-based transcriptional regulators. Future optimization may involve targeting open chromatin regions, using stronger activation domains, or combining multiple TAL effectors to achieve synergistic effects.
