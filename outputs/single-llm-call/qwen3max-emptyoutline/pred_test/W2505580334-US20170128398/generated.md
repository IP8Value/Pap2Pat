## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Septic shock is a life-threatening condition characterized by systemic vasodilation, hypotension, and profound metabolic derangements, among which lactic acidemia (LA) is a hallmark clinical feature strongly associated with poor prognosis and increased mortality. The accumulation of lactate in the bloodstream during septic shock arises from a complex interplay between increased lactate production—primarily in skeletal muscle, gut, and immune cells under conditions of accelerated glycolysis—and impaired lactate clearance, particularly by the liver. While historically attributed to tissue hypoxia and anaerobic metabolism, emerging evidence suggests that LA in septic shock may also result from mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated cellular metabolism, and inflammatory-mediated suppression of hepatic gluconeogenesis, even in the presence of adequate oxygen delivery.

The liver plays a central role in lactate homeostasis through the Cori cycle, wherein lactate is taken up via monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs), converted to pyruvate, and subsequently metabolized either through oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondria or via gluconeogenesis to produce glucose. Hepatic mitochondrial function is therefore critical to maintaining systemic lactate balance. In septic shock, however, mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetic capacity are frequently compromised due to oxidative stress, calcium overload, endotoxin exposure, and inflammatory cytokine signaling. This mitochondrial dysfunction may manifest as reduced oxygen consumption rates (OCR), impaired electron transport chain (ETC) activity, and diminished ATP synthesis, thereby limiting the liver’s ability to metabolize lactate efficiently.

Despite extensive research, the precise contribution of hepatic mitochondrial dysfunction to the pathogenesis of LA in septic shock remains controversial. Some studies report significant impairments in mitochondrial respiration, while others find preserved or only mildly altered function, likely due to differences in experimental models, timing of assessment, and methodologies used to evaluate mitochondrial performance. Traditional approaches to measuring mitochondrial function—such as polarographic oxygen electrode assays or isolated enzyme activity tests—often fail to capture the dynamic, integrated behavior of the entire respiratory chain under physiologically relevant conditions. Moreover, these methods typically assess only coupled respiration (State III) or the respiratory control ratio, potentially overlooking more subtle or context-specific deficits in maximal respiratory capacity.

The advent of the Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer has revolutionized the study of mitochondrial physiology by enabling real-time, high-resolution measurements of OCR in intact, functional mitochondria under multiple metabolic states. This technology allows sequential probing of basal respiration, ADP-stimulated (coupled) respiration, oligomycin-inhibited (leak) respiration, FCCP-uncoupled (maximal) respiration, and rotenone/antimycin A-inhibited (non-mitochondrial) respiration within a single assay. Crucially, the assessment of maximal uncoupled respiration provides a sensitive indicator of the electron transport chain’s reserve capacity—a parameter particularly relevant in septic shock, where energy demands are elevated and mitochondrial resilience is paramount.

Current clinical management of septic shock relies heavily on vasopressor support, with norepinephrine (NE) serving as the first-line agent to restore mean arterial pressure (MAP) and perfusion. While NE effectively reverses hypotension, its impact on mitochondrial function and lactate metabolism remains incompletely understood. Some preclinical studies suggest that NE may improve hepatic oxygen delivery and thereby mitigate ischemic mitochondrial damage, while others report no benefit or even potential harm due to excessive vasoconstriction or direct cellular toxicity. Importantly, even when NE restores hemodynamic stability, it often fails to resolve LA, raising questions about the sufficiency of blood pressure correction alone in addressing the underlying metabolic pathology.

In this context, there is a pressing need for novel therapeutic agents that not only correct hemodynamic instability but also directly target the metabolic and mitochondrial abnormalities driving LA in septic shock. Phenolic compounds—naturally occurring or synthetic molecules containing one or more hydroxyl groups attached to an aromatic ring—have garnered interest due to their potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and redox-modulating properties. Among these, gentisic acid sodium salt (GSS; 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid sodium salt), a known metabolite of aspirin, exhibits unique pharmacological characteristics that distinguish it from conventional vasopressors. Preliminary observations in canine models of sepsis indicate that GSS not only restores MAP but also reduces blood lactate concentrations, suggesting a dual hemodynamic and metabolic benefit.

However, the mechanisms by which GSS exerts these effects—particularly its influence on hepatic mitochondrial function—have not been systematically investigated. Furthermore, no prior art discloses the use of GSS or structurally related dihydroxybenzoic acid derivatives specifically for the treatment of lactic acidemia in septic shock through the restoration of mitochondrial respiratory capacity. The absence of such teachings, combined with the inconsistent efficacy of existing therapies, underscores the novelty and unmet medical need addressed by the present invention.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel therapeutic method for the treatment of lactic acidemia in septic shock by administering gentisic acid sodium salt (GSS) to a subject in need thereof. This method is based on the unexpected discovery that GSS, unlike conventional vasopressors such as norepinephrine, simultaneously restores systemic hemodynamics and improves hepatic mitochondrial function, leading to a significant reduction in blood lactate levels. The invention further encompasses the use of GSS to enhance mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates—particularly coupled and basal respiration—in the liver during septic shock, thereby correcting the metabolic basis of lactic acidemia.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a method of treating lactic acidemia in a subject suffering from septic shock, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of gentisic acid sodium salt (GSS), wherein said administration results in (i) an increase in mean arterial pressure (MAP) to baseline or near-baseline levels, (ii) a reduction in serum lactate concentration, and (iii) an improvement in hepatic mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR), including but not limited to enhanced basal respiration, coupled respiration, and spare respiratory capacity.

In another embodiment, the invention provides a method of improving hepatic mitochondrial function in a subject with septic shock, comprising administering GSS, wherein said administration increases mitochondrial OCR in response to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and restores maximal uncoupled respiration to levels comparable to those observed in non-septic controls. The improvement in mitochondrial function is independent of, or synergistic with, the hemodynamic effects of GSS and is not achieved by norepinephrine under comparable conditions.

In yet another embodiment, the invention includes a pharmaceutical composition comprising GSS in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, formulated for intravenous administration, wherein the composition is adapted for use in the treatment of cardiovascular collapse and lactic acidemia associated with septic shock. The composition may be co-administered with standard supportive care, including fluid resuscitation and antibiotics, but is distinguished by its ability to resolve lactic acidemia without exacerbating myocardial injury or tachycardia, as evidenced by lower heart rates and absence of troponin elevation compared to norepinephrine-treated subjects.

The invention further provides a method of differentiating the therapeutic efficacy of vasopressor agents in septic shock by evaluating their impact on hepatic mitochondrial OCR using the Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer. This analytical method enables the identification of agents like GSS that confer metabolic benefits beyond hemodynamic stabilization, thereby offering a superior clinical profile in the management of septic shock.

Critically, the invention demonstrates that the resolution of lactic acidemia in septic shock does not require the complete normalization of maximal uncoupled respiration, but rather depends on the enhancement of coupled respiration—the physiologically relevant state in which ATP synthesis is actively supported by substrate oxidation. This insight redefines the therapeutic target for mitochondrial rescue in sepsis and explains why GSS, which preferentially boosts ADP-stimulated OCR, is more effective than norepinephrine, which primarily restores maximal respiration without improving coupled efficiency.

Thus, the present invention represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of septic shock, moving beyond mere hemodynamic support to address the root metabolic dysfunction driving lactic acidemia. By leveraging the unique biochemical properties of GSS—a compound with established safety profiles and dual hemodynamic-metabolic actions—the invention offers a clinically translatable strategy to improve outcomes in critically ill patients.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to the use of gentisic acid sodium salt (GSS), chemically identified as sodium 2,5-dihydroxybenzoate, for the treatment of lactic acidemia in subjects suffering from septic shock. GSS is a white to off-white crystalline powder, freely soluble in water, with a molecular formula of C₇H₅NaO₄ and a molecular weight of 176.10 g/mol. It is a dihydroxybenzoic acid derivative bearing hydroxyl groups at the 2- and 5-positions of the benzoic acid ring, conferring potent antioxidant activity through free radical scavenging and metal chelation. Unlike conventional vasopressors that act primarily on adrenergic receptors, GSS exerts its effects through a combination of vascular tone modulation and direct mitochondrial protection, resulting in a unique therapeutic profile that addresses both the hemodynamic and metabolic dimensions of septic shock.

In preferred embodiments, GSS is administered intravenously as a sterile aqueous solution, typically diluted in 5% dextrose in water (D5W) or normal saline, although other pharmaceutically acceptable diluents may be used. The dosage regimen is tailored to the severity of shock and the patient’s hemodynamic status, but generally ranges from 0.1 to 2.0 mg/kg/min, with a preferred range of 0.4 to 0.7 mg/kg/min. In the exemplary canine model described herein, an average infusion rate of 0.55 ± 0.29 mg/kg/min was sufficient to restore mean arterial pressure (MAP) to baseline levels and significantly reduce serum lactate concentrations over a 5-hour post-treatment period. This dosing window is expected to translate to human equivalents using standard allometric scaling principles, yielding a human dose range of approximately 0.08 to 0.56 mg/kg/min, with a preferred human dose of 0.15 to 0.25 mg/kg/min.

The pharmaceutical composition of GSS is prepared under aseptic conditions to ensure sterility and pyrogen-free status, suitable for intravenous infusion in critically ill patients. The pH of the solution is adjusted to between 6.0 and 8.0 to maintain stability and minimize irritation at the infusion site. The composition may include stabilizers such as sodium metabisulfite or edetate disodium to prevent oxidation, although GSS’s inherent stability in aqueous solution often obviates the need for additional preservatives. The final product is packaged in single-use vials or infusion bags, labeled for use in septic shock with instructions for continuous intravenous infusion via a central or peripheral line.

Administration of GSS is initiated upon diagnosis of septic shock, particularly when lactic acidemia is present (serum lactate > 2 mmol/L). The infusion is titrated to achieve a target MAP of at least 65 mmHg, consistent with current Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines, although higher targets (e.g., 75–85 mmHg) may be employed in select patients with chronic hypertension. Unlike norepinephrine, which often induces reflex tachycardia and increases myocardial oxygen demand, GSS achieves hemodynamic stabilization with a more favorable cardiac profile, as evidenced by lower heart rates and absence of troponin elevation in treated subjects. This cardioprotective effect is particularly advantageous in patients with pre-existing coronary artery disease or myocardial dysfunction.

The therapeutic efficacy of GSS is not limited to hemodynamic support but extends to the correction of the underlying metabolic derangement in septic shock. Specifically, GSS enhances hepatic mitochondrial function by increasing oxygen consumption rates (OCR) under physiologically relevant conditions. In the coupled state—wherein ADP is available to drive ATP synthesis through Complex V—GSS-treated mitochondria exhibit significantly higher OCR compared to both untreated septic controls and norepinephrine-treated subjects. This indicates that GSS improves the efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation, allowing the liver to more effectively metabolize lactate through pyruvate oxidation and subsequent entry into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.

Moreover, GSS preserves or restores spare respiratory capacity—the difference between maximal uncoupled respiration and basal respiration—which reflects the mitochondria’s ability to respond to sudden increases in energy demand. In septic shock, this reserve is often depleted, leaving cells vulnerable to energetic failure. By maintaining spare capacity, GSS ensures metabolic flexibility and resilience, which is critical for organ recovery during prolonged critical illness.

The mechanism by which GSS exerts these mitochondrial effects is believed to involve its antioxidant properties. During septic shock, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulate due to inflammatory activation and electron transport chain leakage, leading to oxidative damage of mitochondrial proteins, lipids, and DNA. GSS, with its two ortho-positioned hydroxyl groups, effectively scavenges ROS such as superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, thereby protecting mitochondrial integrity. Additionally, GSS may modulate redox-sensitive signaling pathways, such as those involving nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2), which upregulates endogenous antioxidant defenses.

Importantly, the invention demonstrates that the structural features of GSS are critical to its efficacy. Not all phenolic compounds produce the same metabolic benefits; for instance, ethyl gallate—a trihydroxybenzoate derivative—failed to reduce lactic acidemia in the same model, despite similar antioxidant potential. This suggests that the number, position, and pKa of hydroxyl groups on the aromatic ring influence the compound’s ability to interact with mitochondrial or cellular targets involved in lactate metabolism. The 2,5-dihydroxy configuration of GSS appears optimal, possibly due to enhanced membrane permeability, specific protein interactions, or favorable pharmacokinetics.

In clinical practice, GSS may be used as a monotherapy or in combination with other agents. For example, it could be co-administered with low-dose norepinephrine to achieve synergistic hemodynamic support while minimizing adrenergic side effects. Alternatively, it may replace norepinephrine entirely in patients who develop refractory lactic acidemia or myocardial injury on conventional vasopressors. The invention thus provides a flexible therapeutic platform adaptable to individual patient needs.

### RESULTS

The efficacy of gentisic acid sodium salt (GSS) in treating lactic acidemia in septic shock was rigorously evaluated in a well-controlled canine model of bacteremic shock induced by continuous infusion of Pseudomonas aeruginosa. The study included five experimental groups: (1) septic control (untreated), (2) non-septic control, (3) norepinephrine (NE)-treated septic, (4) GSS-treated septic, and (5) GSS-treated non-septic. All animals were instrumented for continuous hemodynamic monitoring, and blood samples were collected at baseline, during septic shock (defined as MAP ≈ 60–65 mmHg), and at 3 and 5 hours post-treatment. At the conclusion of the protocol, hepatic mitochondria were isolated and analyzed using the Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer to assess oxygen consumption rates (OCR) under multiple metabolic states.

Hemodynamic data revealed that both NE and GSS effectively restored mean arterial pressure (MAP) to baseline levels (~100 mmHg) within minutes of initiation, whereas the septic control group remained hypotensive throughout the study. However, a key distinction emerged in heart rate (HR) responses: GSS-treated animals exhibited significantly lower HR at 3 and 5 hours post-treatment compared to NE-treated and septic control groups, indicating a more favorable cardiovascular profile without excessive sympathetic activation. Notably, troponin levels—an indicator of myocardial injury—rose significantly in the NE group at 5 hours but remained unchanged in the GSS group, further supporting the cardioprotective nature of GSS.

Serum lactate concentrations followed divergent trajectories across groups. In the septic control and NE groups, lactate levels progressively increased, reaching markedly elevated values by 5 hours. In stark contrast, the GSS-treated septic group showed no increase in lactate; in fact, levels trended downward, closely resembling those in the non-septic groups, which exhibited a natural decline over time. This finding demonstrates that GSS, but not NE, effectively prevents the development of lactic acidemia despite comparable hemodynamic stabilization.

Mitochondrial OCR analysis provided the mechanistic basis for these observations. In the septic control group, maximal uncoupled respiration (induced by FCCP) was significantly reduced, indicating impaired electron transport chain capacity. Basal respiration was also modestly decreased. NE treatment fully restored maximal respiration to non-septic levels, confirming its ability to reverse mitochondrial dysfunction when assessed under uncoupled conditions. However, coupled respiration (stimulated by ADP)—the physiologically relevant state for ATP production—remained unchanged from septic controls. In contrast, GSS treatment not only restored basal respiration but also produced a marked increase in coupled respiration, exceeding that of all other groups, including non-septic controls. This enhancement of ADP-stimulated OCR suggests that GSS improves the efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation, enabling the liver to more effectively clear lactate through mitochondrial oxidation.

Spare respiratory capacity—calculated as maximal minus basal OCR—was severely depleted in the septic control group but preserved in both NE and GSS groups. However, only GSS conferred the additional benefit of elevated coupled respiration, which directly supports energy-demanding processes such as gluconeogenesis and lactate uptake. Enzymatic assays of mitochondrial Complex IV (cytochrome c oxidase) showed a significant reduction in activity in the septic control group, with partial restoration in NE and GSS groups, though these differences did not reach statistical significance. This underscores the superior sensitivity of functional OCR measurements over isolated enzyme assays in capturing the integrated mitochondrial phenotype.

Collectively, these results establish that GSS uniquely combines hemodynamic support with metabolic correction in septic shock. While NE rescues maximal respiratory capacity, it fails to improve the coupled, ATP-producing respiration necessary for lactate clearance. GSS, through its antioxidant and mitochondrial-enhancing properties, achieves both, thereby resolving lactic acidemia—a critical determinant of survival in septic shock.

## METHODS

The following methods describe the experimental procedures employed to demonstrate the efficacy of gentisic acid sodium salt (GSS) in treating lactic acidemia in septic shock, as detailed in the preceding sections. These protocols adhere to rigorous scientific and ethical standards and are reproducible in both preclinical and clinical research settings.

### Animal Preparation

The study was conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH Publication No. 85-23, 1996) and was approved by the University Animal Care Committee (Protocol No. 13-046/1/2, AC 10865). Adult male and female dogs (n = 55), weighing 18 to 26 kg, were used in the experiments. Animals were fasted overnight but allowed free access to water prior to surgery. Anesthesia was induced with intravenous propofol and maintained with a continuous infusion of sufentanil citrate (0.05–0.3 μg/kg/min) and midazolam (5 μg/kg/min). Mechanical ventilation was instituted via an endotracheal tube, with 100% oxygen delivered to maintain arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO₂) at approximately 400 mmHg. Ventilator rate was adjusted throughout the experiment to mitigate the development of metabolic acidosis, a common feature of this sepsis model.

Under sterile conditions, vascular catheters were inserted for hemodynamic monitoring and fluid administration. A polyethylene catheter was placed in the right femoral artery to measure mean arterial pressure (MAP) and obtain arterial blood samples. A Swan-Ganz catheter (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA) was advanced through the right jugular vein into the pulmonary artery to measure pulmonary arterial pressure (Ppa), pulmonary wedge pressure (Pwp), right atrial pressure (Rap), and to determine cardiac output (CO) via thermodilution. A third catheter was placed in the left jugular vein for continuous infusion of bacteria, study drugs, and maintenance fluids. All pressure transducers were zeroed and referenced to the level of the left atrium. Hemodynamic data were recorded continuously using a chart recorder (Astro-Med, W. Warwick, RI).

Arterial and mixed venous blood samples were drawn at predetermined intervals for analysis of blood gases (PaO₂, PaCO₂, pH), lactate, hematocrit, white blood cell count, liver function tests, and cardiac troponin. Fluid balance was carefully managed by infusing normal saline to maintain Pwp at baseline levels (~10 mmHg), with adjustments made based on real-time hemodynamic feedback. At the conclusion of the protocol, animals were euthanized with an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (110 mg/kg), and liver tissue was harvested for mitochondrial isolation.

### Experimental Protocols

Five experimental groups were studied: (1) septic control (n = 13), (2) non-septic control (n = 16), (3) norepinephrine (NE)-treated septic (n = 10), (4) GSS-treated septic (n = 11), and (5) GSS-treated non-septic (n = 5). After a 1-hour stabilization period, baseline measurements of hemodynamics and blood parameters were obtained. In septic groups, a continuous infusion of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (ATCC 27853; ~10¹⁰ colony-forming units/hour in normal saline) was initiated. Septic shock was defined as the time point when MAP decreased to 60–65 mmHg, which typically occurred after 4–5 hours of bacterial infusion. In the NE and GSS septic groups, drug infusions were started immediately upon reaching this shock threshold and titrated to restore MAP to baseline values. NE was dissolved in 5% dextrose in water (D5W) and infused at rates ranging from 0.3 to 0.8 μg/kg/min (mean 0.51 ± 0.22 μg/kg/min). GSS was similarly dissolved in D5W and infused at 0.3 to 0.85 mg/kg/min (mean 0.55 ± 0.29 mg/kg/min). The septic control group received D5W vehicle alone. In non-septic groups, equivalent volumes of normal saline were infused instead of bacteria, and "sham shock" measurements were taken at 4.5 hours post-baseline to match the timing of septic shock in infected animals. Post-treatment assessments were conducted at 3 and 5 hours after the onset of shock or sham shock.

### Mitochondrial Preparation

Immediately after euthanasia, a wedge of liver tissue (~2–3 g) was excised and placed in ice-cold mitochondrial isolation buffer (MSHE: 70 mM sucrose, 210 mM mannitol, 5 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, pH 7.2, supplemented with 0.1% fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin). The tissue was minced and homogenized using a drill-driven glass/Teflon Dounce homogenizer. The homogenate was centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C to remove nuclei and debris. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 minutes to pellet mitochondria. This washing step was repeated twice, and the final mitochondrial pellet was resuspended in a small volume of MSHE + BSA. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay, and mitochondria were diluted to 0.4 mg/mL in cold mitochondrial assay solution (MAS: 70 mM sucrose, 220 mM mannitol, 10 mM KH₂PO₄, 5 mM MgCl₂, 2 mM HEPES, 1 mM EGTA, 0.1% BSA, pH 7.2). Twenty microliters of this suspension (~20 μg protein) was plated per well in a custom Seahorse 24-well culture plate, centrifuged at 2000 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C to adhere mitochondria, and overlaid with 450 μL of MAS. Mitochondrial adherence and morphology were confirmed microscopically before assay initiation.

### Mitochondria Measurement of Oxygen Consumption Rates (OCR)

Mitochondrial OCR was measured using the Seahorse XF24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, North Billerica, MA). The instrument uses a solid-state sensor cartridge to transiently seal a 7-μL microchamber above the mitochondrial layer, allowing real-time measurement of oxygen concentration changes over 2–5 minute intervals. Four sequential injections were performed via ports in the sensor cartridge to assess OCR under different metabolic states:

1. **Basal OCR**: Measured after addition of substrates pyruvate (10 mM) and malate (2 mM), which feed electrons into the TCA cycle via pyruvate dehydrogenase and malate dehydrogenase, respectively.
2. **Coupled OCR**: Assessed after injection of ADP (4 mM), which stimulates ATP synthase (Complex V) and increases electron flow through the ETC.
3. **Leak OCR**: Determined after addition of oligomycin (2.0 μM), an ATP synthase inhibitor, which halts proton re-entry through Complex V and reveals proton leak-dependent respiration.
4. **Maximal OCR**: Induced by carbonyl cyanide p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP; 4 μM), a protonophore that uncouples respiration from ATP synthesis, allowing maximal electron transport chain activity.
5. **Non-mitochondrial OCR**: Measured after inhibition of Complex I (rotenone, 2 μM) and Complex III (antimycin A, 2 μM), which shuts down mitochondrial respiration.

Two measurements were taken at each state, and the average was used for analysis. Spare respiratory capacity was calculated as maximal OCR minus basal OCR.

### To Determine Whether MCT1 Protein is Altered in Septic Shock

Although not explicitly performed in the cited study, the determination of monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) expression in hepatic tissue can be accomplished using standard molecular biology techniques. Liver samples collected at euthanasia are flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Total protein is extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein (30–50 μg) are separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane is blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody against MCT1 (e.g., rabbit polyclonal anti-MCT1, 1:1000 dilution). After washing, a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody is applied, and protein bands are visualized using chemiluminescence. Band intensity is quantified by densitometry and normalized to a loading control such as β-actin or GAPDH. This method would allow assessment of whether septic shock or GSS treatment alters MCT1 expression, which could influence hepatic lactate uptake.

### Gallic Acid Septic Group

Although the primary focus of the invention is on gentisic acid sodium salt (GSS), comparative studies with structurally related compounds such as gallic acid (3,4,5-trihydroxybenzoic acid) may be conducted to elucidate structure-activity relationships. In such a protocol, a "gallic acid septic group" would be included, wherein animals undergo the same septic shock induction and are treated with gallic acid (e.g., 0.5 mg/kg/min in D5W) instead of GSS. Hemodynamic, lactate, and mitochondrial OCR parameters would be measured identically. Prior evidence suggests that gallic acid, despite its antioxidant properties, may not replicate the metabolic benefits of GSS, likely due to differences in hydroxyl group positioning and pKa, which affect cellular uptake and mitochondrial targeting. Such comparisons reinforce the specificity of the 2,5-dihydroxy configuration for therapeutic efficacy in septic shock.

### Statistics

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Hemodynamic and laboratory variables across the five groups and four time points (baseline, shock, 3h post, 5h post) were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with split-plot design. When only one time point was compared (e.g., mitochondrial OCR), a one-way randomized ANOVA was used. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Student-Newman-Keuls (SNK) multiple comparison test. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using GB-Stat V8 software (Dynamic Microsystems, Inc., Silver Spring, MD). Sample size calculations were based on preliminary data to ensure adequate power (≥80%) to detect a 20% difference in lactate levels between groups with α = 0.05.
