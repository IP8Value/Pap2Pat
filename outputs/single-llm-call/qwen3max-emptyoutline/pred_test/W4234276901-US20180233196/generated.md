# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNOLOGICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of non-volatile memory systems, and more specifically to high-density resistive crossbar memory arrays based on memristive or other two-terminal resistive switching devices. The invention addresses critical challenges associated with parasitic current paths—commonly referred to as “sneak paths”—that arise in passive crossbar architectures lacking selection transistors or diodes at each memory cell. These sneak paths introduce significant readout ambiguity, increased power consumption, and reduced reliability in large-scale crossbar arrays. The invention provides a novel readout methodology that leverages the spatial correlation of sneak-path currents across rows and columns of the array, combined with adaptive thresholding techniques, to enable accurate, single-access, and power-efficient data retrieval. The disclosed system is particularly suited for emerging memory technologies such as Resistive Random-Access Memory (ReRAM), Conductive Bridge RAM (CBRAM), and Phase-Change Memory (PCM) when implemented in dense, gateless crossbar configurations intended for high-throughput computing applications, including in-memory computing, neuromorphic systems, and energy-constrained embedded platforms. The invention further encompasses architectural enhancements, including the strategic placement of predefined dummy bits and optimized biasing schemes, to minimize read latency, reduce energy per operation, and improve robustness against device variability and fabrication imperfections.

## BACKGROUND

Resistive crossbar memory arrays represent a promising architecture for next-generation high-density non-volatile storage due to their simple two-dimensional structure, scalability, and compatibility with back-end-of-line (BEOL) integration processes. In such arrays, memory cells—typically composed of memristive elements—are positioned at the intersections of orthogonal word lines and bit lines, forming a grid without individual access transistors. This gateless design enables exceptional area efficiency, approaching the theoretical minimum of 4F² per cell (where F is the feature size), which is significantly denser than conventional DRAM or Flash architectures. However, this structural simplicity introduces a fundamental challenge: during a read operation, current not only flows through the selected memory cell but also through alternative conductive paths formed by neighboring cells, collectively known as sneak paths. These parasitic currents distort the sensed signal, making it difficult to distinguish between the logical states (“0” or “1”) of the target cell, especially in large arrays where the cumulative effect of multiple parallel paths becomes dominant.

The magnitude of sneak-path currents is inherently data-dependent, as it relies on the resistance states of unselected cells along the same row and column as the target cell. Since stored data is typically random or pseudo-random, the effective resistance of these sneak paths varies unpredictably from one read operation to another. This results in broad, overlapping distributions of sensed current for the two logical states, rendering fixed-threshold readout schemes ineffective. Prior art has attempted to mitigate this issue through various approaches, including the use of nonlinear selector devices, multi-stage read algorithms, voltage biasing strategies, and reference cells. However, these methods often incur significant penalties in terms of read latency, power consumption, circuit complexity, or area overhead. For instance, multi-stage read techniques require multiple accesses to the same cell to iteratively estimate and cancel out sneak-path contributions, thereby increasing access time and energy per bit. Similarly, grounded-terminal or floating-terminal biasing schemes either fail to suppress sneak paths adequately or consume excessive power due to uncontrolled current flow.

A key observation underlying the present invention is that while sneak-path currents are random in absolute terms, they exhibit strong spatial correlation within a given row or column of the crossbar array. Specifically, the equivalent resistance representing the collective contribution of all unselected cells in a row (denoted as Rr) remains nearly constant across different column positions within that row, provided the array is sufficiently large and the device exhibits a high OFF/ON resistance ratio. An analogous property holds for column-wise sneak-path resistance (Rc). This correlation arises because changing the accessed column within a row alters only one cell in the parallel combination that forms Rr—the newly excluded cell is replaced by the previously excluded one—and for large arrays with high resistance ratios, this swap induces negligible change in the total parallel resistance. Consequently, once the sneak-path characteristics of a row (or column) are characterized—either through an initial calibration read or via a predefined reference element—the same correction can be applied to all subsequent reads within that row (or column).

Existing solutions have not fully exploited this correlation property in a manner that is both computationally efficient and hardware-friendly. Some approaches assume global uniformity of sneak paths, which is invalid in real-world scenarios with random data patterns. Others require complex analog or digital signal processing per read operation, negating the benefits of crossbar density. Furthermore, the impact of device variability—both in fabrication and operational endurance—has not been adequately addressed in the context of adaptive readout. Variability causes the ON and OFF resistance states to spread into distributions rather than discrete values, further exacerbating the overlap in sensed currents and reducing read margins. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a holistic readout framework that integrates sneak-path correlation modeling, adaptive thresholding, strategic use of dummy bits, and optimized biasing to achieve error-free, single-readout operation with minimal hardware and energy overhead. This framework is compatible with standard memory access patterns, such as block-wise data transfer dictated by cache line structures, and scales favorably with array size, making it suitable for practical deployment in gigabit-scale ReRAM systems.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for performing efficient and reliable readout operations in a resistive crossbar memory array that lacks individual selection devices at each memory cell. The core innovation lies in the recognition and exploitation of the spatial correlation of sneak-path currents within rows and columns of the array. By leveraging this correlation, the invention enables the definition of an adaptive threshold for distinguishing between logical “0” and “1” states, which is dynamically adjusted based on the sneak-path characteristics of the specific row or column being accessed. This adaptive thresholding eliminates the need for multiple read cycles per bit, thereby achieving single-access readout with high accuracy and low power consumption.

In one embodiment, the method begins by identifying an “initial bit” in each row (or column) during a block-wise read sequence. This initial bit is read using a multi-stage technique capable of simultaneously estimating both the resistance of the target cell and the effective sneak-path resistance of the row. The estimated sneak-path parameter is then used to compute an adaptive threshold that applies to all subsequent bits in the same row, allowing them to be read in a single access. In an alternative embodiment, the array is augmented with predefined “dummy bits” in each row, whose logical state and resistance are known a priori. A single read of the dummy bit suffices to characterize the row’s sneak-path behavior, enabling immediate application of the adaptive threshold to all other bits in the row without requiring a multi-stage read. The dummy bits may be implemented as static resistors or as pre-programmed memristive cells, offering flexibility in design and robustness against variability.

The invention further incorporates an optimized biasing scheme for the crossbar terminals, wherein unused word and bit lines are biased to an intermediate voltage (e.g., VDD/2) to enhance the nonlinear response of the memristive devices and suppress sneak-path currents. This “connected terminals” configuration not only improves the signal-to-noise ratio of the read operation but also reduces overall power consumption compared to conventional floating or grounded terminal approaches. Additionally, the readout circuitry is designed to be minimally invasive, utilizing either a shared analog sensing front-end with digital post-processing or a fully analog compensated comparator that directly compares the sensed current against a reference derived from the dummy bit.

The proposed system is highly resilient to device variability, as the adaptive nature of the threshold compensates for shifts in absolute resistance values, and the use of dummy bits with low-variability states (e.g., the OFF state) further mitigates estimation errors. Simulation results demonstrate that the invention achieves error-free readout in 256 kb arrays under realistic data patterns and device models, with a figure-of-merit—defined as the inverse of the product of energy per bit and read latency—that surpasses existing gateless techniques by a factor of 7 to 24. The average number of array accesses per bit converges to one for typical cache line sizes, confirming the practicality of the approach for real-world memory hierarchies. Overall, the invention provides a scalable, power-efficient, and hardware-friendly solution to the sneak-path problem, enabling the realization of high-density, high-performance resistive crossbar memories.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Sneak Paths Analysis

In a resistive crossbar memory array, the absence of individual selection devices at each memory cell leads to the formation of parasitic current paths, commonly referred to as sneak paths, during read operations. These paths arise because the selected memory cell is electrically connected in parallel with numerous unselected cells through the shared word and bit lines. When a read voltage is applied across the selected cell, current not only flows through the intended path but also diverts through alternative routes formed by combinations of neighboring cells that happen to be in a low-resistance state. The total current measured at the sense amplifier is therefore the sum of the desired cell current and the aggregate sneak current, which depends on the resistance states of all unselected cells in the same row and column. This dependency renders the sensed current highly data-dependent and unpredictable, as the stored data pattern is typically random or pseudo-random in practical applications.

The impact of sneak paths is twofold. First, they introduce significant ambiguity in the readout process, as the distributions of sensed current corresponding to the logical “0” and “1” states become broad and heavily overlapped. This overlap makes it impossible to define a single, fixed threshold that reliably distinguishes between the two states across all possible data patterns. Second, sneak paths contribute to unnecessary power dissipation, as current flows through unselected cells without conveying useful information. In large arrays, this parasitic power consumption can dominate the total energy budget, undermining the energy efficiency advantages of resistive memory technologies.

To model the sneak-path behavior accurately, the crossbar array can be represented by an equivalent circuit that captures the dominant parasitic components. In the “connected terminals” configuration, where unused word and bit lines are biased to a common reference voltage (VB), the equivalent circuit simplifies to three primary resistive elements: Rr, Rc, and Ra. Rr represents the parallel combination of all unselected cells in the same row as the target cell, excluding the target cell itself. Similarly, Rc represents the parallel combination of all unselected cells in the same column. Ra accounts for the diagonal sneak paths that traverse both unselected rows and columns; however, under the connected terminals biasing scheme with VB applied to unused terminals, Ra is effectively shorted out and can be neglected. The resistance of Rr can be expressed mathematically as the reciprocal of the sum of the conductances of the unselected row cells, i.e., Rr = 1 / Σ(1/Rx), where Rx is the resistance of each unselected cell in the row. Given that each Rx is either Ron (the ON-state resistance) or Roff (the OFF-state resistance), Rr can be rewritten in terms of the number of ON cells (Non) in the row, excluding the target cell: Rr = 1 / [Non / Ron + (L - 1 - Non) / Roff], where L is the total number of cells per row. An analogous expression holds for Rc.

For practical array sizes (e.g., L ≥ 256) and devices with a high OFF/ON resistance ratio (ρ = Roff / Ron >> 1), the value of Rr exhibits remarkable stability across different column positions within the same row. This is because changing the accessed column merely swaps one cell in the parallel combination—excluding the new target cell and including the previously excluded one. The relative change in Rr due to this swap is approximately ΔR/R ≈ 1 / (ρ · Non) for a balanced data pattern, which becomes negligible as ρ increases or as the array size grows. Empirical simulations confirm that even for worst-case data patterns with extreme imbalances in the number of ON and OFF cells, the variation in Rr remains small enough to be treated as constant for the purpose of readout within a given row. The same holds for Rc within a column. This near-constancy of Rr and Rc across spatial locations within a row or column constitutes the foundational insight upon which the present invention is built: the sneak-path characteristics of a row (or column) can be characterized once and then reused for all subsequent reads within that row (or column), enabling efficient and accurate single-access readout.

### Sneak-Paths Correlation

The spatial correlation of sneak-path currents within rows and columns of a resistive crossbar array is a critical property that enables the development of efficient readout techniques. As established in the preceding section, the equivalent resistance representing the collective contribution of unselected cells in a row (Rr) remains nearly invariant across different column positions within that row, provided the array is sufficiently large and the device exhibits a high OFF/ON resistance ratio. This invariance arises from the statistical averaging effect of a large number of parallel resistive elements, where the exclusion or inclusion of a single cell has a negligible impact on the total parallel resistance. The same principle applies to the column-wise sneak-path resistance (Rc). This correlation implies that the sneak-path noise observed at one location in a row can be used to predict the sneak-path noise at any other location in the same row, and similarly for columns.

This correlation property is particularly advantageous in the context of typical memory access patterns employed in modern computer systems. Due to the principle of spatial locality, data is often transferred between memory layers in contiguous blocks, such as cache lines of 64 bytes (512 bits). When reading such a block from a crossbar array, the access sequence typically proceeds in a row-wise or column-wise manner, accessing multiple bits within the same row before moving to the next. In this scenario, the knowledge gained from characterizing the sneak-path behavior of a single bit in a row can be directly applied to all other bits in that row, eliminating the need for repeated characterization. This is in stark contrast to random-access patterns, where each bit might belong to a different row and column, rendering correlation-based techniques less effective. However, since block-wise access is the dominant mode in practical systems, the correlation property offers substantial gains in read efficiency.

The exploitation of sneak-path correlation requires a crossbar architecture that supports consistent and predictable sneak-path behavior across the array. The “connected terminals” configuration, wherein unused word and bit lines are biased to a common reference voltage (VB), provides such consistency. In this configuration, the equivalent circuit of the array simplifies to a well-defined topology with three primary resistive elements (Rr, Rc, and Ra), and the application of VB to unused terminals ensures that the voltage drop across unselected cells is controlled and sub-threshold, thereby enhancing the nonlinear response of the memristive devices and suppressing unwanted current flow. Under these conditions, Rr and Rc become deterministic functions of the data pattern in the respective row and column, and their near-constancy across spatial locations within the row or column becomes a reliable basis for readout optimization.

The correlation property also facilitates the design of adaptive readout circuits that can dynamically adjust their decision thresholds based on the sneak-path characteristics of the current row or column. Instead of relying on a global, fixed threshold that must accommodate the worst-case overlap between the “0” and “1” current distributions, the readout circuit can compute a local threshold that is optimal for the specific sneak-path environment of the accessed row. This local threshold effectively shifts the decision boundary to account for the DC offset introduced by the sneak current, thereby restoring clear separation between the two logical states. The computation of this adaptive threshold can be performed either digitally, using parameters estimated from an initial read, or analogically, by comparing the sensed current directly against a reference derived from a dummy bit. In both cases, the correlation property ensures that the threshold remains valid for all subsequent reads within the same row, enabling single-access readout with high accuracy.

### Adaptive-Threshold Readout

The adaptive-threshold readout technique is a cornerstone of the present invention, enabling accurate and efficient data retrieval from resistive crossbar arrays by dynamically adjusting the decision threshold based on the sneak-path characteristics of the accessed row or column. Unlike conventional fixed-threshold methods, which fail in the presence of data-dependent sneak currents, the adaptive approach leverages the spatial correlation of sneak paths to define a local threshold that compensates for the parasitic current offset, thereby restoring clear separation between the logical “0” and “1” states.

The implementation of adaptive-threshold readout begins with the characterization of the sneak-path environment of the target row or column. In the “connected terminals” crossbar configuration, where unused word and bit lines are biased to an intermediate voltage VB (e.g., VDD/2), the equivalent circuit of the array simplifies to a topology where the sensed current Isense is the sum of the desired cell current Im and the row sneak-current component Ir (when sensing from node n1) or the column sneak-current component Ic (when sensing from node n2). Because of the spatial correlation property, Ir remains nearly constant for all column positions within the same row, and Ic remains nearly constant for all row positions within the same column. This constancy allows the sneak-current component to be treated as a DC offset that can be estimated and subtracted from the sensed current to recover the true cell current.

The adaptive threshold is defined as the midpoint between the expected current values for the “0” and “1” states, adjusted by the estimated sneak-current offset. For example, if the nominal ON and OFF currents of the memory cell are Ion and Ioff, respectively, and the estimated row sneak current is Ir, then the adaptive threshold for sensing from node n1 is given by T = (Ion + Ioff)/2 + Ir. Any sensed current above T is interpreted as a “1,” and any current below T is interpreted as a “0.” This threshold is computed once per row (or column) and applied to all subsequent reads within that row (or column), enabling single-access readout with high accuracy.

The estimation of the sneak-current component can be achieved through two primary methods: the “initial bits” approach and the “dummy bits” approach. In the initial bits method, the first bit accessed in a row (the “initial bit”) is read using a multi-stage technique that simultaneously estimates both the cell current Im and the sneak-current Ir. This multi-stage read may involve applying multiple voltage levels or using iterative sensing and feedback to isolate the two components. Once Ir is estimated, the adaptive threshold is computed and applied to all remaining bits in the row. In the dummy bits method, each row contains a predefined “dummy bit” whose logical state and resistance are known a priori. A single read of the dummy bit yields a current Idummy = Iref + Ir, where Iref is the known current of the dummy cell. Since Iref is known, Ir can be directly computed as Ir = Idummy - Iref, and the adaptive threshold is then derived accordingly. The dummy bits method is more time-efficient, as it requires only a single read to estimate Ir, compared to the multiple reads required by the initial bits method.

The adaptive-threshold readout technique is highly resilient to variations in device characteristics and operating conditions. Because the threshold is computed locally and dynamically, it automatically compensates for shifts in the absolute current levels caused by fabrication variability, temperature fluctuations, or aging effects. This adaptability ensures consistent read margins across the array and over time, enhancing the reliability and longevity of the memory system. Furthermore, the technique is compatible with standard memory access patterns, such as block-wise data transfer, and scales favorably with array size, making it suitable for practical deployment in high-density ReRAM systems.

### Multi-Read for Initial Bits

The “multi-read for initial bits” technique is a method employed within the adaptive-threshold readout framework to characterize the sneak-path environment of a row or column when predefined dummy bits are not available or not utilized. In this approach, the first bit accessed in a given row—referred to as the “initial bit”—is read using a multi-stage readout procedure that enables the simultaneous estimation of both the resistance of the target memory cell (Rm) and the effective sneak-path resistance of the row (Rr). This dual estimation is necessary because, without prior knowledge of either parameter, a single read operation yields only a composite current that is the sum of the desired cell current and the sneak current, leaving two unknowns that cannot be resolved with a single equation.

The multi-stage readout procedure for the initial bit typically involves applying a sequence of carefully controlled voltage stimuli to the crossbar array and measuring the resulting currents at each stage. For example, in a two-stage read, the first stage may apply a read voltage VDD to the selected cell while biasing the unused terminals to VB, yielding a sensed current Isense1 = Im + Ir. The second stage may then apply a different voltage configuration, such as swapping the sense node or altering the bias voltage, to obtain a second equation Isense2 = f(Im, Ir). By solving these two equations simultaneously, both Im and Ir can be uniquely determined. More sophisticated multi-stage techniques may employ additional stages to improve estimation accuracy or to handle nonlinear device characteristics, but the core principle remains the same: multiple independent measurements are used to resolve the two unknowns.

Once the sneak-current component Ir is estimated from the initial bit read, it is used to compute an adaptive threshold that applies to all subsequent bits in the same row. This threshold compensates for the DC offset introduced by the sneak current, enabling single-access readout for the remaining bits. The multi-read technique is particularly useful in scenarios where the addition of dummy bits is undesirable due to area overhead or design constraints. It is also compatible with existing memory architectures that do not include dedicated reference cells.

However, the multi-read technique does incur a latency and energy penalty for the initial bit, as it requires multiple array accesses instead of a single read. This overhead is amortized over the subsequent “regular bits” in the row, which are read in a single access using the adaptive threshold. For typical block-wise access patterns, where a large number of bits are read from the same row (e.g., a full cache line of 512 bits), the average number of array accesses per bit converges to one, making the overhead negligible in practice. Simulations have shown that for array sizes of 256 kb or larger, the average number of readouts per bit drops below 1.01 for cache line sizes of 64 bytes or more, confirming the practicality of the multi-read initial bits approach.

The readout circuitry for the multi-read technique typically consists of a virtual-ground analog-to-digital converter (ADC) for current sensing and a digital processing unit for solving the estimation equations and computing the adaptive threshold. The ADC converts the analog sensed currents into digital values, which are then processed by the digital unit to extract Im and Ir. The computed threshold is stored in a local register and applied to the comparator for all subsequent reads in the row. This hybrid analog-digital architecture offers a good balance between accuracy and complexity, and it can be shared across multiple rows or columns to minimize area overhead.

### Predefined Dummy Bits

The “predefined dummy bits” technique is an alternative and more time-efficient method for characterizing the sneak-path environment of a row or column in a resistive crossbar array. In this approach, each row (or column) of the array is augmented with one or more “dummy bits” whose logical state and resistance are known a priori at design time. These dummy bits serve as built-in reference elements that enable the direct estimation of the sneak-path current with a single read operation, thereby eliminating the need for multi-stage readout procedures.

The dummy bits can be implemented in several ways. In one embodiment, they are realized as standard memristive cells that are pre-programmed during the fabrication or initialization phase to a specific resistance state, typically the OFF state (Roff) due to its lower variability and higher resistance, which makes the dummy current closer to the pure sneak current. In another embodiment, the dummy bits are implemented as static resistors with a fixed resistance value that matches the nominal OFF or ON state of the memory cells. The static resistor approach offers superior stability and immunity to operational variability, as it does not rely on the switching behavior of a memristive device. Regardless of the implementation, the key requirement is that the resistance of the dummy bit is known and stable, allowing its current contribution to be precisely accounted for during readout.

During a block-wise read sequence, the first step in accessing a new row is to read the dummy bit of that row. The sensed current Idummy is the sum of the dummy cell current Iref and the row sneak current Ir, i.e., Idummy = Iref + Ir. Since Iref is known, Ir can be directly computed as Ir = Idummy - Iref. This estimated sneak current is then used to compute an adaptive threshold for the row, which is applied to all subsequent reads of the “regular bits” in the same row. Because the dummy bit read requires only a single array access, this method is significantly faster than the multi-read initial bits approach, which requires multiple accesses to estimate the same parameter.

The area overhead of the dummy bits technique is minimal, as only one dummy bit is needed per row (or column), and the total number of dummy bits scales linearly with the number of rows rather than the total number of cells. For a 256 kb array with 512 rows, for example, the addition of 512 dummy bits represents an overhead of less than 0.2%, which is negligible compared to the benefits in read efficiency and reliability. Moreover, the dummy bits can be placed in dedicated columns that are excluded from the user-addressable memory space, ensuring that they do not interfere with normal data storage.

The readout circuitry for the dummy bits technique can be implemented in two primary ways. The first approach uses an analog sensing front-end (e.g., a transimpedance amplifier) to convert the sensed current into a voltage, followed by a digital comparator that compares the voltage against a reference derived from the dummy bit. The second approach employs a fully analog compensated readout circuit, where the current from the dummy bit is sampled onto a capacitor, and the current from each regular bit is sampled onto a second capacitor in sequence. A differential comparator then directly compares the two capacitor voltages to determine the logical state of the regular bit. This analog approach eliminates the need for ADCs and digital processing, reducing both area and power consumption, and is particularly well-suited for ultra-low-power applications.

### Crossbar Power Consumption

Power consumption is a critical concern in resistive crossbar memory arrays, as parasitic sneak paths can lead to significant energy waste during read operations. The present invention addresses this issue through a combination of architectural and circuit-level optimizations that minimize both the magnitude of sneak currents and the energy required to perform readout. Central to this approach is the use of the “connected terminals” biasing scheme, wherein unused word and bit lines are biased to an intermediate voltage VB (e.g., VDD/2) rather than being left floating or grounded. This biasing strategy exploits the nonlinear current-voltage (I-V) characteristics of memristive devices to suppress sneak-path currents while maintaining a strong signal from the selected cell.

Memristive devices used in ReRAM applications typically exhibit a nonlinear I-V response, where the resistance increases sharply as the applied voltage drops below a certain threshold. In the connected terminals configuration, the selected cell experiences a full read voltage VDD, ensuring a large current difference between its ON and OFF states. In contrast, unselected cells along sneak paths experience a sub-threshold voltage of VDD - VB, which, when VB is set to VDD/2, reduces the voltage across each unselected cell to VDD/2. Due to the device nonlinearity, this halving of the voltage can increase the resistance of the unselected cells by up to two orders of magnitude, thereby drastically reducing the sneak current. Simulations have shown that setting VB = VDD/2 yields the optimal trade-off between signal strength and sneak-path suppression, minimizing total power consumption while maintaining sufficient read margins.

The power consumption of the connected terminals scheme is comparable to that of the baseline floating terminals approach, but with the added benefit of predictable and controllable sneak-path behavior. In contrast, the grounded terminals approach, where unused lines are tied to ground, results in much higher power consumption due to uncontrolled current flow through low-resistance paths. The connected terminals scheme also avoids the signal degradation issues associated with floating terminals, where the lack of a defined voltage on unused lines can lead to capacitive coupling and noise.

In addition to biasing optimization, the invention reduces power consumption through efficient readout techniques that minimize the number of array accesses per bit. Both the multi-read initial bits and predefined dummy bits methods ensure that, after an initial characterization step, all subsequent bits in a row are read in a single access. This single-access readout, combined with the adaptive thresholding that eliminates the need for iterative sensing, significantly reduces the energy per bit compared to prior art techniques that require multiple reads or complex signal processing. Simulations on 256 kb arrays have demonstrated that the total power consumption of the proposed system saturates for larger array sizes due to the dominance of metal line resistances, but remains well below that of competing gateless techniques.

The power efficiency of the invention is further enhanced by the use of low-power readout circuitry, such as the fully analog compensated comparator for the dummy bits technique, which eliminates the need for power-hungry ADCs and digital logic. Overall, the combination of nonlinear device exploitation, optimized biasing, and efficient readout algorithms results in a crossbar memory system that achieves high read accuracy with minimal energy expenditure, making it suitable for battery-powered and energy-constrained applications.

### Figure-of-Merit

The performance of the proposed readout technique is evaluated using a comprehensive figure-of-merit (FoM) that captures the trade-offs between read accuracy, energy efficiency, and speed. The FoM is defined as the inverse of the product of the energy per bit (Ebit) and the read latency per bit (Tbit), i.e., FoM = 1 / (Ebit × Tbit). This metric provides a single scalar value that reflects the overall efficiency of the readout system, with higher values indicating better performance. The energy per bit includes both the dynamic energy consumed during the read operation and the static energy associated with biasing and leakage, while the read latency per bit accounts for the total time required to retrieve a bit of data, including any overhead from multi-stage reads or dummy bit accesses.

Simulation results on 256 kb crossbar arrays, using realistic device models and data patterns derived from NIST RAM images, demonstrate that the proposed technique achieves a FoM that is 7 to 24 times higher than that of existing gateless readout methods reported in the literature. This improvement stems from several key innovations: the exploitation of sneak-path correlation to enable single-access readout for the majority of bits, the use of adaptive thresholding to eliminate read errors without iterative sensing, the optimization of terminal biasing to minimize sneak-path power, and the strategic use of dummy bits to reduce characterization overhead. The FoM advantage is particularly pronounced for large array sizes and block-wise access patterns, where the amortization of initial characterization costs leads to near-ideal single-access efficiency.

The FoM analysis also takes into account the impact of device variability and fabrication imperfections, which can degrade read margins and increase error rates in conventional systems. The adaptive nature of the proposed technique compensates for shifts in absolute resistance values, maintaining high read accuracy even in the presence of significant variability. Furthermore, the use of dummy bits in the low-variability OFF state or as static resistors further enhances robustness, ensuring consistent performance across process corners and operating conditions.

In addition to the FoM, the invention is evaluated on other relevant metrics, including area overhead, circuit complexity, and compatibility with standard memory interfaces. The area overhead is minimal, with less than 0.2% added for dummy bits in a 256 kb array, and the circuit complexity is comparable to or lower than that of prior art, thanks to the elimination of multi-stage read logic and the use of simple analog comparators. The system is fully compatible with standard block-wise memory access protocols, making it easy to integrate into existing memory hierarchies.

Overall, the FoM analysis confirms that the proposed technique represents a significant advancement in the state of the art for resistive crossbar memory readout, offering a compelling combination of high efficiency, low power, and robust reliability that is well-suited for practical deployment in next-generation memory systems.

### Operations Performed by a Computing Device to Efficiently Perform Readout Operations

The efficient readout of data from a resistive crossbar memory array according to the present invention is orchestrated by a computing device that executes a sequence of well-defined operations to leverage sneak-path correlation, adaptive thresholding, and optimized biasing. These operations are designed to minimize read latency and energy consumption while ensuring high accuracy, and they can be implemented in hardware, firmware, or a combination thereof. The computing device, which may be a memory controller, a dedicated readout engine, or a general-purpose processor with integrated memory management capabilities, performs the following steps during a block-wise read operation.

First, the computing device initializes the crossbar array by configuring the terminal biasing scheme. Specifically, it sets the voltage on unused word and bit lines to an intermediate value VB, typically VDD/2, to exploit the nonlinear I-V characteristics of the memristive devices and suppress sneak-path currents. This biasing configuration is maintained throughout the read operation to ensure consistent and predictable sneak-path behavior.

Next, the computing device begins reading a contiguous block of data, such as a cache line, from the array. It processes the block row by row (or column by column, depending on the access pattern). For each new row, the device first determines whether to use the “initial bits” or “dummy bits” method for sneak-path characterization. If dummy bits are present, the device reads the dummy bit of the current row, measures the sensed current Idummy, and computes the row sneak current Ir as Ir = Idummy - Iref, where Iref is the known current of the dummy cell. If dummy bits are not available, the device selects the first bit in the row as the “initial bit” and performs a multi-stage read to estimate both the cell current Im and the sneak current Ir.

Once Ir is estimated, the computing device computes an adaptive threshold T for the row, defined as the midpoint between the nominal ON and OFF currents adjusted by Ir. This threshold is stored in a local register and applied to all subsequent reads within the row. The device then proceeds to read each “regular bit” in the row in a single access, comparing the sensed current against T to determine the logical state of the bit. The result is stored in an output buffer or directly transferred to the requesting processor.

The computing device repeats this process for each row in the block, updating the adaptive threshold as needed for each new row. Throughout the operation, it monitors the readout statistics, such as error rates and power consumption, and may dynamically adjust parameters like VB or the threshold computation algorithm to optimize performance under varying conditions. The entire sequence is executed with minimal overhead, as the characterization cost is amortized over the large number of regular bits in each row, and the single-access readout ensures low latency and energy per bit.

The operations performed by the computing device are highly parallelizable and scalable, making them suitable for implementation in multi-core or many-core memory systems. They also support advanced features such as error correction, wear leveling, and security mechanisms, which can be integrated into the readout pipeline without significant performance penalties. Overall, the computing device plays a crucial role in realizing the full potential of the proposed readout technique, enabling efficient, reliable, and high-performance data retrieval from resistive crossbar memory arrays.
