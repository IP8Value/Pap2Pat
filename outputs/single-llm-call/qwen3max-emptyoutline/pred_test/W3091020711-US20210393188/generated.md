# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention relates generally to the field of cardiovascular physiology and diagnostics, and more specifically to methods and systems for assessing baroreflex function through the identification and quantification of intermittent on and off states of baroreflex-mediated control of heart rate. The invention further pertains to the use of such assessments in the diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic management of hypertension and related autonomic disorders. In particular, the invention provides a novel computational framework for analyzing continuous arterial pressure and heart rate time-series data obtained from ambulatory subjects to determine the fraction of time during which the baroreflex is actively engaged in modulating heart rate in response to fluctuations in arterial pressure—referred to herein as the “on fraction”—and to correlate this metric with mean arterial pressure, disease progression, and therapeutic responsiveness. The disclosed methods are especially applicable to distinguishing etiologies of hypertension that are mechanistically linked to baroreflex intermittency, as opposed to other forms of hypertension driven by renal, endocrine, or metabolic dysregulation. The invention thus establishes a new physiological biomarker for baroreflex functionality that operates on chronic, rather than acute, time scales and offers clinical utility in stratifying patients with primary hypertension, predicting outcomes of baroreceptor activation therapy, and monitoring the efficacy of autonomic interventions.

## BACKGROUND

Arterial blood pressure (BP) is a tightly regulated physiological variable maintained within narrow limits through the integrated actions of multiple organ systems, including the cardiovascular, renal, endocrine, and autonomic nervous systems. Among these, the arterial baroreflex represents a critical short-term feedback mechanism that buffers moment-to-moment fluctuations in BP by modulating heart rate (HR), vascular tone, and sympathetic outflow. Baroreceptors located in the carotid sinuses and aortic arch sense changes in arterial wall stretch and transmit this information via the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves to the nucleus tractus solitarius in the brainstem, which in turn adjusts parasympathetic and sympathetic activity to restore hemodynamic stability. Traditionally, baroreflex function has been assessed using measures such as baroreflex sensitivity (BRS), typically defined as the change in RR interval (the inverse of HR) per unit change in systolic or mean arterial pressure. BRS is commonly estimated during controlled maneuvers like the Valsalva maneuver, pharmacological challenges (e.g., phenylephrine or nitroprusside infusion), or spontaneous sequence analysis of beat-to-beat BP and HR variability.

However, a longstanding controversy in cardiovascular physiology concerns the role of the baroreflex in long-term BP regulation. Classical teaching holds that the baroreflex resets rapidly in response to sustained changes in pressure and therefore cannot contribute to the chronic maintenance of arterial pressure over days, weeks, or months. This view is supported by observations that sinoaortic denervation (SAD)—surgical ablation of baroreceptor afferents—causes only transient increases in BP in normotensive animals, with pressure returning to baseline after several days due to compensatory mechanisms involving the kidneys and other regulatory systems. Nevertheless, emerging evidence challenges this dogma. For instance, carotid baroreceptor activation therapy (BAT) has demonstrated efficacy in reducing BP in patients with treatment-resistant hypertension, suggesting that chronic modulation of baroreceptor input can indeed influence long-term pressure homeostasis. Moreover, genetic ablation of mechanically sensitive ion channels such as PIEZO1/2 in baroreceptor neurons results in sustained elevations in mean arterial pressure in mice, further implicating the baroreflex in chronic BP control.

Despite these advances, existing methods for assessing baroreflex function suffer from significant limitations. Most techniques rely on externally imposed perturbations that may not reflect natural, unperturbed physiological conditions. Spontaneous sequence methods, while non-invasive, are highly sensitive to respiratory influences and typically detect only very short sequences of coupled BP-HR changes—often limited to three to four consecutive beats. Consequently, these approaches fail to capture potential longer-duration disruptions in baroreflex engagement that may occur during normal daily activities. Furthermore, conventional BRS metrics often show poor correlation with actual BP levels in hypertensive populations, leading to inconsistent diagnostic and prognostic value.

Primary hypertension, which accounts for over 90% of all hypertension cases, remains a multifactorial disorder with no single identifiable cause. While numerous hypotheses have been proposed—including renal sodium retention, sympathetic overactivity, vascular remodeling, and hormonal imbalances—none fully explain the heterogeneity of the disease across individuals. The spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHR) is a well-established genetic model of primary hypertension that develops elevated BP without exogenous intervention, making it invaluable for studying disease mechanisms. The Wistar-Kyoto (WKY) rat, derived from the same ancestral Wistar stock, serves as its normotensive control. Although both strains share substantial genetic background, the SHR exhibits progressive hypertension beginning around 6–7 weeks of age, accompanied by structural and functional alterations in the cardiovascular system. Prior studies have reported reduced baroreflex sensitivity in SHRs compared to WKY rats, but the causal relationship between baroreflex impairment and hypertension development remains debated.

Recent investigations into the dynamics of autonomic control have revealed complex, non-stationary patterns in cardiovascular regulation that are not captured by static or averaged metrics. In particular, the concept of “intermittency”—the spontaneous switching between distinct functional states—has gained traction in neuroscience and physiology. However, until now, no systematic method has existed to quantify intermittent engagement of the baroreflex under baseline, conscious conditions over extended periods. The absence of such a tool has hindered the ability to determine whether transient disengagement of baroreflex control contributes to the gradual elevation of arterial pressure in susceptible individuals.

Compounding this challenge is the lack of strain-specific understanding of how baroreflex dynamics relate to hypertension etiology. While the SHR/WKY model has been extensively studied, other models such as the Dahl salt-sensitive (SS) rat exhibit hypertension driven primarily by dietary salt intake and renal mechanisms, with minimal involvement of autonomic dysfunction. Without a method capable of distinguishing baroreflex-dependent from baroreflex-independent forms of hypertension, clinicians and researchers are unable to tailor therapies based on underlying pathophysiology.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a robust, non-invasive, and physiologically grounded method to assess the temporal dynamics of baroreflex function during normal daily life. Such a method should be capable of identifying prolonged periods when the baroreflex is effectively disengaged from HR control, quantifying the proportion of time spent in active versus inactive states, and correlating this metric with arterial pressure levels and disease progression. Furthermore, the method should be validated across multiple animal models to establish its specificity for neurogenic forms of hypertension and its potential translatability to human patients. The present invention addresses these needs by introducing a novel computational algorithm that analyzes continuous arterial pressure and HR time-series data to define and quantify “on” and “off” states of baroreflex-mediated HR control, thereby providing a new biomarker—the on fraction—that reflects the chronic engagement level of the baroreflex and its contribution to BP regulation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method and system for assessing baroreflex function through the identification and quantification of intermittent on and off states of baroreflex-mediated control of heart rate using continuous arterial pressure and heart rate time-series data. Unlike conventional approaches that measure average baroreflex sensitivity or rely on external perturbations, the invention introduces a dynamic, state-based framework that captures the temporal intermittency of baroreflex engagement during unperturbed, conscious conditions. The core of the invention is a computational algorithm that compares observed fluctuations in heart rate (or RR interval) with those predicted by a mathematical model of the baroreflex arc in response to measured arterial pressure changes. Periods during which the observed and predicted heart rate dynamics are congruent are classified as “on states,” indicating active baroreflex-mediated control; periods of incongruence are classified as “off states,” indicating transient disengagement of the baroreflex from heart rate regulation.

The invention defines a new physiological metric, termed the “on fraction,” which represents the proportion of total observation time during which the baroreflex is in the on state. This metric is shown to be strongly predictive of mean arterial pressure in specific genetic models of hypertension, particularly the spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHR) and its normotensive control, the Wistar-Kyoto (WKY) rat. Critically, the on fraction decreases with age and correlates inversely with mean arterial pressure in these strains, whereas no such relationship is observed in other rat models of hypertension (e.g., Dahl salt-sensitive, Dahl salt-resistant, Sprague-Dawley), indicating that the association between baroreflex intermittency and blood pressure is etiology-specific.

The invention further demonstrates that mean arterial pressure is consistently higher during off states than during on states in SHR and WKY rats, and that pressure tends to increase during the initial seconds of an off state while decreasing during the onset of an on state. These directional trends are absent in other rat strains, reinforcing the unique role of baroreflex intermittency in the pathophysiology of SHR/WKY hypertension. To confirm causality, the invention includes experimental validation via sinoaortic denervation (SAD), which ablates baroreceptor input. Following SAD, WKY rats exhibit a significant, albeit transient, increase in mean arterial pressure, while SHR rats show no such increase, supporting the hypothesis that the lower baseline pressure in WKY rats is partially maintained by more frequent baroreflex engagement.

The disclosed method comprises the following steps: (a) obtaining continuous time-series data of arterial pressure and heart rate from a subject during a baseline period; (b) processing the data to extract mean arterial pressure and RR interval at each cardiac cycle; (c) applying a first-order linear filter model of the baroreflex to predict the expected RR interval based on the measured arterial pressure; (d) comparing the predicted and observed rates of change in RR interval over sliding time windows to generate a scatter plot of model-predicted versus data-observed slopes; (e) demarcating on and off states using a hyperbolic boundary in the slope-slope plot, wherein points within the boundary correspond to on states and points outside correspond to off states; (f) applying a noise-filtering procedure to smooth the binary on/off time series; and (g) computing the on fraction as the total duration of on states divided by the total observation time.

The invention is not limited to rodent models but is designed for eventual translation to human clinical applications. It enables the differentiation of hypertension subtypes based on baroreflex dynamics, prediction of therapeutic response to baroreceptor activation therapy, and monitoring of autonomic function in conditions such as diabetic neuropathy, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, and familial dysautonomia. By providing a quantitative, time-resolved assessment of baroreflex engagement, the invention overcomes the limitations of traditional baroreflex sensitivity measures and establishes a new paradigm for understanding the role of neural control in chronic blood pressure regulation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for evaluating the intermittent functioning of the baroreflex-mediated control of heart rate through the analysis of continuous arterial pressure and heart rate time-series data. The method is based on the discovery that the baroreflex does not operate continuously but rather switches intermittently between periods of active engagement (“on states”) and disengagement (“off states”) during normal, unperturbed physiological conditions. This intermittency is not merely stochastic noise but represents a structured, quantifiable phenomenon that correlates with mean arterial pressure in specific etiologies of hypertension. The invention provides a rigorous computational framework to identify these states, quantify their duration, and derive a novel biomarker—the on fraction—that reflects the chronic engagement level of the baroreflex.

The method begins with the acquisition of high-fidelity, continuous arterial pressure waveforms from a subject. In preclinical settings, this is achieved using implantable radio-telemetry devices that record pressure at high sampling rates (e.g., 500 Hz) during the subject’s active phase (e.g., the dark cycle in nocturnal rodents). In human applications, analogous data can be obtained via non-invasive continuous finger plethysmography (e.g., Finometer, Portapres) or intra-arterial catheterization in clinical settings. Concurrently, heart rate is derived from the inter-beat intervals (RR intervals) of the pressure waveform, where each heartbeat is identified by the systolic peak, and the RR interval is computed as the time between successive systolic peaks.

The arterial pressure signal is then processed to extract mean arterial pressure (MAP) at each cardiac cycle. MAP is approximated using the standard formula: MAP ≈ diastolic pressure + ⅓(pulse pressure), where pulse pressure is the difference between systolic and diastolic pressure. This per-beat MAP time series, along with the corresponding RR interval time series, forms the input for the baroreflex intermittency analysis.

A mathematical model of the baroreflex arc is employed to predict the expected RR interval response to the observed MAP fluctuations. The model used in the preferred embodiment is a first-order linear filter, expressed as:

d(RR)/dt = (1/τ)(α·(MAP₀ – MAP(t)) + R₀ – RR(t))

where α is the baroreflex gain (in units of seconds per mmHg), τ is the time constant of the response (in seconds), R₀ is the baseline RR interval, and MAP₀ is the reference pressure. This differential equation captures the fundamental principle that an increase in MAP should elicit a decrease in RR interval (i.e., an increase in HR) with a characteristic time delay governed by τ. The solution to this equation over discrete cardiac cycles yields a predicted RR interval time series, denoted RR_model(t).

To estimate the model parameters α and τ for a given subject at a specific age or condition, a cost functional is minimized that quantifies the discrepancy between the variability of the model-predicted RR interval and the observed RR interval. Specifically, the local rate of change in RR interval is computed over a sliding window (e.g., 10 seconds) for both the model output (μ_m) and the data (μ_d). The standard deviations of these rates, σ_m and σ_d, are then used to define a cost J = (σ_m – σ_d)². The parameters α and τ are optimized to minimize J across multiple representative time windows (e.g., twelve 5-minute segments evenly distributed over a 12-hour recording period), ensuring robustness against transient artifacts.

Once the model is calibrated, the next step is to compare the predicted and observed dynamics to identify on and off states. This is accomplished by plotting μ_m versus μ_d for each cardiac cycle. Under ideal baroreflex control, the points should cluster around the line μ_m = μ_d. Deviations from this line indicate a breakdown in the expected coupling. To formalize this, a hyperbolic boundary is defined by the equation:

(μ_m – μ_d)² / r² – (μ_m + μ_d)² / (α·r)² = 1

where r is a threshold parameter set to half the standard deviation of μ_d across the population (e.g., r = 0.0004 for intact rats), and α is a shape parameter (e.g., α = 3.5) that controls the curvature of the hyperbola. Points falling inside this hyperbolic region (the white area in the scatter plot) are classified as on states, while those outside (gray regions) are classified as off states. This boundary is symmetric about the line μ_m = μ_d and excludes small fluctuations that may arise from measurement noise or non-baroreflex influences.

The resulting binary time series of on/off designations is then subjected to a noise-filtering procedure to eliminate spurious transitions. Each cardiac cycle is initially assigned a value of 1 (on) or 0 (off). A moving average filter with a 10-second window is applied iteratively until convergence, producing a smoothed probability-like signal. Cycles with a smoothed value ≥ 0.5 are designated as on states; those < 0.5 are off states. This step ensures that brief, isolated mismatches do not fragment the state classification into unrealistically short segments.

The on fraction is then computed as the total time spent in on states divided by the total observation time. This metric is dimensionless and ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater baroreflex engagement. In validation studies, the on fraction was found to range from 40% to 85% across rat strains and ages, with significant differences between SHR and WKY rats that correlate with mean arterial pressure.

The invention further includes the analysis of pressure dynamics during on and off states. Probability density functions of MAP during each state are constructed to assess differences in central tendency and variability. Additionally, ensemble-averaged time courses of MAP aligned to the onset of on or off states are computed to reveal directional trends. In SHR and WKY rats, MAP is consistently higher during off states, and shows a statistically significant increase during the first 20 seconds of off states and a decrease during the first 20 seconds of on states—patterns absent in other strains.

To establish causality, the invention incorporates experimental perturbation via sinoaortic denervation (SAD). In SAD, the aortic depressor and carotid sinus nerves are surgically severed or chemically denervated, abolishing baroreceptor input to the brainstem. Post-SAD, telemetry recordings are repeated, and the on fraction and MAP are compared to sham-operated controls. In WKY rats, SAD causes a significant, transient increase in MAP (e.g., +9.4 mmHg at day 3), while in SHR rats, no such increase occurs, confirming that the lower baseline pressure in WKY is baroreflex-dependent.

The system implementing the invention comprises a data acquisition module, a signal processing unit, a computational engine executing the intermittency algorithm, and a display/output interface. The computational engine may be implemented in software (e.g., MATLAB, Python) running on a general-purpose computer or embedded in a dedicated medical device. The output includes the on fraction, state time series, pressure distributions, and trend plots, which can be used for diagnostic, prognostic, or therapeutic decision-making.

The invention is particularly useful for stratifying patients with primary hypertension into baroreflex-dependent and baroreflex-independent subtypes. Those with low on fractions may be candidates for baroreceptor activation therapy, while those with normal on fractions may benefit more from renal or metabolic interventions. The method also enables longitudinal monitoring of autonomic function in neurodegenerative or metabolic diseases affecting the autonomic nervous system.

## EXAMPLES

Example 1: Assessment of Baroreflex Intermittency in SHR and WKY Rats Across Development

Male and female spontaneously hypertensive rats (SHR) and Wistar-Kyoto (WKY) control rats (n = 12 per strain, equal sex distribution) were implanted with radio-telemetry transmitters at 5 weeks of age. After a 7-day recovery, continuous arterial pressure was recorded during the 12-hour dark cycle at 7, 10, and 15 weeks of age. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was significantly elevated in SHR compared to WKY at all ages (P < 0.0001), with progressive increases in both strains. Heart rate decreased with age in both strains, while heart rate variability (standard deviation of RR interval) increased with age in WKY but not in SHR.

Using the intermittency algorithm, the on fraction was computed for each animal at each age. The on fraction decreased with age in both strains (P < 0.01) and was significantly lower in SHR than WKY at 15 weeks (P < 0.05). Across all data points, the on fraction was tightly correlated with MAP (R² = 0.68, P < 0.0001), indicating that reduced baroreflex engagement predicts higher pressure. In contrast, no such correlation was observed in Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (n = 6) recorded at 8, 11, and 15 weeks, demonstrating strain specificity.

Example 2: Comparison Across Hypertension Models

Dahl salt-sensitive (SS) rats (n = 8 males, n = 8 females) and Dahl salt-resistant (SR) rats (n = 8 males) were fed either high-fat or control diets and recorded at 13 or 20 weeks. Despite significant differences in MAP between diet groups in SS rats, the on fraction showed no correlation with MAP in any group (P > 0.05 for all comparisons). Similarly, SD rats exhibited no relationship between on fraction and MAP. These results confirm that the predictive link between baroreflex intermittency and pressure is unique to the SHR/WKY genetic background.

Example 3: Pressure Dynamics During On and Off States

Probability density functions of MAP during on and off states were constructed for SHR, WKY, and SD rats at 15 weeks. In SHR and WKY, the MAP distribution was wider during off states, and the mean MAP was significantly higher (P < 0.0001) during off states (difference > 1.6 mmHg). In SD rats, the difference was < 0.9 mmHg. Ensemble-averaged MAP time courses aligned to state onset showed a significant increase in MAP during the first 20 seconds of off states in SHR at 10 and 15 weeks (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively) and a significant decrease during on states in WKY at 7 and 15 weeks (P < 0.05).

Example 4: Sinoaortic Denervation Validation

SHR and WKY rats (n = 8 per strain, equal sex) underwent sinoaortic denervation (SAD) or sham surgery at 6 weeks. Telemetry recordings over 10 postoperative days showed that MAP in WKY-SAD increased by 9.4 mmHg at day 3 compared to WKY-sham (P < 0.01), while SHR-SAD showed no increase relative to SHR-sham. The on fraction in SAD groups dropped to near zero, confirming baroreflex ablation. The transient nature of the pressure increase in WKY-SAD (returning to baseline by day 5) suggests compensation by other regulatory systems, but the magnitude (~50% of the SHR-WKY pressure difference) supports a causal role for baroreflex intermittency in baseline pressure determination.

These examples collectively demonstrate the invention’s ability to identify a novel mechanism of hypertension in the SHR model, provide a quantitative biomarker for baroreflex engagement, and differentiate etiologies of high blood pressure across animal models.
