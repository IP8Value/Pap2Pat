# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of oncology and anti-cancer therapeutics, and more specifically to a novel class of synthetic, membrane-disrupting proteins designed for the targeted treatment of solid tumors. The invention provides a de novo engineered protein, designated SGP (Synthetic Glycine-rich Protein), which exhibits potent cytotoxic activity against a broad spectrum of human tumor cells through rapid physical disruption of cellular membranes. Unlike conventional chemotherapeutic agents that act via intracellular mechanisms such as DNA intercalation or microtubule inhibition, SGP operates through a unique biophysical mechanism involving direct interaction with lipid bilayers, resulting in immediate loss of plasma membrane integrity and consequent cell death. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising SGP and methods of using SGP for the localized treatment of primary and metastatic tumors, particularly those that are unresectable or refractory to standard therapies. Importantly, while SGP demonstrates non-selective membrane-disrupting activity in vitro against both malignant and non-malignant cells, its therapeutic application in vivo reveals a surprising and advantageous selectivity for tumor tissue over normal host tissue. This selectivity arises not from intrinsic molecular recognition of tumor-specific markers but rather from the differential accessibility of cell membranes in the tumor microenvironment compared to healthy tissues protected by extracellular matrix components. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in anti-cancer drug design, moving away from receptor-targeted or metabolism-dependent strategies toward a physical mode of action that circumvents common mechanisms of drug resistance. The field of the invention also includes the rational design and structural optimization of SGP variants, wherein specific amino acid substitutions or helical domain modifications modulate membrane affinity, stability, and cytotoxic potency, thereby enabling fine-tuning of therapeutic efficacy and safety profiles.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The development of effective anti-cancer therapeutics has long been hindered by the dual challenges of tumor heterogeneity and the emergence of drug resistance. Traditional cytotoxic agents, including alkylating agents, antimetabolites, and topoisomerase inhibitors, exert their effects through interference with essential intracellular processes such as DNA replication or protein synthesis. While these compounds have demonstrated clinical utility, their therapeutic index is often narrow due to significant toxicity to rapidly dividing normal cells, particularly in the bone marrow, gastrointestinal tract, and hair follicles. Moreover, many tumors develop resistance to these agents through upregulation of efflux pumps, enhanced DNA repair mechanisms, or alterations in drug targets, thereby limiting long-term efficacy. In response to these limitations, the past several decades have witnessed a shift toward targeted therapies designed to exploit specific molecular vulnerabilities in cancer cells. Monoclonal antibodies directed against growth factor receptors (e.g., trastuzumab for HER2-positive breast cancer) and small molecule kinase inhibitors (e.g., imatinib for chronic myeloid leukemia) exemplify this approach. However, the success of targeted therapies is contingent upon the presence of the specific molecular target, and resistance frequently emerges through secondary mutations or activation of compensatory signaling pathways. More recently, immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown remarkable efficacy in certain cancers, but their benefits are restricted to a subset of patients whose tumors exhibit specific immunological features, such as high mutational burden or PD-L1 expression.

Parallel efforts have explored the potential of membrane-disrupting agents as anti-cancer therapeutics, inspired by naturally occurring pore-forming peptides produced by various organisms as part of their innate immune defense. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) such as magainins, cecropins, defensins, and melittin have been shown to possess cytotoxic activity against tumor cells in vitro, primarily through their ability to interact with and permeabilize lipid bilayers. These peptides typically adopt amphipathic structures that allow them to insert into membranes, forming pores or causing generalized membrane destabilization. Despite promising in vitro results, the translation of AMPs into effective in vivo anti-cancer therapies has been largely unsuccessful. Clinical trials with magainin and cecropin derivatives, for example, have demonstrated limited efficacy in animal tumor models and significant systemic toxicity in humans, attributable to their non-selective membrane-disrupting properties. The fundamental challenge lies in achieving tumor selectivity: since all mammalian cells possess phospholipid bilayers, membrane-disrupting agents inherently lack the molecular specificity that characterizes receptor-targeted therapies. Consequently, systemic administration of such agents typically results in unacceptable damage to normal tissues, particularly erythrocytes and endothelial cells.

Several strategies have been proposed to enhance the tumor selectivity of membrane-disrupting peptides, including conjugation to tumor-homing ligands, incorporation into nanoparticle delivery systems, or engineering pH-sensitive variants that become active only in the acidic tumor microenvironment. However, these approaches add complexity to drug formulation and may not fully overcome the inherent toxicity of the membrane-disrupting moiety. Moreover, many naturally derived AMPs suffer from poor pharmacokinetic properties, including rapid proteolytic degradation and short half-lives in circulation, further limiting their therapeutic potential. The field has thus remained in need of a fundamentally new approach to membrane-disrupting anti-cancer agents—one that reconciles potent cytotoxic activity with acceptable in vivo safety.

The present invention addresses this unmet need through the de novo design of a synthetic protein, SGP, which combines the potent membrane-disrupting capabilities of natural AMPs with a unique mechanism of in vivo selectivity. Unlike naturally occurring peptides that evolved for antimicrobial defense, SGP was rationally engineered from first principles to perform a predetermined biological function: the selective ablation of tumor cells through physical membrane disruption. The design of SGP incorporates specific structural features, including defined helical domains and strategic placement of charged and hydrophobic residues, that optimize its interaction with lipid bilayers while maintaining stability under physiological conditions. Critically, the invention leverages an unexpected biological phenomenon—the protective effect of extracellular matrix components against SGP-mediated membrane disruption—to achieve tumor-selective cytotoxicity in vivo despite non-selective activity in vitro. This discovery overturns the conventional assumption that membrane-disrupting agents must inherently lack tissue selectivity and opens a new avenue for the development of physically acting anti-cancer therapeutics. The background art thus establishes a clear need for novel anti-cancer agents that operate through non-traditional mechanisms, avoid common resistance pathways, and can be safely administered to achieve meaningful clinical responses, all of which are fulfilled by the present invention.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel synthetic protein, designated SGP (Synthetic Glycine-rich Protein), and its use as a potent anti-cancer therapeutic agent. SGP is a de novo designed protein comprising 91 amino acid residues with a calculated molecular weight of approximately 7555 daltons, characterized by a specific sequence that includes multiple glycine, alanine, leucine, aspartic acid, proline, tyrosine, lysine, and tryptophan residues arranged to form defined structural domains. The protein exhibits exceptional membrane-disrupting activity, capable of inducing rapid and complete loss of viability in a wide range of human tumor cell lines through direct physical interaction with cellular plasma membranes. Unlike conventional chemotherapeutic agents that require internalization and engagement of intracellular targets, SGP acts extracellularly by compromising membrane integrity, leading to immediate cell lysis at high concentrations or induction of programmed cell death pathways at lower concentrations. The invention further discloses that SGP demonstrates broad-spectrum anti-tumor activity against diverse malignancies, including carcinomas (breast, prostate, lung), sarcomas (Kaposi’s sarcoma), and melanomas, as evidenced by significant reduction in tumor volume and inhibition of metastasis in multiple human tumor xenograft models established in immunocompromised mice.

A key aspect of the invention is the discovery that SGP exhibits a remarkable discrepancy between its in vitro and in vivo activities: while SGP shows non-selective cytotoxicity against both malignant and non-malignant cells in culture, it demonstrates selective anti-tumor effects when administered locally to tumor-bearing animals, without causing detectable damage to surrounding normal tissues or distant organs. This selectivity is not mediated by molecular recognition of tumor-specific antigens but rather arises from the differential susceptibility of tumor cells versus normal cells to SGP-mediated membrane disruption in the context of their native microenvironments. Specifically, the invention reveals that extracellular matrix components, including matrigel and polymeric fibronectin, effectively shield normal cells from SGP activity by preventing the protein’s access to the plasma membrane, whereas tumor cells, which often exist in a matrix-deficient or disorganized stromal environment, remain vulnerable to SGP attack. This mechanism explains the absence of skin toxicity observed upon intradermal or subcutaneous injection of SGP in animal models, despite its potent membrane-disrupting properties in vitro.

The invention also encompasses structurally modified variants of SGP, including SGP-L and SGP-E, which serve as critical controls demonstrating the structure-function relationship underlying SGP’s activity. In SGP-L, the central alanine-rich helical domain is replaced with a leucine-rich sequence, while in SGP-E, positively charged lysine residues are substituted with negatively charged glutamic acid residues. Both variants exhibit substantially reduced membrane-disrupting activity and increased LC50 values (by at least ten-fold) across all tested cell types, confirming that the precise arrangement of hydrophobic and charged residues in the native SGP sequence is essential for its cytotoxic function. These findings establish that SGP’s activity is intrinsically linked to its three-dimensional conformation and electrostatic properties, rather than being a nonspecific detergent-like effect.

Pharmaceutical compositions comprising SGP formulated in physiologically compatible buffers for local or intratumoral administration are provided as part of the invention. Methods of treating cancer in a subject are disclosed, comprising administering a therapeutically effective amount of SGP directly to the tumor site, resulting in significant tumor regression, prevention of metastasis, and prolonged survival without systemic toxicity. The invention is particularly suited for the treatment of localized solid tumors that are difficult to resect surgically or resistant to conventional therapies, including Kaposi’s sarcoma, malignant melanoma, and unresectable carcinomas of the breast, prostate, and lung. The rapid onset of action, broad applicability across tumor types, and favorable safety profile distinguish SGP from existing anti-cancer modalities and position it as a valuable addition to the oncologist’s therapeutic arsenal.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### EXAMPLE 1

The synthetic protein SGP was prepared according to standard Fmoc (9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl) solid-phase peptide synthesis protocols using an automated Miligen Model 9050 peptide synthesizer. Synthesis was initiated from Fmoc-Leu-PEG (polyethylene glycol) resin, with real-time monitoring of Fmoc deprotection efficiency achieved through UV absorbance measurements at 214 nm. Following completion of the linear chain assembly, the crude peptide was cleaved from the resin using trifluoroacetic acid containing appropriate scavengers to prevent side reactions during deprotection. The resulting crude product was then purified by preparative reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Cosmosil ODS column (20 mm inner diameter × 250 mm length) with a linear gradient of water and acetonitrile, both containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid as ion-pairing agent. Fractions corresponding to the main peak were collected, lyophilized, and analyzed for purity and identity. Amino acid composition analysis was performed after acid hydrolysis of the purified peptide in 5.7 M hydrochloric acid under vacuum-sealed conditions at 110°C for 24 hours, followed by post-column ninhydrin detection. The observed amino acid ratios closely matched theoretical values based on the designed sequence: glycine (6.2 observed vs. 6 theoretical), alanine (9.5 vs. 10), leucine (26.5 vs. 25), aspartic acid (3.0 vs. 3), proline (2.9 vs. 3), tyrosine (3.1 vs. 3), and lysine (18.9 vs. 18). Molecular weight confirmation was obtained by fast atom bombardment mass spectrometry (FAB-MS) using a JEOL JMX-HX100 instrument, which detected a base peak at m/z 7555.1, in excellent agreement with the calculated monoisotopic mass of 7554.8 for the protonated molecular ion [C367H639O77N91 + H]+. Peptide concentration for biological assays was determined spectrophotometrically by measuring UV absorbance at 280 nm, taking into account the extinction coefficients of the single tryptophan residue and three tyrosine residues (ε = 8000 M−1cm−1). The final purified SGP preparation exhibited greater than 95% homogeneity as assessed by analytical HPLC and was stored lyophilized at −20°C until use, with reconstitution immediately prior to experimental procedures in appropriate physiological buffers.

### EXAMPLE 2

To evaluate the structural characteristics of SGP, a three-dimensional molecular model was generated using the Insight II molecular modeling software package (Molecular Simulations Inc.) running on a Silicon Graphics Octane SSE workstation. The modeling protocol employed a combination of homology modeling based on known helical bundle structures and energy minimization using the Discover force field with consistent valence parameters. The initial model was constructed by defining the secondary structure elements predicted from the amino acid sequence, particularly focusing on the central alanine-rich region expected to form an α-helix based on helical propensity scales. The model was then subjected to multiple rounds of energy minimization and molecular dynamics simulation in explicit solvent to refine the tertiary structure and optimize side chain packing. The final model revealed a compact globular structure with a prominent central helical domain flanked by flexible loops containing the charged residues (lysines and aspartic acids) that likely mediate initial electrostatic interactions with membrane surfaces. The surface electrostatic potential map showed a distinct segregation of positive and negative charges, creating an amphipathic character that would facilitate insertion into lipid bilayers. This computational model provided crucial insights into the structure-function relationship of SGP and guided the design of the inactive variants SGP-L and SGP-E, wherein specific structural perturbations were introduced to test hypotheses regarding the importance of helix hydrophobicity and net charge for membrane-disrupting activity.

### EXAMPLE 3

The in vitro cytotoxic activity of SGP was systematically evaluated across a panel of human cell lines representing diverse tumor types and normal cell counterparts. The Kaposi’s sarcoma-derived cell line KS1767, breast carcinoma line MDA-MB-435, prostate carcinoma line PC-3, lung carcinoma line H358, and dermal microvessel endothelial cells were maintained in standard culture conditions using Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics. Cell viability assays were performed using a dual-fluorescence staining method with acridine orange and ethidium bromide, which allows discrimination between live cells (green nuclei), early apoptotic cells (condensed green nuclei), and late apoptotic/necrotic cells (red nuclei due to compromised membrane integrity). For each experiment, 500 cells per condition were scored in a blinded manner by two independent observers. Treatment of KS1767 cells with SGP concentrations exceeding 10 micromolar resulted in immediate and complete loss of viability within 60 seconds, as confirmed by Trypan Blue exclusion assay, indicating rapid plasma membrane disruption. At concentrations between 5 and 10 micromolar, SGP induced classical necrotic morphology over a 60-minute period, characterized by cellular swelling and loss of membrane integrity without nuclear condensation. Concentrations below 5 micromolar triggered a slower apoptotic response over 24 hours, evidenced by nuclear margination, chromatin condensation, and plasma membrane blebbing, which was biochemically confirmed by caspase-3 activation assays. Control experiments with a scrambled peptide sequence at 100 micromolar showed no cytotoxic effects, while the structurally modified variants SGP-L (central alanine helix replaced with leucine) and SGP-E (lysines replaced with glutamic acids) exhibited dramatically reduced potency, with LC50 values increased by at least ten-fold across all tested cell lines. These results established that SGP’s membrane-disrupting activity is highly dependent on its specific amino acid sequence and three-dimensional conformation, rather than being a nonspecific physicochemical effect.

### EXAMPLE 4

The in vivo anti-tumor efficacy of SGP was evaluated in multiple human tumor xenograft models established in athymic nude mice. Female or male Balb/c nude mice (2 months old) were implanted subcutaneously with 10^6 tumor cells (MDA-MB-435 breast carcinoma, KS1767 Kaposi’s sarcoma, PC-3 prostate carcinoma, or H358 lung carcinoma) in 200 microliters of serum-free medium. Tumors were allowed to establish for 1-2 weeks until reaching volumes of 100-600 mm³ before initiation of treatment. SGP was administered via direct intratumoral injection at concentrations of 100 micromolar or 1 millimolar in a total volume of 40 microliters, delivered in 5-microliter increments to ensure even distribution throughout the tumor mass. Control animals received equivalent volumes of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Treatments were administered once weekly for four consecutive weeks, followed by an eight-week observation period without further intervention. Tumor dimensions were measured weekly using digital calipers under Avertin anesthesia, and volumes were calculated using the formula (length × width²)/2. In the MDA-MB-435 model, both SGP treatment groups showed statistically significant reduction in tumor growth compared to PBS controls, with final average tumor volumes approximately five-fold smaller in treated animals. Notably, no difference was observed between the 100 micromolar and 1 millimolar dose groups, suggesting a plateau effect at the lower concentration. Histopathological examination of excised tumors revealed extensive areas of cell death characterized by pyknotic nuclei and loss of cellular architecture in SGP-treated specimens, while the extracellular matrix remained intact. Similar anti-tumor effects were observed in all other xenograft models tested, demonstrating the broad applicability of SGP across different cancer histologies. Importantly, surgical exploration at study termination revealed complete absence of residual tumor tissue in many SGP-treated animals, suggesting potential for curative outcomes with appropriate dosing regimens.

### EXAMPLE 5

The safety profile of SGP was rigorously evaluated through comprehensive toxicological assessments in the same animal models used for efficacy studies. Following completion of the anti-tumor efficacy experiments, major organs (liver, kidney, heart, lungs, spleen, brain) were harvested from SGP-treated and control mice, fixed in Bouin’s solution, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for histopathological analysis. No evidence of overt pathology, inflammatory infiltration, or cellular damage was observed in any organ examined from SGP-treated animals, confirming the absence of systemic toxicity despite repeated intratumoral administrations. To specifically assess local tissue toxicity, a dedicated skin toxicity study was conducted in which 10 microliters of 100 micromolar SGP or PBS was injected intradermally into the dorsal skin of nude mice. The injection sites were monitored daily for two weeks for signs of inflammation, necrosis, ulceration, or other adverse reactions. Strikingly, no visible skin reactions were observed in any SGP-treated animal throughout the observation period, in stark contrast to the expected severe tissue damage that would result from a non-selective membrane-disrupting agent. This finding was particularly remarkable given SGP’s potent cytotoxic activity in vitro and provided the first indication of its unique in vivo selectivity. Additional safety assessments included monitoring of body weight, general behavior, and feeding patterns throughout the treatment period, all of which remained normal in SGP-treated animals compared to controls. These comprehensive safety data established that SGP can be administered locally at therapeutically effective concentrations without causing detectable harm to normal tissues, either locally or systemically.

### EXAMPLE 6

The mechanism underlying SGP’s selective anti-tumor activity in vivo was investigated through a series of in vitro experiments designed to mimic the protective effects of extracellular matrix components. KS1767 cells were cultured under standard conditions and then either left untreated or coated with a thin layer of matrigel (a basement membrane preparation rich in laminin, collagen IV, and entactin) or polymeric fibronectin (sFN), both of which represent key components of the normal tissue microenvironment. Following matrix coating and a brief incubation period to allow polymerization, cells were exposed to 1 millimolar SGP and monitored for viability over time using phase-contrast microscopy and the acridine orange/ethidium bromide staining method. In the absence of any matrix coating, SGP caused complete and rapid cell death within 10 minutes, consistent with its known membrane-disrupting properties. However, when cells were protected by either matrigel or sFN, they remained completely viable even after 24 hours of continuous SGP exposure, demonstrating that these extracellular matrix components effectively shield cells from SGP-mediated cytotoxicity. Control experiments with conventional cytotoxic agents confirmed the specificity of this protective effect: ethanol (10% v/v) and doxorubicin (20 micrograms per well) induced massive cell death regardless of matrix presence, as these agents can readily diffuse through the matrix barrier to reach their intracellular targets. Additional control experiments verified that fibronectin alone (non-polymerized) did not provide protection, confirming that the polymeric, three-dimensional structure of the matrix is essential for the shielding effect. These findings provided a mechanistic explanation for SGP’s in vivo selectivity: tumor cells, which often grow in a disorganized stroma with disrupted or absent basement membranes, remain accessible to SGP, while normal cells embedded in intact extracellular matrix are protected from its membrane-disrupting effects.

### EXAMPLE 7

The concentration-dependent modes of cell death induced by SGP were further characterized through detailed morphological and biochemical analyses. Time-lapse microscopy of KS1767 cells exposed to different SGP concentrations revealed distinct kinetic profiles and morphological endpoints. At concentrations above 10 micromolar, cells underwent immediate swelling and rupture within seconds, consistent with osmotic lysis following catastrophic membrane failure. Electron microscopy of these cells showed complete loss of plasma membrane continuity without preservation of organelle structure, confirming a non-apoptotic, non-necrotic mechanism that might be classified as “oncosis” or primary necrosis. At intermediate concentrations (5-10 micromolar), cells displayed classical necrotic features over 30-60 minutes, including progressive swelling, mitochondrial swelling, and eventual plasma membrane rupture, but without the nuclear condensation characteristic of apoptosis. Biochemical assays confirmed the absence of caspase activation and DNA laddering in these conditions. At low concentrations (1-5 micromolar), cells exhibited the hallmark morphological features of apoptosis over 12-24 hours, including cell shrinkage, membrane blebbing, nuclear fragmentation, and formation of apoptotic bodies. These morphological changes were accompanied by robust activation of caspase-3, as measured by fluorometric substrate cleavage assays, and characteristic DNA fragmentation patterns on agarose gels. The ability of SGP to trigger different cell death pathways depending on concentration represents a unique feature among membrane-disrupting agents and may have important implications for immune responses to tumor cell death, as apoptotic versus necrotic cell death can differentially influence anti-tumor immunity. Furthermore, the observation that 100 micromolar SGP induced apoptosis in vivo despite causing immediate lysis in vitro suggests that the effective concentration reaching tumor cells in the xenograft setting may be lower than the administered dose, possibly due to binding to extracellular components or rapid clearance from the tumor interstitium.

### EXAMPLE 8

The therapeutic potential of SGP was extended beyond primary tumor treatment to include evaluation of its effects on metastatic disease. In a separate cohort of mice bearing MDA-MB-435 xenografts, animals were monitored for up to four months post-tumor implantation following SGP treatment. At the conclusion of the observation period, thorough necropsies were performed to assess the presence of metastatic lesions in common sites of breast cancer dissemination, including lymph nodes, lungs, liver, and bone marrow. Remarkably, no metastatic deposits were detected in any SGP-treated animal, whereas control animals frequently developed macroscopic and microscopic metastases in multiple organs. This finding suggests that SGP treatment not only eradicates the primary tumor but also prevents the establishment of metastatic disease, possibly through elimination of circulating tumor cells or micrometastases that remain accessible to the drug during the treatment period. The long-term tumor-free survival observed in many SGP-treated animals (up to four months, which represents a significant portion of the mouse lifespan) further supports the potential for durable remissions with this therapeutic approach. Additionally, the invention contemplates combination therapy strategies wherein SGP is administered alongside conventional chemotherapeutic agents or immunotherapies to enhance overall anti-tumor efficacy. Preliminary experiments combining suboptimal doses of SGP with doxorubicin showed additive or synergistic effects in vitro, suggesting that the physical membrane disruption caused by SGP may enhance the intracellular delivery of co-administered drugs. The broad applicability of SGP across different tumor types, coupled with its unique mechanism of action and favorable safety profile, positions it as a promising candidate for clinical development, particularly for localized tumors that are difficult to treat with existing modalities or for palliative management of advanced disease where tumor debulking could provide symptomatic relief.
