# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular biology and epigenetic analysis, particularly to methods and compositions for the detection and quantification of DNA methylation status in nucleic acid samples. More specifically, the invention provides novel primer design strategies and associated amplification protocols that enable accurate and proportional amplification of both methylated and unmethylated DNA templates following sodium bisulfite conversion. The invention addresses a long-standing technical challenge in the art—namely, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) bias that systematically favors the amplification of unmethylated DNA over methylated DNA during methylation-independent PCR (MIP). This bias has historically compromised the reliability and quantitative accuracy of downstream methylation analyses, including sequencing, restriction enzyme digestion, melting curve analysis, and high-resolution melting (HRM). By incorporating a limited number of CpG dinucleotides into primer sequences and strategically optimizing annealing temperature, the present invention enables controlled modulation of primer-template binding stringency, thereby compensating for inherent amplification bias and achieving balanced representation of methylated and unmethylated alleles in the amplified product. The invention further encompasses kits, reagents, and analytical methods that implement this improved primer design and amplification strategy, as well as their use in the diagnosis, prognosis, and monitoring of diseases associated with aberrant DNA methylation, such as cancer, developmental disorders, and other epigenetic pathologies.

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

### Importance of DNA Methylation

DNA methylation is a fundamental epigenetic modification that plays a critical role in the regulation of gene expression, genomic imprinting, X-chromosome inactivation, and the maintenance of chromosomal stability. In mammals, DNA methylation occurs predominantly at the 5-position of cytosine residues within CpG dinucleotides, resulting in the formation of 5-methylcytosine (5mC). CpG dinucleotides are unevenly distributed throughout the genome, often clustered in regions known as CpG islands, which are frequently located in promoter regions of genes. Hypermethylation of CpG islands in promoter regions is generally associated with transcriptional silencing, whereas hypomethylation is linked to active gene expression. Aberrant DNA methylation patterns—both global hypomethylation and locus-specific hypermethylation—are hallmark features of numerous human diseases, particularly cancer. For example, hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters can lead to their inactivation, contributing to uncontrolled cell proliferation and tumorigenesis. Conversely, global loss of methylation can result in genomic instability and activation of oncogenes or transposable elements. Given its central role in normal development and disease pathogenesis, accurate and reliable assessment of DNA methylation status at specific genomic loci has become an essential tool in both basic research and clinical diagnostics. Over the past several decades, a variety of techniques have been developed to analyze DNA methylation, with the majority relying on sodium bisulfite conversion of genomic DNA as a foundational step. Sodium bisulfite treatment deaminates unmethylated cytosines to uracil, while leaving 5-methylcytosines intact. Subsequent PCR amplification and analysis then allow discrimination between originally methylated and unmethylated sequences based on sequence differences introduced by the conversion process. Despite the widespread adoption of bisulfite-based methods, a persistent and underappreciated problem has plagued their accuracy: PCR amplification bias, which systematically distorts the true ratio of methylated to unmethylated DNA in the sample. This bias arises from differential amplification efficiency between bisulfite-converted methylated and unmethylated templates, typically favoring the unmethylated form due to its higher AT content and lower melting temperature. As a result, even when primers are designed to be “methylation-independent,” the amplified product may not faithfully reflect the original methylation status of the sample, leading to false negatives or underestimation of methylation levels. This issue has significant implications for both research reproducibility and clinical decision-making, where accurate quantification of methylation is crucial. While early studies identified this phenomenon and proposed partial solutions—such as the use of the Stoffel fragment of Taq polymerase or careful primer design excluding all CpG sites—these approaches have proven insufficient to fully eliminate bias in many contexts. Moreover, the exclusion of CpG sites from primers severely limits primer design options, especially in CpG-rich regions that are common targets for methylation analysis. The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel primer design paradigm that deliberately incorporates a limited number of CpG dinucleotides into primer sequences, coupled with precise control of PCR annealing temperature, to actively counteract amplification bias and achieve proportional representation of methylated and unmethylated alleles.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for the accurate and proportional amplification of bisulfite-converted DNA templates that differ in their methylation status, thereby enabling reliable downstream analysis of DNA methylation. The core innovation lies in the strategic design of PCR primers that include a limited number of CpG dinucleotides—typically one, but up to two or three in certain contexts—positioned away from the 3' end of the primer. These primers, referred to herein as “bias-correcting methylation-independent primers,” are engineered to exhibit differential binding affinity for methylated versus unmethylated templates depending on the stringency of the PCR annealing conditions. At lower annealing temperatures, the primers bind with minimal discrimination, resulting in preferential amplification of unmethylated templates due to their lower melting temperature—a phenomenon consistent with conventional PCR bias. However, at higher annealing temperatures, the presence of CpG sites in the primer sequence confers stronger binding to methylated templates (which retain cytosines at CpG positions after bisulfite treatment), thereby reversing the bias and favoring amplification of methylated DNA. Critically, at an intermediate, empirically determined annealing temperature, the primer-induced bias toward methylated templates precisely offsets the inherent amplification bias toward unmethylated templates, resulting in proportional amplification of both species. This balance ensures that the relative abundance of methylated and unmethylated sequences in the PCR product accurately reflects their ratio in the original sample. The invention further includes methods for determining the optimal annealing temperature for a given primer pair through empirical testing using serial dilutions of fully methylated DNA spiked into unmethylated DNA, followed by quantitative analysis of the amplification products via melting curve analysis, sequencing, or other suitable techniques. Additionally, the invention encompasses kits comprising such primers, optimized PCR reagents, and instructions for use, as well as their application in the detection, diagnosis, and monitoring of diseases characterized by abnormal DNA methylation patterns. By overcoming the longstanding problem of PCR bias in methylation analysis, the present invention significantly enhances the sensitivity, specificity, and quantitative accuracy of epigenetic assays, making it particularly valuable for clinical applications where precise methylation quantification is essential.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “DNA methylation” refers to the covalent addition of a methyl group to the 5-carbon position of cytosine residues in DNA, primarily occurring at CpG dinucleotides in mammalian genomes. “Bisulfite-converted DNA” denotes genomic DNA that has been treated with sodium bisulfite, resulting in the deamination of unmethylated cytosines to uracil, while 5-methylcytosines remain unchanged; subsequent PCR amplification converts uracil to thymine, thereby creating sequence differences between originally methylated and unmethylated templates. A “methylation-independent primer” (MIP) is a PCR primer designed to amplify both methylated and unmethylated bisulfite-converted DNA templates without preference, traditionally achieved by excluding CpG dinucleotides from the primer sequence. In contrast, a “bias-correcting methylation-independent primer” as defined by the present invention is a primer that intentionally includes a limited number of CpG dinucleotides—typically one, but up to two or three—strategically positioned away from the 3' end, and is used in conjunction with optimized annealing temperatures to achieve proportional amplification of methylated and unmethylated alleles. The term “PCR bias” refers to the differential amplification efficiency between methylated and unmethylated bisulfite-converted DNA templates during PCR, which typically results in overrepresentation of unmethylated sequences in the final amplicon. “Proportional amplification” means that the ratio of methylated to unmethylated DNA in the PCR product is equivalent to the ratio in the original bisulfite-converted sample, within acceptable experimental error. “Annealing temperature” is the temperature at which PCR primers hybridize to the template DNA during the amplification cycle, and “optimal annealing temperature” for a given primer pair in the context of this invention is the temperature at which primer-induced bias toward methylated templates compensates for inherent amplification bias toward unmethylated templates, resulting in proportional amplification. “CpG dinucleotide” refers to a cytosine followed by a guanine in the DNA sequence, linked by a phosphate bond; in bisulfite-converted DNA, the cytosine in a CpG site remains as cytosine if originally methylated, or is converted to thymine if originally unmethylated. “Salt-adjusted melting temperature (Tm)” is the calculated temperature at which 50% of the primer-template duplexes are dissociated, adjusted for the ionic strength of the PCR buffer, and is used to guide the selection of appropriate annealing temperatures. The term “empirically determined” indicates that a parameter, such as optimal annealing temperature, is established through experimental validation rather than theoretical prediction alone.

### Samples

The methods of the present invention are applicable to a wide range of biological samples containing genomic DNA, including but not limited to whole blood, serum, plasma, saliva, buccal swabs, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, tissue biopsies (fresh, frozen, or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded), cell lines, circulating tumor cells, and cell-free DNA. Prior to analysis, genomic DNA is extracted from the sample using standard techniques known in the art, such as phenol-chloroform extraction, silica-based column purification, or magnetic bead-based methods. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA are typically assessed using spectrophotometry, fluorometry, or gel electrophoresis to ensure suitability for downstream processing. The DNA is then subjected to sodium bisulfite conversion using commercially available kits or established laboratory protocols, which involve denaturation of the DNA, sulfonation, hydrolytic deamination, and desulfonation steps. The efficiency of bisulfite conversion is critical and should exceed 99% to minimize artifacts; incomplete conversion can lead to false-positive signals for methylation. Following conversion, the DNA is purified to remove residual bisulfite and salts, and may be eluted in low-ionic-strength buffer or water. The bisulfite-converted DNA serves as the template for PCR amplification using the bias-correcting methylation-independent primers described herein. It is important to note that bisulfite treatment fragments and degrades DNA to some extent, so input DNA quantity and integrity must be considered when designing assays, particularly for clinical samples that may yield limited material. The invention is especially advantageous for analyzing samples with low levels of methylation, heterogeneous methylation patterns (e.g., in tumor biopsies containing mixed cell populations), or samples where accurate quantification of methylation percentage is required for diagnostic or prognostic purposes.

### Modification of DNA

The modification of DNA in the context of the present invention refers specifically to sodium bisulfite conversion, a chemical treatment that differentially modifies cytosine residues based on their methylation status. Unmethylated cytosines are deaminated to uracil, whereas 5-methylcytosines are resistant to deamination and remain as cytosine. This conversion creates sequence differences between originally methylated and unmethylated DNA strands, enabling their discrimination by PCR and downstream analytical methods. The bisulfite conversion protocol involves several key steps: first, genomic DNA is denatured into single strands, typically by alkaline treatment or heat. The single-stranded DNA is then incubated with sodium bisulfite under controlled pH and temperature conditions, allowing sulfonation of cytosine at the C6 position, followed by hydrolytic deamination to form sulfonated uracil. Finally, desulfonation under alkaline conditions yields uracil. After conversion, the DNA is purified to remove bisulfite reagents and concentrated if necessary. The resulting bisulfite-converted DNA contains uracils in place of unmethylated cytosines, while methylated cytosines remain as cytosines. During subsequent PCR amplification, uracil is recognized as thymine by DNA polymerases, so the amplified product will contain thymine at positions corresponding to originally unmethylated cytosines and cytosine at positions corresponding to originally methylated cytosines. This sequence divergence forms the basis for methylation analysis. It is essential that the bisulfite conversion is complete and uniform across the sample; incomplete conversion can lead to misinterpretation of methylation status. Quality control measures, such as amplification of non-CpG cytosine-containing regions (which should be fully converted to thymine in all templates), are recommended to verify conversion efficiency. The modified DNA is then used as template in PCR reactions employing the novel primers of the invention.

### Methylation-Independent Primer

The methylation-independent primers of the present invention represent a significant departure from conventional design principles. Traditionally, MIP primers were designed to exclude all CpG dinucleotides to ensure equal binding to both methylated and unmethylated bisulfite-converted templates. However, this constraint often renders primer design impractical in CpG-rich regions, which are precisely the loci of greatest interest in methylation studies. The present invention overcomes this limitation by incorporating a limited number of CpG sites—typically one per primer, though up to two or three may be tolerated depending on sequence context and position—into the primer sequence. Crucially, these CpG sites are positioned away from the 3' end of the primer, preferably in the middle or 5' region, to avoid conferring absolute specificity for methylated templates (as seen in methylation-specific PCR primers, where CpGs near the 3' end prevent extension on unmethylated templates). The inclusion of CpG sites introduces a controlled degree of sequence complementarity difference between the primer and methylated versus unmethylated templates: the primer matches the methylated template perfectly at CpG positions (C-G pairing), but contains a mismatch (C-T) when binding to the unmethylated template. This mismatch reduces binding stability to the unmethylated template, particularly at higher annealing temperatures. By tuning the annealing temperature, the degree of discrimination can be modulated—from minimal discrimination at low stringency (favoring unmethylated amplification due to lower Tm) to strong discrimination at high stringency (favoring methylated amplification)—with an intermediate temperature yielding balanced amplification. Primer length is typically 18–30 nucleotides, with a salt-adjusted Tm of approximately 65°C to facilitate PCR at 60°C or nearby temperatures, ensuring both specificity and compatibility with standard thermal cyclers. Additionally, it is desirable to include one or more thymines at or near the 3' end that correspond to non-CpG cytosines in the original sequence; since non-CpG cytosines are always unmethylated and thus converted to thymine, this feature ensures that amplification occurs only from properly bisulfite-converted DNA and not from unconverted genomic DNA, which could yield false positives. Secondary structure, self-dimerization, and cross-dimerization potential are minimized using standard bioinformatic tools to ensure robust amplification performance.

### Amplifying Step

The amplifying step of the present invention involves performing PCR on bisulfite-converted DNA using the bias-correcting methylation-independent primers under carefully optimized thermal cycling conditions. The PCR reaction mixture typically includes a thermostable DNA polymerase (such as Hot Start Taq), dNTPs, MgCl₂, buffer, and the primers at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 µM. The choice of polymerase is important; while standard Taq polymerase is commonly used, enzymes with proofreading activity are generally avoided due to their inability to efficiently amplify bisulfite-converted DNA, which contains uracil. The thermal cycling protocol begins with an initial denaturation step (e.g., 95°C for 10 minutes) to activate the polymerase and fully denature the template. This is followed by 35–50 cycles of denaturation (95°C for 15–30 seconds), annealing at the empirically determined optimal temperature (typically between 55°C and 65°C), and extension (72°C for 20–60 seconds, depending on amplicon length). The critical parameter is the annealing temperature, which must be optimized for each primer pair to achieve proportional amplification. Optimization is performed by running parallel PCRs across a gradient of annealing temperatures (e.g., 55°C to 65°C in 1°C increments) using a reference sample containing a known mixture of methylated and unmethylated DNA (e.g., 10% methylated in unmethylated background). The resulting amplicons are then analyzed by melting curve analysis, sequencing, or another quantitative method to determine the methylation ratio in the product. The annealing temperature that yields a product methylation ratio closest to the input ratio is selected as optimal. Once optimized, this temperature is used for all subsequent analyses with that primer pair. The amplification step produces sufficient quantities of the target region for downstream methylation analysis, with the key advantage that the amplicon composition accurately reflects the original sample’s methylation status, free from the distorting effects of PCR bias.

### Analysis of Amplified CpG-Containing Nucleic Acids

Following amplification, the CpG-containing nucleic acid amplicons are subjected to analytical methods that determine the methylation status of the interrogated region. The choice of analytical technique depends on the required resolution, throughput, and quantitative precision. Common methods compatible with the present invention include nucleic acid sequencing, restriction enzyme digestion, primer extension, melting curve analysis, and high-resolution melting (HRM). Sequencing provides single-base resolution of methylation status across the entire amplicon and is considered the gold standard for detailed methylation mapping. Restriction enzyme digestion exploits methylation-sensitive or methylation-dependent restriction enzymes that cleave only methylated or only unmethylated sequences at specific recognition sites within the amplicon; the presence or absence of cleavage products indicates methylation status. Primer extension assays, such as the SNaPshot method, use fluorescently labeled ddNTPs to extend a primer adjacent to a CpG site, with the incorporated base indicating whether the site was methylated (C) or unmethylated (T). Melting curve analysis and HRM take advantage of the fact that methylated sequences (richer in C-G pairs) have higher melting temperatures than unmethylated sequences (richer in T-A pairs); the shape and position of the melting curve provide information about the methylation level and heterogeneity within the sample. Because the amplification step of the present invention ensures proportional representation of methylated and unmethylated templates, all these downstream analyses yield results that accurately reflect the true biological methylation status of the original sample. This is particularly important for quantitative applications, such as determining the percentage of methylated alleles in a heterogeneous sample (e.g., tumor tissue), where conventional MIP methods might underestimate methylation due to amplification bias.

### Nucleic Acid Sequencing

Nucleic acid sequencing is a preferred method for comprehensive methylation analysis following amplification with the primers of the present invention. Both Sanger sequencing and next-generation sequencing (NGS) platforms can be employed. In Sanger sequencing, the PCR product is purified and sequenced using standard dye-terminator chemistry. The resulting chromatogram displays overlapping peaks at CpG sites if the sample is heterogeneously methylated, with the relative peak heights providing a semi-quantitative estimate of methylation percentage. For higher resolution and absolute quantification, cloning of the PCR product followed by sequencing of individual clones can be performed, though this is labor-intensive. NGS approaches, such as amplicon-based bisulfite sequencing, allow deep coverage of the target region, enabling precise quantification of methylation at each CpG site across thousands of individual DNA molecules. This is especially powerful for detecting low-level methylation or characterizing methylation heterogeneity in complex samples. Because the amplification step using the bias-correcting primers preserves the original methylated-to-unmethylated ratio, sequencing data generated from these amplicons provide an accurate representation of the methylation landscape in the source DNA. This stands in contrast to conventional MIP amplification, where underrepresentation of methylated templates can lead to false-negative calls or underestimation of methylation levels, particularly at low abundance. The fidelity of sequencing results is thus significantly enhanced by the present invention, making it suitable for applications requiring high confidence in methylation quantification, such as biomarker validation or minimal residual disease monitoring.

### Primer Extension

Primer extension assays offer a targeted, medium-throughput approach to methylation analysis at specific CpG sites within the amplified region. In this method, a primer is designed to anneal immediately upstream of the CpG site of interest. A DNA polymerase then extends the primer by a single nucleotide using fluorescently labeled dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs). If the CpG site was originally methylated, the bisulfite-converted template retains a cytosine, and a ddGTP (complementary to C) is incorporated. If the site was unmethylated, the template contains a thymine, and a ddATP (complementary to T) is incorporated. The identity of the incorporated ddNTP is detected by capillary electrophoresis or mass spectrometry, allowing determination of methylation status at that specific site. Multiplexing is possible by using primers of different lengths or labeled with distinct fluorophores. The accuracy of primer extension assays is highly dependent on the representativeness of the input amplicon; if PCR bias has skewed the methylated-to-unmethylated ratio, the extension results will be correspondingly inaccurate. By ensuring proportional amplification, the present invention provides amplicons that yield primer extension data reflective of the true biological state, enhancing the reliability of this technique for clinical and research applications.

### Restriction Enzyme Digestion

Restriction enzyme digestion leverages the differential sensitivity of certain restriction enzymes to DNA methylation. Methylation-sensitive enzymes (e.g., HpaII) cleave only unmethylated recognition sites, while methylation-dependent enzymes (e.g., GlaI) cleave only methylated sites. Following amplification with the bias-correcting primers, the PCR product is digested with the appropriate enzyme, and the resulting fragments are analyzed by gel electrophoresis or capillary electrophoresis. The presence of undigested product indicates methylation (for methylation-sensitive enzymes) or lack thereof (for methylation-dependent enzymes). Quantitative interpretation is possible by comparing band intensities, though this method is generally less precise than sequencing or HRM. The key advantage of using amplicons generated by the present invention is that the relative amounts of methylated and unmethylated templates are preserved, so the digestion pattern accurately reflects the original sample composition. This avoids the common pitfall of conventional MIP, where preferential amplification of unmethylated DNA can lead to complete digestion even in samples containing significant methylated alleles, resulting in false-negative conclusions.

## Disorders

The methods and kits of the present invention are particularly useful in the context of disorders characterized by aberrant DNA methylation patterns. Cancer is the most prominent example, where promoter hypermethylation of tumor suppressor genes (e.g., BRCA1, MLH1, CDKN2A/p16, MGMT) and global hypomethylation are well-established hallmarks. Accurate detection of these changes is critical for early diagnosis, risk stratification, treatment selection (e.g., MGMT methylation predicts response to temozolomide in glioblastoma), and monitoring of minimal residual disease or recurrence. Beyond oncology, the invention is applicable to imprinting disorders such as Prader-Willi syndrome and Angelman syndrome, which result from abnormal methylation at imprinted loci on chromosome 15. Neurological and psychiatric conditions, including Rett syndrome (caused by MECP2 mutations affecting methyl-CpG binding), autism spectrum disorders, and schizophrenia, have also been linked to epigenetic dysregulation. Additionally, aging-related diseases, autoimmune disorders, and cardiovascular conditions show associations with altered methylation profiles. The ability of the present invention to provide quantitative, bias-free methylation data makes it uniquely suited for developing and implementing methylation-based biomarkers in these diverse clinical contexts, where precise measurement is essential for meaningful interpretation and patient management.

## Kit

The present invention further provides a kit for performing methylation analysis of bisulfite-converted DNA. The kit comprises one or more pairs of bias-correcting methylation-independent primers designed according to the principles disclosed herein, along with optimized PCR reagents including a thermostable DNA polymerase, dNTPs, MgCl₂, and reaction buffer. The kit may also include positive and negative control DNA samples (e.g., fully methylated and fully unmethylated bisulfite-converted DNA), as well as detailed instructions for performing PCR amplification at the empirically determined optimal annealing temperature for each primer pair. Optional components may include reagents for downstream analysis, such as sequencing primers, restriction enzymes, or HRM-compatible dyes. The kit is designed to be user-friendly and compatible with standard laboratory equipment, enabling researchers and clinicians to implement high-fidelity methylation analysis without the need for extensive optimization. By providing pre-validated primers and protocols, the kit reduces the risk of PCR bias and ensures reliable, reproducible results across different laboratories and sample types.

## Use

The methods and kits of the present invention are used for the detection, quantification, and analysis of DNA methylation in biological samples. Specifically, they are employed in research settings to study epigenetic mechanisms in development, disease, and environmental responses. In clinical diagnostics, they are used to identify methylation biomarkers for early cancer detection, predict therapeutic response, monitor disease progression, and assess prognosis. The invention is also useful in pharmacodynamic studies to evaluate the effects of epigenetic therapies, such as DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (e.g., azacitidine, decitabine). Furthermore, the technology supports the development of liquid biopsy assays that detect tumor-derived methylated DNA in circulating cell-free DNA, enabling non-invasive cancer screening and monitoring. The core utility of the invention lies in its ability to overcome PCR bias, thereby ensuring that methylation measurements are accurate, quantitative, and biologically meaningful—critical attributes for both scientific discovery and clinical application.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, a primer pair was designed to amplify a 120-bp region of the MGMT promoter, a clinically relevant locus in glioblastoma. Conventional MIP design guidelines were initially followed, excluding all CpG sites, but no suitable primers could be identified due to the high CpG density. Using the present invention, a forward primer was designed containing one CpG dinucleotide at position 8 from the 5' end, and a reverse primer containing one CpG at position 10 from the 5' end. Both primers had a salt-adjusted Tm of 65°C and included a 3' thymine corresponding to a non-CpG cytosine. PCR was performed across an annealing temperature gradient (55–65°C) using a reference sample with 20% methylated DNA in unmethylated background. Melting curve analysis showed that at 58°C, the product melting profile matched that of the 20% methylated control, indicating proportional amplification. At 55°C, the curve shifted toward unmethylated, and at 62°C, toward methylated. The 58°C condition was selected for all subsequent analyses. When applied to clinical glioblastoma samples, the method detected MGMT methylation in 45% of cases, compared to 30% with conventional MIP, demonstrating improved sensitivity without increased false positives (confirmed by pyrosequencing).

### Example 2

A primer set targeting the BRCA1 promoter was developed using the invention. The forward primer contained two CpG sites at positions 6 and 12, and the reverse primer contained one CpG at position 9. Annealing temperature optimization using 10% methylated reference DNA identified 60°C as optimal. The assay was validated on 50 breast tumor samples and 20 normal controls. All normal samples showed <5% methylation, while 18 tumors showed >10% methylation (range 12–85%). Conventional MIP failed to detect methylation in 7 of these 18 tumors, confirming that PCR bias had masked low-level methylation. Sequencing of cloned amplicons confirmed the quantitative accuracy of the new method, with methylation percentages correlating strongly (R²=0.96) with input ratios in spike-in experiments.

### Example 3

To demonstrate applicability to non-cancer disorders, the invention was used to analyze the SNRPN imprinting control region in patients with suspected Prader-Willi syndrome. Primers were designed with one CpG each, positioned centrally. Optimal annealing temperature was 59°C. Analysis of 15 patient samples revealed abnormal methylation patterns in 12, consistent with clinical diagnosis. Conventional MIP showed ambiguous results in 5 cases due to biased amplification, highlighting the superior reliability of the new approach for imprinting disorder diagnostics.

### Example 4

A multiplex assay was developed for simultaneous analysis of three methylation markers (MGMT, RASSF1A, CDKN2A) in lung cancer. Each primer pair incorporated one CpG and was optimized individually. A common annealing temperature of 59°C was found to work for all three. Testing on 100 plasma samples from lung cancer patients and controls showed that the multiplex assay detected methylated DNA in 68% of stage I patients, compared to 45% with conventional MIP, demonstrating enhanced sensitivity for early detection in liquid biopsies.

### Example 5

The invention was applied to monitor epigenetic therapy response in myelodysplastic syndrome patients treated with azacitidine. Serial bone marrow samples were collected before and after treatment. Using bias-correcting primers for the CDKN2A locus, a significant decrease in methylation percentage was observed in responders (from 70% to 20% on average), while non-responders showed no change. Conventional MIP underestimated baseline methylation and failed to detect significant changes in 30% of responders, underscoring the importance of unbiased amplification for pharmacodynamic assessment.
