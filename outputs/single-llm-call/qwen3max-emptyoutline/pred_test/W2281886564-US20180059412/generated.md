# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of integrated nanophotonics and, more specifically, to methods and systems for high-precision, permanent, and scalable tuning of optical resonators. The invention provides a novel approach for selectively modifying the physical dimensions and, consequently, the optical resonance characteristics of photonic microcavities or nanocavities by means of resonant cavity-enhanced photoelectrochemical (PEC) etching. This technique enables fine control over the spectral position of whispering gallery modes (WGMs) supported by semiconductor-based optical resonators, such as gallium arsenide (GaAs) disk resonators, with sub-picometer precision and without degrading the quality factor (Q-factor) of the resonances. The method is applicable both in liquid and gaseous environments and is particularly suited for applications requiring collective alignment of multiple resonators along a common waveguide, such as in photonic integrated circuits, quantum photonics, optical sensing, and frequency comb generation. The invention further encompasses photonic devices configured to implement the disclosed tuning methodology, including specialized installation setups that facilitate in situ monitoring and real-time feedback during the tuning process. By leveraging the intrinsic optical absorption of high-Q resonators at their resonant wavelengths—even when pumped below the semiconductor bandgap—the invention achieves spatially localized etching that preferentially smooths surface irregularities, thereby improving optical performance while enabling deterministic spectral engineering.

## PRIOR ART

Prior to the present invention, various techniques have been developed to tune the resonance frequencies of optical microcavities and nanocavities, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Traditional post-fabrication tuning methods include thermal tuning, electro-optic tuning, mechanical stress tuning, and chemical or plasma-based etching. Thermal tuning exploits the thermo-optic effect, wherein changes in temperature alter the refractive index of the cavity material, thereby shifting its resonant wavelength. While simple to implement, thermal tuning is inherently transient—requiring continuous power to maintain the shift—and suffers from limited precision, slow response times, and potential thermal crosstalk in densely integrated photonic circuits. Electro-optic tuning, which relies on the application of an electric field to modulate the refractive index via the Pockels or Kerr effect, offers faster response but is restricted to materials exhibiting strong electro-optic coefficients, such as lithium niobate, and is generally ineffective in centrosymmetric semiconductors like GaAs or silicon. Mechanical stress tuning, achieved through piezoelectric actuators or MEMS structures, can induce permanent or reversible shifts but requires complex fabrication processes and introduces mechanical instability and hysteresis.

Chemical and plasma-based etching methods have also been explored for permanent resonance tuning. For instance, isotropic wet etching using hydrofluoric acid or other selective etchants can reduce cavity dimensions and blue-shift resonances. However, such approaches lack spatial selectivity—they affect all exposed surfaces uniformly—and offer poor control over the etch depth, often leading to excessive roughening of cavity sidewalls and consequent degradation of the Q-factor. Similarly, reactive ion etching (RIE) or inductively coupled plasma (ICP) etching, while offering better anisotropy, are typically performed ex situ and cannot be dynamically controlled during optical characterization. Moreover, these dry etching techniques are not compatible with real-time feedback, making it impossible to stop the etch precisely at a target resonance wavelength.

More recently, laser-assisted or photochemical tuning strategies have emerged. In one approach, ultraviolet (UV) laser irradiation is used to induce localized oxidation or structural modification in silica or silicon nitride resonators. While offering site-specificity, UV-based methods often require high photon energies that can generate defects, increase optical loss, and are incompatible with direct bandgap semiconductors like GaAs due to strong bulk absorption. Another strategy involves non-resonant photoelectrochemical etching, wherein a semiconductor immersed in an electrolyte is illuminated above its bandgap to generate electron-hole pairs that drive dissolution reactions. Although effective for macroscopic patterning, this method lacks the spatial resolution needed for individual nanocavities and cannot distinguish between resonant and non-resonant structures on the same chip.

A critical limitation of all prior art methods is their inability to simultaneously achieve high precision, permanence, spatial selectivity, and scalability across multiple resonators. Existing techniques either require individual addressing of each resonator—which becomes impractical beyond a few elements—or sacrifice precision for speed. Furthermore, none of the known methods intrinsically improve the optical quality of the resonator during tuning; most, in fact, degrade the Q-factor due to induced surface roughness or defect formation. The absence of a technique capable of collectively aligning an arbitrary number of resonators to a common wavelength with sub-picometer accuracy has thus remained a significant bottleneck in the development of large-scale photonic integrated circuits, especially those relying on coherent coupling between multiple high-Q cavities. The present invention addresses these long-standing challenges by introducing a resonant cavity-enhanced PEC etching mechanism that operates below the semiconductor bandgap, leverages the cavity’s own optical field for self-selective etching, and enables both single-resonator fine-tuning and collective spectral alignment with unprecedented control and fidelity.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and system for high-precision, permanent, and scalable tuning of optical resonators through resonant cavity-enhanced photoelectrochemical (PEC) etching. The invention exploits the fact that high-quality-factor (high-Q) optical resonators, even when fabricated from semiconductors with low intrinsic absorption at wavelengths below the bandgap, exhibit residual linear absorption at their resonant wavelengths due to surface states or defects. When such a resonator is immersed in or exposed to a fluid environment containing ionic species—such as water, aqueous solutions, or humid air—and illuminated at its resonant wavelength with light of sufficient intensity, the absorbed photons generate charge carriers that drive localized electrochemical dissolution of the resonator material. This process, termed resonant PEC etching, results in a controlled reduction of the resonator’s physical dimensions, thereby inducing a permanent blue shift in its optical resonance frequency. Crucially, because the etching rate is enhanced only when the incident light is resonant with a specific cavity mode, the process is inherently spatially selective: only resonators tuned to the laser wavelength undergo significant etching, while off-resonant neighboring structures remain unaffected.

The invention further discloses a method for fine-tuning a single optical resonator by repeatedly sweeping the wavelength of a tunable laser across the resonance, with each sweep cycle producing a quantized, sub-picometer shift in the resonant wavelength. This sweep-cycle tuning mode enables deterministic control over the final resonance position with a precision surpassing that of conventional post-fabrication techniques by more than two orders of magnitude. In alternative embodiments, continuous tracking of the drifting resonance during etching allows for even higher spectral accuracy, limited only by the stability and referencing of the laser source.

Moreover, the invention introduces a scalable collective tuning protocol for aligning multiple resonators to a common target wavelength using a single laser and a single wavelength sweep. By initiating etching at the longest-wavelength resonator and progressively sweeping the laser toward shorter wavelengths, the method causes resonators to sequentially merge into a spectrally aligned group, ultimately resulting in the simultaneous tuning of all resonators to the same optical frequency. This collective alignment does not require individual identification or addressing of each resonator and is robust against variations in their initial spectral positions, making it ideal for correcting fabrication-induced inhomogeneities in large photonic arrays.

The invention is applicable to a wide range of semiconductor materials—including GaAs, silicon, gallium nitride, and II–VI compounds—provided that a suitable ionic environment is employed to sustain the PEC reaction. It is also adaptable to various cavity geometries, such as disks, rings, photonic crystal cavities, and slot waveguides. The tuning process may be carried out in liquid media for rapid etching or in controlled gaseous atmospheres for ultra-high-precision adjustments. Importantly, the resonant PEC etching not only shifts the resonance but also improves the optical Q-factor by smoothing surface irregularities through localized material removal, thereby enhancing device performance. The invention thus provides a comprehensive solution for post-fabrication spectral engineering of photonic resonators, combining permanence, precision, selectivity, scalability, and performance enhancement in a single, unified methodology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### The Photonic Device

The photonic device according to the present invention comprises at least one optical resonator fabricated from a semiconductor material capable of supporting high-quality-factor (high-Q) optical modes, such as whispering gallery modes (WGMs), and configured to undergo resonant cavity-enhanced photoelectrochemical (PEC) etching when illuminated at its resonant wavelength in the presence of an ionic environment. In a preferred embodiment, the optical resonator is a disk-shaped microcavity made of gallium arsenide (GaAs), having a thickness of approximately 320 nanometers and a radius of about 1 micrometer. The disk resonator is supported by a pedestal composed of aluminum gallium arsenide (AlGaAs), specifically Al₀.₈Ga₀.₂As, which serves to isolate the resonator optically and mechanically from the underlying GaAs substrate. This pedestal structure is essential for minimizing substrate-induced optical losses and enabling efficient evanescent coupling to adjacent waveguides.

The device further includes at least one integrated optical waveguide, fabricated from the same GaAs layer as the resonator, which is positioned in close proximity—typically within a few hundred nanometers—to the perimeter of the disk resonator to facilitate evanescent optical coupling. The waveguide is designed with a tapered section that narrows to nanoscale transverse dimensions near the resonator, thereby enhancing the overlap between the guided mode and the WGM of the disk. This configuration allows light propagating through the waveguide to couple into the resonator when the wavelength matches a resonant mode, resulting in a characteristic dip in the transmitted optical spectrum. The entire structure is suspended above the substrate following a selective under-etching step that removes the AlGaAs layer beneath the resonator and waveguide using a dilute hydrofluoric acid solution, leaving the photonic elements freestanding and surrounded by air or fluid.

Due to the high refractive index of GaAs (approximately 3.5 in the near-infrared), the optical confinement within both the waveguide and the disk resonator remains robust even when the chip is immersed in a transparent liquid with a lower refractive index, such as water or isopropanol. This property is critical for the operation of the invention, as it enables the device to function in a liquid environment without significant degradation of optical performance. The presence of a microliter-scale droplet of liquid covering multiple resonators does not impede evanescent coupling, allowing in situ optical characterization and tuning to be performed directly during the PEC process. The photonic device is thus engineered to support high-Q WGMs with mode volumes less than 1 cubic micrometer, making it an ideal platform for cavity quantum electrodynamics, nonlinear optics, and precision sensing applications where spectral control is paramount.

### Installation Set-Up

The installation set-up for implementing the resonant PEC tuning method comprises an optical interrogation system integrated with a fluidic or atmospheric control chamber, enabling real-time monitoring and feedback during the etching process. The core of the set-up is a tunable continuous-wave laser source, preferably operating in the near-infrared range (e.g., 1250–1350 nm for GaAs resonators), whose output wavelength can be precisely swept or stepped with sub-picometer resolution. The laser light is coupled into the input port of the integrated GaAs waveguide via a lensed optical fiber or a grating coupler, depending on the chip design. The transmitted light is collected at the output port and directed to a high-resolution optical spectrum analyzer or a balanced photodetection system capable of resolving resonance linewidths corresponding to Q-factors exceeding 50,000.

The photonic chip is mounted on a temperature-stabilized stage within a sealed chamber that can be filled with a liquid electrolyte or a controlled gas mixture. In the liquid immersion mode, the chamber is designed to accommodate a small droplet or a continuous flow of the chosen ionic fluid—such as deionized water, ammonia solution, or isopropanol—covering the resonators of interest while maintaining optical access through transparent windows. A microfluidic inlet and outlet allow for the exchange of fluids without disturbing the optical alignment. In the gaseous mode, the chamber is equipped with humidity or vapor control systems, including mass flow controllers and saturators, to regulate the partial pressure of water vapor or other reactive gases around the chip.

The entire system is interfaced with a computer-controlled data acquisition and feedback loop. Software algorithms continuously acquire transmission spectra during laser wavelength sweeps and detect resonance shifts in real time. Based on predefined tuning protocols—such as sweep-cycle mode or continuous tracking—the system adjusts the laser parameters (wavelength, power, sweep rate) to achieve the desired spectral outcome. Optional components include a microscope objective for visual inspection, a CCD camera for recording real-time movies of the tuning process, and environmental sensors (e.g., hygrometers, pH meters) to monitor the ionic conditions. This integrated installation set-up ensures that the resonant PEC etching is performed under controlled, reproducible conditions with full in situ diagnostic capability, enabling both research-grade experimentation and potential industrial deployment.

### Method for Tuning an Optical Resonator (FIG. 7)

The method for tuning a single optical resonator according to the present invention involves a sequence of steps that leverage resonant cavity-enhanced photoelectrochemical etching to achieve precise, permanent, and performance-enhancing spectral shifts. Initially, the photonic device containing the target resonator is placed in an ionic environment, which may be a liquid such as water or a humid gaseous atmosphere. A tunable laser is then coupled into the integrated waveguide, and an initial transmission spectrum is acquired to identify the resonant wavelength of the target mode. Once the resonance is located, the laser wavelength is set to match this resonance, and optical power is injected into the cavity at a level sufficient to initiate PEC etching—typically in the range of 1 microwatt to 100 microwatts, depending on the desired etch rate and environmental conditions.

The key innovation lies in the use of a sweep-cycle tuning protocol: instead of maintaining a fixed wavelength, the laser is rapidly swept back and forth across the resonance over a narrow spectral window (e.g., ±50 pm). Each sweep serves a dual purpose: it acquires a high-resolution transmission spectrum for real-time monitoring, and it delivers a quantized dose of resonant optical energy that drives a discrete etching event. Due to the resonant enhancement, the absorption—and hence the etching rate—is maximized precisely at the cavity resonance, causing a small but consistent reduction in the resonator’s radius with each cycle. Experimental data show that each sweep cycle produces a blue shift of approximately 7.2 picometers in the resonant wavelength for a GaAs disk resonator, corresponding to a radial change of about 8 picometers—well below the interatomic spacing in GaAs (approximately 280 pm per monolayer).

This process is repeated iteratively, with the resonance wavelength being tracked and recorded after each cycle. The cumulative shift is linear with the number of cycles, enabling predictive control over the final spectral position. Once the target wavelength is reached, the laser is switched off, and the sample is removed from the ionic environment and dried. Post-tuning characterization confirms that the resonance has been permanently shifted, with no relaxation over time, and that the loaded Q-factor has increased—evidencing a reduction in optical scattering losses due to surface smoothing during etching. This method provides a level of tuning precision unattainable by conventional techniques, as it avoids the stochastic nature of bulk etching and instead exploits the deterministic, mode-selective nature of cavity-enhanced absorption.

### Method for Tuning Several Resonators at a Targeted Value (FIG. 8)

The method for collectively tuning multiple optical resonators to a common targeted wavelength represents a significant advancement in scalability and efficiency. Unlike prior approaches that require individual addressing, this method exploits the inherent spectral ordering of resonators and the self-selective nature of resonant PEC etching to align an ensemble using a single laser and a single wavelength sweep. The procedure begins by immersing a photonic chip containing N nominally identical but spectrally dispersed resonators—due to nanofabrication variations—in an ionic fluid. An initial transmission spectrum reveals N distinct resonance groups, ordered by increasing wavelength λ₁ < λ₂ < … < λₙ, corresponding to decreasing resonator radii.

The laser is first tuned to the longest-wavelength resonance, λₙ, associated with the largest resonator (Resonator N). Resonant PEC etching commences exclusively in this resonator, causing its radius to shrink and its resonance to blue-shift. As the resonance drifts, the laser wavelength is slowly and continuously swept toward shorter wavelengths to maintain resonance condition, thereby sustaining the etching process. When the drifting resonance of Resonator N approaches and merges with that of Resonator N−1, both resonators now share the same resonant wavelength and are simultaneously etched by the same laser field. Their combined resonance continues to blue-shift as a unified entity.

This cascading alignment continues sequentially: once the merged resonance of Resonators N and N−1 reaches λₙ₋₂, Resonator N−2 joins the group, and so on, until all N resonators are spectrally overlapped at a final common wavelength. Critically, this process does not require knowledge of the physical location or identity of each resonator along the waveguide; the spectral dynamics alone dictate the alignment sequence. The final wavelength can be predetermined by selecting the sweep endpoint, or it can be dynamically adjusted based on real-time spectral feedback. Experimental demonstrations with five GaAs disk resonators confirm complete spectral alignment within minutes, with all resonances overlapping to within the measurement resolution (<10 pm). This collective tuning method dramatically reduces the complexity and time required for calibrating large photonic arrays, making it indispensable for applications such as multi-channel filters, quantum networks, and optical neural networks.

### Different Embodiments for Varying the Laser Wavelength in Order to Tune Optical Resonators

The invention encompasses multiple embodiments for varying the laser wavelength during the resonant PEC tuning process, each optimized for different trade-offs between speed, precision, and operational simplicity. In a first embodiment, the sweep-cycle mode employs discrete, rapid back-and-forth scans across the resonance, as previously described. This mode is ideal for applications requiring moderate precision (sub-10 pm) and fast turnaround, as each cycle takes only seconds and the total tuning time scales linearly with the desired shift. The quantized nature of the shift per cycle allows for open-loop control, eliminating the need for continuous feedback.

In a second embodiment, continuous tracking mode is employed, wherein the laser wavelength is dynamically adjusted in real time to follow the instantaneous resonance position as it blue-shifts during etching. This is achieved using a feedback loop that analyzes the transmission spectrum at high speed and commands a piezoelectric or electro-optic wavelength tuner to maintain resonance lock. Because the etching proceeds quasi-statically under constant resonant excitation, the process achieves the highest possible spectral accuracy—limited only by the laser’s absolute wavelength stability and referencing. In humid air, where etching rates are extremely slow (six orders of magnitude slower than in water), this mode has demonstrated tuning precision beyond 1 pm, enabling metrology-grade spectral engineering.

A third embodiment utilizes a single, slow, unidirectional wavelength sweep from the red-detuned side of the entire resonance ensemble to a target blue wavelength. This approach is particularly effective for collective tuning of multiple resonators, as it automatically triggers the cascading alignment mechanism without any intermediate intervention. The sweep rate is chosen to be slow enough to allow complete merging at each stage but fast enough to minimize non-selective background etching. Simulations and experiments confirm that this “one-shot” alignment is robust and reproducible.

Finally, a hybrid mode combines elements of the above: an initial coarse sweep brings resonators into approximate alignment, followed by fine sweep cycles or continuous tracking for final precision adjustment. These embodiments provide flexibility for diverse application scenarios, from rapid prototyping to ultra-high-precision manufacturing, all within the same fundamental framework of resonant cavity-enhanced PEC etching.

### Parameters and Experimental Data

Experimental implementation of the invention has yielded quantitative data that validate its performance across multiple parameters. For GaAs disk resonators (320 nm thick, ~1 μm radius) in water, the baseline loaded Q-factor before tuning is approximately 41,000, with resonance linewidths of 32 pm at 1300 nm. After resonant PEC tuning, the Q-factor increases to 52,000–70,000, with linewidths narrowing to 19–25 pm, indicating reduced scattering losses. The tuning precision in sweep-cycle mode is 7.2 pm per cycle, corresponding to a radial change of 8 pm per cycle, or less than 1/30th of a GaAs monolayer. At 1 μW of coupled optical power, the etching rate is 0.5–1 nm of radius reduction per second, scaling linearly with power.

In collective tuning experiments with five resonators initially spanning a 4.5 nm spectral range, complete alignment was achieved in five sequential steps or via a single continuous sweep, with final resonance overlap better than 10 pm. Non-selective background etching in water contributes a slow additional blue shift (~0.1 nm over 10 minutes), which can be calibrated out or minimized by using purer electrolytes. In humid air (relative humidity ~50%), the etching rate drops to ~1 fm/s, enabling 10-hour tuning sessions that achieve sub-picometer precision. Control experiments confirm that off-resonant resonators experience negligible etching (<0.1 pm shift under identical illumination), demonstrating exceptional spatial selectivity.

Material compatibility tests show that resonant PEC etching also occurs in ammonia and isopropanol, suggesting broad applicability across ionic liquids. The method has been successfully adapted to different WGM orders and polarization states, confirming mode generality. These parameters establish the invention as a robust, quantifiable, and highly controllable tuning platform.

### Characteristics of the Tuned Optical Resonator

The optical resonators produced by the present invention exhibit a unique combination of spectral, structural, and performance characteristics that distinguish them from those tuned by conventional methods. Spectrally, the resonances are permanently shifted to a user-defined wavelength with sub-picometer accuracy and exhibit no temporal drift after removal from the ionic environment. The resonance lineshape remains Lorentzian with high contrast, indicating minimal introduction of new loss channels. Structurally, the etching is confined to the evanescent field region of the WGM—typically the outer 100–200 nm of the disk perimeter—resulting in localized smoothing of surface roughness without altering the central portion of the resonator. This surface refinement is directly responsible for the observed increase in Q-factor, as confirmed by atomic force microscopy showing reduced RMS roughness post-tuning.

Performance-wise, the tuned resonators demonstrate enhanced light-matter interaction strength due to the combined effect of higher Q and preserved small mode volume. The method also preserves the coupling condition to the bus waveguide, as evidenced by unchanged extinction ratios. Critically, the tuning is irreversible and stable under ambient conditions, making the devices suitable for long-term deployment. Furthermore, because the process is self-limiting—etching ceases immediately when the laser is detuned or turned off—the risk of over-etching is eliminated. These characteristics collectively make the tuned resonators ideal for applications demanding spectral purity, long coherence times, and high reproducibility, such as in quantum information processing, narrow-linewidth lasers, and high-sensitivity biosensors.
