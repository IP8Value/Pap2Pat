## BACKGROUND

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates to the field of neurotherapeutics, specifically to the treatment of acute spinal cord injury (SCI) through the targeted inhibition of pathological hemichannel activity. More particularly, the invention provides a novel monoclonal antibody that selectively binds to the extracellular domain of connexin 43 (Cx43) and inhibits the opening of Cx43 hemichannels without interfering with the function of Cx43 gap junctions. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising said antibody, methods of using the antibody to treat acute SCI, and diagnostic kits for assessing hemichannel activity in the context of central nervous system trauma. The therapeutic strategy disclosed herein is designed to mitigate secondary injury processes following SCI, thereby preserving neural tissue, reducing glial scar formation, and improving functional neurological outcomes.

### 2. Description of Related Art

Spinal cord injury is a devastating neurological condition that results in significant morbidity and long-term disability. The primary mechanical insult to the spinal cord initiates a cascade of secondary pathological events that expand the initial lesion and exacerbate functional deficits. This secondary injury phase involves a complex interplay of vascular disruption, ionic imbalance, excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and robust neuroinflammation. A key feature of this process is the dysregulation of astrocyte function, which normally serves to maintain homeostasis in the central nervous system (CNS). Following SCI, astrocytes become reactive, contributing to the formation of a glial scar that acts as a physical and chemical barrier to axonal regeneration. Moreover, reactive astrocytes release pro-inflammatory cytokines and other cytotoxic molecules that perpetuate neuronal damage and demyelination.

Connexin 43 (Cx43) is the predominant gap junction protein expressed in astrocytes throughout the CNS, including the spinal cord. Cx43 forms two distinct types of channels: gap junctions, which directly connect the cytoplasm of adjacent cells to allow for the exchange of ions and small metabolites, and hemichannels, which are unapposed halves of gap junctions that open to the extracellular space under specific pathological conditions. Under normal physiological conditions, Cx43 hemichannels are largely closed, maintaining cellular integrity. However, in response to various stressors associated with CNS injury—such as mechanical trauma, ischemia, elevated extracellular potassium, and inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-1β (IL-1β)—Cx43 hemichannels can be pathologically activated. The opening of these hemichannels permits the uncontrolled efflux of vital intracellular components, including ATP and glutathione, and the influx of toxic extracellular substances, such as calcium and reactive oxygen species. This aberrant channel activity contributes significantly to the cytotoxic milieu that characterizes the secondary injury phase of SCI.

Previous research has implicated Cx43 in the pathogenesis of SCI. Studies using astrocyte-specific Cx43 knockout mice have demonstrated reduced lesion size, attenuated inflammation, and improved functional recovery following SCI, strongly suggesting a detrimental role for Cx43 in the injury response. However, these genetic models do not distinguish between the contributions of gap junctions and hemichannels, as both channel types are eliminated. This is a critical limitation because gap junctions are essential for normal astrocyte network communication and metabolic support, and their chronic inhibition could be deleterious. Consequently, there has been a long-standing need in the art for a therapeutic agent that can selectively target the pathological hemichannel function of Cx43 while sparing its beneficial gap junction activity.

Several non-selective pharmacological agents have been explored for this purpose. For example, carbenoxolone (CBX) is a well-known broad-spectrum inhibitor of both connexin and pannexin channels. While CBX has shown some efficacy in reducing secondary damage in animal models of SCI, its lack of specificity and potential off-target effects limit its clinical utility. Similarly, Cx43 mimetic peptides, such as Peptide5, have been developed to block Cx43 channels. These peptides, however, also inhibit both hemichannels and gap junctions, and their short half-life and poor blood-brain barrier penetration pose significant challenges for therapeutic development.

The development of a highly specific, biologic agent that can discriminate between the two conformational states of Cx43—the hemichannel on the free cell surface versus the gap junction at the tightly apposed intercellular junction—has remained an elusive goal. Antibodies, due to their high specificity and affinity, represent a promising class of therapeutics for this application. However, generating an antibody that can selectively bind to and inhibit the hemichannel conformation without affecting the gap junction has proven difficult, primarily due to the structural similarity of the extracellular domains in both channel types and the inaccessibility of the gap junction interface to large molecules.

The present invention addresses this critical unmet need by providing a novel monoclonal antibody, designated MHC1, that was specifically engineered to achieve this selectivity. As demonstrated herein, MHC1 binds with high affinity to the extracellular domain of Cx43 and potently inhibits the opening of Cx43 hemichannels in response to a variety of pathological stimuli relevant to SCI, including low extracellular divalent cations, IL-1β, and mechanical stress. Crucially, MHC1 does not impair the function of Cx43 gap junctions, as confirmed by scrape-loading dye transfer assays in primary astrocyte cultures. This unprecedented selectivity allows for the targeted blockade of a key driver of secondary injury while preserving the essential homeostatic functions of astrocytic networks. The invention thus provides a powerful new tool for the acute treatment of SCI, with the potential to significantly improve patient outcomes by limiting the expansion of the initial lesion and preserving neurological function.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel monoclonal antibody, designated MHC1, that specifically targets and inhibits the opening of connexin 43 (Cx43) hemichannels without affecting the function of Cx43 gap junctions. In one embodiment, the invention is a monoclonal antibody or an antigen-binding fragment thereof that binds to the extracellular domain of human or murine Cx43 with a dissociation constant (KD) of less than 1 micromolar, and wherein the antibody inhibits the opening of Cx43 hemichannels by at least 50% as measured by an ethidium bromide (EtBr) uptake assay in HeLa cells stably expressing Cx43, but does not inhibit Cx43 gap junction intercellular communication by more than 20% as measured by a scrape-loading dye transfer assay in primary astrocyte cultures. The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising the MHC1 antibody or an antigen-binding fragment thereof in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, which is useful for the treatment of acute spinal cord injury. A method of treating a subject suffering from an acute spinal cord injury is also provided, which comprises administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of the MHC1 antibody or a pharmaceutical composition thereof within 24 hours of the injury, preferably within 30 minutes to 2 hours post-injury. The invention additionally encompasses a kit for the diagnosis or prognosis of spinal cord injury, comprising the MHC1 antibody and instructions for its use in detecting Cx43 hemichannel activity in a biological sample from a subject. The MHC1 antibody of the invention is a mouse-human chimeric antibody, comprising the variable regions of a murine monoclonal antibody fused to the constant regions of a human IgG1 isotype. The specific amino acid sequences of the heavy and light chain variable regions are provided in the accompanying Sequence Listing. The therapeutic administration of the MHC1 antibody has been shown to significantly reduce secondary injury, decrease lesion size, attenuate reactive astrogliosis and glial scar formation, preserve white and gray matter, protect neurons from death, and improve locomotor function in preclinical models of partial spinal cord injury. The invention represents a significant advance in the field of neurotrauma therapeutics by offering a highly selective mechanism to interrupt a key pathological pathway in secondary injury without compromising the essential homeostatic functions of astrocytic gap junctions.

## DESCRIPTION

### I. ANTIBODIES

The present invention is centered on a novel monoclonal antibody, designated MHC1, that exhibits a unique and highly desirable pharmacological profile: it is a potent and selective inhibitor of connexin 43 (Cx43) hemichannels while being completely inert with respect to Cx43 gap junction function. This selectivity is a critical feature that distinguishes the MHC1 antibody from all previously known Cx43 inhibitors, which are invariably non-selective and block both channel types. The MHC1 antibody is a mouse-human chimeric immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) molecule, constructed by fusing the variable heavy (VH) and variable light (VL) chain domains derived from a murine hybridoma with the constant regions of a human IgG1 heavy chain and a human kappa light chain. This chimeric design confers the high specificity of a murine monoclonal antibody with the favorable pharmacokinetic and reduced immunogenicity profile of a humanized therapeutic antibody.

The MHC1 antibody was generated through a classical hybridoma technology approach. Mice were immunized with a synthetic peptide corresponding to a specific extracellular domain of the murine Cx43 protein. Following immunization, splenocytes were harvested and fused with myeloma cells to generate a library of hybridoma clones. These clones were then screened for their ability to produce antibodies that bound to the Cx43 peptide and, more importantly, for their functional ability to inhibit Cx43 hemichannel activity in a cell-based assay. The lead clone, designated HC1, was selected based on its superior functional profile. The genes encoding the VH and VL domains of the HC1 antibody were then cloned and sequenced. These murine variable region sequences were subsequently grafted onto vectors containing the human IgG1 constant region genes to create the final chimeric MHC1 antibody, which was produced in human embryonic kidney (HEK293F) cells via transient transfection and purified using standard protein A affinity chromatography.

The binding specificity of the MHC1 antibody has been rigorously characterized. It demonstrates high-affinity binding to the extracellular domain of Cx43, as confirmed by multiple orthogonal techniques. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using a biotinylated Cx43 extracellular domain peptide revealed an EC50 of approximately 76.3 picomolar, indicating a very tight interaction at equilibrium. Bio-layer interferometry (BLI) using an Octet RED96 system, which measures the real-time kinetics of the binding interaction, determined a dissociation constant (KD) of approximately 421 nanomolar. This apparent discrepancy between the two methods is common and can be attributed to the different experimental conditions and what each assay measures; ELISA typically reflects a steady-state equilibrium binding, while BLI provides a dynamic measurement of the association (kon) and dissociation (koff) rates. Regardless, both assays confirm that MHC1 binds to its target with high affinity. The specificity of MHC1 for Cx43 was further validated by immunocytochemistry, where it robustly labeled HeLa cells that had been stably transfected to express Cx43 (HeLa-Cx43), but showed no signal in parental HeLa cells that do not express the protein. The labeling was observed specifically at the cell borders, consistent with the known localization of Cx43. Importantly, MHC1 was also shown to bind to Cx43 on the surface of live, suspended MLO-Y4 cells, confirming its ability to recognize the native, cell-surface conformation of the protein.

The functional selectivity of the MHC1 antibody is its defining characteristic. Its ability to inhibit Cx43 hemichannels was demonstrated in a series of EtBr uptake assays. In HeLa-Cx43 cells, depletion of extracellular calcium and magnesium—a well-established trigger for hemichannel opening—led to a significant increase in EtBr uptake. This uptake was almost completely blocked by pre-incubation with MHC1, but not by a control IgG antibody. This inhibitory effect was also observed under more physiologically relevant conditions, such as in the presence of normal levels of divalent cations, and in response to the inflammatory cytokine IL-1β, which is a key mediator released after SCI. Furthermore, MHC1 was able to inhibit hemichannel opening induced by direct mechanical stress in primary spinal cord astrocytes, a model that closely mimics the physical trauma of SCI. To confirm that this inhibition was specific to Cx43 and not a general effect on other large-pore channels, the effect of MHC1 on pannexin 1 channels was tested. In MDA-MB231 cells, which express pannexin 1 but not Cx43, MHC1 had no effect on ATP-induced pannexin channel opening, whereas known pannexin inhibitors (probenecid) and non-selective blockers (carbenoxolone) were effective. This confirms that MHC1’s action is specific to the Cx43 protein.

The most critical aspect of the MHC1 antibody’s profile is its lack of effect on gap junctions. This was conclusively demonstrated using the scrape-loading dye transfer assay in primary cultures of rat cortical astrocytes. In this assay, a fluorescent, gap junction-permeable dye (Lucifer yellow) is introduced into a line of cells by scraping, and its spread to neighboring, non-scraped cells is a direct measure of functional gap junction coupling. Treatment with MHC1, even for an extended period of 24 hours, did not reduce the spread of the dye, indicating that gap junction communication remained intact. In stark contrast, the positive control, carbenoxolone, a known gap junction blocker, completely abolished dye transfer. This experiment provides definitive proof that the MHC1 antibody can discriminate between the hemichannel and gap junction conformations of Cx43, a feat that has not been achieved with any other known therapeutic agent. This selectivity is believed to be due to the structural and spatial differences between the two channel types. Hemichannels are located on the free, non-junctional surface of the cell, making them fully accessible to a large molecule like an antibody. Gap junctions, however, are formed by the tight docking of two hemichannels from adjacent cells, creating a narrow, restricted extracellular space that is likely inaccessible to the bulky MHC1 antibody. Therefore, the antibody can bind to and stabilize the hemichannel in a closed state without being able to reach or interfere with the gap junction.

In summary, the MHC1 antibody is a novel, high-affinity, and exquisitely selective inhibitor of Cx43 hemichannels. Its chimeric nature makes it suitable for therapeutic development, and its unique functional profile—potent hemichannel blockade with no gap junction inhibition—makes it an ideal candidate for the treatment of conditions like acute spinal cord injury where pathological hemichannel activity is a key driver of secondary damage.

### II. TREATMENT OF DISEASES

The primary therapeutic application of the MHC1 antibody and its pharmaceutical compositions is the treatment of acute spinal cord injury (SCI). The invention provides a method for treating a subject who has suffered an acute SCI, which comprises administering a therapeutically effective amount of the MHC1 antibody to the subject. The term "acute SCI" as used herein refers to the initial phase of injury, typically within the first 24 hours post-trauma, during which the secondary injury cascade is actively expanding the initial lesion. The administration of the MHC1 antibody is intended to be performed as early as possible within this window to maximize its therapeutic benefit. The research data presented herein strongly indicate that the optimal time for administration is within the first 30 minutes to 2 hours after the injury occurs. This is because the pathological opening of Cx43 hemichannels is an early event in the secondary injury cascade, triggered by the immediate release of inflammatory mediators like IL-1β and the mechanical stress of the trauma itself. By blocking this early event, the MHC1 antibody can prevent the subsequent release of cytotoxic molecules (e.g., ATP, glutamate) and the influx of damaging ions, thereby halting the vicious cycle of inflammation and cell death before it becomes widespread.

The therapeutic efficacy of the MHC1 antibody has been extensively validated in multiple, well-established preclinical mouse models of SCI. Two primary models of partial (incomplete) SCI were used, which are highly relevant to the human condition as the majority of SCI patients suffer from incomplete injuries. In both models, a single intraperitoneal injection of MHC1 (25 mg/kg) administered 30 minutes after the injury resulted in a profound and sustained improvement in functional recovery. This was measured using the Basso Mouse Scale (BMS), a standardized and widely accepted locomotor rating scale. MHC1-treated mice showed significantly greater improvement in hind limb function compared to control groups treated with either saline or a non-specific IgG antibody. This functional improvement was not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful, with MHC1-treated animals achieving much higher BMS scores over the entire 8-week observation period. Importantly, the control IgG group showed no detrimental effects, confirming that the observed benefits are due to the specific action of MHC1 and not a general effect of immunotherapy.

The functional improvements observed in MHC1-treated animals are directly correlated with significant neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects at the tissue level. Histopathological analysis of the spinal cords of treated animals revealed a dramatic reduction in the overall lesion size. This was quantified using Eriochrome cyanine R (ECR) staining to differentiate spared white and gray matter from the lesion core. MHC1 treatment led to a significant preservation of both white matter, which contains the long-tract axons essential for motor and sensory function, and gray matter, which houses the neuronal cell bodies. This tissue preservation is a direct consequence of the antibody's ability to protect neurons from death. Immunohistochemical staining for the neuronal marker microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) showed that MHC1 treatment significantly preserved MAP2-positive neurons in the perilesional area, both at 14 and 56 days post-injury, compared to control groups. This indicates that the antibody's early intervention has a long-lasting protective effect on neuronal survival.

Furthermore, the MHC1 antibody was shown to significantly attenuate reactive astrogliosis and glial scar formation, a major impediment to recovery after SCI. Staining for the astrocyte activation marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) revealed markedly lower levels of GFAP expression in the perilesional areas of MHC1-treated mice across all three SCI models tested, including a model of complete SCI. The reduction in gliosis is likely a downstream consequence of the reduced secondary injury and neuronal death, as fewer damaged cells mean fewer signals to activate astrocytes. However, it is also possible that by blocking the release of pro-inflammatory factors from astrocytes via their own hemichannels, MHC1 directly dampens the astrocyte activation loop. This dual action—neuroprotection and anti-gliosis—creates a more permissive environment for potential recovery and plasticity.

It is crucial to note that the therapeutic window for MHC1 is limited to the acute phase. When the antibody was administered 30 days after the injury, a time point when the blood-spinal cord barrier has resealed and the secondary injury cascade has largely subsided, no functional improvement was observed, despite the fact that the antibody was able to reach the injury site via intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection. This finding underscores that the MHC1 antibody works by preventing the *expansion* of the initial injury, not by promoting regeneration or repair in the chronic phase. Its mechanism is prophylactic against secondary damage, not restorative. Therefore, the method of the invention is specifically directed to the acute treatment of SCI, and its success is contingent upon timely administration.

While the primary focus is on SCI, the underlying mechanism of action of the MHC1 antibody—selective inhibition of pathological Cx43 hemichannel activity—suggests that it may be beneficial in the treatment of other acute neurological conditions characterized by similar secondary injury cascades. These could include traumatic brain injury (TBI), ischemic stroke, and certain neuroinflammatory disorders. In all these conditions, the dysregulation of astrocyte function and the pathological opening of Cx43 hemichannels are believed to contribute to neuronal damage and poor outcomes. The MHC1 antibody, by virtue of its unique selectivity, offers a powerful new tool to test this hypothesis and potentially develop new therapies for these devastating conditions.

### III. KITS AND DIAGNOSTICS

The present invention also provides diagnostic and prognostic kits that utilize the MHC1 antibody to assess the status of Cx43 hemichannel activity in a subject, particularly in the context of spinal cord injury or other neurological traumas. These kits are designed to aid clinicians and researchers in evaluating the severity of an injury, monitoring the progression of the secondary injury cascade, and potentially predicting the response to therapy. The core component of the kit is the MHC1 antibody, which can be provided in a lyophilized or liquid form, along with a set of necessary reagents and detailed instructions for use.

One type of kit is designed for in vivo or ex vivo detection of Cx43 hemichannel activity. This kit would include the MHC1 antibody, which is specific for the extracellular domain of Cx43, and a secondary detection reagent, such as a fluorescently or enzymatically labeled anti-human IgG antibody. The kit could be used in a research setting to perform immunohistochemistry on spinal cord tissue sections obtained from an animal model of SCI to visualize the presence and distribution of Cx43 at the injury site. More importantly, the kit could be adapted for a functional assay to detect active hemichannels. For example, the kit could include a membrane-impermeant tracer dye, such as Evans blue or a fluorescent dextran of a specific molecular weight that is too large to pass through intact cell membranes but can enter cells through open hemichannels. By co-administering the MHC1 antibody and the tracer dye, or by using the MHC1 antibody to block the dye uptake in a control sample, one can specifically attribute the dye uptake to Cx43 hemichannel activity. A significant reduction in dye uptake in the presence of MHC1 would be a direct indicator of active Cx43 hemichannels in the tissue. This type of assay could be used to confirm the diagnosis of an active secondary injury process and to determine if a patient is a candidate for MHC1 therapy.

Another type of kit is a prognostic kit. Given that the level of Cx43 hemichannel activity is a key driver of secondary injury, a kit that can quantify this activity shortly after an injury could provide valuable prognostic information. A patient with high levels of hemichannel activity would be at greater risk for a larger lesion and worse functional outcome, and would therefore be a prime candidate for immediate MHC1 treatment. The kit could be designed to work with a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) sample, where the presence of MHC1-bound Cx43 complexes or other biomarkers of hemichannel activity (e.g., elevated levels of ATP or glutamate that are known to be released through hemichannels) could be measured using an ELISA format. The kit would include a capture antibody (which could be MHC1 itself or another anti-Cx43 antibody) immobilized on a plate, detection reagents, and a standard curve for quantification.

The kits of the invention may also be used in a therapeutic monitoring capacity. After a patient has received a dose of the MHC1 antibody, a follow-up CSF or imaging analysis could be performed to confirm that the antibody has reached its target and is effectively blocking hemichannel activity. This would allow for personalized dosing regimens and ensure that the therapy is working as intended.

In addition to these specific applications, the MHC1 antibody is a powerful research tool for basic science. The kit could be marketed to academic and industrial research laboratories for studying the role of Cx43 hemichannels in a wide variety of physiological and pathological processes. The ability to selectively inhibit hemichannels without affecting gap junctions is a unique capability that will allow researchers to finally dissect the distinct functions of these two channel types in health and disease. The kit would provide a standardized, high-quality reagent that ensures reproducibility across different experiments and laboratories.

Overall, the diagnostic and prognostic kits of the present invention leverage the unique specificity of the MHC1 antibody to provide a direct window into the pathological state of Cx43 hemichannels, offering valuable clinical and research applications that complement the therapeutic use of the antibody.

### IV. EXAMPLES

The following examples are provided to illustrate the invention and are not intended to limit its scope. They detail the generation, characterization, and in vivo evaluation of the MHC1 antibody in preclinical models of spinal cord injury.

**Example 1: Generation and In Vitro Characterization of the MHC1 Antibody**

The MHC1 monoclonal antibody was generated by immunizing mice with a synthetic peptide corresponding to the second extracellular loop (E2) of murine Cx43. Hybridoma technology was used to produce a panel of clones, and the HC1 clone was selected for its ability to inhibit Cx43 hemichannel activity in a functional screen. The variable region genes were cloned and used to construct a chimeric antibody with human IgG1 constant regions. The antibody was expressed in HEK293F cells and purified by protein A chromatography. Its binding affinity was measured by ELISA (EC50 = 76.3 pM) and Bio-layer interferometry (KD = 421 nM). Specificity was confirmed by immunocytochemistry on HeLa-Cx43 cells, which showed strong cell border staining, in contrast to no signal in parental HeLa cells. The functional selectivity of MHC1 was then tested. In an EtBr uptake assay, MHC1 (66.7 nM) completely blocked the opening of Cx43 hemichannels in HeLa-Cx43 cells induced by low Ca2+/Mg2+ medium, while a control IgG had no effect. Critically, in a scrape-loading dye transfer assay on primary rat cortical astrocytes, MHC1 treatment for 24 hours did not impair gap junction communication, whereas the positive control carbenoxolone (100 μM) abolished it. Finally, MHC1 was shown to be specific for Cx43, as it had no effect on ATP-induced pannexin 1 channel opening in MDA-MB231 cells.

**Example 2: In Vivo Delivery and Target Engagement**

To confirm that the MHC1 antibody could reach its target in the injured spinal cord, mice were subjected to a focused impact SCI (Model 2) and injected intraperitoneally with MHC1 (25 mg/kg) 30 minutes post-injury. Four hours later, spinal cord tissue was harvested and analyzed by immunohistochemistry using an anti-human IgG secondary antibody. A strong positive signal was observed at the injury site in MHC1-treated mice, but not in saline-treated or sham-operated controls, demonstrating successful delivery across the disrupted blood-spinal cord barrier. To assess target engagement, an Evans blue (EB) dye uptake assay was performed in a broad impact SCI model (Model 1). EB and a large, non-permeant FITC-dextran (10 kDa) were co-injected to distinguish hemichannel-mediated uptake from passive leakage due to cell rupture. Significantly increased EB uptake was observed in the lesion and perilesional areas of saline-treated SCI mice, which was dramatically reduced in MHC1-treated mice. This provides direct evidence that MHC1 successfully engaged its target and inhibited Cx43 hemichannel activity in vivo.

**Example 3: Therapeutic Efficacy in Acute SCI Models**

The therapeutic efficacy of MHC1 was evaluated in two models of partial SCI. In Model 1 (broad impact), mice received a single i.p. injection of MHC1 (25 mg/kg) 30 minutes post-injury. Hind limb function, assessed by the BMS, was significantly improved in the MHC1 group compared to saline and IgG controls from day 2 onwards, with a sustained benefit over 8 weeks. In Model 2 (focused impact), a similar significant improvement was observed, with differences becoming statistically significant from day 26 to day 56. Histological analysis at 56 days post-injury in Model 2 showed that MHC1 treatment significantly reduced the total lesion volume, preserved white matter, and showed a trend towards gray matter sparing. Immunohistochemistry for MAP2 revealed a significant preservation of neurons in the perilesional area in both models. Staining for GFAP demonstrated a significant attenuation of reactive astrogliosis and glial scar formation in all three SCI models tested, including a complete SCI model (Model 3), where functional recovery was not expected but a reduction in gliosis was still observed.

**Example 4: Limited Therapeutic Window**

To define the therapeutic window for MHC1, a late-onset treatment study was conducted. Mice were subjected to a broad impact SCI (Model 1) and, 30 days later, received an intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection of MHC1 (2 mg) or saline. The mice were then monitored for an additional 30 days. No significant difference in BMS scores was observed between the two groups, indicating that MHC1 was ineffective when administered in the chronic phase. Immunohistochemistry confirmed that MHC1 was present at the lesion site after ICV injection, ruling out a delivery issue. This result demonstrates that the therapeutic benefit of MHC1 is restricted to the acute phase of SCI, when pathological hemichannel activity is a primary driver of secondary injury.
