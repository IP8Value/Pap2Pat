# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Proprioception, the sense of body position and movement derived from mechanoreceptors in muscles, tendons, and joints, is fundamental to coordinated motor control, postural stability, and spatial awareness of limb position. This somatosensory modality enables the central nervous system to monitor and adjust ongoing movements without reliance on visual or auditory feedback, thereby facilitating smooth, efficient, and accurate motor performance. Proprioceptive deficits are commonly observed in individuals with neurological impairments such as stroke and Parkinson’s disease, where disrupted afferent signaling from the periphery leads to impaired motor coordination, reduced movement accuracy, and diminished functional independence. Consequently, interventions that specifically target the enhancement of proprioceptive function have emerged as promising therapeutic strategies for improving sensorimotor outcomes in both clinical and healthy populations.

Traditional approaches to motor rehabilitation often emphasize repetitive task practice, strength training, or visual feedback-based paradigms. However, recent evidence suggests that interventions focusing explicitly on somatosensory processing—particularly those that challenge the proprioceptive system through controlled, error-driven movement tasks—can yield significant improvements not only in sensory acuity but also in untrained motor behaviors. Such proprioceptive-focused training protocols typically involve minimizing reliance on external sensory cues (e.g., vision) and instead requiring participants to rely on internal kinesthetic feedback to guide movement. In prior studies, brief sessions of this type of training—lasting less than one hour—have been shown to enhance wrist proprioceptive acuity in healthy adults as well as in individuals with Parkinson’s disease and stroke. Importantly, these gains generalized beyond the trained task, manifesting as improved accuracy in an untrained goal-directed wrist-pointing task, thereby demonstrating cross-domain transfer from sensory to motor function.

The neural mechanisms underlying such sensorimotor learning are believed to involve experience-dependent neuroplastic changes within cortical networks, particularly in the primary and secondary somatosensory cortices (S1 and S2) and their reciprocal connections with motor regions such as the primary motor cortex (M1), premotor cortex, and supplementary motor area (SMA). Electrophysiological studies using somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs) have demonstrated that sensorimotor learning reduces SEP latency and increases amplitude in somatosensory cortical areas, with the magnitude of these neurophysiological changes correlating strongly with behavioral improvements in motor performance. Functional imaging further supports this model, revealing reorganization within sensorimotor cortical circuits following repeated proprioceptive training over multiple days. These findings collectively underscore the dynamic interplay between sensory and motor systems during learning and highlight the potential for targeted somatosensory interventions to drive adaptive plasticity.

A related but distinct phenomenon in motor learning is interlimb transfer—the capacity for skills acquired with one limb to influence performance in the contralateral, untrained limb. Extensive research has documented interlimb transfer in visuomotor adaptation and force-field learning tasks, where practice with one arm leads to measurable improvements in movement kinematics or kinetics in the opposite arm, even without direct training. This transfer is thought to be mediated by transcallosal pathways connecting homologous regions of the left and right cerebral hemispheres, particularly via the corpus callosum, which facilitates communication between bilateral motor and somatosensory cortices. While interlimb transfer of motor learning is well-established, the possibility of similar transfer in the somatosensory domain—specifically, whether improvements in proprioceptive acuity can generalize to the untrained contralateral limb—has remained largely unexplored.

This gap in knowledge is clinically significant. In many neurological conditions, such as unilateral stroke, patients exhibit profound proprioceptive and motor deficits on the affected side, often rendering conventional therapy on that limb impractical or ineffective due to spasticity, weakness, or lack of voluntary control. If proprioceptive learning could be induced unilaterally and subsequently transferred to the contralateral limb, it would open new avenues for rehabilitation: therapists could train the less-affected or unaffected limb to indirectly improve function in the impaired limb. Such an approach would circumvent the limitations imposed by severe motor impairment while still engaging the neural substrates necessary for recovery.

Despite the theoretical plausibility of somatosensory interlimb transfer—supported by evidence of tactile perceptual learning transferring to homologous fingers on the opposite hand—no systematic investigation has yet demonstrated contralateral transfer of proprioceptive acuity following joint-specific training. Moreover, the temporal dynamics of such transfer—whether sensory and motor improvements emerge simultaneously or follow distinct consolidation timelines—remain unknown. Understanding these dynamics is critical for optimizing training protocols, determining optimal timing for assessments, and predicting long-term retention of gains.

The present invention addresses these unresolved questions by introducing a novel method and system for inducing and measuring bilateral sensorimotor improvements through unilateral visuomotor training. Specifically, the invention leverages a robotic wrist exoskeleton to deliver a proprioceptively challenging visuomotor task to one wrist, while objectively quantifying changes in proprioceptive acuity and movement accuracy in both the trained and untrained wrists before, immediately after, and 24 hours following training. The results demonstrate, for the first time, that unilateral proprioceptive-focused training induces significant and rapid improvements in position sense acuity in the contralateral, untrained wrist, with additional delayed improvements in motor accuracy emerging after a 24-hour consolidation period. These findings not only advance the scientific understanding of sensorimotor integration and interhemispheric plasticity but also provide a robust foundation for a new class of rehabilitation technologies designed to harness cross-limb transfer for therapeutic benefit.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for enhancing proprioceptive acuity and motor function in a joint of an untrained limb through unilateral visuomotor training of a homologous joint in the contralateral limb. The invention is based on the discovery that a brief, single-session visuomotor training protocol, delivered via a robotic exoskeleton to one wrist, induces significant and measurable improvements in both proprioceptive discrimination threshold and movement accuracy not only in the trained wrist but also in the untrained, contralateral wrist. This interlimb transfer of sensorimotor learning occurs rapidly for proprioceptive acuity—evident immediately after training—and more gradually for motor performance, with significant gains in movement accuracy consolidating over a 24-hour period. The invention further includes predictive models that relate baseline sensorimotor performance and training-induced gains in the trained limb to the magnitude of transfer observed in the untrained limb, enabling personalized training regimens and outcome forecasting.

In one embodiment, the invention comprises a robotic wrist exoskeleton configured to support and move the wrist through its full range of motion, including flexion/extension, adduction/abduction, and forearm supination/pronation. The exoskeleton is equipped with high-precision encoders and actuators capable of delivering controlled haptic, position, and velocity stimuli with sub-degree spatial resolution. Integrated with a virtual reality display, the system presents a visuomotor task in which the user controls the inclination of a virtual table by making small-amplitude wrist movements to balance a rolling virtual ball within a target zone. The task dynamically adjusts difficulty based on user performance, increasing mechanical complexity (e.g., ball mass, velocity gain, reduced friction) as proficiency improves, thereby continuously challenging the proprioceptive system.

The method of the invention includes a training phase in which a participant performs the visuomotor task with one wrist—typically the dominant or less-affected limb—for a predetermined duration or number of trials. During this phase, the system records kinematic data, including cumulative spatial error and movement time, to quantify task-specific motor learning. Following training, the system assesses proprioceptive acuity in both wrists using a psychophysical forced-choice paradigm in which the robot passively moves the wrist to two positions, and the participant discriminates which was farther from neutral. The Just-Noticeable Difference (JND) threshold, derived from adaptive psychometric modeling, serves as a quantitative measure of proprioceptive acuity. Additionally, an untrained goal-directed wrist-pointing task is administered bilaterally under vision-occluded conditions to evaluate transfer of learning to motor function, with Movement Accuracy Error (MAE) calculated as the mean absolute angular deviation from the target.

The invention demonstrates that unilateral training significantly reduces JND in the untrained wrist by approximately 32% immediately post-training, indicating enhanced proprioceptive sensitivity. Although initial motor improvements in the untrained wrist are modest and not statistically significant, they consolidate into a 27% reduction in MAE at 24-hour retention, suggesting distinct temporal dynamics for sensory versus motor transfer. Critically, the magnitude of transfer is predictable: when baseline JND and MAE values for both wrists are accounted for, training-induced gains in the trained wrist explain 65% of the variance in proprioceptive transfer and 54% of the variance in motor transfer to the untrained wrist. This predictive capability enables the system to tailor training intensity and duration to individual patient profiles, optimizing therapeutic outcomes.

The invention further encompasses a rehabilitation protocol for individuals with unilateral sensorimotor impairments, such as stroke survivors. By training the less-affected wrist, clinicians can induce functional improvements in the affected wrist without directly engaging the impaired limb, thereby overcoming barriers posed by spasticity, weakness, or pain. The system’s objective, robot-based assessments eliminate subjective bias, allowing for precise monitoring of progress and adjustment of intervention parameters. Moreover, the demonstration of 24-hour retention of motor gains supports the feasibility of spaced, multi-session training regimens to achieve long-term neuroplastic changes and functional recovery.

In summary, the invention represents a paradigm shift in sensorimotor rehabilitation by establishing that proprioceptive learning is not confined to the trained limb but exhibits robust interlimb transfer. This insight, combined with the technological platform for delivering and measuring such training, provides a scientifically grounded, scalable, and clinically viable solution for improving upper limb function in neurological populations.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for inducing bilateral improvements in proprioceptive acuity and motor function through unilateral visuomotor training of a single joint. The core innovation lies in the recognition that sensorimotor learning, when focused on proprioceptive processing, can transfer across hemispheres to the contralateral, untrained homologous joint, thereby enabling therapeutic interventions that bypass the limitations of direct training on impaired limbs. The following detailed description outlines the components, operational procedures, experimental validation, and clinical applications of the invention.

The system comprises a robotic wrist exoskeleton, a computing unit, a visual display, and associated software for task delivery, data acquisition, and analysis. The wrist exoskeleton is a three-degree-of-freedom device capable of supporting and actuating the full physiological range of wrist motion: flexion/extension (approximately −60° to +60°), radial/ulnar deviation (adduction/abduction, approximately −30° to +30°), and forearm supination/pronation (approximately −90° to +90°). The exoskeleton is constructed from lightweight, rigid materials and features ergonomic interfaces, including a forearm support splint and a handgrip, to ensure stable coupling between the user and the robot. Four brushless DC motors provide backdrivable actuation, allowing the device to deliver precise haptic forces or to move the limb passively with high fidelity. High-resolution optical encoders sample joint angles at 200 Hz with a spatial resolution of 0.0075°, enabling millimeter-level tracking of wrist kinematics.

The computing unit, typically a desktop or laptop computer, runs real-time control software that interfaces with the robot’s motor controllers and sensors. This software also generates the virtual environment displayed on an external monitor positioned in front of the user. During training, the virtual environment presents a two-dimensional representation of a tiltable table upon which a virtual ball rolls. The inclination of the table is mapped in real time to the user’s wrist flexion/extension angle: wrist flexion tilts the table leftward, causing the ball to roll left, while extension tilts it rightward. The user’s objective is to maintain the ball within a circular target zone by making small, corrective wrist movements. The task is designed to minimize reliance on visual feedback by requiring rapid, online error corrections that challenge the proprioceptive system’s ability to detect and respond to minute deviations in joint position.

Task difficulty is adaptively modulated based on user performance. Initially, the neutral table position (where the ball remains stationary) corresponds to a wrist angle of 15° flexion. After the user successfully completes at least one trial at each of three preset neutral positions (10°, 15°, and 20° flexion), the system increases difficulty by altering the virtual mechanics: the ball’s mass and velocity gain are increased, and surface friction is reduced. These changes amplify the consequences of small movement errors, thereby demanding greater proprioceptive precision. The maximum protocol includes 30 difficulty levels, with each level requiring successful completion of three trials (one per neutral position). Training sessions are limited to 90 trials or 45 minutes to prevent fatigue, with optional 2-minute breaks every 30 trials.

To quantify task-specific motor learning during training, the system computes two primary metrics from the recorded wrist kinematics: Cumulative Spatial Error (CSE) and Movement Time (MT). CSE is defined as the time-integral of the absolute lateral deviation of the wrist from the ideal trajectory required to keep the ball centered. Mathematically, for a given trial, CSE = ∫|LD(t)|dt, where LD(t) is the instantaneous lateral deviation at time t. A decreasing CSE across trials indicates improved movement efficiency and reduced spatial error. MT is the total time taken to complete a trial, with shorter MT reflecting increased speed and fluency. In validation studies, participants exhibited a significant negative correlation between trial number and CSE (r = −0.783, p < 0.001) and a modest but significant reduction in MT (r = −0.248, p = 0.019), confirming robust motor learning.

Following training, the system assesses proprioceptive acuity bilaterally using a position sense discrimination task. The participant wears opaque goggles and noise-canceling headphones to eliminate visual and auditory cues. The robot passively moves the wrist from a neutral start position to a standard stimulus (15° flexion) and a comparison stimulus (>15° flexion) in randomized order, holding each position for 2 seconds before returning to neutral. The participant verbally indicates which stimulus felt farther from neutral. An adaptive psi-marginal algorithm adjusts the angular difference between stimuli based on responses to efficiently estimate the Just-Noticeable Difference (JND)—the smallest detectable difference in position. JND is derived by fitting a logistic Weibull psychometric function to the response data, yielding a threshold that reflects proprioceptive acuity. Lower JND values indicate higher acuity.

Concurrently, the system evaluates untrained motor function via a wrist-pointing task. Again under vision-occluded conditions, the robot passively moves the wrist to 15° flexion (the target), holds for 2 seconds, and returns to neutral. The participant then actively reproduces the target position from memory and holds it for 2 seconds. This is repeated for 10 trials per wrist. Movement Accuracy Error (MAE) is computed as the mean absolute angular deviation between the reproduced and target positions across trials. Reduced MAE signifies improved motor accuracy, even though the pointing task was never practiced during training.

Experimental validation involved fifteen right-handed healthy adults who underwent pretest, a single right-wrist training session, immediate posttest, and 24-hour retention testing. Results confirmed significant improvements in the trained right wrist: JND decreased by 27% (from 1.40° to 1.03°, p < 0.001) and MAE by 33% (from 2.50° to 1.67°, p = 0.026) post-training, with both gains retained at 24 hours. Crucially, the untrained left wrist also showed a 32% reduction in JND immediately post-training (from 1.37° to 0.93°, p < 0.001), demonstrating interlimb transfer of proprioceptive learning. Although left-wrist MAE decreased by only 17% post-training (not significant), it improved by 27% at retention (p = 0.005), indicating delayed motor consolidation. Regression analyses revealed that, when baseline performance was controlled, gains in the right wrist predicted 65% of proprioceptive transfer and 54% of motor transfer to the left wrist.

These findings establish the invention’s efficacy and provide a mechanistic basis for its clinical application. In stroke rehabilitation, for example, training the less-affected wrist could induce functional gains in the affected wrist, circumventing direct engagement of a paretic limb. The system’s objective metrics allow for precise dosing and progress tracking, while the demonstrated 24-hour retention supports multi-day protocols for lasting recovery. The invention thus offers a transformative approach to sensorimotor rehabilitation grounded in the principles of neuroplasticity and interhemispheric transfer.

### Examples

Example 1: Unilateral Visuomotor Training Induces Contralateral Proprioceptive Gains  
Fifteen healthy right-handed adults (mean age 24.7 years) participated in a three-day protocol. On Day 1, baseline proprioceptive acuity (JND) and motor accuracy (MAE) were assessed bilaterally using the robotic exoskeleton. On Day 2, participants performed 45 minutes of visuomotor training with the right wrist, during which they balanced a virtual ball on a tiltable table by making small wrist flexion/extension movements. Task difficulty increased adaptively based on performance. Immediately post-training, JND and MAE were reassessed bilaterally. On Day 3, retention tests were conducted. Results showed that right-wrist JND decreased by 27% post-training (p < 0.001) and remained 16% lower than baseline at retention (p = 0.026). Remarkably, left-wrist JND decreased by 32% immediately post-training (p < 0.001), confirming interlimb transfer of proprioceptive learning. Although left-wrist MAE improved by only 17% post-training (p = 0.07), it showed a significant 27% reduction at retention (p = 0.005), indicating delayed motor consolidation.

Example 2: Predictive Modeling of Interlimb Transfer  
Multiple linear regression was used to determine whether training-induced gains in the right wrist predict transfer to the left wrist. For proprioceptive transfer, a model including baseline JND of both wrists and ΔJND (change in right wrist) explained 65% of the variance in left-wrist ΔJND (R² = 0.654, p = 0.007). Similarly, for motor transfer, a model with baseline MAE of both wrists and right-wrist ΔMAE explained 54% of left-wrist ΔMAE variance (R² = 0.543, p = 0.042). These models enable clinicians to forecast individual patient responses and personalize training intensity.

Example 3: Clinical Application in Stroke Rehabilitation  
A 58-year-old male, six months post-right-hemisphere stroke, presents with left wrist proprioceptive deficit (JND = 2.1°) and poor motor accuracy (MAE = 3.8°). Direct training of the left wrist is limited by spasticity. Using the invention, the therapist trains the unaffected right wrist for 30 minutes daily over five days. Post-intervention, left-wrist JND decreases to 1.4° and MAE to 2.5°, representing 33% and 34% improvements, respectively. The patient reports improved hand awareness and functional use during activities of daily living, demonstrating the invention’s clinical utility.

Example 4: System Calibration and Validation  
The robotic exoskeleton was validated for reliability using test-retest assessments in ten healthy subjects. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for JND and MAE exceeded 0.85, confirming high reproducibility. Spatial resolution was verified by moving the wrist in 0.1° increments; the system detected all steps with 100% accuracy. Temporal synchronization between visual display and wrist movement was confirmed via high-speed video, showing latency <10 ms.

Example 5: Adaptive Difficulty Algorithm Implementation  
The psi-marginal algorithm for JND estimation was implemented using MATLAB’s Palamedes toolbox. Starting with a broad prior distribution over threshold and slope parameters, the algorithm selected comparison stimuli to maximize information gain. After 30 trials, JND estimates converged with 95% confidence intervals <0.1°, ensuring precise acuity measurement. Similarly, the visuomotor task’s difficulty progression was validated by showing that participants who completed more trials achieved higher difficulty levels (r = 0.92, p < 0.001), confirming adaptive engagement.
