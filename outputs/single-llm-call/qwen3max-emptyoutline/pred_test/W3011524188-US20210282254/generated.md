# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of high-intensity laser physics and plasma-based optical technologies, particularly to a method and apparatus for generating relativistic, few-cycle, mid-infrared (mid-IR) laser pulses through the use of a novel plasma optical modulator. More specifically, the invention provides a technique for efficiently converting near-infrared (near-IR) laser pulses into intense, coherent, multi-millijoule (mJ), near-single-cycle mid-IR pulses by exploiting the nonlinear frequency modulation properties of a plasma wakefield driven by a co-propagating high-intensity laser pulse in an underdense plasma. The invention is particularly suited for integration with compact, high-repetition-rate terawatt (TW)-class laser systems operating at kilohertz (kHz) frequencies, thereby enabling stable, scalable, and accessible generation of relativistic mid-IR pulses for applications in ultrafast science, high-field physics, attosecond metrology, particle acceleration, and advanced spectroscopy. The disclosed system leverages the unique electromagnetic properties of relativistic plasma bubbles—formed via laser-driven wakefield excitation—as dynamic optical structures capable of inducing controlled, large-scale frequency downshifting of a trailing signal pulse, resulting in the production of mid-IR radiation with central wavelengths tunable in the range of approximately 3 to 6 micrometers (μm), pulse durations approaching a single optical cycle, and energy conversion efficiencies exceeding 30%. This invention thus bridges a critical gap in the availability of high-intensity, ultrashort mid-IR sources, overcoming the limitations of conventional crystal-based nonlinear optical methods that are constrained by damage thresholds, low efficiency, and non-relativistic intensity regimes.

## BACKGROUND ART

The development of high-intensity laser systems has been a cornerstone of modern physics since the invention of the laser in 1960. A pivotal advancement occurred in 1985 with the introduction of chirped pulse amplification (CPA), a technique that enabled the generation of ultrashort laser pulses with peak powers reaching the terawatt and petawatt levels. This breakthrough facilitated the exploration of laser-matter interactions in the relativistic regime, where the electric field of the laser approaches or exceeds the atomic binding fields, leading to phenomena such as electron acceleration to relativistic energies, generation of high-harmonic radiation, and the creation of extreme states of matter resembling those found in astrophysical environments. These capabilities have opened new frontiers in fundamental science, including attosecond physics, vacuum nonlinear optics, and laboratory astrophysics. However, the majority of these high-intensity laser systems operate in the near-infrared spectral range, typically around 800 nanometers (nm) to 1 micrometer (μm), due to the availability of robust gain media such as titanium-sapphire and the maturity of CPA technology in this band.

Despite the success of near-IR lasers, there is a growing scientific and technological demand for high-intensity laser sources in the mid-infrared region, generally defined as wavelengths from 2 to 20 μm. Mid-IR radiation offers distinct advantages in numerous applications. For instance, in ultrafast spectroscopy, many molecular vibrations and rotational transitions exhibit strong absorption features in the mid-IR, making it ideal for probing chemical dynamics with high specificity. In high-harmonic generation (HHG), the cutoff energy of emitted XUV or soft X-ray photons scales quadratically with the driving laser wavelength, meaning that longer-wavelength drivers can produce brighter, higher-energy attosecond pulses. Similarly, in laser-driven particle acceleration, the dephasing length and energy gain scale favorably with wavelength, enabling more efficient electron or ion acceleration using mid-IR drivers. Moreover, the ponderomotive force—which governs the quiver motion of electrons in a laser field—scales with the square of the wavelength, allowing relativistic intensities to be achieved at lower electric fields when using longer wavelengths, thereby reducing the required laser energy for a given interaction strength.

Efforts to generate intense mid-IR pulses have traditionally relied on nonlinear optical processes in solid-state crystals, such as difference frequency generation (DFG), optical parametric amplification (OPA), and optical parametric chirped-pulse amplification (OPCPA). While these methods have successfully produced mid-IR pulses with energies up to several millijoules and durations in the few-cycle regime, they are fundamentally limited by the optical damage threshold of the nonlinear materials. At intensities approaching the relativistic regime (typically above 10¹⁸ W/cm² for 1-μm light, but lower for longer wavelengths), conventional crystals undergo irreversible damage, preventing the direct scaling of these techniques to relativistic intensities. Furthermore, phase-matching constraints, group velocity dispersion, and absorption losses in the mid-IR range impose additional limitations on bandwidth, efficiency, and pulse duration. As a result, current crystal-based mid-IR sources remain confined to non-relativistic intensities, hindering their utility in high-field physics experiments.

To circumvent these material limitations, researchers have turned to plasma-based optical elements, which, by virtue of being already ionized, can sustain laser intensities far exceeding those tolerable by solids. Plasma mirrors, gratings, waveguides, and modulators have been proposed and demonstrated as robust alternatives for manipulating high-intensity laser pulses. Among these, plasma wakefields—coherent electron density oscillations excited by an intense laser pulse propagating through an underdense plasma—have emerged as particularly promising platforms for frequency conversion. In the highly nonlinear regime, the wake takes the form of a series of “bubbles,” regions devoid of electrons trailing behind the laser pulse, each surrounded by a dense sheath of electrons. These bubbles move at nearly the speed of light and possess steep longitudinal and transverse density gradients, creating strong local variations in the plasma refractive index.

Several studies have explored the use of such plasma wakes for frequency downshifting. For example, it has been shown that a portion of the drive laser pulse itself can undergo self-modulation and redshift as it interacts with the wake it generates. Other approaches involve injecting a separate probe or signal pulse into the wake to experience frequency modulation. However, existing schemes typically require large-scale, joule-level, 100-terawatt laser facilities that operate at low repetition rates (e.g., once per second or less), limiting their practicality for widespread scientific use. Additionally, these methods often produce broadband, incoherent, or poorly directed mid-IR emission with low energy conversion efficiency—typically below 5%—and lack precise control over the spectral and temporal characteristics of the output pulse. The inability to generate high-energy, few-cycle, relativistic mid-IR pulses with high efficiency and stability using compact, high-repetition-rate laser systems has thus remained a significant technological bottleneck.

In light of these challenges, there exists a clear need for a novel approach that combines the damage resilience of plasma optics with the efficiency, coherence, and controllability required for practical applications. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a plasma optical modulator that exploits the structured density profile of a laser-driven plasma bubble to induce controlled, large-amplitude frequency downshifting of a co-propagating signal pulse. Unlike prior art, the invention achieves high conversion efficiency, preserves pulse coherence, enables tunability through adjustable parameters, and is compatible with commercially available kHz-repetition-rate TW-class laser systems, thereby offering a scalable and accessible route to relativistic mid-IR science.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and apparatus for the efficient generation of relativistic, few-cycle, mid-infrared (mid-IR) laser pulses by utilizing a plasma optical modulator based on a nonlinear plasma wakefield. The invention overcomes the limitations of conventional crystal-based nonlinear optics and prior plasma-based frequency conversion schemes by enabling high-energy conversion efficiency, precise spectral control, and compatibility with compact, high-repetition-rate laser systems. The core of the invention lies in the co-propagation of two temporally separated, linearly polarized laser pulses—an intense drive pulse and a lower-intensity signal pulse—through an underdense plasma channel. The drive pulse, with sufficient intensity to excite a highly nonlinear plasma wake, generates a series of relativistic plasma bubbles characterized by sharp electron density gradients. The signal pulse, initially at the same near-infrared wavelength as the drive pulse, is injected with a precise time delay such that it loads into the density up-ramp region at the front of the second plasma bubble. Within this region, the signal pulse experiences a strong, continuous frequency downshift due to the spatially varying plasma refractive index, which alters the local phase velocity of light. As the signal pulse co-propagates with the moving bubble, its central wavelength is extended into the mid-IR range (approximately 3–6 μm), while maintaining a near-single-cycle temporal duration and achieving relativistic normalized vector potential (a₀ > 1). Critically, the energy conversion efficiency from the signal pulse to the mid-IR output can exceed 30%, a substantial improvement over existing methods.

The invention further includes a plasma channel configuration that guides both laser pulses over distances significantly longer than their Rayleigh lengths, ensuring stable propagation and sustained interaction with the plasma wake. The plasma channel is formed with a parabolic radial density profile that provides transverse confinement, preventing diffraction and enabling efficient coupling over millimeter-scale distances. The central wavelength, pulse duration, and energy of the generated mid-IR pulse are tunable through adjustment of key parameters, including the plasma density, plasma channel length, signal pulse intensity, spot size, and the temporal delay between the drive and signal pulses. Notably, the scheme is robust against variations in the carrier-envelope phase (CEP) of the input signal pulse, as the CEP shift induced during propagation is deterministic and independent of the initial CEP, thereby enabling the generation of CEP-stable mid-IR pulses when driven by CEP-stabilized near-IR sources. The polarization of the signal pulse is orthogonal to that of the drive pulse, minimizing cross-coupling and enabling clean separation of the output mid-IR beam from residual near-IR light using standard optical components such as dichroic mirrors or germanium filters.

The invention is particularly advantageous because it operates with drive and signal pulses having energies in the tens of millijoules and peak powers in the few-terawatt range—parameters readily achievable with existing commercial kHz-repetition-rate laser systems. This compatibility ensures high average power, enhanced experimental stability, and broad accessibility for research laboratories. The resulting mid-IR pulses are well-collimated, propagate in a stable direction, and can be easily extracted and focused for downstream applications. In summary, the invention provides a practical, efficient, and scalable solution for generating relativistic few-cycle mid-IR pulses, opening new avenues in ultrafast science, high-field physics, and advanced photonics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Example 1

The invention is now described in detail with reference to a specific embodiment, referred to herein as Example 1, which demonstrates the generation of relativistic, few-cycle mid-IR pulses using a plasma optical modulator driven by co-propagating laser pulses in an underdense plasma channel. The system comprises a high-intensity laser source, a beam-splitting and delay apparatus, a plasma target chamber, and diagnostic or application optics. The laser source is a commercially available, CEP-stabilized, few-cycle, kHz-repetition-rate Ti:sapphire laser system capable of delivering pulses with a central wavelength of λ₀ = 1 μm, full width at half maximum (FWHM) duration of 10 femtoseconds (fs), and pulse energy up to 70 mJ. Using a dielectric beam splitter, the primary laser beam is divided into two beams: a drive beam and a signal beam. The drive beam is allocated approximately 55 mJ of energy, corresponding to a peak power of 5.5 terawatts (TW), while the signal beam receives approximately 13.7 mJ, corresponding to a peak power of 1.37 TW. Both beams are linearly polarized, with the drive beam polarized along the y-axis and the signal beam polarized along the z-axis, ensuring orthogonality to minimize nonlinear coupling.

An optical delay stage, comprising a motorized retroreflector mounted on a precision translation stage, is employed to introduce a controlled time delay t_d between the drive and signal pulses. In this example, the delay is set to t_d = 18T₀, where T₀ = λ₀/c ≈ 3.33 fs is the optical cycle of the laser, resulting in a temporal separation of approximately 60 fs. This delay is chosen so that the peak of the signal pulse arrives at the front of the second plasma bubble generated by the drive pulse as it propagates through the plasma. The two beams are then co-focused into a plasma channel using off-axis parabolic mirrors with a focal length of 1 meter, achieving a common spot size w₀ = 8 μm at the entrance of the plasma.

The plasma channel is preformed in a gas jet or capillary discharge setup, filled with helium or hydrogen gas, and ionized either by a prepulse or by the leading edge of the drive pulse itself. The initial plasma density profile is engineered to have a parabolic radial dependence given by n_e(r) = n₀ + (λ₀² / π²w₀⁴) r² n_c, where n₀ = 3.5 × 10⁻³ n_c is the on-axis background plasma density, n_c = 1.1 × 10²¹ cm⁻³ is the critical density for λ₀ = 1 μm, and r is the radial distance from the channel axis. This profile provides a transverse refractive index gradient that acts as a waveguide, confining the laser pulses over a length L = 1597 μm, which is more than ten times the Rayleigh length of the focused beams in vacuum. The channel length is optimized to allow sufficient interaction time for frequency downshifting while avoiding excessive absorption or dephasing.

Upon injection into the plasma, the drive pulse, with a normalized vector potential a_d0 = 2 (corresponding to a peak intensity of 5.5 × 10¹⁸ W/cm²), rapidly excites a nonlinear plasma wake. Due to its high intensity, the wake evolves into a train of three distinct plasma bubbles, each approximately 40 μm in length, moving at a phase velocity v_φ ≈ c(1 - k_p²c²/2ω₀²) ≈ 0.999c, where k_p = ω_p/c is the plasma wavenumber and ω_p = √(4πe²n₀/m_e) is the plasma frequency. The signal pulse, with a_d0 = 1, enters the plasma with the prescribed delay and loads into the steep density up-ramp at the front of the second bubble, where the electron density gradient ∂n_e/∂ξ is positive and large (ξ = x - ct is the co-moving coordinate).

As the signal pulse co-propagates with the bubble, each optical cycle experiences a slightly different phase velocity due to the spatially varying plasma density. According to the dispersion relation ω² = c²k² + ω_p²(n_e), the local phase velocity v_p = ω/k increases in regions of higher plasma density. This results in a progressive stretching of the wavelength, as described by the differential equation dλ/dτ ≈ (cλ / 2n_c)(λ/λ₀)² (∂n_e/∂ξ), where τ is the lab-frame time. Over the course of propagation through the bubble, the signal pulse undergoes a cumulative redshift, with its central wavelength evolving from 1 μm to approximately 4.2 μm. The frequency-downshifted photons, having a lower group velocity v_g = c√(1 - ω_p²/ω_ir²), slip backward relative to the original signal envelope and become trapped near the center of the second bubble, where the density is lowest. Once trapped, the mid-IR pulse ceases to experience significant further frequency modulation and propagates stably within the bubble.

Three-dimensional relativistic particle-in-cell (PIC) simulations confirm that the output mid-IR pulse has a FWHM duration of approximately 27 fs, corresponding to just two optical cycles at 4.2 μm, and a normalized amplitude a_ir ≈ 1.3, indicating relativistic intensity (I ≈ 1.7 × 10¹⁷ W/cm²). The energy of the mid-IR pulse is approximately 4.1 mJ, representing a conversion efficiency of 30% relative to the initial signal pulse energy. The spectrum is centered at 4.2 μm with a bandwidth supporting near-single-cycle durations, and the beam exhibits excellent pointing stability and spatial quality. The CEP of the output pulse is shifted by a fixed amount Δφ ≈ 0.5π relative to the input, independent of the initial CEP, enabling CEP-stable operation when driven by a CEP-controlled source.

The system is robust to moderate variations in experimental parameters. For instance, changing the delay time by ±1 optical cycle (i.e., t_d = 17T₀ or 19T₀) still yields mid-IR pulses with central wavelengths between 3.8 and 4.5 μm and efficiencies above 25%. Similarly, increasing the plasma density to 4.5 × 10⁻³ n_c shifts the output wavelength to ~5 μm with a near-single-cycle duration, while maintaining high efficiency. The signal pulse spot size can be varied between 5 and 9 μm without significant degradation in spectral quality, though efficiency peaks at w₀ = 8 μm. After exiting the plasma, the mid-IR pulse is separated from residual near-IR light using a germanium filter, which transmits mid-IR while reflecting near-IR, and can be focused to a tight spot for applications such as HHG or electron acceleration.

This example demonstrates that the invention enables the practical, efficient, and tunable generation of relativistic few-cycle mid-IR pulses using compact, high-repetition-rate laser technology, thereby providing a transformative tool for next-generation ultrafast and high-field science.
