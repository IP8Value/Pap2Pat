# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) remains a significant global public health challenge despite remarkable advances in antiretroviral therapy. Combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), which typically involves the concurrent administration of three or more antiretroviral agents from at least two different drug classes, has transformed HIV/AIDS from a uniformly fatal disease into a manageable chronic condition for millions of individuals worldwide. When administered effectively and adhered to consistently, cART suppresses viral replication to undetectable levels, restores immune function, prevents opportunistic infections, and dramatically reduces both morbidity and mortality associated with HIV infection. These benefits have been demonstrated across diverse geographic and socioeconomic settings, including resource-rich nations as well as low- and middle-income countries where access to treatment has expanded substantially over the past two decades. Nevertheless, the long-term success of cART is increasingly threatened by the emergence of drug resistance mutations (DRMs) that compromise the efficacy of existing antiretroviral agents, as well as by the cumulative toxicities and metabolic complications associated with prolonged exposure to current drug regimens. These challenges underscore the urgent need for novel antiviral therapeutics that operate through distinct mechanisms of action, target previously unexploited viral components, and exhibit improved safety and pharmacokinetic profiles.

Among the potential targets for next-generation antiretrovirals, the HIV-1 capsid (CA) protein has emerged as a particularly promising candidate due to its multifaceted roles throughout the viral life cycle. The CA protein is a structural component derived from the proteolytic cleavage of the Gag polyprotein during viral maturation. It consists of two structurally independent domains—the N-terminal domain (CA-NTD) and the C-terminal domain (CA-CTD)—connected by a flexible linker of approximately five amino acid residues. The CA-NTD comprises seven α-helices (designated α1 through α7), while the CA-CTD contains four α-helices (α8 through α11) along with a short 310-helix. In the mature infectious virion, CA self-assembles into a conical capsid core composed of approximately 250 hexameric and 12 pentameric building blocks, forming a protective shell that encases the viral RNA genome and essential enzymes required for early replication events.

The functional significance of the capsid core extends far beyond its role as a passive container for viral components. During the early phase of infection, following fusion of the viral envelope with the host cell membrane, the intact capsid core is released into the cytoplasm where it undergoes a tightly regulated process of disassembly known as uncoating. Although the precise timing and molecular mechanisms governing uncoating remain subjects of active investigation and debate, accumulating evidence indicates that this process is intimately linked to the initiation and completion of reverse transcription, wherein the viral RNA genome is converted into double-stranded DNA. Moreover, the capsid core plays an active role in facilitating nuclear entry of the preintegration complex (PIC), which contains the newly synthesized viral DNA along with integrase and other accessory proteins. Notably, recent studies have demonstrated that CA itself enters the nucleus alongside the PIC, suggesting a direct involvement of the capsid in the integration of viral DNA into the host genome—a critical step that establishes permanent infection. In the late phase of the viral life cycle, during virion assembly at the plasma membrane, the Gag precursor orchestrates the formation of immature, non-infectious particles that subsequently undergo proteolytic maturation to yield infectious progeny virions with properly formed conical capsids. Disruption of any of these CA-dependent processes—uncoating, nuclear import, integration, or capsid assembly—can profoundly impair viral replication, thereby validating CA as a high-value target for therapeutic intervention.

Over the years, numerous small-molecule compounds and peptide-based inhibitors have been developed to interfere with CA function, although none have yet progressed to clinical approval. Early efforts identified benzodiazepine (BD) and benzimidazole (BM) derivatives that bind to a pocket within the CA-NTD near the cyclophilin A (CypA)-binding loop, disrupting proper capsid assembly. Another class of inhibitors, exemplified by CAP-1, was found to bind to a distinct site on the CA-NTD and interfere with capsid multimerization. More recently, compounds such as PF-3450074 (PF74) and pyrrolopyrazolones (e.g., BI-2) were discovered to occupy a hydrophobic pocket located at the interface between the CA-NTD and CA-CTD within the hexameric lattice. This binding site overlaps with the interaction surfaces used by key host cellular factors, including cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor 6 (CPSF6) and nucleoporin 153 (NUP153), which are essential for nuclear import and integration. By competing with these host factors, PF74 and BI-2 disrupt multiple stages of the viral life cycle, including both early post-entry events and late-stage virion maturation. Additionally, the antibiotic coumermycin A1 (C-A1) was serendipitously found to inhibit HIV-1 integration, with resistance mapping to CA residues N74 and A105, implicating CA as its molecular target. Peptide inhibitors such as NYAD-1 have also been reported to interfere with CA function, though their development has been limited by poor pharmacokinetic properties.

Despite these advances, the clinical translation of CA inhibitors has been hampered by suboptimal potency, unfavorable resistance profiles, or inadequate drug-like characteristics. This landscape changed significantly with the recent disclosure of GS-CA1 and its analog GS-6207 (also referred to as GS-CA2), which represent a new generation of ultrapotent CA inhibitors with exceptional antiviral activity. GS-CA1 exhibits picomolar efficacy against HIV-1 in both T-cell lines and primary peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), with EC50 values of 240 pM and 140 pM, respectively. GS-6207 demonstrates even greater potency, with an EC50 of 100 pM in MT-4 cells and a mean EC50 of 50 pM (range: 20–160 pM) against a panel of 23 diverse clinical isolates spanning multiple HIV-1 subtypes. Importantly, both compounds display prolonged pharmacokinetics in preclinical animal models, with a single subcutaneous injection maintaining plasma concentrations above the effective threshold for more than 12 weeks, highlighting their potential as long-acting injectable therapeutics. Structural studies indicate that GS-CA1 and GS-6207 bind to the same interdomain pocket as PF74, CPSF6, and NUP153, but engage in more extensive interactions with the CA hexamer, likely accounting for their superior binding affinity and antiviral potency. However, the precise molecular determinants of this enhanced activity, as well as strategies to further optimize CA-targeting agents, remain areas of active investigation. The present invention addresses these needs by leveraging structural insights from known CA inhibitors and host factor interactions to design novel peptide-based therapeutics with improved binding characteristics and antiviral potential.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel cyclic and linear peptides that specifically bind to the HIV-1 capsid (CA) protein with high affinity and inhibit viral replication by interfering with essential CA-mediated processes. These peptides are rationally designed based on conserved structural motifs observed in diverse CA-binding ligands, including small-molecule inhibitors such as PF74, BI-2, GS-CA1, and GS-6207, as well as host-derived peptides from CPSF6 and NUP153. Through comprehensive computational modeling and experimental validation, the inventors have identified key chemical and spatial features common to these ligands that enable high-affinity interaction with a conserved pocket at the interface between the N-terminal domain (CA-NTD) and C-terminal domain (CA-CTD) of the CA hexamer. Exploiting these shared features, the invention discloses a cyclic peptide, designated Pep-1, which mimics the critical binding elements of natural and synthetic CA ligands and demonstrates superior binding affinity compared to established inhibitors such as PF74.

In one aspect, the invention provides a cyclic peptide comprising a sequence that forms a constrained macrocyclic structure capable of adopting a conformation that complements the topology of the CA interdomain binding pocket. The cyclic peptide includes aromatic and hydrophobic moieties positioned to engage conserved CA residues such as Leu56, Met66, Gln67, Asn74, Ala105, Lys70, and Arg173, which are known to be critical for ligand binding and viral fitness. The constrained cyclic architecture enhances binding affinity by reducing conformational entropy loss upon binding and by preorganizing key pharmacophoric elements in an optimal orientation for interaction with the target site. In a specific embodiment, the cyclic peptide exhibits a dissociation constant (Kd) for CA binding of less than 50 nanomolar, and in particular embodiments, less than 35 nanomolar, representing a significant improvement over reference compounds such as PF74 (Kd ≈ 212 nM).

In another aspect, the invention encompasses linear peptide variants derived from the cyclic scaffold, which retain substantial binding affinity and antiviral activity despite the absence of macrocyclization. These linear peptides may be advantageous for certain pharmaceutical applications due to simplified synthesis and formulation requirements, while still benefiting from the rational design principles underlying the parent cyclic structure. Both cyclic and linear peptides of the invention are shown to bind specifically to the CA hexamer without significant interaction with monomeric CA or unrelated proteins, indicating high target selectivity.

The peptides of the invention are useful for the prevention and treatment of HIV infection in mammals, including humans. Pharmaceutical compositions comprising the peptides, optionally in combination with one or more additional antiretroviral agents, are provided for systemic or localized administration. Due to their high potency and potential for extended half-life, particularly in the case of cyclic variants, the peptides are especially suited for long-acting injectable formulations that could reduce dosing frequency and improve patient adherence. Methods of inhibiting HIV replication in a subject in need thereof are disclosed, comprising administering an effective amount of a peptide of the invention. Additionally, the peptides may be employed as research tools to probe CA structure-function relationships or to develop diagnostic assays for monitoring CA-ligand interactions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

As used herein, the term “HIV-1 capsid (CA) protein” refers to the structural protein encoded by the HIV-1 *gag* gene that assembles into the conical capsid core of the mature virion. The CA protein comprises an N-terminal domain (CA-NTD; residues 1–145) and a C-terminal domain (CA-CTD; residues 151–231) connected by a flexible linker (residues 146–150). The term encompasses full-length CA, as well as functional fragments or oligomeric assemblies thereof, including but not limited to CA monomers, dimers, hexamers, and pentamers, provided they retain the ability to bind the peptides of the invention.

The phrase “interdomain binding pocket” denotes a conserved hydrophobic cavity located at the interface between the CA-NTD and CA-CTD within the CA hexamer. This pocket is formed by residues from helices 3, 4, 5, and 7 of the CA-NTD and helix 9 of the CA-CTD, and serves as the binding site for multiple small-molecule inhibitors (e.g., PF74, GS-CA1) and host-derived peptides (e.g., CPSF6, NUP153). Key residues lining this pocket include Leu56, Met66, Gln67, Asn74, Ala105, Lys70, Thr107, Tyr130, Leu172, Arg173, and Gln179.

A “cyclic peptide” as used herein refers to a peptide in which the N-terminus and C-terminus are covalently linked, either directly or via a bridging moiety, to form a macrocyclic ring structure. Cyclization may be achieved through amide bond formation, disulfide bridges, lactam linkages, or other chemistries known in the art. The cyclic constraint restricts conformational flexibility and stabilizes bioactive conformations that enhance target binding.

A “linear peptide” refers to a peptide lacking covalent linkage between its termini, thus possessing greater conformational freedom compared to its cyclic counterpart. Linear peptides of the invention are derived from the sequences of cyclic peptides but lack the macrocyclic closure.

The term “binding affinity” refers to the strength of the interaction between a peptide and the CA protein, quantified by the equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd). A lower Kd value indicates higher affinity. Binding affinity is determined experimentally using techniques such as MicroScale Thermophoresis (MST), isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), or surface plasmon resonance (SPR).

“Pharmaceutically acceptable” means that the substance or composition must be compatible with the other ingredients of a formulation and not deleterious to the recipient thereof. Such carriers, diluents, and excipients are well known in the art and include materials such as water, saline, buffers, and various polymers suitable for parenteral, oral, or topical delivery.

### Overview

The present invention is directed to novel peptides that bind with high affinity to the HIV-1 capsid (CA) protein and inhibit viral replication. These peptides are designed based on a deep structural understanding of the CA interdomain binding pocket and the molecular features shared by diverse natural and synthetic ligands that target this site. Through comparative analysis of crystal structures and computational models of CA in complex with inhibitors such as PF74, BI-2, GS-CA1, GS-6207, and host peptides from CPSF6 and NUP153, the inventors identified a conserved spatial arrangement of aromatic and hydrophobic groups that is critical for high-affinity binding. Specifically, a phenyl or difluorobenzyl moiety in small molecules, or a phenylalanine side chain in host peptides, occupies a key subpocket formed by CA residues Leu56, Asn57, and Met66. Additional polar and hydrophobic interactions with residues such as Gln67, Lys70, Ala105, and Arg173 further stabilize the complex.

Leveraging these insights, the inventors engineered a cyclic peptide, Pep-1, that incorporates structural mimics of these conserved pharmacophores within a constrained macrocyclic scaffold. The cyclic architecture enforces a bioactive conformation that optimally presents these groups for interaction with the CA pocket, resulting in enhanced binding affinity and specificity. Experimental validation using MicroScale Thermophoresis confirmed that Pep-1 binds CA with a Kd of 32 ± 3 nM, representing a ~7-fold improvement over PF74 (Kd = 212 ± 7 nM). This superior affinity translates into potent antiviral activity, positioning Pep-1 and related peptides as promising candidates for the development of next-generation HIV therapeutics.

The invention further encompasses linear derivatives of Pep-1 that, while lacking the macrocyclic constraint, retain significant binding capability due to the preservation of key interacting residues. These linear variants offer alternative development pathways with potentially simpler manufacturing processes. Both cyclic and linear peptides are amenable to pharmaceutical formulation and can be administered alone or in combination with other antiretrovirals to achieve synergistic inhibition of HIV replication.

### Cyclic Peptides

The cyclic peptides of the present invention are characterized by a macrocyclic backbone that constrains the peptide into a conformation complementary to the HIV-1 CA interdomain binding pocket. The design of these peptides is informed by structural alignment of known CA ligands, revealing that despite differences in chemical class—small molecules versus peptides—they converge on a common binding mode defined by the placement of an aromatic/hydrophobic group in a specific subpocket of CA. In GS-CA1 and GS-6207, this role is fulfilled by a difluorobenzyl moiety; in PF74 and BI-2, by phenyl rings; and in CPSF6 and NUP153, by phenylalanine side chains (Phe321 and Phe1417, respectively). All these groups engage in van der Waals contacts with Leu56, Asn57, and Met66 of CA-NTD, forming a hydrophobic anchor that is essential for high-affinity binding.

Accordingly, the cyclic peptides of the invention incorporate one or more aromatic residues—such as phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, or non-natural aromatic amino acids—positioned to mimic this conserved interaction. In addition, the peptides include residues capable of forming hydrogen bonds or salt bridges with polar CA residues such as Gln67, Lys70, Asn74, and Arg173. For example, a glutamine or asparagine side chain may interact with Gln67, while a carboxylate-containing residue (e.g., aspartate or glutamate) may form a salt bridge with Lys70 or Arg173. Hydrophobic residues such as leucine, isoleucine, or valine may be included to contact Ala105, Thr107, or Leu172.

The macrocyclic structure is formed by linking the N- and C-termini of a linear precursor peptide through a covalent bond. In preferred embodiments, cyclization is achieved via an amide bond between the α-amino group of the N-terminal residue and the α-carboxyl group of the C-terminal residue, yielding a head-to-tail cyclic peptide. Alternatively, side chain-to-side chain, side chain-to-terminus, or backbone-to-backbone cyclization strategies may be employed using chemistries such as lactam formation (e.g., between lysine and aspartic acid side chains), disulfide bridging (between cysteine residues), or click chemistry (e.g., azide-alkyne cycloaddition). The ring size is optimized to balance conformational rigidity with synthetic accessibility, typically ranging from 6 to 20 amino acid residues.

A representative cyclic peptide, Pep-1, was designed with a sequence that places a phenylalanine residue in the position analogous to Phe321 of CPSF6 and the difluorobenzyl group of GS-CA1. Flanking residues were selected to provide additional contacts with CA, including a basic residue to interact with Glu/Gln residues in the pocket and hydrophobic residues to fill adjacent cavities. Computational docking using Induced-Fit Docking (IFD) predicted that Pep-1 binds the CA hexamer with a pose overlapping those of GS-CA1, PF74, and host peptides, engaging the same key residues. Experimental validation by MST confirmed high-affinity binding with Kd = 32 nM.

Cyclic peptides of the invention may be further optimized through medicinal chemistry approaches, including incorporation of D-amino acids to enhance proteolytic stability, N-methylation to improve membrane permeability, or PEGylation to extend plasma half-life. Such modifications do not substantially alter the core binding motif but confer desirable pharmacokinetic properties for in vivo application.

### Linear Peptides

Linear peptides according to the invention are derived from the sequences of the cyclic peptides but lack the covalent macrocyclic constraint. While generally exhibiting reduced binding affinity compared to their cyclic counterparts due to greater conformational entropy in the unbound state, linear peptides retain significant activity if they preserve the critical pharmacophoric elements identified in the design process. Specifically, linear peptides include at least one aromatic residue (e.g., phenylalanine) positioned to interact with the Leu56/Asn57/Met66 subpocket of CA, along with auxiliary residues that contribute to binding through hydrogen bonding or hydrophobic interactions.

The linear peptides may be identical in sequence to the open-chain form of the cyclic peptide or may be truncated or extended variants that maintain the essential binding motif. For example, a minimal linear peptide might consist of only the core tripeptide or tetrapeptide segment responsible for key CA contacts, flanked by solubilizing residues such as lysine or arginine to improve aqueous solubility. Alternatively, longer linear peptides may include additional secondary structure-promoting elements (e.g., helix-inducing residues) to partially preorganize the binding conformation even in the absence of cyclization.

Linear peptides offer several practical advantages, including straightforward solid-phase peptide synthesis, ease of purification, and compatibility with standard formulation techniques. They may be particularly suitable for applications where ultra-high affinity is not strictly required, or where cost-of-goods considerations favor simpler molecular architectures. Despite their linear nature, these peptides demonstrate measurable inhibition of CA-dependent processes in biochemical and cellular assays, validating their utility as antiviral agents or research probes.

In some embodiments, linear peptides are used as intermediates in the synthesis of cyclic peptides, wherein the linear precursor is cyclized in solution or on-resin. In other cases, linear peptides serve as controls to assess the contribution of macrocyclization to binding affinity and antiviral potency. Comparative studies between cyclic and linear variants provide valuable structure-activity relationship (SAR) data to guide further optimization.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Computational modeling of GS-CA1 and GS-6207 binding to the HIV-1 capsid hexamer was performed to elucidate the molecular basis of their exceptional antiviral potency. The X-ray crystal structure of the native HIV-1 CA hexamer in complex with PF74 (PDB entry 4XFZ) was used as the receptor template. Initial three-dimensional structures of GS-CA1 and GS-6207 were generated using ChemSketch and subjected to energy minimization with MacroModel followed by LigPrep to assign appropriate protonation states and tautomeric forms. The CA hexamer structure was prepared using PrepWizard to add hydrogen atoms, assign bond orders, and optimize hydrogen bonding networks. Induced-Fit Docking (IFD) simulations were conducted using the Schrödinger Suite, with a docking grid centered on the PF74 binding site (36 Å × 36 Å × 36 Å). The IFD protocol allowed for side chain flexibility and backbone refinement in the binding pocket to accommodate ligand-induced conformational changes.

The top-scoring pose for GS-CA1 revealed binding in a pocket formed by CA-NTD residues Leu56, Met66, Gln67, Asn74, and Ala105—residues previously identified in in vitro resistance selection studies as critical for GS-CA1 susceptibility. Additional interactions were observed with Ile37, Pro38, Ser41, Asn53, Thr54, Asn57, Gln63, Leu69, Lys70, Ile73, Thr106, Thr107, Tyr130, Tyr169, Leu172, Arg173, and Gln179. Notably, the difluorobenzyl moiety of GS-CA1 occupied a hydrophobic subpocket lined by Leu56, Asn57, and Met66, mirroring the position of phenyl rings in PF74 and phenylalanine side chains in CPSF6/NUP153 peptides. The Glide docking score for GS-CA1 was −11.271.

GS-6207, which differs from GS-CA1 by three fluorine substitutions (cyclopropane → methyl on sulfonamide; difluoroethyl → trifluoroethyl on indazole; difluoromethyl → trifluoromethyl on cyclopenta-pyrazole), yielded a slightly better Glide score of −14.362. The trifluoromethyl group on the cyclopenta-pyrazole ring was oriented toward solvent, while the trifluoroethyl group engaged in hydrophobic contacts. A key difference was the repositioning of the Lys70 side chain, which moved ~5 Å toward the ligand and formed a hydrogen bond with the amide carbonyl of GS-6207—an interaction absent in the GS-CA1 complex. This additional polar contact likely contributes to the improved docking score and may underlie the enhanced antiviral potency of GS-6207.

Sequence analysis of HIV-1 subtype C (the most prevalent global subtype) revealed near-complete conservation of the GS-CA binding pocket, with only a Tyr169→Phe substitution. Given the proximity of this residue to the ligand (<3.8 Å), the conservative nature of this change suggests minimal impact on inhibitor binding, consistent with the reported pan-subtype activity of GS-CA compounds.

### Example 2

Comparative structural analysis was conducted to map the binding modes of diverse CA ligands onto a common framework. Superposition of the CA/PF74 crystal structure (4XFZ) with the modeled CA/GS-CA1 complex showed remarkable overlap of core aromatic rings: the indole ring of PF74 aligned with the cyclopenta-pyrazole of GS-CA1, while PF74’s phenyl rings matched the difluorobenzene and indazole moieties of GS-CA1. Polar groups, such as the acetamide in both compounds, also superposed closely, indicating convergent evolution of pharmacophores targeting the same pocket.

Resistance mutation profiles further supported binding site overlap. PF74 resistance mutations include Q67H, K70R, T107N, L111I, and H87P, whereas GS-CA1 selections yielded L56I, M66I, Q67H, N74D, and A105E. The shared Q67H mutation confirms a common interaction hotspot, while other GS-CA1 resistance residues (L56, M66, N74, A105) lie within van der Waals distance of PF74, explaining cross-resistance patterns. Notably, K70 did not emerge in GS-CA1 selections, consistent with the absence of a K70–ligand interaction in the GS-CA1 model—unlike PF74, which forms a hydrogen bond with K70. This interaction is restored in GS-6207, predicting that K70 mutations may arise under GS-6207 pressure.

BI-2, a pyrrolopyrazolone inhibitor, shares resistance mutations A105T and T107N with GS-CA1 and binds the same pocket, as confirmed by crystallography. Superposition of CA/BI-2, CA/PF74, and CA/GS-CA1 complexes demonstrated a conserved binding mode, with BI-2’s phenyl ring occupying the same subpocket as GS-CA1’s difluorobenzyl group.

Host factor peptides CPSF6 (residues 313–327) and NUP153 (residues 1410–1423) also bind this pocket, with Phe321 (CPSF6) and Phe1417 (NUP153) superposing precisely on the difluorobenzyl group of GS-CA1. Additional interactions include hydrophobic contacts between CPSF6/NUP153 and Pro38 of CA, mirrored by the methylsulfonyl group of GS-CA1. These parallels validate the strategy of mimicking host factor interfaces to design high-affinity inhibitors.

In contrast, CAP-1, BD, and BM compounds bind a distinct site near the CypA loop (residues 85–93), with only partial overlap (e.g., shared contact with Met66). Coumermycin A1 (C-A1) was predicted by prior studies to bind at the monomer–monomer interface involving Asn74 and Ala105, but our IFD simulations failed to reproduce this pose, suggesting an alternative resistance mechanism not involving direct binding defects.

### Example 3

A cyclic peptide inhibitor, Pep-1, was designed by integrating structural motifs from GS-CA1, PF74, BI-2, CPSF6, and NUP153. The peptide sequence was engineered to position a phenylalanine side chain in the location equivalent to Phe321 of CPSF6 and the difluorobenzyl group of GS-CA1, ensuring engagement with the Leu56/Asn57/Met66 subpocket. Flanking residues were selected to form hydrogen bonds with Gln67 and Arg173 and hydrophobic contacts with Ala105 and Leu172. The linear precursor was cyclized via head-to-tail amide bond formation to constrain the bioactive conformation.

Computational docking of Pep-1 into the CA hexamer (4XFZ) using IFD predicted binding in the canonical interdomain pocket, with a pose overlapping those of GS-CA1 and host peptides. Independent docking with PatchDock yielded a similar binding mode, confirming robustness of the prediction. The cyclic constraint reduced the entropic penalty of binding by preorganizing the pharmacophores, as evidenced by a favorable docking score comparable to GS-CA1.

To experimentally validate binding, recombinant HIV-1 CA hexamers were labeled with Alexa Fluor 647 and subjected to MicroScale Thermophoresis (MST) in the presence of increasing Pep-1 concentrations. The thermophoretic movement of labeled CA changed in a dose-dependent manner, reflecting complex formation. Data fitting to a quadratic binding equation yielded a Kd of 32 ± 3 nM for Pep-1. As a control, PF74 was tested under identical conditions and exhibited a Kd of 212 ± 7 nM, in excellent agreement with literature values (ITC-determined Kd = 262 nM). Thus, Pep-1 binds CA with ~7-fold higher affinity than PF74, demonstrating the success of the rational design approach.

### Example 4

The high-affinity binding of Pep-1 to HIV-1 CA translates into potent antiviral activity. In cell-based assays using TZM-bl reporter cells infected with HIV-1NL4-3, Pep-1 inhibited viral replication with an EC50 of 45 nM, compared to 310 nM for PF74. Cytotoxicity assays in MT-4 cells showed no significant toxicity up to 10 μM, yielding a selectivity index (CC50/EC50) >200. Time-of-addition experiments indicated that Pep-1 acts at early post-entry steps, consistent with disruption of CA-dependent nuclear import and/or uncoating.

Mechanistic studies using fate-of-capsid assays demonstrated that Pep-1 accelerates capsid disassembly in infected cells, as measured by reduced levels of particulate CA at 8 hours post-infection. Furthermore, quantitative PCR analysis revealed a significant reduction in integrated proviral DNA, but not in late reverse transcription products, suggesting that Pep-1 primarily blocks nuclear entry or integration rather than reverse transcription per se.

In combination studies, Pep-1 exhibited additive to synergistic effects when paired with reverse transcriptase inhibitors (e.g., tenofovir) or integrase strand transfer inhibitors (e.g., dolutegravir), supporting its potential use in cART regimens. Pharmacokinetic evaluation in mice following intravenous administration showed a plasma half-life of 4.2 hours for the linear form and 18.7 hours for the cyclic Pep-1, highlighting the stabilizing effect of macrocyclization.

These results establish Pep-1 as a prototype for a new class of peptide-based CA inhibitors with improved binding affinity, antiviral potency, and drug-like properties compared to existing small-molecule leads. The design principles disclosed herein are broadly applicable to the development of next-generation antivirals targeting protein–protein or protein–ligand interfaces.
