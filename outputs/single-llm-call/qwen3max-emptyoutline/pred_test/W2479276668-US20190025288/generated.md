# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of oligonucleotide therapeutics, particularly single-stranded oligonucleotides (SSOs) designed to modulate gene expression through antisense mechanisms. More specifically, the invention provides a novel in vitro assay system for predicting the hepatotoxic potential of SSOs prior to in vivo animal testing or clinical evaluation. This predictive assay utilizes primary mammalian hepatocytes, including both rodent and human cells, cultured under conditions that mimic physiological exposure to SSOs—specifically through gymnotic delivery, which refers to the unassisted cellular uptake of oligonucleotides without the use of transfection reagents or conjugation strategies. The assay measures a defined set of biomarkers, including lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels, glutathione (GSH) content, caspase-3/7 activity, and the secretion of specific cytokines such as interleukin-1α (IL1α) and macrophage inflammatory protein 1α (MIP1α), as well as the hepatocyte-specific microRNA miR-122. These readouts collectively enable the discrimination between SSOs that are likely to induce hepatotoxicity in vivo and those that are not. The invention further encompasses methods for using this assay to guide the selection and optimization of safer SSO drug candidates during preclinical development, thereby reducing reliance on animal models, accelerating lead prioritization, and mitigating clinical safety risks associated with liver injury. Additionally, the invention includes stereodefined oligonucleotide embodiments and libraries of sequence variants that can be screened using the disclosed assay to identify structural and sequence features correlated with reduced toxicity while maintaining therapeutic efficacy.

## BACKGROUND

Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) therapeutics represent a promising class of drugs capable of selectively modulating gene expression by hybridizing to target RNA molecules and inducing their degradation or functional inhibition. Single-stranded oligonucleotides (SSOs), particularly those incorporating modified nucleotides such as locked nucleic acids (LNAs) in a gapmer configuration, have demonstrated significant potential in targeting disease-relevant mRNAs through recruitment of RNase H1, an endogenous enzyme that cleaves the RNA strand of RNA-DNA heteroduplexes. Despite over three decades of research and development, the clinical success of SSO-based drugs has been limited, with only a small number of compounds receiving regulatory approval. A major barrier to broader therapeutic application has been the occurrence of dose-dependent toxicities, most notably hepatotoxicity and immunotoxicity, which have led to high attrition rates during preclinical and clinical development. Historically, these toxicities have been assessed primarily through in vivo studies in rodents and non-rodent species, which are resource-intensive, ethically challenging, and often fail to accurately predict human responses due to interspecies differences in metabolism, immune function, and cellular uptake mechanisms.

Preclinical in vivo studies have consistently shown that after systemic administration, SSOs accumulate predominantly in the liver, kidney, and spleen, with the liver being the primary site of both therapeutic activity and adverse effects. Toxic SSOs have been associated with elevated plasma levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), histopathological evidence of hepatocellular necrosis and apoptosis, and inflammatory responses including lymphoid hyperplasia and cellular infiltrates. While certain sequence motifs have been tentatively linked to hepatotoxic risk, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Two primary hypotheses have emerged: (1) hybridization-dependent off-target effects, where partial complementarity to non-target RNAs leads to unintended gene silencing; and (2) hybridization-independent effects, wherein the oligonucleotide acts as an aptamer, binding to intracellular proteins and disrupting their normal function. Both mechanisms may contribute to cellular stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative damage, and activation of cell death pathways.

Efforts to develop in vitro models capable of recapitulating in vivo hepatotoxicity have been hampered by the limitations of immortalized cell lines such as HepG2, which lack the full complement of metabolic enzymes, transporters, and uptake mechanisms present in primary hepatocytes. Moreover, many prior in vitro approaches relied on transfection reagents or lipid-based delivery systems to introduce oligonucleotides into cells, which artificially enhances uptake and may mask or exaggerate toxicity unrelated to the physiological mode of action. Gymnotic delivery—wherein oligonucleotides enter cells via natural endocytic pathways without exogenous assistance—has been shown to more accurately reflect the in vivo pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profile of SSOs. However, until now, no in vitro system employing gymnotic delivery in primary hepatocytes has been validated to reliably predict in vivo hepatotoxic outcomes across a diverse set of oligonucleotide sequences.

The absence of a predictive in vitro screening tool has necessitated the use of large animal cohorts in early safety assessments, delaying candidate selection and increasing development costs. Furthermore, discrepancies between rodent and human responses to SSOs have raised concerns about the translatability of preclinical findings. There is therefore a critical need for a human-relevant, mechanism-informed, and physiologically representative in vitro assay that can stratify SSOs based on their hepatotoxic liability before advancing to in vivo studies. Such an assay would not only improve the efficiency of drug discovery but also enhance patient safety by enabling the early elimination of high-risk candidates. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing a robust, reproducible, and scalable platform for hepatotoxicity prediction that integrates species-specific primary hepatocytes, gymnotic delivery, and a minimal yet highly informative panel of functional and biomarker readouts.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for predicting the in vivo hepatotoxic potential of single-stranded oligonucleotides (SSOs) using an in vitro assay system that closely mimics physiological conditions of exposure. The method comprises administering an SSO to primary mammalian hepatocytes—preferably from mouse or human origin—via gymnotic delivery, i.e., without the use of transfection reagents, lipid nanoparticles, or conjugate moieties, thereby allowing natural cellular uptake mechanisms to govern internalization. Following a defined exposure period, typically 48 to 72 hours, the hepatocytes are assessed for changes in key viability and stress markers, including extracellular lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity, intracellular ATP levels, reduced glutathione (GSH) content, and caspase-3/7 activation. In addition, the assay measures the secretion of specific biomarkers into the culture supernatant, notably interleukin-1α (IL1α), macrophage inflammatory protein 1α (MIP1α), and microRNA-122 (miR-122), all of which have been empirically validated to correlate with in vivo hepatotoxicity in murine models and, in the case of miR-122 and cytokines, are recognized indicators of human liver injury.

The invention further discloses that a combination of LDH and ATP measurements after 72 hours of continuous exposure to SSOs at concentrations ranging from 1 to 30 μM is sufficient to accurately discriminate between hepatotoxic and non-hepatotoxic oligonucleotides, as confirmed by comparison with historical in vivo data from mouse studies. This minimal readout set enables high-throughput screening while maintaining high predictive value. The assay is performed in freshly isolated or cryopreserved primary hepatocytes, which retain native expression of nucleic acid transporters, endocytic machinery, and metabolic pathways essential for authentic SSO processing and response. Importantly, the method has been validated across a diverse panel of 34 SSOs targeting multiple mRNAs, demonstrating a concordance rate of over 94% with in vivo hepatotoxicity outcomes.

In a preferred embodiment, the assay is conducted in co-cultures of primary hepatocytes and non-parenchymal cells (NPCs), such as Kupffer cells and liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, to account for potential contributions of immune-mediated inflammation to hepatotoxicity. However, the invention demonstrates that monocultures of primary hepatocytes alone are equally effective in predicting toxicity, indicating that direct hepatocellular damage is a primary driver of SSO-induced liver injury. The assay has also been successfully adapted to human primary hepatocytes, where it correctly identified the hepatotoxic potential of two clinically relevant SSOs known to cause grade 3 elevations in liver enzymes during Phase I trials, thereby establishing its translational relevance.

The invention additionally encompasses the use of this assay to guide the design and selection of safer SSO candidates. By screening libraries of sequence variants or stereodefined oligonucleotides—wherein the stereochemistry of phosphorothioate linkages is controlled—the method enables structure-toxicity relationship analysis. This facilitates the identification of chemical modifications or sequence motifs that minimize hepatotoxic risk without compromising target knockdown efficacy. The assay thus serves not only as a predictive safety screen but also as a mechanistic tool for understanding the molecular basis of SSO toxicity, ultimately supporting the rational engineering of next-generation antisense therapeutics with improved safety profiles.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Prediction of In Vivo Toxicity

The core innovation of the present invention lies in the establishment of a predictive in vitro assay that accurately forecasts the in vivo hepatotoxic potential of single-stranded oligonucleotides (SSOs) before any animal testing is initiated. This capability represents a significant advancement over prior art, which relied heavily on empirical in vivo observation without reliable preclinical screening tools. The predictive power of the assay stems from its faithful recapitulation of the physiological context in which SSOs interact with liver cells following systemic administration. Specifically, the assay employs primary hepatocytes—either murine or human—that are exposed to SSOs under gymnotic conditions, meaning the oligonucleotides are added directly to the culture medium without the aid of transfection reagents, electroporation, or conjugation to delivery-enhancing moieties. This approach ensures that cellular uptake occurs through the same endocytic and trafficking pathways operative in vivo, thereby preserving the natural kinetics, subcellular localization, and concentration gradients that influence toxicity.

Validation of the assay’s predictive accuracy was achieved through a rigorous comparative analysis involving a panel of 34 distinct SSOs, each with documented in vivo hepatotoxicity profiles in mice. These SSOs varied in target mRNA, sequence composition, and chemical architecture, including LNA-modified gapmers with phosphorothioate backbones. For each compound, in vivo hepatotoxicity was defined by elevation of plasma ALT levels and histopathological evidence of liver damage. In parallel, the same SSOs were tested in freshly isolated primary mouse hepatocytes using the disclosed in vitro protocol. After 72 hours of continuous exposure, two key readouts—extracellular LDH and intracellular ATP—were measured. LDH, a cytosolic enzyme released upon plasma membrane rupture, serves as a marker of necrotic cell death, while ATP levels reflect overall cellular metabolic health and viability. The results demonstrated a striking correlation: 32 out of 34 SSOs exhibited concordant outcomes between in vitro and in vivo assessments. Those that induced significant LDH release and ATP depletion in hepatocytes invariably caused hepatotoxicity in mice, whereas SSOs showing no such effects in vitro were benign in vivo. Only two compounds showed discordance, suggesting either a rare mechanism of toxicity not captured by the current readouts or a threshold effect requiring higher exposure than achieved in vitro.

This high degree of concordance establishes the assay as a reliable gatekeeper for early-stage candidate selection. By identifying “bad actors” before committing to costly and time-consuming animal studies, the method significantly reduces development risk and aligns with the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) in animal research. Moreover, the assay’s predictive scope extends beyond simple binary classification; it enables quantitative ranking of SSOs based on the magnitude of biomarker changes, facilitating the selection of leads with the widest therapeutic index. The ability to predict in vivo toxicity from in vitro data also supports regulatory submissions by providing mechanistic rationale for safety margins and dose selection. Importantly, the predictive model is not limited to a specific target or sequence class but is generalizable across diverse SSO chemistries, making it a versatile platform applicable throughout the antisense drug discovery pipeline.

### Primary Mammalian Hepatocyte Cells

Central to the functionality and physiological relevance of the disclosed assay is the use of primary mammalian hepatocytes as the cellular substrate. Unlike immortalized cell lines such as HepG2 or Huh7, which exhibit altered expression of drug transporters, metabolic enzymes, and endocytic receptors, primary hepatocytes retain the native molecular machinery necessary for authentic interaction with SSOs. This includes scavenger receptors (e.g., SR-B1, Stabilin-1/2), which mediate the uptake of phosphorothioate-modified oligonucleotides, as well as endosomal sorting complexes required for trafficking to the nucleus and cytoplasm—compartments where RNase H-mediated target cleavage and potential off-target interactions occur. The invention specifically utilizes hepatocytes derived from mice (C57BL/6 strain) and humans, both of which have been shown to support gymnotic uptake of SSOs and exhibit toxicity responses that mirror in vivo observations.

Primary mouse hepatocytes are isolated via a well-established two-step collagenase perfusion technique, which yields high-viability, non-transformed cells suitable for short-term culture. These cells are seeded onto collagen-coated plates to promote attachment and maintain differentiated functions, such as albumin synthesis and cytochrome P450 activity, for up to 72 hours—the duration of the standard assay. Human primary hepatocytes, obtained from commercial sources as cryopreserved vials, are similarly cultured under serum-free conditions after an initial attachment period. Although human hepatocytes generally exhibit lower sensitivity to SSOs—requiring higher concentrations to elicit comparable biomarker changes—their relative ranking of toxic versus non-toxic compounds aligns with murine data and, critically, with clinical outcomes for known hepatotoxic SSOs.

The choice of primary hepatocytes is not merely a matter of convenience but a fundamental requirement for assay fidelity. Control experiments using HepG2 cells under identical gymnotic conditions failed to detect any toxicity signals, even for SSOs known to be highly hepatotoxic in vivo, underscoring the inadequacy of transformed lines for this purpose. Primary hepatocytes, by contrast, not only internalize SSOs efficiently but also manifest the downstream consequences of toxic insult—oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, caspase activation, and cytokine release—in a manner that is quantitatively and temporally consistent with in vivo pathology. Thus, the invention mandates the use of primary hepatocytes as the gold standard cellular model for hepatotoxicity prediction, rejecting surrogate systems that lack physiological authenticity.

### Administering the Oligonucleotide—Gymnotic Delivery (Gymnosis)

A defining feature of the present invention is the use of gymnotic delivery—also known as gymnosis—for administering oligonucleotides to primary hepatocytes. Gymnosis refers to the process by which single-stranded oligonucleotides, particularly those with phosphorothioate-modified backbones, are taken up by cells in the absence of any transfection reagent, lipid carrier, or conjugate moiety. This mode of delivery is critical because it replicates the natural pharmacokinetic behavior of SSOs following systemic administration in vivo, where they circulate in plasma bound to proteins and are internalized by hepatocytes via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Artificial enhancement of uptake through cationic lipids or polymers, as commonly practiced in cell-based assays, distorts the intracellular concentration, subcellular distribution, and kinetics of oligonucleotide exposure, potentially leading to false-positive or false-negative toxicity signals.

In the disclosed method, SSOs are dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and added directly to the culture medium of attached primary hepatocytes at final concentrations typically ranging from 1 to 30 μM. The cells are then incubated for 48 to 72 hours, during which time the oligonucleotides are gradually internalized through natural endocytic pathways. This slow, sustained uptake mirrors the in vivo scenario, where peak liver concentrations are achieved over several hours to days post-dose. Crucially, all SSOs tested in the assay demonstrate target mRNA knockdown under these conditions, confirming productive intracellular delivery and engagement with the intended molecular machinery (e.g., RNase H1). The absence of transfection reagents also eliminates confounding cytotoxic effects associated with these agents, ensuring that observed toxicity is attributable solely to the oligonucleotide itself.

The importance of gymnotic delivery is further evidenced by the failure of non-primary cell models to respond to SSOs under the same conditions. HepG2 cells, for instance, show negligible uptake and no toxicity, highlighting that only cells equipped with the appropriate uptake machinery—such as primary hepatocytes—can serve as valid models. By adhering strictly to gymnotic protocols, the invention ensures that the assay reflects the true biological interaction between SSOs and liver cells, thereby enhancing its predictive validity for in vivo outcomes. This approach represents a paradigm shift from conventional in vitro screening, which often prioritizes signal strength over physiological relevance, and instead emphasizes fidelity to the in vivo context as the cornerstone of reliable toxicity prediction.

### Control Data/Reference Values

To ensure the reliability, reproducibility, and interpretability of the hepatotoxicity assay, the invention incorporates a robust framework of control data and reference values. These controls serve multiple purposes: they validate the performance of each experimental run, establish baseline biomarker levels for healthy hepatocytes, and provide a benchmark against which test SSOs are evaluated. The assay employs both negative and positive controls. Negative controls consist of vehicle-treated hepatocytes (e.g., PBS or buffer alone) and non-toxic reference SSOs—such as compounds 32, 33, and 35 from the myd88-targeting tool set—which have been extensively characterized in vivo and shown to lack hepatotoxic effects despite achieving significant target knockdown. These controls define the normal range for LDH, ATP, GSH, caspase activity, and cytokine/miR-122 secretion under assay conditions.

Positive controls include known hepatotoxic SSOs, such as compounds 36, 37, 43, and 47, which consistently induce marked increases in LDH, decreases in ATP, and elevations in IL1α, MIP1α, and miR-122. These reference toxicants are included in every screening batch to confirm that the cellular system is responsive and that assay conditions are optimal. Additionally, inter-assay variability is minimized by normalizing raw data to vehicle controls and expressing results as fold-changes or percent inhibition relative to baseline. Statistical thresholds for toxicity calls are established using historical data from the reference compound panel; for example, an SSO is classified as hepatotoxic if it induces LDH release exceeding two standard deviations above the mean of negative controls and/or reduces ATP levels by more than 30% compared to vehicle.

The invention also defines reference concentration ranges for SSO testing. While concentrations from 1 to 30 μM are used to assess dose-response relationships, the primary decision point for candidate selection is typically at 10 μM, a level that approximates hepatic exposure achieved in vivo at therapeutic doses. This standardized concentration allows for cross-compound comparisons and facilitates the generation of structure-toxicity maps. Furthermore, the assay includes quality control metrics for hepatocyte health, such as baseline albumin secretion and morphology, to exclude runs compromised by poor cell viability. By embedding these controls and reference standards into the assay workflow, the invention ensures that toxicity predictions are not only accurate but also consistent across laboratories, operators, and time, thereby supporting its adoption as a regulatory-acceptable screening tool.

### (Determination of) Hepatotoxicity

The determination of hepatotoxicity in the present invention is based on a multiparametric assessment of cellular health and stress responses in primary hepatocytes following exposure to single-stranded oligonucleotides (SSOs). Rather than relying on a single endpoint, the assay integrates a battery of complementary readouts that collectively capture the spectrum of events leading to liver injury. The primary determinants of hepatotoxicity are extracellular lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity and intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels, measured after 72 hours of continuous SSO exposure. LDH, a stable cytosolic enzyme, is released into the culture medium upon plasma membrane damage, serving as a sensitive indicator of necrotic cell death. ATP, the central energy currency of the cell, reflects mitochondrial integrity and overall metabolic function; its depletion signifies bioenergetic collapse and is often an early event in toxicant-induced cell death.

Secondary markers provide mechanistic insight and enhance diagnostic confidence. Reduced glutathione (GSH) levels are measured to assess oxidative stress, as many hepatotoxicants deplete this key antioxidant, leading to reactive oxygen species accumulation and macromolecular damage. Caspase-3/7 activity is quantified to detect the initiation of apoptotic pathways, which the invention shows are activated as early as 48 hours post-exposure to toxic SSOs. In addition, the assay monitors the secretion of specific biomarkers into the supernatant: interleukin-1α (IL1α) and macrophage inflammatory protein 1α (MIP1α), which are pro-inflammatory cytokines upregulated in response to cellular stress and damage, and microRNA-122 (miR-122), a liver-enriched miRNA that is passively released upon hepatocellular injury and is a well-validated clinical biomarker of drug-induced liver injury (DILI).

A compound is classified as hepatotoxic if it induces statistically significant and concentration-dependent changes in at least two of the primary readouts (LDH and ATP), with supportive evidence from secondary markers. For instance, a toxic SSO will typically show >2-fold increase in LDH, >30% reduction in ATP, elevated caspase activity, and increased miR-122 and cytokine secretion. Non-toxic SSOs, by contrast, produce minimal or no changes across all parameters, even at high concentrations. The temporal profile of these responses is also informative: MIP1α rises within 24 hours, IL1α by 48 hours, and miR-122 by 48–72 hours, allowing for staged assessment if needed. This comprehensive, tiered approach to hepatotoxicity determination ensures high sensitivity and specificity, minimizing false classifications and providing a rich dataset for mechanistic follow-up.

### Parent Oligonucleotide

The term “parent oligonucleotide” as used in the present invention refers to a lead single-stranded oligonucleotide (SSO) candidate that has demonstrated desired pharmacological activity—such as potent target mRNA knockdown—but whose hepatotoxic potential requires evaluation and potential mitigation. The parent oligonucleotide serves as the starting point for structure-toxicity relationship studies and is typically a gapmer design comprising a central DNA region flanked by modified nucleotides, such as locked nucleic acids (LNAs) or 2′-O-methoxyethyl (MOE) residues, to enhance binding affinity and nuclease resistance. The backbone is usually composed of phosphorothioate linkages, which confer protein-binding properties that facilitate tissue distribution and cellular uptake but may also contribute to hybridization-independent toxicity.

In the context of the disclosed assay, the parent oligonucleotide is first tested in primary hepatocytes under gymnotic conditions to establish its baseline hepatotoxicity profile. If the parent compound is found to be hepatotoxic—as indicated by elevated LDH, reduced ATP, and other biomarker changes—it becomes the template for generating a library of variants aimed at reducing toxicity while preserving efficacy. These variants may differ in sequence (e.g., by altering nucleotides in the wings or gap region), chemical modification pattern (e.g., substituting LNA with constrained ethyl (cEt) or other high-affinity chemistries), or stereochemistry (in the case of stereodefined phosphorothioate linkages). Each variant is then screened using the same assay protocol, and those showing improved safety profiles are advanced for further characterization.

The invention emphasizes that hepatotoxicity is not an inevitable consequence of SSO activity; rather, it is a property that can be engineered out through informed design. For example, certain sequence motifs—particularly CpG dinucleotides or GC-rich stretches—have been associated with immune activation and cytotoxicity, and their removal or modification in the parent sequence can yield safer derivatives. Similarly, fine-tuning the length of the DNA gap or the number of modified wings can modulate RNase H recruitment efficiency and off-target hybridization potential. By anchoring the optimization process in the predictive hepatotoxicity assay, the invention enables a rational, data-driven approach to lead refinement, transforming a potentially toxic parent oligonucleotide into a clinical candidate with an acceptable safety margin.

### Library of Variants

The invention provides a systematic methodology for generating and screening a library of oligonucleotide variants derived from a parent SSO to identify derivatives with reduced hepatotoxic potential. This library is constructed by introducing controlled variations into the parent sequence or chemical architecture, guided by emerging insights into structure-toxicity relationships. Variants may differ in one or more of the following attributes: (1) nucleotide sequence, particularly in regions implicated in off-target hybridization or protein binding; (2) type and position of sugar modifications (e.g., LNA, cEt, MOE, 2′-fluoro); (3) length of the central DNA gap and flanking wings; (4) number and placement of phosphorothioate linkages; and (5) stereochemical configuration of chiral phosphorothioate centers, in the case of stereodefined oligonucleotides.

The library is designed to explore a multidimensional parameter space, enabling the identification of key determinants of toxicity. For instance, a subset of variants might systematically replace each LNA residue in the parent sequence with a different high-affinity modification to assess the impact on hepatocyte stress responses. Another subset might truncate or extend the DNA gap to modulate RNase H activity and hybridization stringency. Sequence-scrambled or mismatched controls are included to distinguish hybridization-dependent from hybridization-independent effects. Each variant is synthesized using standard solid-phase techniques and purified to homogeneity before being subjected to the hepatotoxicity assay.

Screening is performed in parallel under identical conditions: primary hepatocytes are exposed to each variant at a fixed concentration (e.g., 10 μM) for 72 hours via gymnotic delivery, and LDH and ATP are measured as primary endpoints. Variants that show significant attenuation of toxicity signals—defined as LDH and ATP levels indistinguishable from negative controls—are flagged as hits. These hits are then validated in dose-response experiments and assessed for target knockdown efficacy to ensure that safety improvements have not come at the cost of potency. The resulting data generate a toxicity landscape that maps structural features to biological outcomes, informing the design of next-generation libraries with even greater safety margins. This iterative cycle of design, synthesis, and screening, powered by the predictive assay, accelerates the discovery of oligonucleotide therapeutics that are both effective and well-tolerated.

### Stereodefined Oligonucleotide Embodiments:

A significant advancement enabled by the present invention is the ability to evaluate and optimize stereodefined oligonucleotides—compounds in which the stereochemistry of each chiral phosphorothioate linkage is controlled rather than randomized. Conventional phosphorothioate SSOs contain a mixture of Rp and Sp diastereomers at each modified internucleoside linkage, resulting in a complex ensemble of stereoisomers with potentially divergent biological properties. Recent synthetic breakthroughs now allow for the production of oligonucleotides with defined stereochemistry, offering a new dimension for tuning pharmacokinetics, efficacy, and safety. The invention discloses that stereodefined embodiments can be screened using the hepatotoxicity assay to identify configurations that minimize adverse effects.

In practice, a parent SSO with a fully randomized phosphorothioate backbone is compared against a series of stereodefined analogs, each differing in the pattern of Rp/Sp linkages. These analogs may be fully stereopure (all linkages of defined configuration) or partially stereodefined (selected linkages controlled). The assay reveals that certain stereochemical patterns are associated with reduced LDH release, higher ATP retention, and lower cytokine induction, suggesting that protein-binding interactions—mediated by the chiral phosphorothioate centers—can be modulated to avoid toxic pathways. For example, an Sp-rich configuration in the wing regions might reduce affinity for a hepatotoxicity-inducing protein, while an Rp-preference in the gap could maintain RNase H recruitment.

The ability to link stereochemistry to toxicity outcomes represents a powerful tool for rational design. By integrating stereodefined synthesis with the predictive assay, developers can move beyond empirical screening toward precision engineering of oligonucleotide therapeutics. The invention thus encompasses not only the assay itself but also the use of its outputs to guide the creation of safer, stereodefined drug candidates. This approach is particularly valuable for rescuing promising but toxic leads, as minor stereochemical adjustments may abrogate harmful interactions without altering sequence or overall chemistry. As stereodefined manufacturing becomes more scalable, the embodiments disclosed herein will play a pivotal role in bringing safer antisense drugs to patients.

### Primary Hepatocytes Assay Example:

An exemplary implementation of the hepatotoxicity assay is described as follows. Primary hepatocytes are isolated from male C57BL/6 mice (10–13 weeks old) via retrograde two-step collagenase perfusion. The liver is first perfused with calcium-free, EGTA-supplemented Hank’s balanced salt solution to chelate divalent cations and disrupt cell-cell adhesions, followed by a collagenase-containing solution with calcium to digest the extracellular matrix. The resulting cell suspension is filtered, washed, and purified by Percoll gradient centrifugation to remove dead cells and non-parenchymal contaminants. Viable hepatocytes are resuspended in William’s Medium E (WME) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, penicillin, and streptomycin, and seeded onto collagen-coated 96-well plates at a density of 25,000 cells per well. After a 3–4 hour attachment period, the serum-containing medium is replaced with serum-free WME, and SSOs dissolved in PBS are added to achieve final concentrations of 1, 10, and 30 μM. The plates are incubated at 37°C in 5% CO₂ for 72 hours.

At the end of the exposure period, the supernatant is collected for LDH and miR-122 analysis, while the cell layer is processed for ATP and GSH assays. LDH activity is measured using a colorimetric kit, with absorbance read at 490 nm. ATP levels are quantified via luminescence using the CellTiter-Glo® reagent, which generates a signal proportional to viable cell number. GSH is assessed by fluorescence after labeling with monochlorobimane. In parallel, caspase-3/7 activity is measured at 48 hours using a luminescent substrate. For cytokine profiling, a subset of wells is used to collect supernatant at 24, 48, and 72 hours for multiplex ELISA analysis of IL1α and MIP1α. All experiments are performed in triplicate, with vehicle and reference toxic/non-toxic SSOs included as controls. Data are normalized to vehicle and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Compounds showing LDH >200% of control and ATP <70% of control at 10 μM are classified as hepatotoxic. This standardized protocol ensures reproducibility and enables direct comparison across compound series.

## Definitions

### Oligonucleotide

As used herein, the term “oligonucleotide” refers to a linear polymer composed of a plurality of covalently linked nucleotide monomers, typically ranging from 8 to 50 nucleotides in length, though lengths outside this range are also contemplated. Each nucleotide monomer consists of a sugar moiety (e.g., ribose, deoxyribose, or a modified sugar such as 2′-O-methoxyethyl or locked nucleic acid), a phosphate or phosphorothioate group forming the internucleoside linkage, and a nitrogenous base (adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine, or uracil, or modified bases thereof). Oligonucleotides of the invention are single-stranded and may be unmodified or chemically modified to enhance stability, binding affinity, or pharmacokinetic properties. They are designed to hybridize to a complementary RNA target sequence through Watson-Crick base pairing, thereby modulating gene expression via mechanisms such as RNase H-mediated degradation, steric blockade of translation, or modulation of splicing.

### Antisense Oligonucleotides (ASOs)

The term “antisense oligonucleotides” or “ASOs” denotes a subclass of oligonucleotides that are specifically designed to bind to a target RNA molecule in a sequence-specific manner, leading to the downregulation or functional alteration of the corresponding gene product. ASOs of the invention are typically single-stranded and may adopt a gapmer architecture, wherein a central block of DNA nucleotides (capable of recruiting RNase H) is flanked by blocks of modified RNA-like nucleotides (e.g., LNA, cEt, MOE) that enhance binding affinity and nuclease resistance. The primary mechanism of action involves the formation of a heteroduplex with the target RNA, which is recognized by RNase H1, resulting in cleavage and degradation of the RNA strand. ASOs may also act through steric hindrance, preventing ribosomal assembly or spliceosome recognition, depending on the target site and chemical design.

### Modified Internucleoside Linkages

“Modified internucleoside linkages” refer to alterations in the phosphate backbone of the oligonucleotide that replace one or more of the non-bridging oxygen atoms with other atoms or groups, most commonly sulfur to form phosphorothioate linkages. Phosphorothioate modifications are widely used in therapeutic oligonucleotides to increase resistance to nucleases, enhance binding to plasma proteins (thereby improving pharmacokinetics), and facilitate cellular uptake. Other modifications include phosphorodithioates, methylphosphonates, and boranophosphates. In the context of the present invention, phosphorothioate linkages are particularly relevant as they introduce chiral centers, and the stereochemistry of these linkages can influence toxicity, as disclosed in the stereodefined embodiments.

### Modulation of Expression

“Modulation of expression” encompasses any alteration in the level or function of a gene product, including but not limited to downregulation (reduction) or upregulation (increase) of mRNA or protein levels. In the context of antisense oligonucleotides, modulation typically refers to the reduction of target gene expression through RNA degradation or translational inhibition. However, the term also includes splice-switching events that alter isoform expression, as well as steric blockade of non-coding RNA function. The extent of modulation is quantifiable by methods such as qRT-PCR, Western blotting, or functional assays, and is a key parameter in evaluating the efficacy of an oligonucleotide candidate.

### Modified Oligonucleotides

“Modified oligonucleotides” are oligonucleotides that incorporate one or more chemical alterations to the sugar, base, or phosphate backbone, relative to naturally occurring DNA or RNA. Common modifications include 2′-O-alkyl (e.g., 2′-O-methoxyethyl), 2′-fluoro, locked nucleic acid (LNA), constrained ethyl (cEt), and phosphorothioate linkages. These modifications are introduced to improve nuclease resistance, binding affinity (melting temperature, Tm), cellular uptake, tissue distribution, and pharmacokinetic properties. The invention specifically contemplates modified oligonucleotides in the form of gapmers, where modified nucleotides are positioned in the wings to protect the central DNA gap, which is essential for RNase H activity.

### RNase H Activity and Recruitment

“RNase H activity and recruitment” refers to the enzymatic cleavage of the RNA strand in an RNA-DNA heteroduplex by the endogenous enzyme RNase H1. This mechanism is a cornerstone of antisense technology, as it allows for the catalytic degradation of target mRNA without requiring the oligonucleotide to be internalized into the nucleus. Recruitment of RNase H1 occurs when the oligonucleotide contains a contiguous stretch of at least 4–5 DNA nucleotides hybridized to the target RNA. The efficiency of recruitment and cleavage depends on the length and position of the DNA gap, as well as the stability of the duplex, which is influenced by the chemistry of the flanking regions. The invention leverages this mechanism for therapeutic gene silencing while using the hepatotoxicity assay to ensure that RNase H recruitment does not inadvertently trigger toxic pathways.

### Efficacy

“Efficacy” in the context of the present invention refers to the ability of an oligonucleotide to achieve the desired pharmacological effect, typically measured as the extent of target mRNA or protein reduction in vitro or in vivo. Efficacy is evaluated alongside safety, as an ideal candidate must demonstrate potent target modulation without inducing unacceptable toxicity. In the hepatotoxicity assay, efficacy is confirmed by measuring target knockdown in parallel with toxicity readouts, ensuring that non-toxic classification is not due to lack of cellular uptake or target engagement. The therapeutic index—the ratio of toxic dose to efficacious dose—is a key metric derived from combined efficacy and toxicity data.

### Conjugate Moieties

“Conjugate moieties” are chemical groups attached to an oligonucleotide to enhance its delivery, stability, or targeting. Common examples include N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) for hepatocyte-specific delivery via the asialoglycoprotein receptor, cholesterol for improved membrane association, or peptides for cell-penetrating properties. However, the hepatotoxicity assay of the present invention explicitly excludes the use of conjugate moieties during the initial screening phase, as their presence would interfere with the assessment of intrinsic oligonucleotide toxicity under gymnotic conditions. Conjugates may be evaluated in later stages once the base oligonucleotide has been deemed safe.

### Stereodefined Oligonucleotides

“Stereodefined oligonucleotides” are oligonucleotides in which the stereochemistry of each chiral phosphorothioate linkage is controlled, resulting in a single, defined diastereomer or a specific mixture of diastereomers, as opposed to the random stereochemistry found in conventional phosphorothioate oligonucleotides. The invention recognizes that stereochemistry can profoundly influence protein binding, cellular uptake, and toxicity, and thus provides methods for evaluating stereodefined variants using the hepatotoxicity assay to identify configurations with improved safety profiles.

## Immunotoxicity Biomarker Assay:

In addition to hepatotoxicity, the invention provides an integrated immunotoxicity biomarker assay that detects pro-inflammatory responses induced by SSOs in liver cell cultures. This assay is particularly relevant given that SSOs can activate innate immune pathways, leading to cytokine release and systemic inflammation. The method involves measuring the secretion of specific cytokines—namely interleukin-1α (IL1α) and macrophage inflammatory protein 1α (MIP1α)—into the supernatant of primary hepatocyte or hepatocyte-NPC co-cultures following SSO exposure. These biomarkers were selected based on their consistent upregulation in response to hepatotoxic SSOs and their established roles in liver inflammation and injury. MIP1α, a chemokine that recruits monocytes and lymphocytes, shows early induction (within 24 hours), while IL1α, a potent pro-inflammatory cytokine released by damaged cells, peaks at 48–72 hours. Supernatants are collected at multiple time points and analyzed using multiplex ELISA or Luminex platforms. Elevated levels of these cytokines, particularly in a time- and concentration-dependent manner, serve as indicators of immunotoxic potential and complement the hepatotoxicity readouts. The assay can be performed in parallel with the main hepatotoxicity screen, providing a comprehensive safety profile that captures both direct cellular damage and immune-mediated components of SSO toxicity.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Example 1 demonstrates the validation of the hepatotoxicity assay using a panel of seven LNA-modified gapmer SSOs targeting mouse myd88 mRNA. Primary mouse hepatocytes were isolated and treated with each SSO at 10 μM for 72 hours via gymnotic delivery. Target knockdown was confirmed by qRT-PCR, showing 50–90% reduction in myd88 mRNA for all compounds, confirming cellular uptake and activity. Hepatotoxicity was assessed by measuring LDH release and ATP levels. SSOs 36, 37, 43, and 47 induced significant LDH elevation (2–4 fold over vehicle) and ATP depletion (40–70% reduction), classifying them as hepatotoxic. In contrast, SSOs 32, 33, and 35 showed no significant changes, consistent with their known in vivo safety. This example established the assay’s ability to differentiate toxic from non-toxic SSOs within a closely related sequence family.

### Example 2

Example 2 evaluates the time course of apoptotic signaling in response to toxic SSOs. Primary mouse hepatocytes were treated with SSO 43 (10 μM) and caspase-3/7 activity was measured at 24, 48, and 72 hours. No activation was observed at 24 hours, but a 3-fold increase was detected at 48 hours, persisting at 72 hours. Non-toxic SSO 32 showed no caspase activation at any time point. This demonstrates that apoptosis is an early and sustained event in SSO-induced hepatotoxicity, providing a mechanistic link between initial cellular stress and eventual cell death.

### Example 3

Example 3 compares the response of primary hepatocytes versus HepG2 cells to the same SSOs. HepG2 cells were treated with toxic SSO 43 and non-toxic SSO 32 under identical gymnotic conditions. No significant changes in LDH, ATP, or caspase activity were observed in HepG2 cells, even at 30 μM, whereas primary hepatocytes showed clear toxicity signals. This confirms that primary hepatocytes are essential for the assay’s predictive power and that immortalized lines are inadequate surrogates.

### Example 4

Example 4 investigates the role of non-parenchymal cells (NPCs) in SSO toxicity. Mouse hepatocyte-NPC co-cultures were treated with SSOs 32, 35 (safe) and 43, 47 (toxic). Toxic SSOs induced LDH release, ATP depletion, and GSH reduction in co-cultures, similar to hepatocyte monocultures. Additionally, NPCs alone showed LDH elevation in response to toxic SSOs, indicating that both cell types are susceptible. Cytokine profiling revealed time-dependent increases in MIP1α (24h) and IL1α (48h) only in co-cultures treated with toxic SSOs, highlighting the contribution of immune cells to the inflammatory response.

### Example 5

Example 5 validates the assay with an expanded panel of 34 SSOs targeting various mRNAs. Primary mouse hepatocytes were treated with each SSO at 10 μM for 72 hours, and LDH/ATP were measured. Results showed 32/34 concordance with in vivo hepatotoxicity data, with only two false negatives. This large-scale validation confirms the assay’s generalizability across diverse sequences and targets.

### Example 6

Example 6 adapts the assay to human primary hepatocytes using the myd88 SSO panel. At 10–30 μM, toxic SSOs 37 and 43 induced LDH release and ATP depletion, while safe SSOs 32, 33, and 35 did not. Although human cells required higher concentrations, the relative ranking matched mouse data, supporting cross-species relevance.

### Example 7

Example 7 tests two clinically hepatotoxic SSOs (targeting Survivin and Bcl2) in both mouse and human hepatocytes. Both compounds induced LDH elevation and ATP reduction in both species, correctly predicting their known clinical liver toxicity. This demonstrates the assay’s translational value for human risk assessment.

### Example 8

Example 8 explores oxidative stress as a mechanism of toxicity. GSH levels were measured in hepatocytes treated with SSOs. Toxic SSOs 37, 43, and 47 caused significant GSH depletion (50–80%), while safe SSOs had no effect. This implicates oxidative stress as a contributing factor to SSO-induced hepatotoxicity.

### Example 9

Example 9 evaluates miR-122 as a biomarker. Supernatants from hepatocyte monocultures and co-cultures treated with toxic SSOs 43 and 47 showed 4–10 fold increases in miR-122 after 48 hours, correlating with LDH release. This confirms miR-122 as a sensitive and specific indicator of hepatocellular damage in the assay.
