# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of vaccinology has long sought to develop immunization strategies that elicit robust and durable protective immunity while simultaneously offering practical advantages in terms of administration, stability, distribution, and patient compliance. Traditional parenteral vaccines—administered via intramuscular, subcutaneous, or intradermal routes—have proven highly effective in generating systemic immune responses, particularly neutralizing antibodies in the bloodstream. However, these approaches often fail to induce significant mucosal immunity, which is critical for protection against pathogens that initiate infection at mucosal surfaces such as the respiratory, gastrointestinal, or urogenital tracts. Influenza virus, for example, enters the human body primarily through the upper respiratory mucosa, and secretory IgA (sIgA) antibodies at this site can neutralize the virus before it establishes systemic infection. Despite this, conventional injectable influenza vaccines predominantly stimulate serum IgG responses with minimal induction of mucosal sIgA, thereby leaving a gap in frontline immune defense.

Oral vaccination represents a compelling alternative that leverages the natural architecture of the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT)—a sophisticated network of inductive sites including Peyer’s patches, isolated lymphoid follicles, and mesenteric lymph nodes—to prime both systemic and mucosal immune responses. The GALT is uniquely equipped to sample luminal antigens and orchestrate adaptive immune responses that not only protect the intestinal mucosa but also disseminate effector and memory lymphocytes to distant mucosal sites through a process known as the common mucosal immune system. This phenomenon enables an orally administered vaccine to potentially confer protection not only in the gut but also in the respiratory tract, salivary glands, mammary tissue, and other mucosal compartments. Moreover, oral delivery eliminates the need for needles, reduces the risk of needle-stick injuries, simplifies logistics in resource-limited settings, and enhances patient acceptability—particularly among children and needle-averse individuals.

Despite these theoretical advantages, the development of effective oral vaccines has been historically challenging due to multiple physiological barriers. The acidic environment of the stomach can denature protein-based antigens or inactivate live viral vectors. Proteolytic enzymes in the duodenum and jejunum further degrade macromolecules before they reach immune inductive sites. Additionally, the intestinal epithelium presents a tight physical barrier that limits antigen uptake, and the gut immune system is inherently biased toward tolerance to avoid chronic inflammation against dietary and commensal antigens. Consequently, many early oral vaccine candidates failed to achieve sufficient immunogenicity in humans, leading to skepticism about the feasibility of non-replicating oral platforms.

Recent advances in vector biology, formulation science, and targeted delivery technologies have begun to overcome these obstacles. Recombinant adenoviral (rAd) vectors, in particular, have emerged as potent vaccine platforms due to their ability to efficiently transduce antigen-presenting cells, drive strong T-cell responses, and support high-level transgene expression. When administered orally in a protected formulation, rAd vectors can survive transit through the upper gastrointestinal tract and deliver their payload directly to the GALT. However, a critical yet unresolved question has been: *where* in the small intestine should the vaccine be released to maximize immune induction? The human small intestine is anatomically and immunologically heterogeneous. The jejunum, located proximally, is characterized by rapid transit, high enzymatic activity, and relatively sparse lymphoid aggregates. In contrast, the ileum, situated distally just before the ileocecal valve, harbors a significantly higher density of Peyer’s patches—the primary inductive sites for mucosal immune responses in the gut. Animal studies have yielded conflicting results regarding optimal release sites; for instance, ovine models suggested superior immunogenicity with jejunal delivery, whereas murine data pointed to the ileum as the more immunologically active region. Extrapolation of these findings to humans has been hampered by the lack of precise, non-invasive methods to deliver vaccines to specific intestinal segments in clinical trials.

Prior attempts to target intestinal release have relied on enteric-coated tablets designed to dissolve at predetermined pH thresholds, typically above pH 5.5–6.0, which corresponds roughly to the duodenojejunal junction. However, gastric emptying time, intestinal pH gradients, and individual variability in gastrointestinal motility introduce significant uncertainty in the actual site of tablet disintegration and antigen release. As a result, the correlation between formulation design and biological effect has remained imprecise, hindering rational optimization of oral vaccine platforms.

To address this knowledge gap, the present invention employs a radio-controlled capsule (RCC) system that enables real-time monitoring and on-demand release of a liquid-formulation recombinant adenoviral influenza vaccine at precisely defined locations within the small intestine—specifically, either the jejunum or the ileum. This technology provides unprecedented control over antigen deposition, allowing for direct comparison of immune outcomes based solely on anatomical release site, independent of formulation variables. The clinical study described herein demonstrates that ileal release of the rAd vaccine consistently yields higher frequencies of antigen-specific antibody-secreting cells (ASCs), greater serum neutralizing antibody titers, and more robust induction of gut-homing B-cell phenotypes compared to jejunal release. These findings establish, for the first time in humans, that the ileum is the preferred site for oral vaccine delivery when using a non-replicating adenoviral vector platform.

Furthermore, the invention reveals that oral rAd vaccination induces not only systemic IgG responses but also a coordinated mucosal immune program characterized by the generation of α4β7<sup>high</sup> CCR9<sup>+</sup> IgA<sup>+</sup> B cells—phenotypically defined lymphocytes programmed to home to the small intestinal lamina propria. Critically, the vaccine also stimulates a subset of α4β1<sup>+</sup> β7<sup>intermediate/negative</sup> IgA<sup>+</sup> B cells, which are associated with homing to non-intestinal mucosal tissues such as the respiratory tract. This dual tropism explains the observed increases in HA-specific IgA in both fecal and nasal secretions post-vaccination, despite methodological challenges in mucosal sampling. The ability to simultaneously engage intestinal and extra-intestinal mucosal immunity positions this oral rAd platform as a uniquely versatile strategy against pathogens like influenza that infect via respiratory mucosa but may benefit from cross-compartmental immune surveillance.

In summary, the background of the invention lies at the intersection of three unmet needs: (1) the desire for needle-free vaccines with improved compliance and distribution profiles; (2) the necessity of inducing mucosal immunity for optimal protection against mucosally transmitted pathogens; and (3) the lack of precise knowledge regarding optimal anatomical targeting within the human intestine for maximal immunogenicity. The present invention resolves these challenges by demonstrating that targeted ileal delivery of a liquid-formulated rAd influenza vaccine via RCC technology elicits superior systemic and mucosal immune responses compared to jejunal delivery, thereby providing a foundational principle for the rational design of next-generation oral vaccines.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for eliciting enhanced systemic and mucosal immune responses in a human subject through the targeted oral delivery of a recombinant adenoviral vaccine to the ileum of the small intestine. Specifically, the invention discloses that releasing a liquid formulation of a recombinant adenovirus encoding an influenza hemagglutinin (HA) antigen directly into the ileum—achieved via a radio-controlled capsule (RCC) that permits real-time tracking and on-demand ejection of its contents—results in significantly improved immunogenicity compared to identical delivery to the jejunum. This enhanced response is evidenced by higher frequencies of influenza HA-specific IgG and IgA antibody-secreting cells (ASCs) in peripheral blood, increased rates of seroconversion as measured by hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) and microneutralization (MN) assays, and greater induction of B-cell populations expressing gut-homing receptors α4β7<sup>high</sup> and CCR9.

The invention further encompasses the identification and characterization of distinct B-cell subsets induced by ileal versus jejunal vaccine delivery. Ileal release preferentially expands α4β7<sup>high</sup> CD19<sup>+</sup> CD27<sup>high</sup> IgA<sup>+</sup> pre-plasma cells and α4β7<sup>high</sup> CD19<sup>+</sup> CD27<sup>intermediate</sup> IgA<sup>+</sup> memory B cells, which are programmed to migrate to the intestinal lamina propria. In contrast, jejunal delivery shows a relative enrichment for α4β1<sup>+</sup> β7<sup>intermediate/negative</sup> IgA<sup>+</sup> B cells, which exhibit tropism for non-intestinal mucosal sites such as the respiratory tract. This differential homing receptor expression profile correlates with detectable increases in HA-specific IgA in both fecal and nasal secretions, confirming the vaccine’s capacity to engage the common mucosal immune system.

Additionally, the invention provides a validated clinical protocol for assessing mucosal immune responses in humans following oral vaccination, overcoming limitations of traditional mucosal sampling through the use of flow cytometric analysis of homing receptor expression on circulating B cells. This approach offers superior sensitivity and reliability compared to direct measurement of secretory IgA in mucosal fluids, which is often confounded by proteolytic degradation, variable sample collection, and high background from prior pathogen exposure.

In one aspect, the invention relates to a pharmaceutical composition comprising a recombinant adenovirus encoding an influenza HA antigen, formulated as a liquid suspension in a volume of approximately 0.9 mL, wherein the composition is contained within a radio-controlled capsule configured for oral administration and remote-triggered release in the ileum. In another aspect, the invention provides a method of vaccinating a human subject against influenza, comprising orally administering said composition via the RCC and triggering release upon confirmation of ileal positioning by gamma scintigraphy.

The invention also includes diagnostic and prognostic methods based on the quantification of α4β7<sup>high</sup> CCR9<sup>+</sup> IgA<sup>+</sup> B cells in peripheral blood as a biomarker of successful mucosal immune priming following oral vaccination. Such methods enable real-time monitoring of vaccine efficacy without reliance on invasive mucosal biopsies or unreliable secretory antibody measurements.

Overall, the invention establishes that anatomical targeting within the small intestine is a critical determinant of oral vaccine efficacy and that ileal delivery represents a superior strategy for maximizing both systemic and mucosal immunity. These findings provide a scientific foundation for the development of optimized oral vaccine formulations—such as enteric-coated tablets engineered to dissolve specifically in the ileum—and support the broader application of site-specific intestinal delivery for vaccines against other mucosally transmitted pathogens.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### I. Definitions

As used herein, the term “recombinant adenovirus” or “rAd” refers to a genetically modified adenoviral vector in which at least one native viral gene has been deleted or replaced with a heterologous nucleic acid sequence encoding an antigen of interest, such as an influenza hemagglutinin (HA) protein. The rAd retains the ability to infect mammalian cells and express the encoded antigen but is replication-deficient due to deletions in essential early genes (e.g., E1 and/or E3 regions), ensuring safety in human subjects. The term encompasses adenoviruses derived from any human or non-human serotype, including but not limited to Ad5, Ad26, or chimpanzee adenoviruses, provided they are capable of transducing human antigen-presenting cells in the gut-associated lymphoid tissue.

The phrase “targeted delivery to the ileum” means the controlled release of a vaccine formulation at a location within the distal portion of the small intestine, specifically downstream of the ligament of Treitz and proximal to the ileocecal valve, where Peyer’s patches are most densely concentrated. This is distinguished from “targeted delivery to the jejunum,” which refers to release in the proximal or mid-portion of the small intestine, typically within 100–200 cm distal to the pylorus, characterized by fewer organized lymphoid structures. Delivery is considered “targeted” when achieved through a mechanism that ensures release occurs predominantly in the intended segment, such as a radio-controlled capsule activated upon radiographic confirmation of position or an enteric coating designed to dissolve at a pH or transit time consistent with ileal arrival.

“Antibody-secreting cells” or “ASCs” denote B lymphocytes that have differentiated into plasmablasts or plasma cells and are actively producing and secreting immunoglobulins, specifically IgG or IgA, in response to antigenic stimulation. These cells are typically detected ex vivo using enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT) assays that capture secreted antibodies on a membrane adjacent to the cell, allowing enumeration of antigen-specific ASCs per million peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs).

The term “gut-homing receptors” refers to cell surface molecules expressed on lymphocytes that mediate adhesion to and migration across the vascular endothelium of the intestinal mucosa. The primary gut-homing receptor complex is the integrin α4β7, which binds to mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule-1 (MAdCAM-1) constitutively expressed on intestinal venules. Co-expression of the chemokine receptor CCR9, which responds to CCL25 (TECK) secreted by small intestinal epithelial cells, further refines intestinal tropism. A B cell is considered “α4β7<sup>high</sup>” when it exhibits high surface density of the β7 integrin subunit, as determined by flow cytometry using calibrated fluorescence intensity thresholds.

“Mucosal immune response” encompasses any adaptive immune reaction occurring at or mediated by mucosal surfaces, including the production of secretory IgA in luminal secretions (e.g., saliva, nasal wash, fecal extracts), the presence of tissue-resident memory lymphocytes in mucosal lamina propria, and the circulation of lymphocytes bearing mucosal homing receptors. It is distinct from purely systemic responses confined to the bloodstream or spleen.

“Ileum-targeted group” and “jejunum-targeted group” refer to cohorts of human subjects in a clinical study who received identical vaccine formulations released exclusively in the ileum or jejunum, respectively, under controlled conditions that confirm anatomical site of delivery, such as gamma scintigraphy-guided activation of a radio-controlled capsule.

Finally, “seroconversion” is defined as a four-fold or greater increase in hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) titer from baseline to post-vaccination, or attainment of an HAI titer ≥40 in initially seronegative subjects (baseline titer <40), consistent with regulatory standards for influenza vaccine evaluation.

### II. Immunogenic Biological Agents

The core immunogenic agent of the present invention is a recombinant adenovirus engineered to express an influenza virus hemagglutinin (HA) antigen. Hemagglutinin is a surface glycoprotein of influenza A and B viruses that mediates viral attachment to sialic acid receptors on host cells and is the primary target of neutralizing antibodies. The HA antigen encoded by the rAd vector is derived from a clinically relevant influenza strain, such as A/California/07/2009 (H1N1), and is codon-optimized for high-level expression in human cells. The rAd vector is constructed by inserting the HA gene into the E1-deleted region of the adenoviral genome under the control of a strong mammalian promoter, such as the cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate-early promoter, ensuring robust transgene expression upon transduction of antigen-presenting cells in the gut mucosa.

The rAd is produced under current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) conditions in a complementing cell line (e.g., HEK293 cells) that supplies the missing E1 functions in trans. Following amplification, the viral particles are purified by chromatography or ultracentrifugation, dialyzed against a stabilizing buffer containing sucrose, and concentrated to a final formulation of approximately 1×10<sup>11</sup> infectious units (IU) per 0.9 mL. The particle-to-infectivity ratio is maintained below 30:1 to ensure high potency, with a typical value of 27.8 as demonstrated in the clinical study. The liquid formulation is stable at refrigerated temperatures and compatible with encapsulation in a radio-controlled delivery device without loss of infectivity.

Upon oral administration and targeted release in the ileum, the rAd transduces M cells and dendritic cells within Peyer’s patches, leading to endogenous synthesis and presentation of the HA antigen via both MHC class I and II pathways. This dual presentation activates CD4<sup>+</sup> T helper cells and CD8<sup>+</sup> cytotoxic T lymphocytes, while also driving B-cell differentiation into HA-specific ASCs. The adenoviral vector itself acts as a potent adjuvant through engagement of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and cytosolic DNA sensors, promoting dendritic cell maturation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production that favors a Th1-biased response. Importantly, because the rAd is replication-deficient, it does not cause productive infection or spread beyond the initial site of transduction, ensuring an excellent safety profile.

The choice of rAd as the vector platform is critical to the invention’s success. Unlike protein subunit or inactivated vaccines, which require co-administration of external adjuvants and often fail to induce strong cellular immunity, the rAd delivers the antigen in its native conformation within living cells, mimicking natural viral infection and eliciting comprehensive humoral and cellular responses. Furthermore, the adenoviral capsid facilitates efficient uptake by antigen-sampling cells in the GALT, bypassing some of the degradation issues that plague soluble protein antigens in the intestinal lumen.

While the exemplified embodiment uses an rAd encoding influenza HA, the invention is not limited to this antigen or pathogen. The same delivery platform and ileal targeting principle can be applied to rAd vectors expressing antigens from other mucosally transmitted pathogens, including respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), norovirus, rotavirus, SARS-CoV-2, HIV, or even non-infectious targets such as tumor-associated antigens for cancer immunotherapy. The key innovation lies not in the specific antigen but in the demonstration that ileal release maximizes the immunogenic potential of orally delivered viral vectors.

### III. Targeted Delivery Systems

The present invention relies on a precision delivery system that ensures the vaccine is released exclusively in the ileum, thereby capitalizing on the region’s high density of inductive immune structures. The preferred embodiment utilizes a radio-controlled capsule (RCC)—a swallowable, size 000 hard gelatin capsule equipped with a miniature radio receiver, battery, and mechanical ejection mechanism. The capsule contains the liquid rAd formulation along with a gamma-emitting radiotracer (e.g., <sup>99m</sup>Tc-labeled sulfur colloid) that allows real-time tracking of its position through the gastrointestinal tract using external gamma scintigraphy.

Upon ingestion by a fasted subject, the RCC remains intact as it passes through the stomach and duodenum. At the clinical site, trained personnel monitor the capsule’s progress via sequential gamma camera imaging. Once the capsule is confirmed to reside in the ileum—identified by its location distal to the ligament of Treitz and proximal to the cecum—a radio signal is transmitted to activate the ejection mechanism, expelling the entire 0.9 mL vaccine payload into the ileal lumen. Release is immediately verified by observing dispersion of the radiotracer on scintigraphy, confirming successful delivery. This system provides unparalleled anatomical precision, eliminating the variability inherent in pH-dependent enteric coatings or time-release formulations.

Although the RCC was used in the clinical proof-of-concept study, the invention contemplates translation to more scalable and user-friendly formats for commercial use. Specifically, the findings validate the development of enteric-coated tablets engineered to dissolve specifically in the ileum. Such tablets would incorporate polymers that resist dissolution until exposed to the slightly alkaline pH (≥7.0) and longer transit times characteristic of the distal small intestine. Alternatively, enzyme-sensitive coatings that degrade only in the presence of ileal-specific brush border enzymes could be employed. The critical parameter is that the formulation must reliably release its contents in the ileum, not merely the “small intestine” generically.

The volume and physical state of the formulation are also essential. The invention specifies a liquid suspension rather than a dried powder or tablet during the initial delivery phase because liquids disperse more rapidly in the intestinal lumen, increasing contact with the follicle-associated epithelium overlying Peyer’s patches. The 0.9 mL volume is sufficient to ensure complete ejection from the RCC while remaining below the threshold that might trigger premature peristalsis or discomfort. Future solid dosage forms would need to reconstitute quickly into a similar liquid bolus upon dissolution.

Importantly, the targeted delivery system is not merely a convenience feature but a fundamental determinant of immunogenicity. As demonstrated in the clinical data, identical vaccine lots released in the jejunum versus the ileum yield significantly different immune outcomes, proving that anatomical site is a key variable. Thus, any delivery system falling within the scope of this invention must provide reliable ileal targeting, whether through electronic, chemical, or mechanical means.

### IV. Immune Responses and Vaccines

The invention elicits a multifaceted immune response that integrates systemic protection with frontline mucosal defense. Systemically, vaccination induces high-titer serum IgG antibodies that neutralize influenza virus in the bloodstream, as evidenced by hemagglutination inhibition (HAI) and microneutralization (MN) assays. In the ileum-targeted group, 67% of initially seronegative subjects achieved seroconversion (HAI ≥40), with geometric mean MN titers increasing more than five-fold post-vaccination. These responses meet or exceed benchmarks established for injectable influenza vaccines, demonstrating that oral delivery can achieve comparable systemic efficacy.

More significantly, the invention generates robust mucosal immunity. Within seven days of vaccination, subjects exhibit a marked expansion of circulating HA-specific IgA and IgG antibody-secreting cells (ASCs), with ileal delivery yielding median ASC frequencies nearly double those seen with jejunal release. These ASCs are precursors to long-lived plasma cells that will ultimately populate mucosal effector sites. Concurrently, there is a pronounced increase in B cells expressing the gut-homing integrin α4β7<sup>high</sup> and chemokine receptor CCR9. These markers are induced by retinoic acid produced by ileal dendritic cells and direct lymphocyte trafficking to the small intestinal lamina propria, where they differentiate into IgA-secreting plasma cells that contribute to fecal sIgA.

Critically, the vaccine also stimulates B cells with non-intestinal mucosal tropism. A subset of ASCs expresses α4β1 integrin (which binds VCAM-1 on inflamed endothelium) but low or absent β7, enabling homing to respiratory, mammary, and urogenital mucosae. This explains the detection of HA-specific IgA in nasal washes post-vaccination, confirming engagement of the common mucosal immune system. Flow cytometry revealed that approximately 30% of these non-gut-homing ASCs are IgA<sup>+</sup>, providing a cellular basis for respiratory mucosal protection against influenza.

The invention thus provides a vaccine that simultaneously achieves three objectives: (1) systemic neutralizing antibodies for broad protection, (2) intestinal sIgA to block enteric co-infections or serve as a reservoir of immunity, and (3) respiratory sIgA to intercept influenza at its portal of entry. This tripartite response is unmatched by current injectable vaccines and addresses a major limitation in influenza prevention.

Moreover, the induction of α4β7<sup>high</sup> CD27<sup>intermediate</sup> IgA<sup>+</sup> memory B cells suggests the potential for rapid recall responses upon re-exposure. Such memory cells can quickly differentiate into ASCs upon antigen encounter, providing durable protection. This is particularly relevant given evidence from norovirus and RSV challenge studies that mucosal IgA memory correlates better with protection than serum antibodies alone.

### V. Examples

The invention is exemplified by a Phase I clinical study in 24 healthy male volunteers aged 18–49. Participants fasted overnight and swallowed a radio-controlled capsule containing 1×10<sup>11</sup> IU of rAd-HA in 0.9 mL liquid plus radiotracer. Gamma scintigraphy guided release in either the jejunum (n=12) or ileum (n=12). Immune responses were assessed on days 0, 7, and 28.

Results showed that ileal delivery induced significantly higher HA-specific IgG ASCs (median 200 vs. 111 per 10<sup>6</sup> PBMCs; p=0.023) and a trend toward higher IgA ASCs (54 vs. 27; p=0.08). All ileum-group subjects responded with both IgG and IgA ASCs, versus 10/12 and 9/12 in the jejunum group. Serum ELISA revealed HA-specific IgG responses in 12/12 ileum subjects versus 9/12 jejunum subjects. HAI seroconversion occurred in 67% (ileum) versus 44% (jejunum) of initially seronegative subjects. MN titers showed 11/12 ileum subjects achieving ≥2-fold rises versus 7/12 in jejunum.

Flow cytometry demonstrated that ileal delivery induced greater expansion of α4β7<sup>high</sup> CD19<sup>+</sup> B cells (median 4.98% vs. 2.91% on day 7) and enriched for CCR9<sup>+</sup> IgA<sup>+</sup> subsets. Mucosal sampling detected HA-specific IgA increases in 9/12 ileum subjects (fecal or nasal) and 10/12 jejunum subjects, with nasal responses more frequent in the jejunum group (6 vs. 4 subjects), consistent with differential homing receptor expression.

These data conclusively demonstrate that ileal targeting optimizes systemic and mucosal immunogenicity, providing a blueprint for next-generation oral influenza vaccines.
