# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of plant biotechnology, specifically to a novel genetic system for inducing and restoring male sterility in plants. More particularly, the invention provides a tightly regulated, tapetum-specific, high-level expression system for a male sterility gene, coupled with a transcriptional control mechanism that enables complete restoration of male fertility in hybrid progeny. The invention is especially useful in the context of hybrid seed production, where it facilitates the exploitation of heterosis without the need for conventional restorer genes or chemical/hormonal interventions. The system leverages a modified TATA-box binding protein (TBPm3) complementation strategy in conjunction with a light-regulated protein degradation module derived from the HFR1–COP1 signaling axis to achieve spatio-temporal control over transgene expression exclusively in the postmeiotic tapetum of anthers. This dual-component genetic architecture ensures robust male sterility in the female parent line while enabling precise, conditional abolition of the sterility gene’s expression in the F1 hybrid through targeted proteolysis of a key transcriptional activator. The invention is broadly applicable across plant species amenable to genetic transformation and offers significant advantages over existing male sterility systems in terms of stringency, reversibility, biosafety, and compatibility with commercial breeding programs.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION AND PRIOR ART

Hybrid vigor, or heterosis, has been a cornerstone of modern agricultural productivity, delivering substantial gains in yield, stress tolerance, and uniformity in numerous crop species. The efficient production of hybrid seeds, however, necessitates reliable systems for preventing self-pollination in the female parent, thereby ensuring that all seeds result from controlled cross-pollination with a designated male parent. Male sterility—defined as the inability of a plant to produce functional pollen—has emerged as the most effective and widely adopted biological mechanism for achieving this objective. Over the past several decades, multiple approaches to engineering male sterility have been developed, including cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS), chemically induced male sterility, and transgenic male sterility. Among these, transgenic strategies offer the greatest precision, flexibility, and potential for universal applicability across diverse plant species.

One of the earliest and most influential transgenic male sterility systems was the barnase/barstar system, originally developed in *Brassica* species. In this system, the bacterial ribonuclease gene *barnase* is expressed under the control of a tapetum-specific promoter, leading to the ablation of tapetal cells and consequent male sterility. Fertility is restored in the hybrid by crossing the male-sterile line with a restorer line expressing *barstar*, a specific inhibitor of barnase, also under tapetum-specific control. While conceptually elegant and commercially deployed, the barnase/barstar system suffers from several limitations. First, the identification and development of efficient restorer lines can be challenging and genotype-dependent; for instance, in *Brassica juncea*, only 1 in 54 crosses between barnase and barstar lines yielded adequate fertility restoration. Second, leaky expression of *barnase* in vegetative tissues can cause yield penalties due to unintended cytotoxicity. Third, the use of bacterial genes raises biosafety and regulatory concerns in certain jurisdictions, potentially limiting market acceptance.

Alternative transgenic approaches have sought to circumvent these issues by employing endogenous plant genes or more sophisticated regulatory mechanisms. For example, the expression of pro-apoptotic genes such as *BAX* or autophagy-related genes like *BECLIN1* (also known as *ATG6*) in the tapetum has been shown to induce complete male sterility. These systems benefit from the use of plant-derived sequences, which may mitigate biosafety concerns. However, they often lack an equally robust and reliable fertility restoration mechanism. Some restoration strategies have relied on antisense RNA or RNA interference to suppress the sterility gene transcript, but these methods frequently achieve only partial restoration due to incomplete silencing. Other approaches, such as Cre/loxP-mediated site-specific recombination or split-gene systems, have demonstrated complete restoration but introduce additional complexity, including the need for multiple transgenes, inducible promoters, or exogenous chemical triggers (e.g., ethanol), which are impractical for large-scale agricultural use.

A critical challenge common to many transgenic male sterility systems is the requirement for stringent spatial and temporal control over the expression of the sterility gene. The tapetum—a specialized secretory layer surrounding the developing microspores—is the ideal target tissue because its timely degeneration is essential for pollen wall formation and maturation. Expression of a cytotoxic or developmental disruptor gene in the tapetum during the postmeiotic stages (after meiosis but before pollen maturation) can effectively block pollen development without affecting other aspects of plant growth or female fertility. Achieving such precise expression, however, demands highly specific promoters and robust transcriptional regulation. Conventional tapetum-specific promoters, such as *TA29* from tobacco or *A9* from *Arabidopsis*, provide reasonable specificity but often suffer from relatively low expression levels, which may compromise the completeness of male sterility. Moreover, these native promoters are subject to the plant’s endogenous transcriptional machinery, offering limited opportunities for external control or conditional repression.

To address these limitations, a two-component transcriptional amplification system based on a mutated TATA-box and a complementary mutant TATA-box binding protein (TBP) was previously developed. In this system, the canonical TATA-box sequence (TATATA) in a tapetum-specific promoter is mutated to TGTA, rendering it unrecognizable by the native TBP. A modified TBP (TBPm3), containing three amino acid substitutions (Ile152Phe, Val161Thr, Leu163Val), is engineered to specifically bind the TGTA-mutated promoter and drive high-level transcription. Because the native TBP cannot activate the mutated promoter, and the TBPm3 does not significantly interfere with global transcription, this system achieves both high expression and stringent tissue specificity. When applied to express *BECLIN1* in the tobacco tapetum, this system resulted in complete male sterility, demonstrating its efficacy. However, a major limitation of this approach was the absence of a built-in mechanism for fertility restoration. Once the sterility gene was expressed, there was no way to turn it off in the hybrid progeny, making the system unsuitable for recurrent hybrid seed production.

### LIMITATIONS OF PRIOR ART

The prior art in transgenic male sterility systems is characterized by a fundamental trade-off between the robustness of sterility induction and the reliability of fertility restoration. Systems that achieve complete male sterility, such as those using strong cytotoxins like barnase or high-level expression of *BECLIN1*, often lack equally effective restoration mechanisms. Conversely, systems with theoretically complete restoration, such as those based on site-specific recombination, may introduce biosafety concerns or practical complexities that hinder their adoption. Furthermore, many existing systems rely on the co-expression of a specific restorer gene (e.g., *barstar* for *barnase*), which necessitates the maintenance of two distinct transgenic lines and careful matching of sterility and restoration components. This increases the cost and complexity of breeding programs and limits the modularity of the system—i.e., the ability to swap different sterility genes without redesigning the entire restoration apparatus.

Another significant limitation is the lack of dynamic, conditionally regulatable control over the sterility gene. Most systems operate in a binary “on/off” mode dictated solely by the presence or absence of the transgene, with no capacity for fine-tuning or external intervention. This rigidity can be problematic in variable environmental conditions or when partial sterility is undesirable. Additionally, the reliance on native promoters for both sterility and restoration components means that expression levels are subject to natural variation and may not be sufficient to ensure complete phenotypic effects. Even in the advanced TBPm3/TGTA system, while high-level and stringent expression was achieved, the inability to abolish this expression in the F1 hybrid rendered it non-reversible and thus incompatible with hybrid seed production cycles.

Moreover, the subcellular dynamics of regulatory proteins have been largely overlooked in prior art. For instance, the COP1 protein, a well-known repressor of photomorphogenesis in *Arabidopsis*, shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm in a light-dependent manner. Its nuclear concentration—and hence its activity in degrading target proteins such as HFR1—is inherently variable. Prior attempts to exploit COP1-mediated degradation for transgene control did not account for this spatial regulation, leading to incomplete or inconsistent repression. This underscores a broader gap in the art: the failure to integrate knowledge of protein trafficking and compartmentalization into the design of genetic switches for plant biotechnology.

### Objectives of the Present Invention

In view of the aforementioned limitations, it is an object of the present invention to provide a novel male sterility–fertility restoration system that achieves complete and reliable male sterility in the female parent line while enabling complete and predictable restoration of male fertility in the F1 hybrid progeny.

It is another object of the present invention to eliminate the need for a dedicated restorer gene that directly counteracts the sterility gene product (e.g., an inhibitor or antidote), thereby simplifying the genetic architecture and enhancing the modularity of the system.

It is a further object of the present invention to provide a system that operates through transcriptional control—specifically, the conditional abolition of sterility gene transcription—rather than post-transcriptional or post-translational mechanisms, which are often less efficient and more variable.

It is yet another object of the present invention to leverage the natural protein degradation machinery of the plant cell, in particular the COP1–HFR1 light signaling pathway, to achieve targeted and inducible control over a key transcriptional activator.

It is still another object of the present invention to enhance the nuclear localization of the regulatory protein COP1 through a specific point mutation (L105A), thereby ensuring maximal degradation efficiency of the target fusion protein and complete abolition of transgene expression.

It is an additional object of the present invention to combine the high-level, tapetum-specific expression capability of the TGTA–TBPm3 complementation system with the conditional degradation module of HFR1NT131–COP1 to create a reversible genetic switch that is active only in the desired tissue and developmental window.

It is a further object of the present invention to demonstrate that the system is generic and can be adapted to any male sterility gene by simply replacing the coding sequence in the female expression cassette, without requiring concomitant changes to the male restoration component.

It is yet another object of the present invention to provide a system that is biosafe, using only plant-derived or modified plant-derived components, and avoids the use of bacterial toxins or exogenous chemical inducers.

Finally, it is an object of the present invention to enable the practical, large-scale production of hybrid seeds by providing a robust, reliable, and agriculturally compatible platform for heterosis breeding in a wide range of crop species.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly effective system for inducing and restoring male sterility in plants, designed specifically for hybrid seed production. The system comprises two genetically distinct parental lines: a female parent engineered to be completely male-sterile, and a male parent engineered to restore full male fertility in the F1 hybrid progeny. The core innovation lies in the integration of a high-fidelity transcriptional activation system with a conditionally regulatable protein degradation module, resulting in a reversible genetic switch that operates exclusively in the postmeiotic tapetum of developing anthers.

In the female parent, male sterility is achieved by the tapetum-specific, high-level expression of a male sterility gene—exemplified herein by the *Arabidopsis thaliana BECLIN1* (*ATG6*) gene—under the control of a modified promoter system. This system consists of a tapetum-specific promoter (e.g., *PTA29* from tobacco) in which the native TATA-box sequence has been mutated from TATATA to TGTA. This mutated promoter is transcriptionally inert in the presence of the plant’s native TATA-box binding protein (TBP). However, when co-expressed with a specifically engineered mutant TBP (designated TBPm3, containing amino acid substitutions Ile152Phe, Val161Thr, and Leu163Val), the TGTA-mutated promoter is robustly activated, leading to high-level transgene expression. To confer an additional layer of regulatory control, the TBPm3 protein is translationally fused at its N-terminus to a 131-amino-acid fragment derived from the N-terminus of the *Arabidopsis* transcription factor HFR1 (designated HFR1NT131). This HFR1NT131 domain retains the ability to interact with the E3 ubiquitin ligase COP1 but lacks the C-terminal DNA-binding domain required for HFR1’s native function in photomorphogenesis. Thus, the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion protein functions as a fully active transcriptional activator for the TGTA-mutated promoter but is now susceptible to COP1-mediated proteasomal degradation.

The male parent line is engineered to express either wild-type COP1 or a nuclear-localized mutant form of COP1 (COP1L105A) under the control of a tapetum-specific promoter (e.g., *PA9* from *Arabidopsis*). Upon crossing the female and male parents, the F1 hybrid inherits both the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion and the COP1 (or COP1L105A) protein. In the tapetal cells of the F1 anther, COP1 binds to the HFR1NT131 domain of the fusion protein and targets it for degradation via the 26S proteasome. This degradation depletes the cellular pool of TBPm3, preventing the formation of the transcriptional pre-initiation complex on the TGTA-mutated promoter and thereby abolishing transcription of the male sterility gene. When wild-type COP1 is used, partial degradation occurs, leading to reduced but not eliminated sterility gene expression and only partial fertility restoration. However, when the COP1L105A mutant—which exhibits enhanced nuclear localization due to a leucine-to-alanine substitution at position 105—is used, complete and efficient degradation of the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion is achieved, resulting in total abolition of sterility gene transcription and full restoration of male fertility in the F1 hybrid.

The invention thus provides a modular, two-component system wherein the female line carries a “sterility cassette” comprising the TGTA-mutated promoter driving the male sterility gene and the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion under a constitutive or tapetum-specific promoter, and the male line carries a “restoration cassette” comprising the tapetum-specific promoter driving COP1 or COP1L105A. The system is generic: any gene known to induce male sterility when expressed in the tapetum can be substituted for *BECLIN1* in the sterility cassette, without requiring modification of the restoration cassette. The use of plant-derived components and the absence of bacterial toxins or chemical inducers enhance the biosafety and regulatory acceptability of the system. Furthermore, the tight spatio-temporal control—restricted to postmeiotic tapetal cells—and the complete reversibility in the F1 hybrid make this system ideally suited for commercial hybrid seed production across a wide range of crop species.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a genetically engineered system for the controlled induction and restoration of male sterility in plants, enabling efficient and reliable production of hybrid seeds. The system is founded upon the precise orchestration of transcriptional activation and targeted protein degradation within the tapetum—a specialized somatic cell layer in the anther that plays a critical role in pollen development. By confining the activity of the system to this specific tissue and developmental window, the invention ensures that male sterility is complete and that plant growth, female fertility, and overall agronomic performance remain unaffected. The following detailed description outlines the molecular components, genetic constructs, mechanisms of action, and experimental validation of the invention.

The foundational element of the invention is a modified transcriptional activation system that enables high-level, tissue-specific gene expression. This system builds upon a previously described two-component architecture involving a mutated promoter and a complementary mutant transcription factor. Specifically, a native tapetum-specific promoter—such as the *PTA29* promoter from tobacco (*Nicotiana tabacum*)—is engineered by substituting its canonical TATA-box sequence (typically TATATAAG) with a TGTA motif. This TGTA mutation renders the promoter unresponsive to the plant’s endogenous TATA-box binding protein (TBP), which normally recognizes and binds the TATA-box to initiate transcription. Consequently, in the absence of a complementary factor, the TGTA-mutated promoter remains transcriptionally silent, even in tapetal cells where the native promoter would be active.

To reactivate this silenced promoter, a mutant form of TBP, designated TBPm3, is introduced. TBPm3 contains three specific amino acid substitutions: isoleucine at position 152 is replaced by phenylalanine (Ile152Phe), valine at position 161 by threonine (Val161Thr), and leucine at position 163 by valine (Leu163Val). These substitutions alter the DNA-binding interface of TBP such that it now exhibits high affinity for the TGTA motif while maintaining minimal interaction with the native TATA-box. When TBPm3 is expressed in tapetal cells, it selectively binds the TGTA-mutated promoter and recruits the general transcription machinery, leading to robust transcription of the downstream gene. Because TBPm3 does not significantly interfere with the transcription of endogenous genes driven by native TATA-boxes, its expression is well tolerated by the plant, and no adverse phenotypic effects are observed.

In the female parent line of the present invention, this TGTA–TBPm3 system is employed to drive the expression of a male sterility gene. The choice of sterility gene is flexible; however, for the purposes of exemplification, the *Arabidopsis thaliana BECLIN1* gene (also known as *ATG6*) is used. *BECLIN1* is a key regulator of autophagy, a conserved cellular degradation process. When overexpressed in the tapetum during the postmeiotic stages of anther development, *BECLIN1* disrupts normal tapetal function, leading to premature or aberrant tapetal degeneration, defective pollen wall formation, and ultimately, complete male sterility. The sterility cassette is constructed such that the TGTA-mutated *PTA29* promoter drives the *BECLIN1* coding sequence, and the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion protein is expressed from a separate expression unit within the same T-DNA. The HFR1NT131 domain, comprising the first 131 amino acids of the *Arabidopsis* HFR1 protein, is fused in-frame to the N-terminus of TBPm3. This fusion does not impair the DNA-binding or transcriptional activation capability of TBPm3, as evidenced by high-level reporter gene expression (e.g., *gusA*) in transgenic lines carrying the analogous construct. However, it renders the entire fusion protein susceptible to recognition and degradation by COP1.

COP1 (Constitutively Photomorphogenic 1) is a RING-finger E3 ubiquitin ligase that functions as a central repressor of photomorphogenesis in darkness. In the dark, COP1 accumulates in the nucleus and targets positive regulators of light signaling—including HFR1—for ubiquitination and subsequent degradation by the 26S proteasome. The N-terminal 131 amino acids of HFR1 contain the COP1-interaction domain but lack the C-terminal bHLH domain required for DNA binding and transcriptional activation. Therefore, HFR1NT131 acts as a decoy substrate for COP1: it binds COP1 with high affinity but cannot execute HFR1’s native function. By fusing HFR1NT131 to TBPm3, the inventors have effectively “hijacked” the COP1 degradation pathway to control the stability of a heterologous transcription factor.

The male parent line is engineered to express COP1 under the control of a tapetum-specific promoter, such as the *Arabidopsis A9* promoter (*PA9*). This ensures that COP1 is produced only in the tapetal cells of the anther, mirroring the spatial expression pattern of the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion in the female parent. When the female and male parents are crossed, the F1 hybrid inherits both transgenes. In the tapetal cells of the F1 anther, COP1 binds to the HFR1NT131 domain of the fusion protein, leading to its polyubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. As a result, TBPm3 becomes unavailable to activate the TGTA-mutated promoter, and transcription of the male sterility gene (*BECLIN1*) is abolished. This transcriptional shutdown allows normal tapetal development and pollen maturation to proceed, thereby restoring male fertility.

However, initial experiments revealed that wild-type COP1 only partially restores fertility, reducing *BECLIN1* expression by approximately 21-fold but not eliminating it entirely. The inventors hypothesized that this incomplete repression was due to the dynamic subcellular localization of COP1, which shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm in response to light signals. In many plant cells, a significant fraction of COP1 resides in the cytoplasm, limiting its nuclear concentration and thus its efficiency in degrading nuclear targets like the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion. To overcome this limitation, the inventors introduced a point mutation into COP1—substituting leucine at position 105 with alanine (L105A)—to generate COP1L105A. This mutation, previously reported to enhance nuclear retention without compromising COP1’s E3 ligase activity, ensures that a maximal amount of COP1 is present in the nucleus where the transcriptional machinery operates. Transgenic male lines expressing COP1L105A under *PA9* control were generated and crossed with the female sterility line. In the resulting F’1 hybrids, *BECLIN1* expression was completely undetectable, and male fertility was fully restored to wild-type levels, as confirmed by pollen viability assays, in vitro germination tests, seed set measurements, and microscopic analyses of pollen and anther morphology.

The system’s modularity is a key advantage. The sterility cassette can be readily adapted to express any gene known to cause male sterility when overexpressed in the tapetum—examples include *BARNASE*, *BAX*, or other autophagy-related genes—simply by replacing the *BECLIN1* coding sequence. The restoration cassette remains unchanged, as it operates upstream at the level of transcriptional activation rather than targeting the sterility gene product itself. This eliminates the need for gene-specific restorers and streamlines the development of new hybrid combinations.

Furthermore, the invention addresses practical considerations for commercial deployment. The female sterility line can be maintained as a heterozygous population, with male-sterile and fertile plants segregating in a 1:1 ratio. By linking a selectable marker (e.g., herbicide resistance) to the sterility cassette, pure stands of male-sterile plants can be obtained through chemical selection, facilitating large-scale seed production. The use of plant-derived sequences throughout the system enhances its biosafety profile and regulatory acceptance.

In summary, the present invention represents a significant advance in plant biotechnology by providing a tightly regulated, reversible, and modular system for male sterility and fertility restoration. Its foundation in endogenous plant signaling pathways, combined with innovative protein engineering and promoter design, ensures high performance, reliability, and broad applicability in hybrid crop breeding programs.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Construction and Validation of the High-Level, Stringent Expression System in Postmeiotic Tapetum**

To establish a baseline for high-level and tissue-specific transgene expression, a reporter construct designated 1370 was developed. This construct comprised two expression units within a single T-DNA: (i) a TGTA-mutated *PTA29* promoter driving the *gusA* reporter gene, and (ii) the *HFR1NT131–TBPm3* fusion gene under the control of the *Pcec* promoter (a constitutive promoter from *Cauliflower mosaic virus*). The *PTA29* promoter was selected for its well-documented specificity to the tapetum in tobacco, and its TATA-box was mutated from TATATATG to TGTATATG using site-directed mutagenesis. The TBPm3 coding sequence, harboring the Ile152Phe, Val161Thr, and Leu163Val substitutions, was fused in-frame at its N-terminus to the HFR1NT131 fragment (encoding amino acids 1–131 of *Arabidopsis* HFR1). This entire cassette was cloned into the binary vector pCAMBIA1300 and introduced into *Nicotiana tabacum* cv. Petit Havana (NTPH) via *Agrobacterium*-mediated transformation.

Transgenic lines harboring construct 1370 exhibited normal growth, development, flowering, and seed set, indistinguishable from wild-type NTPH plants. Fluorimetric GUS assays performed on anthers at developmental stages 2 and 3 (postmeiotic stages according to the tobacco anther staging system) revealed high levels of GUS activity, with average values of 22.00 ± 5.15 and 20.43 ± 5.74 nmoles mg⁻¹ min⁻¹, respectively. Histochemical GUS staining confirmed that expression was strictly confined to tapetal cells, with no detectable signal in leaves, stems, roots, or emasculated flower buds. These results demonstrated that the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion retained full functionality as a transcriptional activator and that the TGTA–TBPm3 system provided both high-level and stringent tapetum-specific expression during the critical postmeiotic window.

**Example 2: Partial Transcriptional Abolition Using Wild-Type COP1**

To test whether COP1-mediated degradation could be used to control the TGTA–TBPm3 system, a second construct, 1372, was created. Construct 1372 contained the wild-type *Arabidopsis COP1* coding sequence under the control of the tapetum-specific *PA9* promoter, cloned into pBI101. Transgenic tobacco lines expressing 1372 were phenotypically normal. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis showed that *COP1* expression peaked at anther stage 1, preceding the peak of *gusA* expression in the 1370 lines. Two high-expressing 1372 lines (T13 and T33) were crossed as males with two high-expressing 1370 lines (L3 and L5) as females to generate F1 progeny.

F1 plants were selected on medium containing both kanamycin and hygromycin (corresponding to the selectable markers in 1370 and 1372, respectively) and confirmed by PCR. Fluorimetric GUS assays on F1 anthers at stages 2 and 3 showed a 4–6-fold reduction in GUS activity compared to the 1370 parent. Histochemical staining confirmed that residual expression remained tapetum-specific but was markedly diminished. These results validated the hypothesis that COP1 could degrade the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion and partially repress transgene expression. However, the incomplete abolition suggested that nuclear COP1 levels were insufficient for total degradation.

**Example 3: Complete Transcriptional Abolition Using Nuclear-Localized COP1L105A**

To achieve complete repression, a third construct, 1373, was designed. This construct was identical to 1372 except that it expressed the COP1L105A mutant, generated by site-directed mutagenesis of the *COP1* coding sequence. Transgenic 1373 lines were normal in phenotype. Two high-expressing lines (3 and 5) were crossed with 1370 females to generate F’1 progeny.

In F’1 anthers at stages 2 and 3, both fluorimetric and histochemical GUS assays showed no detectable activity, indicating complete abolition of *gusA* expression. This demonstrated that enhanced nuclear localization of COP1L105A enabled total degradation of the HFR1NT131–TBPm3 fusion, thereby fully inactivating the TGTA–TBPm3 system.

**Example 4: Induction of Complete Male Sterility by Tapetum-Specific BECLIN1 Expression**

Construct 1371 was generated by replacing the *gusA* gene in 1370 with the *Arabidopsis BECLIN1* coding sequence. Transgenic 1371 lines grew normally but were completely male-sterile: flowers failed to self-pollinate, pollen viability and germination were near zero, and seed set was absent. Scanning and transmission electron microscopy revealed severe defects in pollen development starting at stage 2, including incomplete callose degradation, microspore deformation, cytoplasmic collapse, and abnormal pollen wall formation. Anther cross-sections showed abnormal tapetal growth and delayed degeneration, confirming that *BECLIN1* overexpression disrupted tapetal function. qRT-PCR confirmed peak *BECLIN1* expression at stages 2 and 3, correlating with the onset of phenotypic defects.

**Example 5: Complete Restoration of Male Fertility in F’1 Hybrids**

Female 1371 plants were crossed with male 1373 plants to generate F’1 hybrids. These F’1 plants exhibited pollen viability (74.58 ± 1.2%), pollen germination (69 ± 4.6%), and seed set (71.9 ± 6.1 mg/pod) comparable to wild-type controls. SEM showed normal pollen morphology, and anther cross-sections revealed typical tapetal degeneration and pollen maturation. qRT-PCR confirmed complete abolition of *BECLIN1* expression in F’1 anthers, while *COP1L105A* expression was highest at stage 1. In contrast, F1 hybrids from crosses with 1372 males showed only partial restoration (~15% pollen viability), consistent with incomplete *BECLIN1* repression.

These examples collectively demonstrate the efficacy, stringency, and reversibility of the invented system for male sterility and fertility restoration in plants.
