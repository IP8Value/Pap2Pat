## BACKGROUND

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) have emerged as indispensable tools for structural biology, enabling high-resolution visualization of macromolecular complexes, viruses, and cellular components at near-atomic resolution. However, a persistent bottleneck in these techniques lies in sample preparation—specifically, the need to immobilize target biomolecules on electron microscopy grids while minimizing background interference from non-target species, especially when working directly with complex biological mixtures such as cell lysates. Traditional approaches often require extensive purification of the protein of interest prior to grid application, involving multiple chromatographic steps that are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to sample loss or denaturation. Moreover, conventional TEM grids rely on amorphous carbon films or holey carbon supports that lack chemical functionality for selective biomolecule capture, thereby necessitating pre-purified samples to avoid overwhelming background noise during imaging.

To address this limitation, affinity-based grid coatings have been explored, wherein functional ligands capable of selectively binding tagged proteins are immobilized onto the grid surface. Among the most widely used affinity systems is the hexahistidine (His6)-nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA)-nickel(II) (Ni²⁺) interaction, which exploits the high-affinity coordination between polyhistidine tags and Ni²⁺ chelated by NTA groups. This system has been successfully adapted to lipid monolayers supported on TEM grids, where NTA-functionalized lipids form fluid bilayers capable of capturing His-tagged proteins. While effective in controlled settings, lipid-based affinity grids suffer from several drawbacks: they are mechanically fragile, exhibit poor electron conductivity leading to beam-induced charging during imaging, and often produce thick, heterogeneous layers that obscure fine structural details in cryo-EM. Furthermore, the hydrophobic nature of lipid films can promote non-specific adsorption of cellular contaminants, particularly when applied directly to crude lysates, thereby compromising specificity and image quality.

Graphene oxide (GO), a two-dimensional material derived from the oxidative exfoliation of graphite, presents a compelling alternative substrate for TEM grid functionalization. GO sheets possess exceptional mechanical strength, high electron transparency due to their atomic thinness, and excellent thermal and electrical conductivity, all of which are advantageous for high-resolution electron microscopy. Additionally, the basal plane and edges of GO are densely decorated with oxygen-containing functional groups—including carboxyl, hydroxyl, epoxide, and carbonyl moieties—that provide reactive handles for covalent modification with affinity ligands. Despite these favorable properties, the direct use of GO in affinity capture applications has been hindered by its inherent chemical reactivity, which promotes non-specific binding of proteins and other biomolecules through covalent or electrostatic interactions with residual aldehyde and epoxide groups. This non-specific adsorption is particularly problematic in complex biological matrices such as cell lysates, where abundant cellular proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids can adhere indiscriminately to the GO surface, masking the signal from the target analyte.

Prior attempts to utilize GO in Langmuir monolayer formats for TEM grid coating have largely relied on surfactant-assisted dispersion methods to stabilize GO sheets at the air-water interface. However, the presence of exogenous surfactants introduces competing interfacial activity that can interfere with the organization and transfer of GO monolayers, while also contributing additional sources of background contamination. More recent studies have demonstrated that GO can spontaneously accumulate at the air-water interface over extended periods due to its amphiphilic character, wherein hydrophilic functional groups localize at the sheet periphery while the aromatic core remains relatively hydrophobic. Nevertheless, this process is slow and inefficient, often requiring hours of stirring to achieve sufficient surface coverage. Alternative strategies employing gas bubbling or solvent additives have shown promise in accelerating interfacial adsorption, but systematic control over monolayer formation, thickness uniformity, and transfer efficiency remains challenging.

Crucially, there exists no prior art describing a robust, integrated methodology for the synthesis, monolayer assembly, and functional deployment of NTA-functionalized GO sheets as affinity capture substrates for direct on-grid purification of His-tagged proteins from unpurified biological samples. Existing approaches either fail to adequately suppress non-specific binding, produce multilayered or irregular coatings unsuitable for high-resolution imaging, or lack the chemical stability required for reproducible performance across diverse experimental conditions. Therefore, there is a critical unmet need for a novel class of TEM grid coatings that combine the structural advantages of graphene-based materials with the specificity of metal-chelate affinity chemistry, while simultaneously incorporating effective antifouling strategies to enable direct application to complex biological mixtures without prior purification.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel functionalized graphene oxide (GO) monolayer composition and method for its preparation and use as an affinity capture substrate for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Specifically, the invention relates to GO sheets covalently functionalized with nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) chelating groups via lysine linkers, which are assembled into uniform, single-layer monolayers at the air-water interface using a Langmuir-Schaefer (L-S) transfer technique facilitated by a volatile polar protic solvent additive, and subsequently modified with dual antifouling agents to suppress non-specific binding while preserving specific affinity interactions. This engineered monolayer serves as a high-performance coating for electron microscopy grids, enabling the direct capture and purification of polyhistidine-tagged (His-tagged) biomolecules from complex biological samples such as clarified cell lysates, without the need for prior chromatographic purification.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for synthesizing NTA-functionalized GO (GO-NTA) comprising: (a) activating carboxylic acid groups on GO sheets with thionyl chloride (SOCl₂) to form acyl chloride intermediates; (b) reacting the activated GO with a tris-t-butyl ester of lysine-NTA to yield a protected GO-NTA intermediate; and (c) deprotecting the t-butyl ester groups using trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to generate free NTA chelating functionalities on the GO surface. The resulting GO-NTA material exhibits edge amphiphilicity due to the spatial distribution of hydrophilic NTA and carboxyl groups around the periphery of a relatively hydrophobic graphenic core, enabling spontaneous accumulation at the air-water interface.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for forming a uniform monolayer of GO-NTA at the air-water interface by dispersing exfoliated GO-NTA sheets in a subphase containing a mixture of water and a volatile polar protic solvent, such as 2-propanol (IPA), followed by compression using a Langmuir trough to achieve close-packed, edge-to-edge alignment of the sheets at a surface pressure of approximately 15 mN/m. The inclusion of IPA in the subphase reduces surface tension and enhances the interfacial activity of GO-NTA, facilitating rapid monolayer formation and improving transfer efficiency during L-S deposition onto solid supports.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a method for transferring the compressed GO-NTA monolayer onto electron microscopy grids or silicon wafers via horizontal L-S transfer, yielding coatings with controlled thickness (~1.3 nm), high uniformity, and minimal multilayer overlap. The transferred films are characterized by single-layer morphology as confirmed by atomic force microscopy (AFM), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), selected area electron diffraction (SAED), and epifluorescence microscopy.

In a further aspect, the invention provides a dual antifouling strategy to deactivate reactive functional groups on the GO-NTA surface that contribute to non-specific binding. This involves: (i) covalent coupling of 4-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) to residual epoxide and aldehyde groups on GO-NTA, thereby blocking sites prone to random covalent attachment of biomolecules; and (ii) subsequent adsorption of bovine serum albumin (BSA) onto the PABA-modified surface to sterically shield remaining hydrophobic patches and charged residues. The resulting BSA-PABA-GO-NTA coating exhibits dramatically reduced non-specific adsorption while retaining the ability to specifically capture His-tagged targets through Ni²⁺-mediated NTA coordination.

In still another aspect, the invention provides a method for on-grid affinity purification of His-tagged proteins or viruses directly from clarified cell lysates by: (a) activating the BSA-PABA-GO-NTA coated grid with Ni²⁺ ions; (b) incubating the grid with the lysate to allow specific capture of His-tagged species; (c) washing away non-bound contaminants; and (d) optionally eluting the captured target with imidazole. This workflow enables immediate visualization of purified targets by negative-stain TEM or cryo-EM without intermediate purification steps.

In a final aspect, the invention demonstrates the utility of the BSA-PABA-GO-NTA grid system for high-resolution single-particle cryo-EM reconstruction, as exemplified by the successful capture and structural analysis of His6-GroEL from E. coli lysate, yielding a density map at 8.1 Å resolution that clearly resolves the engineered His-tag extensions within the central pore of the GroEL complex. The combination of atomic thinness, superior electron dissipation, and specific on-grid purification renders this platform uniquely suited for accelerating structural biology workflows and reducing sample consumption.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a functionalized graphene oxide monolayer system designed for high-efficiency, specific capture of polyhistidine-tagged biomolecules directly from complex biological mixtures, particularly for applications in transmission electron microscopy and cryogenic electron microscopy. The core innovation lies in the integration of three key technological advances: (1) the covalent functionalization of graphene oxide with nitrilotriacetic acid (NTA) chelators via lysine spacers to create a stable, high-density affinity surface; (2) the controlled assembly of these functionalized sheets into uniform, single-layer monolayers at the air-water interface using a solvent-assisted Langmuir-Schaefer transfer protocol; and (3) the implementation of a dual antifouling strategy combining covalent passivation with 4-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) and proteinaceous blocking with bovine serum albumin (BSA) to eliminate non-specific binding while preserving specific metal-chelate interactions.

Graphene oxide (GO) serves as the foundational material due to its unique combination of physical and chemical properties. Synthesized via modified Hummers’ method, GO consists of single-atom-thick sheets of sp²-hybridized carbon atoms interrupted by sp³ domains bearing oxygenated functional groups. These include carboxylic acids predominantly at sheet edges, as well as hydroxyls, epoxides, and carbonyls distributed across the basal plane. This chemical heterogeneity provides multiple sites for covalent derivatization while conferring amphiphilic character that facilitates interfacial behavior. Critically, the lateral dimensions of GO sheets (typically 0.5–20 μm) and their thickness (~1 nm for monolayers) make them ideal for TEM applications, offering superior electron transparency compared to conventional amorphous carbon films and enhanced thermal/electrical conductivity that mitigates beam-induced damage and charging artifacts.

The functionalization of GO with NTA groups is achieved through a multi-step synthetic sequence that ensures high ligand density and chemical stability. Initially, carboxylic acid groups on GO are activated using thionyl chloride (SOCl₂) in the presence of a catalytic amount of dimethylformamide (DMF), converting them to reactive acyl chlorides. These intermediates are then reacted with a tris-t-butyl ester derivative of lysine-NTA, wherein the α-amino group of lysine is linked to the NTA moiety and the ε-amino group is available for conjugation to GO. The reaction proceeds via nucleophilic acyl substitution, forming stable amide bonds between GO and the lysine-NTA construct. The t-butyl protecting groups on the NTA carboxylates prevent unwanted side reactions during conjugation and are subsequently removed by treatment with trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), yielding free NTA chelating arms capable of coordinating divalent metal ions such as Ni²⁺, Co²⁺, or Cu²⁺. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirms successful functionalization through the appearance of characteristic C–H stretches from the lysine backbone and the disappearance of t-butyl vibrations after deprotection, while UV-vis spectroscopy shows a hypsochromic shift in the absorption maximum from ~240 nm (pristine GO) to ~280 nm (GO-NTA), consistent with n→π* transitions from newly introduced carbonyl functionalities.

The amphiphilic nature of GO-NTA arises from the spatial segregation of hydrophilic and hydrophobic domains. The graphenic core, though partially oxidized, retains significant hydrophobic character, whereas the periphery is densely functionalized with ionizable carboxylates (from both GO and NTA) and polar amide linkages. This edge amphiphilicity drives spontaneous adsorption at the air-water interface, where the hydrophobic core minimizes contact with water while the hydrophilic edges remain solvated. Unlike traditional surfactant-assisted methods that introduce foreign molecules and complicate downstream analysis, the present approach leverages the intrinsic interfacial activity of GO-NTA, enabling clean, surfactant-free monolayer formation.

Monolayer assembly is optimized using a Langmuir trough equipped with movable barriers to compress the interfacial film. A key innovation is the use of a subphase composed of water mixed with a volatile polar protic solvent, preferably 2-propanol (IPA), at concentrations ranging from 5% to 20% (v/v). IPA reduces the surface tension of the subphase, enhancing the mobility and packing efficiency of GO-NTA sheets during compression. Without IPA, compression leads to heterogeneous, multilayered aggregates due to kinetic trapping and insufficient sheet rearrangement. With IPA, however, the system exhibits a well-defined surface pressure-area isotherm characterized by three distinct regimes: (i) a low-pressure region corresponding to isolated, non-interacting sheets; (ii) a steep rise indicating close-packing and edge-to-edge contact; and (iii) a plateau region beyond ~15 mN/m where further compression induces out-of-plane deformations such as wrinkling and sliding rather than irreversible collapse. This behavior mirrors that of phospholipid monolayers and allows precise control over film morphology.

Langmuir-Schaefer (L-S) transfer is performed by horizontally contacting a solid substrate—such as a silicon wafer, bare copper TEM grid, or holey carbon-coated grid—with the compressed monolayer at a surface pressure of 15 mN/m. The presence of IPA in the subphase significantly improves transfer homogeneity and yield, with efficiencies of 75–85% as determined by area loss measurements. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) of transferred films reveals uniform thicknesses of approximately 1.3 nm, consistent with single-layer GO, whereas transfers from pure water subphases show variable thicknesses exceeding 3 nm due to multilayer stacking. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) corroborates these findings, showing continuous, non-overlapping sheet coverage on IPA-assisted transfers versus patchy, aggregated morphologies in controls. Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns from transferred films exhibit sharp hexagonal spots indicative of crystalline graphenic order, confirming the preservation of structural integrity during processing.

Despite the specificity of the NTA-Ni²⁺-His6 interaction, unmodified GO-NTA surfaces exhibit substantial non-specific binding due to residual aldehyde and epoxide groups that can form Schiff bases or Michael adducts with lysine residues on proteins. To address this, a dual antifouling protocol is employed. First, the GO-NTA surface is treated with 4-aminobenzoic acid (PABA), which reacts selectively with electrophilic sites (epoxides and aldehydes) via nucleophilic ring-opening or imine formation, respectively. This covalent passivation reduces—but does not eliminate—non-specific adsorption, as evidenced by negative-stain TEM showing residual phage binding in the absence of Ni²⁺. Second, the PABA-modified surface is incubated with a dilute solution of bovine serum albumin (BSA, 0.1 mg/mL), which adsorbs non-covalently to remaining hydrophobic patches and charged regions, creating a steric and electrostatic barrier against non-target molecules. The resulting BSA-PABA-GO-NTA coating exhibits near-complete suppression of background binding while maintaining high affinity for His-tagged targets upon Ni²⁺ activation.

The operational workflow for on-grid purification is straightforward and compatible with standard EM protocols. Grids coated with BSA-PABA-GO-NTA are first activated by brief incubation with NiSO₄ (1 mM, 5 min), followed by washing to remove excess metal ions. The activated grid is then exposed to a clarified cell lysate containing the His-tagged target for 2 minutes, allowing specific capture via NTA-Ni²⁺-His6 coordination. Unbound cellular components are removed by gentle washing with buffer and water, and the grid is either negatively stained for TEM or vitrified for cryo-EM. Specificity is confirmed by control experiments showing negligible binding in the absence of Ni²⁺ or in the presence of imidazole (500 mM), which competes with His-tags for Ni²⁺ coordination and elutes captured targets.

The system has been validated using two model His-tagged systems: C-terminally His6-tagged T7 bacteriophage and N-terminally His6-tagged GroEL chaperonin. In both cases, specific capture from E. coli lysates was achieved with high signal-to-noise ratios, enabling clear visualization of intact viral particles and GroEL complexes, respectively. For cryo-EM, optimal results were obtained using lacey carbon-supported 400-mesh grids, which accommodate the larger GO-NTA sheet dimensions (~16 μm) better than finer meshes. Single-particle analysis of His6-GroEL yielded a 3D reconstruction at 8.1 Å resolution (gold-standard FSC 0.143 criterion), revealing not only the canonical GroEL barrel structure but also additional density within the central pore corresponding to the engineered His-tag and linker sequences (MRGSHHHHHHTDPALRA at the N-terminus and GLCGR at the C-terminus). This observation underscores the system’s sensitivity and fidelity in preserving native conformational states, even for flexible terminal extensions.

The advantages of the present invention over prior art are manifold. Compared to lipid-based NTA grids, the GO-NTA platform offers superior mechanical stability, reduced thickness, enhanced electron conductivity, and resistance to beam-induced motion. Unlike drop-cast GO films, which form multilayered, heterogeneous coatings, the L-S transfer method ensures monolayer uniformity essential for high-resolution imaging. The dual antifouling strategy effectively eliminates the non-specific binding that plagues unmodified GO surfaces, enabling direct application to crude lysates—a capability absent in existing commercial grids. Finally, the entire workflow—from grid coating to data collection—can be completed in under two hours, dramatically accelerating structural biology pipelines and reducing sample requirements by orders of magnitude.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Synthesis of GO-NTA began with the preparation of graphene oxide (GO) according to the modified Hummers’ method as described by Marcano et al. Briefly, graphite powder was oxidized in a mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid, sodium nitrate, and potassium permanganate under controlled temperature conditions, followed by quenching with hydrogen peroxide and extensive dialysis to yield a stable aqueous dispersion of GO. The resulting GO material was isolated by lyophilization and stored under inert atmosphere. For functionalization, 335 mg of dried GO was suspended in 60 mL of thionyl chloride (SOCl₂) containing 1.5 mL of dimethylformamide (DMF) as a catalyst and stirred at 70°C for 72 hours to convert surface carboxylic acids to acyl chlorides. Excess SOCl₂ and DMF were removed under reduced pressure, and the residue was washed three times with dry dichloromethane (DCM) to eliminate residual reagents. The activated GO was then dispersed in 50 mL of acetonitrile (ACN) with 3 mL of triethylamine (Et₃N) to neutralize any acidic byproducts, and 533 mg of tris(O-t-butyl)-Nα,Nα-dicarboxymethyllysine ester was added. The mixture was heated to 100°C and stirred for 72 hours to allow amide bond formation between the acyl chloride groups on GO and the ε-amino group of the lysine-NTA derivative. The product, designated GO-NTA-(O-t-Bu)₃, was isolated by centrifugation at 9,000 rpm for 15 minutes, washed alternately with tetrahydrofuran (THF) and water three times each, and dried under vacuum at 60°C for 24 hours. Deprotection of the t-butyl ester groups was achieved by dissolving 180 mg of the protected intermediate in a mixture of 10 mL trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and 30 mL THF, followed by heating at 60°C for 5 hours. The final GO-NTA product was purified by centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 15 minutes, washed three times each with THF and water, and stored as a solid or aqueous dispersion. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy confirmed the successful introduction of NTA groups by the appearance of aliphatic C–H stretches at 2933 cm⁻¹ in the protected intermediate and their attenuation after TFA deprotection, while UV-vis spectroscopy showed a characteristic absorption peak at 280 nm, consistent with literature reports for NTA-functionalized graphenic materials.

### Example 2

Exfoliation of GO-NTA sheets was performed to obtain a monodisperse suspension suitable for Langmuir monolayer formation. One milligram of dried GO-NTA was suspended in 1 mL of a 5:1 (v/v) mixture of methanol and water to enhance initial wetting and dispersion. The suspension was subjected to probe sonication at 150 watts using five cycles of 45 seconds on followed by 45 seconds off to prevent overheating. The resulting dispersion was centrifuged at 1,200 × g for 10 minutes to pellet large aggregates, and the supernatant containing exfoliated sheets was collected. This supernatant underwent a second round of identical sonication and centrifugation to further refine the sheet size distribution. The final exfoliated GO-NTA dispersion exhibited a concentration of approximately 1 mg/mL as determined by UV-vis absorbance at 280 nm using a batch-specific extinction coefficient derived from serial dilutions of a gravimetrically prepared standard. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated a hydrodynamic diameter distribution centered around 500–800 nm, consistent with lateral sheet dimensions of 1–2 μm as later confirmed by AFM. The dispersion remained stable for at least one week when stored at 4°C under argon, with no visible precipitation or aggregation.

### Example 3

Langmuir monolayer formation and Langmuir-Schaefer (L-S) transfer of GO-NTA were carried out using a Kibron μTrough equipped with Wilhelmy plate surface pressure detection. The trough was filled with ultrapure water, and 2-propanol (IPA) was added to achieve a final concentration of 10% (v/v) in the subphase. The exfoliated GO-NTA dispersion (1 mg/mL in 5:1 MeOH:H₂O) was loaded into a gastight syringe connected to a syringe pump. The dispersion was injected slowly onto the subphase at a rate of 100 μL/min until the surface pressure reached 5 mN/m, at which point injection was halted. The film was allowed to equilibrate for 5 minutes to permit solvent evaporation and sheet relaxation. Compression was then initiated at a rate of 5 mm/min until a surface pressure of 15 mN/m was attained, as indicated by a sharp increase in the pressure-area isotherm followed by a plateau. At this pressure, the monolayer exhibited close-packed, edge-to-edge alignment of GO-NTA sheets without significant multilayer formation. For L-S transfer, a clean silicon wafer (2.25 cm²) was mounted horizontally on a transfer arm and brought into contact with the monolayer. Upon contact, the film adhered to the hydrophilic silicon surface, and the wafer was withdrawn at a constant speed of 5 mm/min. Post-transfer, the surface pressure dropped by 75–85%, indicating high transfer efficiency. Identical procedures were used to coat 1500-mesh bare copper TEM grids and lacey carbon-coated 400-mesh grids, with the latter requiring careful alignment to ensure coverage of the holey regions.

### Example 4

Characterization of transferred GO-NTA monolayers was performed using multiple complementary techniques. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) in tapping mode revealed uniform film thicknesses of 1.28 ± 0.15 nm across silicon wafers coated from IPA-containing subphases, consistent with single-layer GO. In contrast, transfers from pure water subphases showed thicknesses ranging from 2.5 to 5.0 nm with significant local variations, indicative of multilayer stacking. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at 5 kV acceleration voltage confirmed continuous, non-overlapping sheet coverage on IPA-assisted transfers, whereas water-only transfers exhibited island-like aggregates and voids. Epifluorescence microscopy was employed using fluorescein-labeled PABA-GO-NTA (F-PABA-GO-NTA) prepared by conjugating aminofluorescein to PABA-modified GO-NTA as described in Example 5. Grids coated with F-PABA-GO-NTA showed bright, homogeneous fluorescence across the holes of 1500-mesh grids, with intensity significantly higher than bare copper controls, confirming complete hole coverage by a single fluorescent layer. Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns acquired from F-PABA-GO-NTA films on 2000-mesh bare grids displayed sharp hexagonal diffraction spots with d-spacings of 2.13 Å and 1.23 Å, corresponding to the (100) and (110) lattice planes of graphitic carbon, thereby verifying the crystalline nature and monolayer status of the transferred films.

### Example 5

Chemical deactivation of reactive GO-NTA surfaces was achieved through a two-step antifouling protocol. First, 30 mg of 4-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) was added to 10 mL of a 1 mg/mL GO-NTA dispersion in water. The mixture was probe-sonicated at 150 W for 30 seconds to ensure homogeneity and then rotated end-over-end for 24 hours at room temperature to allow covalent reaction between PABA’s amino group and electrophilic sites (epoxides and aldehydes) on GO-NTA. The resulting PABA-GO-NTA was isolated by centrifugation at 1,200 × g for 10 minutes, washed three times with water, and re-exfoliated as in Example 2. Second, for BSA blocking, L-S transferred PABA-GO-NTA grids were dried overnight in a desiccator and then placed on a Teflon strip. Ten microliters of a 0.1 mg/mL BSA solution in double-deionized water was applied to each grid and incubated for 5 minutes at room temperature. Excess BSA was removed by three sequential washes with 20 μL aliquots of water. The grids were dried again in a desiccator and stored until use. Negative-stain TEM of His6-T7 bacteriophage applied to these grids demonstrated that PABA modification alone reduced non-specific binding by ~60% compared to unmodified GO-NTA, while the addition of BSA reduced it by >95%, achieving near-background levels in the absence of Ni²⁺ activation.

### Example 6

Affinity capture of purified His6-T7 bacteriophage was evaluated to validate the specificity of the BSA-PABA-GO-NTA system. Purified phage (10¹⁰ particles/mL in HEPES buffer, pH 7.4) was applied to grids under four conditions: (i) unmodified GO-NTA without Ni²⁺; (ii) GO-NTA with Ni²⁺ activation (1 mM NiSO₄, 5 min); (iii) PABA-GO-NTA with Ni²⁺; and (iv) BSA-PABA-GO-NTA with Ni²⁺. After 2-minute incubation and washing, grids were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and imaged by TEM. Condition (i) showed dense, random phage adsorption due to covalent coupling with reactive GO groups. Condition (ii) paradoxically showed fewer phage particles, as Ni²⁺ coordination appeared to block or alter the reactive sites responsible for non-specific binding. Condition (iii) exhibited reduced non-specific binding but still showed some background. Condition (iv) displayed high-density, specific phage capture only in the presence of Ni²⁺, with negligible binding in its absence. Elution with 500 mM imidazole confirmed reversibility, as >90% of bound phage was released, demonstrating the integrity of the NTA-Ni²⁺-His6 interaction.

### Example 7

Direct capture of His6-T7 bacteriophage from clarified E. coli lysate was performed to assess performance in complex biological matrices. BL21 cells were infected with His6-T7 phage and lysed by centrifugation to remove cell debris, yielding a supernatant containing ~10⁹ infectious particles/mL. BSA-PABA-GO-NTA grids were activated with Ni²⁺ as in Example 6 and incubated with 5 μL of lysate for 2 minutes. Control grids included: (i) Ni²⁺-free BSA-PABA-GO-NTA; and (ii) Ni²⁺-activated grids pre-treated with 500 mM imidazole to strip metal ions. TEM imaging revealed abundant, well-separated phage particles on Ni²⁺-activated grids, with minimal background from cellular proteins or nucleic acids. In contrast, control grids showed only sparse, non-specific adsorption indistinguishable from blank areas. Quantitative analysis indicated a capture efficiency of ~40% relative to purified phage controls, demonstrating robust performance despite the complexity of the lysate.

### Example 8

On-grid capture of His6-GroEL from E. coli lysate was optimized for cryo-EM applications. Cells expressing N-terminal His6-GroEL from the ASKA library were induced with IPTG, lysed by sonication, and clarified by centrifugation. Initial trials using 1500-mesh BSA-PABA-GO-NTA grids resulted in excessively thick ice layers unsuitable for high-resolution imaging. Switching to lacey carbon-supported 400-mesh grids improved vitrification quality due to better matching between GO-NTA sheet size (~16 μm) and grid hole dimensions (37 μm). Grids were activated with Ni²⁺, incubated with 10-fold diluted lysate for 2 minutes, blotted twice for 6 seconds each at 80% humidity using a Vitrobot Mark IV, and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane. Cryo-EM micrographs acquired at 300 kV on a Titan Krios microscope showed well-dispersed GroEL complexes with minimal background, confirming successful on-grid purification. Particle density was sufficient for automated data collection, with ~25 particles per micrograph on average.

### Example 9

Single-particle cryo-EM reconstruction of His6-GroEL was performed to demonstrate high-resolution structural capability. A total of 217 dose-fractionated micrographs were collected using a Gatan K2 Summit camera at a nominal magnification of 11,000×, yielding a pixel size of 1.32 Å. Movie frames were motion-corrected and averaged using Appion software, and contrast transfer function (CTF) parameters were estimated using CTFFIND4. Particles were manually picked due to challenges in automated picking caused by the BSA blocking layer, yielding 5,363 particle images. Initial 2D classification in EMAN2.1 produced 50 class averages, of which 12 showing clear top and side views were selected for ab initio 3D reconstruction with imposed D7 symmetry. Gold-standard refinement over 12 iterations converged to a final resolution of 8.1 Å (FSC = 0.143). Comparison with the published 4.2 Å map (EMD-5001) using Chimera yielded a cross-correlation resolution of 9 Å (FSC = 0.5). Notably, the reconstructed map displayed additional density within the central pore of GroEL, attributed to the 308-residue N- and C-terminal extensions (including 84 histidines) present in the engineered construct. This observation validates the system’s ability to preserve flexible, low-complexity regions that are often lost in traditional purification workflows, highlighting its potential for studying dynamic protein conformations and post-translational modifications directly from cellular environments.
