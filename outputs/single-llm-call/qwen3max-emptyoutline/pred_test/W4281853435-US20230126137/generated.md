# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) constitute a fundamental class of regulatory molecules that govern the post-transcriptional fate of RNA molecules throughout the eukaryotic cell. These proteins orchestrate a wide array of RNA processing events, including alternative splicing, polyadenylation, RNA editing, nuclear export, subcellular localization, translation efficiency, and RNA stability. By recognizing specific RNA sequences or structural motifs, RBPs exert precise spatiotemporal control over gene expression, thereby enabling complex cellular functions and developmental programs. The human genome encodes an estimated several thousand RBPs, with recent studies suggesting that at least 15% of protein-coding genes possess RNA-binding capabilities, many of which remain functionally uncharacterized. This expanding repertoire underscores the critical yet incompletely understood role of RBPs in maintaining cellular homeostasis.

Dysregulation of RBP function has been causally linked to a broad spectrum of human diseases, ranging from neurodegenerative disorders such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), to various malignancies including leukemia, glioblastoma, and breast cancer. In these pathologies, mutations in RBP genes, aberrant expression levels, or mislocalization of RBPs can lead to widespread alterations in the transcriptome, resulting in the production of dysfunctional protein isoforms or the destabilization of key regulatory RNAs. Consequently, comprehensive mapping of RBP–RNA interactions across diverse cell types and disease states is essential for elucidating disease mechanisms and identifying novel therapeutic targets.

To address this need, high-throughput methodologies have been developed to identify RBP binding sites on a transcriptome-wide scale. Among these, crosslinking and immunoprecipitation (CLIP) and its derivatives—such as HITS-CLIP, iCLIP, and enhanced CLIP (eCLIP)—have emerged as gold-standard techniques. These methods involve ultraviolet (UV) crosslinking of RBPs to their bound RNA targets in living cells, followed by stringent immunoprecipitation using RBP-specific antibodies, RNA fragmentation, and high-throughput sequencing of the co-immunoprecipitated RNA fragments. The eCLIP protocol, in particular, introduced significant improvements in reproducibility and signal-to-noise ratio by incorporating a size-matched input (SMI) control, which accounts for background RNA species that co-migrate with the RBP–RNA complex during gel electrophoresis.

Despite these advances, current CLIP-based approaches face substantial limitations that hinder their scalability and applicability to rare or clinically relevant samples. First, all conventional CLIP protocols require separation of the immunoprecipitated RBP–RNA complexes by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE), followed by transfer to a nitrocellulose membrane and manual excision of the band corresponding to the expected molecular weight of the RBP–RNA complex. This step is not only labor-intensive and time-consuming—typically requiring an additional 1.5 to 2 days—but also introduces significant inter-operator variability due to subjective estimation of band positions. Moreover, the resolution of SDS–PAGE may be insufficient to distinguish between monomeric and higher-order RBP complexes, potentially leading to contamination with non-specific RNA species.

Second, each RBP must be interrogated in a separate immunoprecipitation reaction, necessitating large quantities of input cellular material—often millions of cells per RBP. This requirement becomes prohibitive when studying multiple RBPs simultaneously or when working with limited clinical specimens such as patient biopsies, primary cell isolates, or rare stem cell populations. The inability to multiplex RBP profiling within a single sample represents a major bottleneck in efforts to construct comprehensive RBP interaction maps across the human proteome.

In response to these challenges, there exists a pressing need for a next-generation CLIP methodology that eliminates the reliance on gel-based size selection and enables concurrent profiling of multiple RBPs from a single biological sample. Such a technology would dramatically reduce hands-on time, minimize technical variability, conserve precious input material, and accelerate the functional annotation of the RNA-binding proteome. The present invention addresses these unmet needs through the development of antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC), a novel platform that integrates DNA-barcoded antibodies with proximity-based ligation chemistry to achieve high-fidelity, multiplexed RBP profiling without the need for SDS–PAGE or membrane transfer.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for simultaneously identifying RNA binding sites for multiple RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) in a single biological sample, thereby overcoming the limitations of conventional CLIP-based methodologies. This method, termed antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC), utilizes a set of uniquely DNA-barcoded antibodies, each specific to a distinct RBP, which are pooled and used in a single immunoprecipitation reaction. Following UV crosslinking of RBPs to their cognate RNA targets in intact cells, the cell lysate is incubated with the mixture of barcoded antibodies conjugated to magnetic beads. The immunoprecipitated RBP–RNA complexes remain bound to the beads, eliminating the need for SDS–PAGE and nitrocellulose membrane transfer traditionally used for size selection.

Crucially, the DNA barcode attached to each antibody serves two essential functions. First, it enables proximity-based ligation of the barcode sequence directly to the 3′ end of the crosslinked RNA fragment while the complex is still immobilized on the bead. This ligation event creates a chimeric RNA–DNA molecule in which the identity of the RBP is encoded within the DNA portion of the construct. Second, during library preparation and sequencing, the DNA barcode allows for computational demultiplexing of the sequencing reads, enabling the assignment of each RNA fragment to its corresponding RBP. This dual functionality obviates both the manual gel excision step and the requirement for separate immunoprecipitation reactions for each RBP.

The invention further provides a complement control (CC) strategy for peak calling in multiplexed experiments. Instead of relying on a size-matched input (SMI) control derived from a separate gel lane, the CC leverages the collective binding information from all other RBPs in the multiplexed pool as a dynamic background model. For any given RBP, statistically enriched binding sites are identified by comparing the read density at candidate regions against the aggregate read density from the remaining RBPs in the same sample. This approach not only eliminates the need for an additional SMI library but also provides a more contextually relevant measure of background RNA occupancy, as it accounts for experimental biases inherent to the specific sample and immunoprecipitation conditions.

In one embodiment, the method comprises: (a) crosslinking RBPs to RNA in a population of cells; (b) lysing the cells to generate a lysate containing RBP–RNA complexes; (c) contacting the lysate with a mixture of magnetic beads, each type of bead conjugated to a different antibody specific to a distinct RBP, wherein each antibody is covalently linked to a unique DNA barcode; (d) performing a proximity ligation reaction that joins the DNA barcode of each antibody to the 3′ end of the RNA fragment crosslinked to its corresponding RBP; (e) eluting the chimeric RNA–DNA molecules from the beads; (f) reverse transcribing the RNA portion to generate cDNA; (g) amplifying the cDNA library; and (h) sequencing the library and demultiplexing the reads based on the DNA barcode to assign each RNA fragment to its cognate RBP.

The invention also encompasses kits comprising a set of DNA-barcoded antibodies, each specific to a different RBP, along with reagents for proximity ligation, reverse transcription, and library amplification. These kits are designed to facilitate the implementation of the ABC method in research laboratories and clinical settings, enabling high-throughput profiling of RBP–RNA interactions with minimal input material and reduced technical complexity.

Validation experiments demonstrate that ABC achieves sensitivity and specificity comparable to eCLIP in both singleplex and multiplex configurations. For well-characterized RBPs such as RBFOX2 and SLBP, ABC recovers binding sites with similar motif enrichment, genomic distribution, and functional relevance as eCLIP. In multiplexed experiments involving ten distinct RBPs, ABC accurately recapitulates known binding preferences across genic features—including 5′ UTRs, coding sequences, 3′ UTRs, and splice sites—and shows high concordance with eCLIP data in terms of peak overlap, metagene profiles, and target gene ranking. Critically, a single ABC library prepared from one tube of cells yields data equivalent to ten separate eCLIP experiments, representing a tenfold reduction in required input material and experimental effort.

Thus, the ABC method represents a transformative advance in the field of RNA biology, enabling scalable, cost-effective, and high-fidelity mapping of the RNA-binding proteome. Its application will accelerate the functional annotation of RBPs, facilitate the discovery of disease-associated RBP dysregulation, and support the development of RNA-targeted therapeutics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

As used herein, the term “RNA-binding protein” or “RBP” refers to any protein that directly and specifically interacts with RNA molecules, whether through recognition of sequence motifs, secondary structures, or other RNA features. RBPs include canonical RNA-binding domains such as RRM, KH, ZnF, and Pumilio domains, as well as proteins that bind RNA through non-canonical mechanisms.

The term “DNA barcode” denotes a short, unique nucleotide sequence (typically 10–30 nucleotides in length) that is covalently attached to an antibody and serves as a molecular identifier for that antibody. Each DNA barcode in a multiplexed experiment is distinct and non-overlapping with others in the set, enabling unambiguous assignment of sequencing reads to their corresponding RBP.

“Proximity-based ligation” refers to a ligation reaction that occurs only when two nucleic acid molecules are in close physical proximity, typically facilitated by their co-localization on a solid support such as a magnetic bead. In the context of the present invention, proximity ligation joins the DNA barcode of an antibody to the 3′ end of the RNA fragment crosslinked to the same RBP, ensuring that the barcode accurately reflects the identity of the RBP that captured the RNA.

A “complement control” or “CC” is a computational background model derived from the aggregate sequencing reads of all other RBPs in a multiplexed ABC experiment. For a given RBP, the CC represents the expected distribution of non-specific RNA binding under the experimental conditions and is used to calculate statistical enrichment of binding sites specific to that RBP.

“Size-matched input” or “SMI” refers to a control library used in conventional eCLIP protocols, generated from RNA fragments that migrate in the same gel region as the immunoprecipitated RBP–RNA complex but are not specifically bound to the RBP. SMI accounts for background RNA species that co-purify due to size or other non-specific interactions.

“Demultiplexing” is the process of assigning sequencing reads to their respective RBPs based on the DNA barcode sequence present in each read. This is performed computationally after sequencing using alignment and classification algorithms.

“Uniquely mapped reads” are sequencing reads that align to a single location in the reference genome with high confidence, excluding reads that map to multiple genomic loci (e.g., repetitive elements).

### Methods

The present invention provides a method for multiplexed identification of RNA binding sites for multiple RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) from a single biological sample. The method, termed antibody-barcode eCLIP (ABC), eliminates the need for SDS–PAGE and nitrocellulose membrane transfer by utilizing DNA-barcoded antibodies and proximity-based ligation chemistry.

The method begins with the crosslinking of RBPs to their bound RNA targets in intact cells. This is achieved by irradiating a population of cells with ultraviolet (UV) light at a wavelength of 254 nm and a dose of 400 mJ/cm², which induces covalent bonds between RBPs and adjacent RNA nucleotides. Crosslinking is performed on ice to minimize cellular stress and preserve complex integrity. Following crosslinking, cells are harvested, washed, and flash-frozen for storage at −80°C.

Cell pellets are then lysed in a buffer containing detergents (e.g., Igepal, SDS, sodium deoxycholate), salts (e.g., NaCl, EDTA), and RNase inhibitors to solubilize RBP–RNA complexes while preventing RNA degradation. The lysate is subjected to limited RNase digestion to fragment RNA into manageable sizes (typically 50–150 nucleotides), followed by centrifugation to remove cellular debris.

Concurrently, a set of antibodies, each specific to a distinct RBP, is conjugated to unique DNA barcodes. This is accomplished through a two-step click chemistry reaction. First, antibodies are modified with dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) groups by reacting with DBCO-N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester. Separately, synthetic oligonucleotides containing a unique barcode sequence and an azide group are prepared. The DBCO-modified antibodies are then mixed with the azide-labeled barcodes, resulting in a strain-promoted alkyne-azide cycloaddition (SPAAC) that covalently links the DNA barcode to the antibody. The barcoded antibodies are subsequently conjugated to magnetic beads (e.g., anti-rabbit Dynabeads) via standard immunoglobulin-binding chemistry.

The cell lysate is incubated with a mixture of the barcoded antibody–bead conjugates, allowing simultaneous immunoprecipitation of multiple RBP–RNA complexes. After overnight incubation at 4°C with rotation, the beads are washed extensively with high-salt and low-salt buffers to remove non-specifically bound material.

The key innovation of the ABC method lies in the proximity ligation step. While the RBP–RNA complexes remain bound to the beads, the RNA fragments are phosphorylated at their 5′ ends using T4 polynucleotide kinase (PNK). A proximity ligation reaction is then performed using T4 RNA ligase, which joins the 3′ end of the RNA fragment to the 5′ end of the DNA barcode attached to the same antibody. Because both the RNA and the barcode are immobilized on the same bead in close proximity, ligation occurs preferentially between cognate pairs, minimizing cross-talk between different RBPs.

Following ligation, the chimeric RNA–DNA molecules are eluted from the beads by proteinase K digestion. The RNA portion is reverse transcribed using a primer complementary to the DNA barcode, generating cDNA that retains the barcode sequence. The cDNA is then amplified using PCR with primers that add Illumina sequencing adapters and sample indices. The resulting library is sequenced on a high-throughput platform (e.g., Illumina NextSeq 2000), typically yielding 25 million reads per barcode in a 10-plex experiment.

After sequencing, raw reads are processed through a bioinformatic pipeline. Adapters are trimmed, and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) are extracted to enable deduplication. Reads are demultiplexed based on the DNA barcode sequence, assigning each read to its corresponding RBP. The RNA portion of the read is aligned to the reference genome using a splice-aware aligner such as STAR, and only uniquely mapped reads are retained.

Peak calling is performed using a modified version of the CLIPper algorithm. Instead of comparing against an SMI control, the method employs a complement control (CC) derived from the sum of reads from all other RBPs in the multiplexed sample. For each candidate binding site, a contingency table is constructed comparing the number of reads for the RBP of interest versus the CC within the region and outside it. Statistical significance is assessed using a chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, and peaks with P < 0.001 and greater than eightfold enrichment over CC are deemed significant.

The method is validated by comparing ABC data with eCLIP data for well-characterized RBPs. Concordance is assessed through multiple metrics, including motif enrichment, genomic distribution of peaks, metagene profiles, and overlap coefficients. Results demonstrate that ABC achieves comparable sensitivity and specificity to eCLIP, with the added advantage of multiplexing.

### Kits

The invention further provides kits for performing the ABC method. Each kit includes a set of DNA-barcoded antibodies, with each antibody specific to a different RBP and conjugated to a unique DNA barcode. The kit may include pre-conjugated antibody–bead complexes or separate components for user conjugation. Additionally, the kit contains reagents for proximity ligation, including T4 RNA ligase, ligation buffer, and RNase inhibitors; reagents for reverse transcription, including reverse transcriptase, dNTPs, and RT primers; and reagents for PCR amplification, including high-fidelity DNA polymerase, dNTPs, and indexed Illumina primers.

The kit may also include detailed protocols, quality control standards, and bioinformatic software for data processing and analysis. Optional components include Zeba desalting columns for buffer exchange, MyOne Silane beads for cDNA purification, and qPCR reagents for library quantification.

Kits are available in various formats, including singleplex (one RBP) and multiplex (e.g., 5-plex, 10-plex, 20-plex) configurations, allowing users to select the appropriate level of multiplexing based on their experimental needs. Antibody panels may be tailored to specific biological pathways, disease contexts, or cellular compartments, facilitating targeted RBP profiling.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Singleplex validation of the ABC method was performed using two well-characterized RNA-binding proteins: RBFOX2 and SLBP. RBFOX2 is a splicing regulator that recognizes the GCAUG motif and exhibits position-dependent effects on alternative splicing, promoting exon inclusion when bound upstream of an exon and exclusion when bound downstream. SLBP binds specifically to the stem-loop structure in the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of replication-dependent histone mRNAs and is essential for their processing and stability.

HEK293XT cells were used for RBFOX2 experiments, while K562 cells were used for SLBP. Cells were crosslinked with UV light (254 nm, 400 mJ/cm²), lysed, and subjected to immunoprecipitation using either conventional eCLIP or ABC protocols. For ABC, the anti-RBFOX2 or anti-SLBP antibody was conjugated to a unique DNA barcode via click chemistry as described. Following immunoprecipitation, proximity ligation was performed on-bead, and libraries were prepared and sequenced.

Library complexity was assessed by enumerating the number of “usable” reads—defined as uniquely mapped reads after removal of PCR duplicates. ABC and eCLIP showed comparable library complexity, with no significant difference in the number of unique molecules captured. UV dependence was confirmed by a 32-fold reduction in library yield in a no-UV control, as measured by qPCR.

Genome browser tracks revealed highly similar read density profiles between ABC and eCLIP at known binding sites. For RBFOX2, strong binding was observed in the intronic region of the NDEL1 gene, while for SLBP, enrichment was seen in the 3′ UTR of the H1–2 histone gene. Quantitative comparison showed that the proportion of reads falling within irreproducible discovery rate (IDR)-defined eCLIP peaks was nearly identical between ABC and eCLIP replicates. Similarly, the fraction of reads containing the conserved GCAUG motif was approximately 0.38% for eCLIP and 0.4% for ABC, indicating equivalent motif specificity.

Functional validation was performed by analyzing RBFOX2 binding relative to alternatively spliced exons. Upon RBFOX2 knockdown, 150 cassette exons exhibited significant changes in inclusion levels. ABC-derived peaks reproduced the known positional enrichment: binding upstream of skipped exons and within included exons, consistent with eCLIP data. For SLBP, metagene analysis showed a sharp peak at the stem-loop region in the 3′ UTR of histone mRNAs, with no difference in peak shape or intensity between ABC and eCLIP. Gene-level enrichment analysis demonstrated that both methods ranked histone genes among the top 100 most enriched targets, with nearly identical cumulative counts.

These results establish that the ABC method, even in singleplex format, performs with sensitivity and specificity equivalent to the gold-standard eCLIP protocol, validating the core chemistry and workflow of the invention.

### Example 3

Multiplexed profiling of ten distinct RNA-binding proteins was performed to demonstrate the scalability of the ABC method. The selected RBPs represent a diversity of known binding preferences and functional roles: DDX3 and EIF3G bind 5′ UTRs and regulate translation initiation; IGF2BP2, FAM120A, PUM2, and ZC3H11A bind 3′ UTRs and influence mRNA stability and localization; LIN28B binds coding sequences and blocks let-7 microRNA processing; SF3B4 and PRPF8 are core spliceosomal components that recognize branch points and 5′ splice sites, respectively; and RBFOX2 regulates alternative splicing via intronic binding.

K562 cells were crosslinked and lysed as before. Ten different antibodies, each previously validated by the ENCODE project, were conjugated to unique DNA barcodes and pooled for a single immunoprecipitation reaction. After proximity ligation and library preparation, the resulting 10-plex library was sequenced to a depth of 250 million reads (25 million per barcode). Reads were demultiplexed computationally, and each RBP’s data were processed independently.

Peak calling was performed using the complement control (CC) strategy, where for each RBP, the background was defined as the sum of reads from the other nine RBPs. This approach successfully identified enriched binding sites without the need for an SMI control. De novo motif analysis using HOMER recovered the known binding motifs for RBFOX2 (GCAUG), PUM2 (UGUANAUA), PRPF8 (AG/GURAGU), and SF3B4 (UACUAAC), confirming the specificity of the ABC data.

Genomic annotation of peaks revealed that each RBP exhibited binding distributions consistent with prior knowledge. DDX3 and EIF3G peaks were predominantly in 5′ UTRs; IGF2BP2, FAM120A, PUM2, and ZC3H11A peaks were enriched in 3′ UTRs; LIN28B peaks localized to coding sequences; and SF3B4 and PRPF8 peaks clustered near splice sites. Metagene profiles around splicing junctions showed that PRPF8 peaks were centered precisely at the 5′ splice site, while SF3B4 peaks peaked at the branch point, matching eCLIP observations. Notably, ABC peaks for PRPF8 were slightly closer to the annotated splice site than eCLIP peaks, suggesting improved resolution.

Overlap analysis between ABC and eCLIP datasets showed high concordance. The overlap coefficient—defined as the number of overlapping peaks divided by the total number of peaks in the smaller dataset—ranged from 0.4 to 0.7 across RBPs, significantly higher than expected by chance. Correlation of average read coverage across peaks was also high (Pearson r > 0.8 for all RBPs), indicating quantitative agreement.

Importantly, no degradation in data quality was observed in the 10-plex experiment compared to singleplex. Library complexity, peak numbers, and motif enrichment were preserved, demonstrating that multiplexing does not compromise performance. This example confirms that a single ABC library can replace ten separate eCLIP experiments, achieving a tenfold reduction in input material and experimental effort.

### Example 4

The complement control (CC) strategy was evaluated for its effectiveness in distinguishing specific RBP binding from background noise. In conventional eCLIP, the size-matched input (SMI) control captures non-specific RNA that co-migrates with the RBP–RNA complex on a gel. However, SMI requires a separate immunoprecipitation and gel excision, doubling the experimental workload.

In ABC, the CC is derived from the other RBPs in the same multiplexed sample, providing a built-in background model. To assess its performance, peak calling was performed using three different backgrounds: SMI (from eCLIP), total RNA-seq, and CC (from ABC). For RBFOX2, peaks called against CC showed superior enrichment in intronic regions known to be functionally relevant, compared to RNA-seq background. Similarly, for SLBP, CC-based peaks prioritized histone mRNAs more effectively than RNA-seq.

Moreover, peaks discarded by eCLIP due to insufficient enrichment over SMI (termed “experimental background”) were found to have lower overlap with CC-enriched ABC peaks than with RNA-seq-enriched peaks. This indicates that CC better accounts for experimental biases and reduces false positives.

An additional experiment tested the flexibility of the CC approach. A singleplex SLBP ABC experiment was analyzed using a CC composed of the ten RBPs from the multiplex panel (which did not include SLBP). Despite the absence of SLBP in the CC, histone mRNAs were still highly ranked among top targets, demonstrating that the CC strategy is robust even when the background RBPs are unrelated to the target RBP.

These results validate the CC as a superior and more efficient alternative to SMI, eliminating the need for a separate control library while improving the specificity of peak calling. This feature is a key advantage of the ABC method, particularly in resource-limited settings.
