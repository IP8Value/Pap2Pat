# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The regulation of gene expression in eukaryotic cells is a complex and highly orchestrated process that involves the interplay of numerous molecular mechanisms, among which chromatin structure and epigenetic modifications play pivotal roles. Chromatin-modifying enzymes, particularly those involved in the post-translational modification of histone proteins, are essential for the dynamic control of genomic accessibility, transcriptional activation, and cellular identity. Among these, the COMPlex of Proteins Associated with Set1 (COMPASS) family of histone methyltransferases has emerged as a critical regulator of enhancer activity and developmental gene expression programs. The COMPASS family includes several members, notably Mixed-Lineage Leukemia 3 (MLL3, encoded by KMT2C) and MLL4 (encoded by KMT2D), which function as the primary H3K4 mono-methyltransferases at enhancer regions. These enzymes catalyze the addition of a single methyl group to lysine 4 of histone H3 (H3K4me1), a hallmark of active or poised enhancers, thereby facilitating the recruitment of transcriptional coactivators such as p300 and the H3K27 demethylase UTX (KDM6A). Beyond their catalytic activity, MLL3 and MLL4 also serve as scaffolding platforms that assemble large multiprotein complexes, including subunits such as NCOA6, PTIP, and RBBP5, which collectively contribute to enhancer activation through both enzymatic and structural mechanisms.

The biological significance of MLL3 and MLL4 is underscored by their indispensable roles in mammalian development. Genetic studies in mice have demonstrated that MLL4 is essential for post-implantation embryogenesis in a dosage-dependent manner, whereas MLL3 becomes critical during late gestation for organ maturation. In mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), while MLL4 is dispensable for maintaining pluripotency and self-renewal, it is required for the timely exit from the pluripotent state and the initiation of lineage commitment. This functional dichotomy highlights the context-dependent roles of MLL3 and MLL4 in cellular differentiation and fate determination. Moreover, the integrity of MLL3/4-mediated enhancer regulation is crucial for diverse developmental processes, including myogenesis, adipogenesis, cardiogenesis, macrophage activation, and germinal center B cell development. Disruption of these pathways leads to profound developmental defects, underscoring the non-redundant functions of these epigenetic regulators.

In parallel with their developmental roles, MLL3 and MLL4 have been increasingly recognized as tumor suppressors in human cancers. Large-scale genomic analyses, particularly those conducted by the Catalogue Of Somatic Mutations In Cancer (COSMIC) consortium, have revealed that KMT2C and KMT2D are among the most frequently mutated genes across a wide spectrum of malignancies, including hematological neoplasms and solid tumors such as breast, bladder, prostate, and colorectal cancers. These mutations are predominantly loss-of-function variants, including nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site alterations that result in truncated or absent protein products. Similarly, germline mutations in KMT2D and its cofactor KDM6A are causally linked to Kabuki syndrome, a multisystem congenital disorder characterized by intellectual disability, skeletal abnormalities, and distinctive facial features. Despite the extensive cataloging of these mutations, the molecular mechanisms by which MLL3/4 dysfunction contributes to oncogenesis and developmental pathology remain poorly understood. This knowledge gap represents a significant barrier to the development of targeted therapeutic strategies for patients harboring these genetic lesions.

A central challenge in cancer biology is the identification of context-specific vulnerabilities—molecular dependencies that arise as a consequence of specific genetic alterations and can be exploited for therapeutic benefit. The concept of synthetic lethality, wherein the simultaneous disruption of two genes results in cell death while disruption of either alone is tolerable, has emerged as a powerful paradigm for precision oncology. For example, PARP inhibitors are selectively lethal in BRCA1/2-deficient tumors due to the dual impairment of homologous recombination and base excision repair pathways. However, analogous vulnerabilities associated with MLL3/4 loss have not been systematically defined. While some studies have suggested that MLL4 deficiency may confer sensitivity to metabolic perturbations, such as glycolysis inhibition in lung cancer or mitochondrial dysfunction in fibroblasts, these findings are often tissue-specific and lack mechanistic unification. Furthermore, the relationship between epigenetic dysregulation and metabolic reprogramming—a hallmark of cancer—remains incompletely characterized in the context of COMPASS deficiency.

Metabolic pathways, particularly nucleotide biosynthesis, are fundamental to cellular proliferation and survival. Nucleotides serve as the building blocks of DNA and RNA, participate in energy transfer (e.g., ATP, GTP), and function as signaling molecules (e.g., cAMP, cGMP). De novo nucleotide synthesis, which constructs purine and pyrimidine rings from simple precursors like amino acids and one-carbon units, is especially critical in rapidly dividing cells, including cancer cells, which often upregulate this pathway to meet heightened biosynthetic demands. Inhibitors of de novo purine synthesis, such as lometrexol (LTX)—a potent and selective inhibitor of glycinamide ribonucleotide formyltransferase (GART)—have been explored in clinical oncology, though their efficacy has been limited by toxicity and lack of patient stratification biomarkers. The potential link between epigenetic regulators like MLL3/4 and nucleotide metabolism has not been previously established, leaving open the question of whether MLL3/4-deficient cells exhibit unique metabolic dependencies that could be therapeutically targeted.

Compounding this uncertainty is the complexity of COMPASS complex dynamics. MLL3 and MLL4 share core subunits with other COMPASS family members, such as MLL1, MLL2, and SET1A/B, which primarily mediate H3K4 tri-methylation at promoter regions. It is plausible that the loss of MLL3/4 triggers compensatory mechanisms involving other COMPASS methyltransferases, thereby rewiring the epigenetic landscape and altering transcriptional outputs. Such redistribution could inadvertently activate pro-survival or metabolic pathways, creating new dependencies. Additionally, higher-order chromatin architecture—governed by topologically associating domains (TADs) and A/B compartmentalization—may be perturbed by MLL3/4 loss, further influencing gene expression patterns. However, the interplay between epigenetic compensation, chromatin topology, and metabolic reprogramming in MLL3/4-deficient cells has not been investigated.

Thus, there exists a critical need to elucidate the molecular consequences of MLL3/4 loss and to identify actionable vulnerabilities that arise in this context. The present invention addresses this unmet need by demonstrating that MLL3/4-deficient cells, whether derived from embryonic stem cells or human cancers, exhibit an emergent dependency on de novo purine synthesis. This dependency is not merely a passive consequence of increased nucleotide demand but is actively driven by compensatory upregulation of purine metabolism genes via MLL1/COMPASS activity. Furthermore, the invention reveals that this metabolic vulnerability is exploitable using existing purine synthesis inhibitors, which selectively kill MLL3/4-deficient cells while sparing wild-type counterparts. These findings provide a mechanistic foundation for the development of biomarker-driven therapies for cancers and developmental disorders harboring MLL3/4 loss-of-function mutations.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and compositions for the treatment of diseases associated with loss-of-function mutations in the MLL3 (KMT2C) and/or MLL4 (KMT2D) genes by exploiting a synthetic lethal interaction with inhibitors of de novo purine nucleotide synthesis. Specifically, the invention demonstrates that cells deficient in MLL3 and/or MLL4 exhibit an increased flux through the de novo purine synthesis pathway and a corresponding heightened sensitivity to pharmacological inhibition of this pathway. This vulnerability arises not from the loss of the catalytic H3K4 mono-methyltransferase activity of MLL3/4, but rather from their catalytic-independent scaffolding functions within the COMPASS complex, which normally repress the expression of purine metabolism genes. Upon MLL3/4 loss, compensatory activity by the MLL1/COMPASS complex leads to the transcriptional upregulation of key enzymes in the purine synthesis pathway, thereby creating a dependency that can be therapeutically targeted.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for treating a subject having a disease characterized by a loss-of-function mutation in MLL3 and/or MLL4, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of an inhibitor of de novo purine synthesis. The inhibitor may be selected from the group consisting of lometrexol (LTX), methotrexate (MTX), SHIN1, or any other compound that inhibits a key enzyme in the de novo purine synthesis pathway, such as glycinamide ribonucleotide formyltransferase (GART), dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), or serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT1/2). The disease may be a cancer, such as colorectal cancer, breast cancer, bladder cancer, prostate cancer, or non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, or a developmental disorder such as Kabuki syndrome.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for identifying a subject who is likely to respond to treatment with a de novo purine synthesis inhibitor, comprising detecting a loss-of-function mutation in the MLL3 and/or MLL4 gene in a biological sample from the subject. The mutation may be a truncating mutation, frameshift mutation, nonsense mutation, or splice-site mutation that results in the absence or non-functional expression of the MLL3 or MLL4 protein. The detection may be performed by genomic sequencing, RNA sequencing, or immunohistochemical analysis of MLL3/4 protein expression.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising a de novo purine synthesis inhibitor and a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, wherein the composition is formulated for the treatment of MLL3/4-deficient diseases. The composition may further include an agent that rescues the cytotoxic effects of the inhibitor in normal tissues, such as hypoxanthine or inosine, which can bypass the de novo pathway via the purine salvage pathway, thereby improving the therapeutic index.

The invention further encompasses the use of MLL1/COMPASS inhibitors, such as compounds that disrupt the WDR5-MLL1 interaction, in combination with or as an alternative to purine synthesis inhibitors for the treatment of MLL3/4-deficient diseases. This approach targets the upstream compensatory mechanism that drives purine metabolism gene expression, offering a complementary strategy to direct metabolic inhibition.

Additionally, the invention provides diagnostic and prognostic methods based on the expression signature of purine metabolism genes or mitotic cell cycle genes in MLL3/4-deficient cells. For example, the downregulation of PLK1, AURKA, and other mitotic regulators upon purine synthesis inhibition serves as a pharmacodynamic biomarker of target engagement and therapeutic efficacy.

In summary, the invention establishes a novel synthetic lethal relationship between MLL3/4 loss and de novo purine synthesis, providing a mechanistic rationale for the repurposing of existing antimetabolite drugs and the development of new therapeutic strategies for a broad range of cancers and developmental disorders driven by MLL3/4 dysfunction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is based on the discovery that loss of function of the epigenetic regulators MLL3 and MLL4 creates a specific and targetable vulnerability in cellular metabolism, namely an increased dependence on de novo purine nucleotide synthesis. This finding was made through a comprehensive systems-level analysis combining genome-wide CRISPR screening, metabolomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and chromatin profiling in MLL3/4 double-knockout mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), followed by validation in human cancer cell lines and xenograft models. The detailed experimental evidence supporting this discovery is described below.

To model the consequences of MLL3/4 loss, a double-knockout (DKO) mESC line was generated by targeted deletion of exons 8–9 in Kmt2c (MLL3) and exons 16–22 in Kmt2d (MLL4) using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing. Western blot analysis confirmed the complete ablation of MLL3 and MLL4 protein expression, along with the concomitant loss of MLL3/4-specific COMPASS subunits such as NCOA6, UTX, and PTIP, while core COMPASS components like RBBP5 remained intact. Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-Seq) for H3K4me1 and H3K27ac revealed a significant reduction in these enhancer-associated marks at a subset of MLL4-bound enhancers, particularly those associated with genes involved in cytoskeleton and cell junction organization. Despite these epigenetic alterations, the DKO cells maintained normal pluripotency marker expression (e.g., Pou5f1, Nanog) and exhibited comparable growth rates to wild-type (WT) mESCs under standard culture conditions, indicating that MLL3/4 are dispensable for self-renewal but essential for enhancer-mediated gene regulation during differentiation.

To uncover potential cellular vulnerabilities arising from MLL3/4 loss, a genome-wide CRISPR dropout screen was performed using the Brie pooled sgRNA library in both WT and DKO mESCs. Cells were harvested at day 3 post-infection as a baseline and again at days 15 and 21 to assess negative selection. Analysis of sgRNA depletion using the MAGeCK algorithm identified a striking enrichment of genes involved in nucleobase-containing small molecule metabolism among the top negatively selected hits in DKO cells. Specifically, twelve enzymes critical for de novo purine and pyrimidine synthesis—including Ppat, Mthfd1, Yrdc, Strap, Pfas, Umps, and Slc25a19—were found to be essential for the survival of DKO cells but not WT cells. This specificity was confirmed by calculating β scores of gene essentiality using the MLE algorithm, which showed significantly higher essentiality scores for these metabolic genes in DKO cells. Validation experiments using competitive coculture assays with fluorescently labeled WT and DKO cells infected with individual sgRNAs further demonstrated that DKO cells were preferentially depleted upon knockdown of these nucleotide synthesis genes, confirming the screen results.

To understand the metabolic basis of this dependency, steady-state metabolite profiling was performed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). This analysis revealed significant alterations in the abundance of several nucleotide metabolism intermediates in DKO cells, including elevated levels of UMP, CMP, AICAR, inosine, adenosine, and hypoxanthine. To distinguish between changes in metabolite pool size and actual pathway flux, isotopic tracer studies were conducted. Using 15N-labeled glutamine, a key nitrogen donor for de novo purine synthesis, it was shown that DKO cells exhibited significantly increased incorporation of 15N into purine ring intermediates, indicating enhanced flux through the de novo purine synthesis pathway. In contrast, no significant change in pyrimidine synthesis flux was observed. Additionally, 13C-hypoxanthine tracing revealed a modest increase in purine salvage pathway activity in DKO cells, suggesting a compensatory upregulation of both de novo and salvage routes to maintain purine homeostasis.

Based on these findings, it was hypothesized that DKO cells would be hypersensitive to pharmacological inhibition of de novo purine synthesis. Treatment with lometrexol (LTX), a specific inhibitor of GART, resulted in significantly greater growth inhibition and cytotoxicity in DKO cells compared to WT cells. This effect was completely rescued by the addition of hypoxanthine (HPX), which bypasses the LTX block via the purine salvage pathway, confirming that the sensitivity was specifically due to impaired purine synthesis. Similar hypersensitivity was observed with inhibitors of upstream folate-mediated one-carbon metabolism, such as methotrexate (MTX; DHFR inhibitor) and SHIN1 (SHMT1/2 inhibitor), which restrict the availability of one-carbon units required for purine ring formation. These results establish that MLL3/4-deficient cells are selectively vulnerable to disruption of purine nucleotide biosynthesis.

To determine whether this vulnerability stems from the loss of catalytic activity or scaffolding functions of MLL3/4, a catalytically inactive mutant line (ΔSET) was generated, in which the SET domains of both MLL3 and MLL4 were deleted while preserving the rest of the protein scaffold. Western blot analysis confirmed that the ΔSET cells retained expression of NCOA6 and UTX, and exhibited similar reductions in bulk H3K4me1/me2 levels as the DKO cells. However, unlike DKO cells, the ΔSET cells showed no increased sensitivity to LTX, indicating that the synthetic lethal interaction is primarily driven by the loss of catalytic-independent functions of MLL3/4, likely related to their role in assembling repressive enhancer complexes.

To gain mechanistic insights into the transcriptional and proteomic changes underlying this metabolic reprogramming, RNA-Seq and tandem mass tag (TMT) proteomics were performed. RNA-Seq identified approximately 4,500 differentially expressed genes in DKO cells, with downregulated genes enriched for cytoskeletal and cell adhesion functions, consistent with the ChIP-Seq data. Upregulated genes included those involved in stress response, DNA damage repair, and notably, nucleotide metabolism. TMT proteomics quantified over 7,000 proteins and confirmed the upregulation of 59 proteins directly involved in purine nucleotide metabolic processes, including enzymes such as Ak3, Nqo1, and others. Integration of transcriptomic and proteomic data revealed a strong correlation (r = 0.797) between mRNA and protein changes, validating the robustness of the observed metabolic shift.

Further investigation into the regulatory mechanisms driving purine metabolism gene expression implicated compensatory activity by other COMPASS family members. Bioinformatic prediction using tools like BART and LISA identified COMPASS subunits, particularly WDR5, ASH2L, and MLL1, as potential upstream regulators of the upregulated purine metabolism genes. ChIP-Seq for H3K4me3, MLL1, MLL2, Menin, and SET1A in WT and DKO cells revealed a marked increase in MLL1 and Menin occupancy at the transcription start sites (TSS) of upregulated genes in DKO cells, accompanied by elevated H3K4me3 levels. In contrast, MLL2 and SET1A occupancy decreased at downregulated genes. Global chromatin binding analysis confirmed a significant increase in MLL1 chromatin association in DKO cells, with over 80% of the upregulated purine metabolism genes showing enhanced MLL1 binding. Moreover, approximately 83% of the newly gained MLL1 peaks in DKO cells were de novo, suggesting a redistribution of MLL1/COMPLASS activity to compensate for MLL3/4 loss.

Functional validation of this compensatory mechanism was achieved using a selective inhibitor of the WDR5-MLL1 interaction, WDR5-0103. Treatment with this inhibitor selectively reduced the viability of DKO cells but not WT cells, and led to the downregulation of key purine metabolism genes such as Nqo1 and Ak3, confirming that MLL1/COMPASS activity is responsible for the upregulation of these genes and the resulting metabolic dependency.

In addition to transcriptional compensation, the invention also uncovered alterations in higher-order chromatin structure upon MLL3/4 loss. Hi-C analysis revealed an increase in chromatin looping and a shift toward a more open chromatin state in DKO cells, as evidenced by a higher ratio of A (active) to B (inactive) compartmentalization. Specifically, 6.79% of genomic bins shifted from the B to the A compartment, and genes located within these B-to-A transition regions were significantly upregulated in DKO cells. This suggests that MLL3/4 loss induces global chromatin decompaction, which may facilitate the aberrant activation of metabolic genes, including those involved in purine synthesis.

To translate these findings to human disease, the study examined colorectal cancer cell lines, which frequently harbor MLL4 loss-of-function mutations. Analysis of CCLE and TCGA datasets revealed that MLL4-mutant colorectal cancers share similar gene expression signatures with MLL3/4 DKO mESCs, including downregulation of cytoskeletal and matrisome-associated genes. Functionally, MLL4-mutant cell lines (e.g., RKO, HCT116) exhibited increased sensitivity to LTX compared to MLL4 wild-type lines (e.g., Caco2, HT55). Isotopic tracing with 15N-glutamine confirmed elevated de novo purine synthesis flux in MLL4-mutant cells. Knockdown of GART or PAICS (another key purine synthesis enzyme) selectively impaired the growth and colony formation of MLL4-mutant cells. In vivo, LTX treatment significantly inhibited the growth of HCT116 xenograft tumors and extended survival in mouse models, while HT55 (MLL4 WT) xenografts were resistant to LTX, even at lower doses. Mechanistically, LTX treatment in MLL4-mutant cells induced a unique gene expression signature characterized by the downregulation of mitotic cell cycle genes, including PLK1, AURKA, CDCA3, and CDC20, leading to cell cycle arrest and apoptosis.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that MLL3/4 loss creates a synthetic lethal dependency on de novo purine synthesis, mediated by compensatory MLL1/COMPASS activity and chromatin remodeling. This vulnerability is conserved across species and cell types, from mESCs to human cancer cells, and can be effectively targeted with existing purine synthesis inhibitors.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Generation and Characterization of MLL3/4 Double-Knockout mESCs**

Mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) were genetically engineered to generate a double-knockout (DKO) line lacking both MLL3 (Kmt2c) and MLL4 (Kmt2d) using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Guide RNAs targeting exons 8–9 of Kmt2c and exons 16–22 of Kmt2d were cloned into the px459 vector and electroporated into V6.5 mESCs. Following puromycin selection and clonal isolation, knockout efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting using antibodies against N-terminal and mid-protein regions of MLL3, and N- and C-terminal regions of MLL4. Complete loss of MLL3 and MLL4 protein was observed, along with reduced expression of MLL3/4-specific COMPASS subunits NCOA6, UTX, and PTIP, while core subunit RBBP5 remained unchanged. Pluripotency markers Pou5f1 and Nanog were expressed at levels comparable to wild-type (WT) mESCs, and cellular growth rates were unaffected, indicating that MLL3/4 are dispensable for self-renewal under standard culture conditions.

**Example 2: Genome-Wide CRISPR Screen Identifies Nucleotide Synthesis Dependency**

A genome-wide CRISPR dropout screen was performed using the Brie sgRNA library in both WT and MLL3/4 DKO mESCs. Cells were infected at a low MOI (0.5) to ensure single integrations, and harvested at day 3 (baseline) and days 15 and 21 (selection). Genomic DNA was extracted, and sgRNA abundance was quantified by next-generation sequencing. Analysis using the MAGeCK RRA algorithm identified 300 top negatively selected genes in DKO cells, which were enriched for nucleobase-containing small molecule metabolism (P < 10⁻¹⁰). Twelve key enzymes in de novo purine and pyrimidine synthesis pathways—including Ppat, Mthfd1, Yrdc, Strap, Pfas, Zic3, Umps, and Slc25a19—were among the top hits. β scores calculated using the MLE algorithm confirmed that these genes were essential in DKO but not WT cells. Competitive coculture assays with GFP-labeled WT and mCherry-labeled DKO cells infected with individual sgRNAs validated the selective depletion of DKO cells upon knockdown of these metabolic genes.

**Example 3: Metabolic Flux Analysis Reveals Enhanced Purine Synthesis**

Steady-state metabolite profiling by LC-MS/MS showed altered levels of nucleotide intermediates in DKO cells, including increased UMP, CMP, AICAR, inosine, adenosine, and hypoxanthine. To assess pathway flux, cells were incubated with 15N-(amide)-glutamine or 13C-hypoxanthine for 1 hour, followed by metabolite extraction and LC-MS/MS analysis. 15N-glutamine tracing revealed significantly increased incorporation of 15N into purine ring intermediates (e.g., IMP, AMP, GMP) in DKO cells, indicating enhanced de novo purine synthesis flux. In contrast, pyrimidine synthesis flux was unchanged. 13C-hypoxanthine tracing showed a modest increase in purine salvage pathway activity, as evidenced by elevated 13C-labeling in IMP and AMP.

**Example 4: Selective Sensitivity to Purine Synthesis Inhibition**

WT and DKO mESCs were treated with lometrexol (LTX; 0.3 μM) in the presence or absence of hypoxanthine (HPX; 100 μM). Cell viability was assessed by CellTiter-Glo assay over 72 hours. DKO cells exhibited significantly reduced viability upon LTX treatment compared to WT cells (IC50: 0.15 μM vs. 0.8 μM), and this effect was fully rescued by HPX. Similar results were obtained with methotrexate (MTX; 10 nM) and SHIN1 (1 μM), confirming that the vulnerability extends to upstream folate metabolism. Catalytically inactive MLL3/4 ΔSET cells showed no increased sensitivity to LTX, indicating that the synthetic lethality is mediated by catalytic-independent functions.

**Example 5: Compensatory MLL1/COMPASS Activity Drives Purine Metabolism Gene Expression**

RNA-Seq and TMT proteomics identified 59 purine metabolism-related proteins upregulated in DKO cells. ChIP-Seq for MLL1, Menin, and H3K4me3 revealed increased occupancy at the TSS of these genes in DKO cells. Treatment with WDR5-0103 (5 μM), a selective inhibitor of WDR5-MLL1 interaction, reduced viability of DKO cells by 60% compared to 20% in WT cells and downregulated expression of Nqo1 and Ak3, confirming the role of MLL1 compensation.

**Example 6: In Vivo Efficacy of LTX in MLL4-Mutant Xenografts**

HCT116 (MLL4-mutant) and HT55 (MLL4 WT) colorectal cancer cells were implanted subcutaneously into nude mice. When tumors reached 100 mm³, mice were treated with LTX (25 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle daily for 7 days. LTX treatment reduced HCT116 tumor growth by 75% (P < 0.0001) and extended median survival from 28 to 45 days (P < 0.0001), while HT55 tumors were unaffected. Immunohistochemistry showed decreased Ki-67 and increased cleaved caspase-3 in LTX-treated HCT116 tumors, confirming antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects.

These examples collectively demonstrate the synthetic lethal interaction between MLL3/4 loss and de novo purine synthesis inhibition, providing a robust foundation for clinical translation.
