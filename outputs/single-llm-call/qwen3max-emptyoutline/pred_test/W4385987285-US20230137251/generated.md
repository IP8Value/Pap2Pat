# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to the field of antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) and, more specifically, to a rapid, high-throughput phenotypic method for determining the susceptibility of bacterial populations to antimicrobial agents using wirelessly coupled LC (inductor-capacitor) resonant sensors. The invention further pertains to systems and methods for monitoring bacterial growth in real time through the detection of changes in the complex permittivity of a bacterial culture medium, wherein such changes are induced by the presence, proliferation, or inhibition of bacterial cells in response to antimicrobial exposure. The disclosed technology is particularly suited for integration into standard laboratory formats such as 96-well microtiter plates, enabling scalable, cost-effective, and instrumentally simple AST that can be deployed in clinical microbiology laboratories, hospital settings, and point-of-care environments without requiring extensive sample preparation, species-specific reagents, or complex data processing infrastructure. The invention leverages the capacitive properties of bacterial cells and their metabolic byproducts in conjunction with passive, wireless LC sensor elements embedded within culture wells to generate real-time, quantitative readouts of microbial growth dynamics, thereby facilitating the determination of antibiotic resistance or susceptibility within as little as thirty minutes following inoculation.

### 2. Description of the Related Art

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most pressing global public health threats of the twenty-first century. Infections caused by drug-resistant pathogens are associated with significantly higher morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs compared to infections caused by susceptible organisms. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 2.8 million antimicrobial-resistant infections occur annually in the United States alone, and globally, AMR was directly responsible for an estimated 1.27 million deaths in 2019. Projections suggest that, without effective interventions, AMR-related deaths could escalate to 10 million per year by 2050. Critically ill patients, particularly those in intensive care units (ICUs), are especially vulnerable; studies indicate that up to 30% of ICU patients in high-income countries—and even higher proportions in low- and middle-income countries—suffer from healthcare-associated infections, many of which involve resistant organisms. Timely and accurate identification of effective antimicrobial therapy is therefore paramount, especially in cases of sepsis, where delays in appropriate treatment dramatically reduce survival rates. Experimental models have demonstrated that initiating effective antibiotic therapy just three hours later—15 hours versus 12 hours post-inoculation—can decrease 96-hour survival from 80% to only 13.3%, underscoring the life-or-death importance of rapid diagnostic capabilities.

Conventional methods for antimicrobial susceptibility testing rely primarily on phenotypic approaches that assess bacterial growth in the presence of antimicrobial agents. The gold standard remains broth microdilution, agar disk diffusion, or gradient diffusion techniques, all of which require culturing bacteria to detectable densities—typically 10⁷ to 10⁸ colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL)—a process that generally takes 16 to 48 hours. This prolonged turnaround time forces clinicians to initiate empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, which not only risks treatment failure in cases of resistance but also contributes to the further development and spread of AMR through inappropriate antimicrobial use. While molecular genotypic methods such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), whole-genome sequencing (WGS), and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) offer faster results—sometimes within hours—they are fundamentally limited by their dependence on prior knowledge of specific resistance genes or protein markers. Consequently, these approaches cannot detect novel or uncharacterized resistance mechanisms, nor are they well-suited for polymicrobial infections where multiple species with diverse resistance profiles may coexist.

In response to these limitations, numerous innovative phenotypic AST platforms have been developed in recent years. These include optical imaging systems that track single-cell growth in microfluidic chambers, pH-based sensors that monitor acidification from bacterial metabolism, bioluminescence assays measuring adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels as a proxy for viable cell count, magnetic biosensors functionalized with antibodies to capture specific pathogens, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) devices employing peptide- or antibody-coated electrodes. While promising in terms of sensitivity and speed, many of these technologies suffer from significant practical drawbacks: they often require complex surface modifications, extensive sample preprocessing (e.g., lysis, purification, or labeling), sophisticated instrumentation, or advanced computational algorithms for image or signal analysis. Moreover, single-cell resolution methods may struggle with mixed cultures, and impedance-based systems can be highly susceptible to interference from proteins, salts, and other components commonly found in clinical specimens.

Liquid crystal (LC) and resonant LC sensor technologies have been explored in biomedical contexts since the 1960s, primarily for remote environmental monitoring due to their passive, wireless operation and lack of integrated power requirements. More recently, microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) fabrication has enabled miniaturization of LC sensors for integration into medical devices and lab-on-a-chip platforms. One prior application involved an LC resonant sensor for monitoring bacterial growth in large-volume agitated media; however, this system exhibited a detection time of approximately eight hours and did not address long-term biofouling from protein adsorption. Other impedance-based microbial detection systems have achieved high sensitivity (down to 30 CFU) but rely on intricate electrode coatings and are not designed for standardized AST workflows. Crucially, none of the existing approaches combine rapid phenotypic readout (<1 hour), compatibility with standard multiwell plate formats, minimal sample preparation, species-agnostic operation, and robustness in protein-rich environments—capabilities that are essential for clinical translation.

Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for an AST platform that bridges the gap between the comprehensiveness of traditional phenotypic methods and the speed of molecular diagnostics, while maintaining simplicity, scalability, and resilience in real-world clinical sample matrices. The present invention addresses this need through a novel application of wirelessly coupled LC sensors that exploit the intrinsic dielectric properties of bacterial populations to enable rapid, label-free, and high-throughput antimicrobial susceptibility testing without requiring enrichment, species identification, or complex instrumentation.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a rapid, high-throughput phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) system and method based on wirelessly magnetically coupled LC (inductor-capacitor) resonant sensors integrated into standard multiwell plate formats, such as 96-well microtiter plates. The system enables the detection of bacterial growth and antimicrobial susceptibility within as little as thirty minutes after inoculation, significantly accelerating the diagnostic timeline compared to conventional culture-based methods. The core innovation lies in the design and deployment of passive, unpowered LC sensor elements that are embedded within individual culture wells and function by transducing changes in the complex permittivity of the surrounding bacterial culture medium into measurable shifts in resonant frequency. These permittivity changes arise from the accumulation of bacterial cells and their metabolic byproducts at or near the sensor surface, which alter the local dielectric environment in a manner proportional to microbial activity.

The LC sensor comprises an inductive coil and an interdigitated capacitor (IDC) fabricated on a flexible polyimide substrate, coated with a thin, biocompatible polyurethane layer to prevent direct electrical contact with the culture medium while allowing capacitive coupling with the bacterial suspension. The sensor is positioned vertically along the inner wall of a culture well to minimize interference from sedimentation and to maximize exposure to the growing bacterial population. A corresponding detection coil, connected to an impedance analyzer (e.g., an EIS instrument), is placed beneath the multiwell plate and wirelessly couples with the sensor coil via magnetic induction. By sweeping across a range of frequencies and identifying the resonant frequency of the coupled system, the impedance analyzer captures real-time data that is processed to extract the complex permittivity of the culture medium over time.

Crucially, the invention does not require species-specific surface modifications, fluorescent labels, or genomic primers. Instead, it operates on a universal phenotypic principle: susceptible bacteria exposed to an effective antimicrobial agent will exhibit inhibited growth or lysis, resulting in a diminished or altered permittivity trajectory compared to untreated controls or resistant strains. The system is calibrated using baseline measurements from sterile medium or antibiotic-free cultures, and susceptibility is determined by analyzing the slope of the permittivity-time curve during an initial observation window (e.g., 30 minutes). A steeper positive slope indicates active growth and thus resistance, whereas a flatter or negative slope suggests growth inhibition or cell death, indicative of susceptibility.

The invention demonstrates robust performance even in the presence of host proteins, such as those found in serum, with successful operation demonstrated in media supplemented with up to 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS)—a concentration representative of diluted clinical samples. This resilience is attributed to the optimized geometry of the IDC and the protective coating, which together provide sufficient "free space" near the sensor surface to accommodate both protein adsorption and bacterial interaction without signal saturation. Furthermore, the system is compatible with a wide range of bacterial species, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms, and has been validated against multiple classes of antibiotics (e.g., β-lactams, fluoroquinolones, glycopeptides, aminoglycosides), with results correlating strongly with traditional minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determinations.

By leveraging mature printed circuit board (PCB) manufacturing techniques, the LC sensors can be produced at low cost and in high volume, facilitating widespread adoption. The entire workflow—from sample inoculation to result interpretation—requires only standard laboratory equipment (incubator, multiwell plate, impedance analyzer) and minimal computational resources, making it suitable for integration into automated clinical microbiology pipelines. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in rapid AST, offering a practical, scalable, and clinically relevant solution to the urgent challenge of antimicrobial resistance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a novel system and method for performing rapid antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) using wirelessly coupled LC (inductor-capacitor) resonant sensors integrated into standard multiwell culture plates. The following detailed description sets forth the structural components, operational principles, fabrication methods, and experimental validation of the invention, providing a comprehensive framework for its implementation and use.

The foundation of the invention rests on the physical principle that bacterial cells and their metabolic products possess distinct dielectric properties that influence the complex permittivity of their surrounding medium. When bacteria grow in a liquid culture, they increase the local concentration of polarizable entities—including intact cells, cellular debris, ions, and organic molecules—which collectively alter the capacitance of an adjacent sensing element. The LC sensor of the present invention is specifically engineered to transduce these subtle permittivity changes into a quantifiable electrical signal without requiring direct electrical contact, external power, or chemical labeling.

Each LC sensor consists of two primary components: an inductive coil and an interdigitated capacitor (IDC), both patterned onto a flexible polyimide substrate using conventional printed circuit board (PCB) fabrication techniques. The coil is formed by multiple turns of conductive trace (e.g., copper) arranged in a planar spiral configuration, while the IDC comprises a series of parallel, alternating electrode fingers extending from two opposing bus bars. The geometry of the IDC—including the number of digits (N), digit length (l), digit width (W), and gap between digits (D)—is critically optimized to maximize sensitivity to permittivity changes while maintaining manufacturability. In the preferred embodiment, the sensor features a high turn count (e.g., 50 total turns, distributed symmetrically on both sides of the substrate) and a dense IDC array, which together enhance the quality factor (Q-factor) and frequency resolution of the resonant circuit.

To ensure biocompatibility and prevent short-circuiting in conductive culture media, the entire sensor surface is coated with a thin, conformal layer of oil-based polyurethane, applied via spray coating and cured under controlled conditions (e.g., 48 hours in a desiccator). This coating serves as a dielectric barrier that electrically insulates the conductive traces while permitting capacitive coupling with the aqueous environment. Importantly, the coating thickness and material properties are selected to balance protection against fouling with sufficient penetration depth for bacterial interaction—typically on the order of micrometers, which aligns with the Debye screening length in physiological solutions.

The sensor is designed to fit within a standard 96-well microtiter plate. After fabrication and coating, individual sensors are cut from the substrate sheet, rolled into a cylindrical "cup sleeve" configuration, and inserted vertically into the wells so that the IDC lines the inner wall. This vertical orientation mitigates artifacts from bacterial settling or protein precipitation at the well bottom, ensuring that the sensing region remains exposed to the bulk culture throughout the incubation period. The assembled plate is then sterilized, for example, using ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, to maintain aseptic conditions during testing.

Operation of the system relies on inductive coupling between the sensor coil and an external detection coil connected to an impedance analyzer (e.g., Keysight E4990A-20 EIS). The detection coil is positioned beneath the multiwell plate, separated only by the polystyrene well bottom, which simulations (e.g., COMSOL Multiphysics) confirm is magnetically transparent at the operating frequencies (1–12 MHz). When an alternating current is applied to the detection coil, it generates a time-varying magnetic field that induces a voltage in the sensor coil, powering the passive LC circuit. The resonant frequency (f₀) of this circuit is determined by the inductance (L) of the coil and the capacitance (C) of the IDC, according to the relation f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)). As bacteria grow in the well, they alter the effective permittivity (ε) of the medium adjacent to the IDC, thereby changing C and shifting f₀.

The impedance analyzer performs repeated frequency sweeps (e.g., 1600 points over a 1 MHz bandwidth centered on the initial resonance) at regular intervals (e.g., every 5 minutes) to track f₀ over time. From the measured impedance spectrum, two key frequencies are extracted: the resonant frequency (ω_resonant), where the imaginary part of the sensor impedance is zero, and the zero-inductance frequency (ω_zero-inductance), derived from the real part of the impedance. Using these frequencies in conjunction with known circuit parameters (coil inductance L₂, mutual inductance M, and geometric constants), the complex permittivity ε of the culture medium is calculated according to the following equations:

ε = (C₁ / (kε₀)) − ε_sub + (1 / (kR₁ω_zero-inductance ε₀))

where ε₀ is the permittivity of free space, ε_sub is the permittivity of the polyimide substrate, R₁ and C₁ are the resistive and capacitive components of the IDC, and k is the cell constant of the IDC defined by:

k = [l(N − 1)K(√(1 − (D/(D + W))²))] / [2K(D/(D + W))]

Here, K[·] denotes the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, and all other parameters are derived from the physical dimensions of the IDC.

The resulting permittivity values are plotted as a time series, and the initial slope of this curve (e.g., over the first 30 minutes) serves as the primary metric for AST. A steep positive slope indicates rapid bacterial growth and thus resistance to the tested antimicrobial, whereas a shallow or negative slope signifies growth inhibition or cell lysis, indicating susceptibility. Baseline correction is performed by subtracting the permittivity trajectory of a sterile control (for growth detection) or an antibiotic-free culture (for resistance profiling).

The system has been validated using a panel of clinically relevant bacteria, including Escherichia coli MG1655 (Gram-negative), Staphylococcus aureus ALC2085 (Gram-positive), and Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 (Gram-negative), and a range of antibiotics: ampicillin, ofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, vancomycin, and tobramycin. In all cases, the LC sensor distinguished resistant from susceptible strains within 30 minutes, with results consistent with traditional MIC determinations obtained via broth microdilution. For example, ampicillin-susceptible E. coli MG1655 exhibited a flat permittivity slope in the presence of 100 µg/mL ampicillin, while the isogenic resistant strain E. coli MG1655 ASV showed a steep upward slope, mirroring their respective growth behaviors.

Moreover, the invention demonstrates remarkable tolerance to complex biological matrices. When tested in low-salt Luria-Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum (FBS) to mimic clinical samples, the sensor maintained functionality at FBS concentrations up to 2%, with only minor signal attenuation. At 5% FBS, performance degraded due to protein fouling saturating the sensing volume, but this level exceeds typical dilutions used in clinical microbiology (where positive blood cultures are often diluted 1:10 or more before AST). The saturation thickness (d_sat)—the maximum distance from the sensor surface over which permittivity changes are detectable—can be engineered by adjusting the IDC geometry (W and D) according to the empirical relations:

d_sat = −(D / a₁) ln(0.005 / a₂)

a₁ = 114.97(W + D)³ + 28.75(W + D)² − 9.183(W + D) + 1.631

a₂ = 1293.21(W + D)³ + 164.87(W + D)² − 6.521(W + D) + 6.105

This tunability allows future iterations to accommodate even more complex samples if needed.

In summary, the invention provides a rapid, label-free, species-agnostic, and clinically practical AST platform that overcomes the major limitations of existing technologies. By combining the passive, wireless nature of LC sensors with rational electromagnetic design and compatibility with standard laboratory workflows, it enables same-day antimicrobial guidance for critically ill patients, thereby improving outcomes and supporting global antimicrobial stewardship efforts.
