# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of microfabrication of flexible and stretchable electronic sensors, and more specifically to a novel method for producing adhesive-integrated electrical and mechanical sensors through an inverted fabrication process that eliminates the need for sacrificial layers and intermediate transfer printing steps. The invention provides a streamlined, high-yield manufacturing approach for fabricating ultrathin, conformal sensor systems that can be directly transferred from a weakly-adhering donor substrate to a target adhesive surface through a simple mechanical peel-off procedure. This methodology is particularly advantageous for the production of wearable biosensors, strain gauges, and other flexible electronic devices intended for applications in healthcare monitoring, industrial sensing, consumer electronics, and Internet of Things (IoT) ecosystems. The invention encompasses both the fabrication process itself and the resulting sensor architectures, which maintain high fidelity in signal acquisition while offering significant improvements in manufacturing efficiency, scalability, and compatibility with roll-to-roll processing techniques.

## BACKGROUND

The evolution of electronic systems from rigid, bulky configurations to flexible, stretchable, and minimally obtrusive form factors has been driven by increasing demands for intimate integration with complex, dynamic surfaces such as human skin, soft robotics, and curved industrial components. Traditional microfabrication techniques based on complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) processes are inherently incompatible with flexible substrates due to their reliance on high-temperature processing, rigid silicon wafers, and inflexible material systems. Consequently, researchers have developed alternative approaches to enable the production of flexible and stretchable electronics, including screen-printing methods, thin film nanocomposite engineering, and transfer printing techniques utilizing sacrificial layers and intermediate substrates.

Transfer printing has emerged as a prominent strategy for fabricating ultrathin electronic systems, particularly for epidermal electronics and other conformal sensor applications. In conventional transfer printing processes, electronic components are first fabricated on a donor substrate coated with a sacrificial layer such as poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), or poly(sodium 4-styrene sulfonate) (PSSNa). After device fabrication, the sacrificial layer is dissolved using solvents like acetone, releasing the electronic components from the donor substrate. The released components are then transferred to an intermediate stamp, typically made of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), before being finally transferred to the target receiving substrate through controlled pressure application. While this approach has enabled the production of high-performance flexible electronics with excellent conformality to complex surfaces, it suffers from several significant limitations that hinder widespread commercial adoption.

The multi-step nature of transfer printing processes introduces substantial complexity, time consumption, and potential for yield loss. Each transfer step requires precise alignment, controlled release rates, and careful handling to prevent damage to the delicate ultrathin components. The dissolution of sacrificial layers often leads to unpredictable release dynamics, causing deformation of the intended device geometry, particularly for larger or more complex heterogeneous patterns. Additionally, the requirement for solvent immersion can compromise the integrity of certain materials and necessitates additional cleaning and drying steps. The overall process typically requires significant manual dexterity and expertise, making automation challenging and expensive. Even when automated systems have been implemented for transfer printing, they require high-precision multi-point calibration and continuous visual inspection to ensure high yield post-processing.

Alternative approaches such as screen-printing have achieved low-cost and scalable processing of flexible sensors but often lack the capability for fully integrated electronics with ultra-thin profiles necessary for high-fidelity biosignal acquisition. The fabrication of thin film nanocomposites has shown promise in miniaturizing electronic devices, but many of these methods still rely on complex processing sequences that limit their scalability and cost-effectiveness. The growing demand for inconspicuous sensors driven by the proliferation of wearable devices and IoT applications has created an urgent need for manufacturing methods that can produce high-quality flexible electronics at scale while maintaining performance characteristics comparable to traditional rigid systems.

The challenges associated with current flexible electronics manufacturing methods are particularly acute in healthcare applications, where concerns about sterility, contamination, and patient comfort necessitate single-use, disposable sensor systems. The complexity and cost of existing fabrication methods have proven to be significant barriers to widespread adoption of flexible electronics in clinical settings, home healthcare, and industrial monitoring applications. Furthermore, the inability to easily integrate these manufacturing processes with established industrial techniques such as roll-to-roll processing has limited the potential for mass production and cost reduction.

Previous attempts to address these challenges have focused primarily on optimizing individual steps within existing transfer printing workflows or developing alternative material systems that might improve adhesion characteristics. However, these incremental improvements have failed to address the fundamental complexity inherent in multi-step transfer processes. The need remains for a fundamentally different approach to flexible electronics fabrication that can eliminate entire processing steps while maintaining or improving device performance and reliability.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel microfabrication method for producing adhesive-integrated flexible and stretchable electrical and mechanical sensors that substantially reduces the number of processing steps required compared to conventional transfer printing approaches. This innovative methodology, referred to herein as the adhesive-peel (AP) process, eliminates the need for sacrificial layers, solvent immersion, intermediate transfer stamps, and multiple transfer steps by leveraging a weakly-adhering interface between the sensor components and a donor substrate, combined with an inverted fabrication sequence.

In the AP process, sensors are fabricated directly on a weakly-adhering donor substrate, which may comprise a standard silicon wafer or glass substrate coated with a thin silicone layer, or alternatively a flexible substrate such as Kapton film. The fabrication sequence is inverted relative to conventional approaches, with the conductive metal layers deposited first in contact with the weakly-adhering donor surface, followed by the polymer backing layer. Specifically, a bilayer metal structure comprising gold (Au) and chromium (Cr) is deposited onto the donor substrate, with the Au layer interfacing with the weakly-adhering surface and the Cr layer exposed at the top surface. A photodefinable polyimide (PPI) is then applied and simultaneously patterned to form the polymer backing of the sensor. Subsequent etching removes the exposed Cr-Au regions, leaving behind the desired sensor pattern on the donor substrate.

The key innovation of the AP process lies in its ability to enable direct transfer of the completed sensor from the donor substrate to a target adhesive surface through a simple mechanical peel-off step. This is accomplished by exploiting the controlled weak adhesion between the Au layer and the donor substrate, which is sufficiently strong to maintain sensor integrity during fabrication but weak enough to allow clean separation when contacted with an adhesive material having appropriate adhesion strength. The peel-off force required for successful transfer has been quantified at approximately 0.22 ± 0.03 N, enabling the use of common adhesive materials such as Scotch tape or medical-grade adhesives like Tegaderm.

The AP process offers several significant advantages over conventional transfer printing methods. First, it reduces the total number of processing steps by approximately fifty percent, leading to higher manufacturing yields and reduced processing time. Second, it eliminates the need for solvent immersion and sacrificial layer dissolution, thereby avoiding the deformation and damage that can occur during these processes. Third, it enables dense packing of sensors on the donor substrate since submersion in solvent and wafer dicing are not required, improving material utilization efficiency. Fourth, the process is highly compatible with automation and roll-to-roll manufacturing techniques, as demonstrated by successful peel-off using consumer-grade lint rollers and flexible donor substrates.

The invention encompasses both electrical sensors for biosignal acquisition and mechanical strain sensors, demonstrating the versatility of the AP process across different sensor modalities. Electrical AP sensors have been shown to maintain high fidelity in electrophysiological signal acquisition, with correlation coefficients exceeding 95% when compared to conventional transfer printing sensors in electroencephalogram (EEG) measurements. Mechanical AP strain sensors exhibit reliable performance with gauge factors suitable for various monitoring applications. The process is robust to different metallization techniques, including both electron beam evaporation and sputter coating, with appropriate optimization of the donor substrate properties to accommodate the specific deposition method.

The AP process is particularly well-suited for applications requiring single-use, disposable sensor systems, such as clinical patient monitoring, where concerns about sterility and contamination make reusability undesirable. The direct integration with adhesive materials eliminates the need for additional attachment mechanisms, simplifying device application and improving user experience. Furthermore, the compatibility with flexible donor substrates opens possibilities for integration with existing tape manufacturing infrastructure, enabling cost-effective mass production of flexible sensor systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Exemplary Embodiments

The present invention provides a comprehensive microfabrication methodology for producing adhesive-integrated flexible and stretchable sensors through an inverted fabrication process that leverages controlled weak adhesion between sensor components and a donor substrate. The exemplary embodiments described herein demonstrate the practical implementation of this methodology across various sensor types, substrate configurations, and manufacturing scenarios, establishing the versatility and robustness of the adhesive-peel (AP) process.

In the primary exemplary embodiment, the AP process begins with the preparation of a weakly-adhering donor substrate. A standard four-inch silicon wafer is coated with a thin silicone layer, specifically polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), to create the weakly-adhering interface essential for the subsequent peel-off process. The PDMS layer is prepared by mixing the pre-polymer and crosslinker components in a ratio optimized for the specific metallization technique to be employed. For electron beam evaporation processes, a stiffer PDMS formulation with a 3:1 pre-polymer to crosslinker ratio is utilized to minimize thermal expansion mismatch and prevent buckling or cracking of the deposited metal films during processing. For sputter coating processes, standard PDMS formulations may be employed, as the plasma exposure during sputtering creates a silica-like surface layer that enhances adhesion during fabrication while maintaining the weak interfacial properties necessary for peel-off.

Following donor substrate preparation, the inverted fabrication sequence commences with the deposition of conductive metal layers. A bilayer structure comprising gold (Au) and chromium (Cr) is deposited onto the PDMS-coated donor substrate using either electron beam evaporation or sputter coating techniques. The deposition sequence is intentionally inverted relative to conventional approaches, with the Au layer deposited first in direct contact with the PDMS surface, followed by the Cr layer on top. This inversion is critical to the success of the AP process, as the Au-PDMS interface provides the controlled weak adhesion necessary for subsequent peel-off, while the Cr layer serves as an etch mask and adhesion promoter for the polymer backing layer.

The thickness of the metal layers is carefully controlled to balance electrical conductivity, mechanical flexibility, and adhesion characteristics. Typically, the Au layer is deposited to a thickness of 50-100 nanometers, providing sufficient electrical conductivity while maintaining the weak interfacial adhesion with PDMS. The Cr layer is deposited to a thickness of 5-10 nanometers, serving primarily as an adhesion layer for the subsequent polymer deposition while also functioning as an etch mask during pattern definition.

After metal deposition, a photodefinable polyimide (PPI) is applied to the metal-coated donor substrate using spin coating or other suitable deposition techniques. The PPI serves dual purposes as both the polymer backing material and the photoresist for pattern definition, enabling simultaneous formation and patterning of the sensor's polymer structure in a single processing step. This integration of polymer formation and patterning significantly streamlines the fabrication process compared to conventional approaches that require separate deposition and lithography steps.

The PPI is then exposed to ultraviolet light through a photomask defining the desired sensor pattern, followed by development to remove the unexposed regions. The remaining PPI forms the polymer backing of the sensor, with the underlying Cr layer protecting the corresponding Au regions from subsequent etching. An etch-back process is then performed to remove the exposed Cr-Au regions, leaving behind the complete sensor structure comprising the PPI polymer backing with embedded metal interconnects and sensing elements.

The completed sensors remain on the donor substrate with their Au surfaces in contact with the PDMS layer, held in place by the controlled weak adhesion between these materials. The peel-off force required for separation has been experimentally determined to be approximately 0.22 ± 0.03 N, which is sufficiently low to enable easy transfer using common adhesive materials while being high enough to maintain sensor integrity during fabrication and handling.

For electrical sensor applications, the AP process produces ultrathin, flexible electrodes suitable for electrophysiological signal acquisition. These sensors have been successfully demonstrated in electroencephalogram (EEG) measurements, where they exhibited correlation coefficients exceeding 95% when compared to conventional transfer printing sensors. The sensors are fabricated with appropriate geometries for specific biosignal applications, such as circular or rectangular electrode pads with connecting traces, and can be arranged in various configurations including the standard three-lead mastoid configuration for EEG measurements.

The electrical sensors are particularly well-suited for low-amplitude, high-frequency signal acquisition due to gold's favorable impedance characteristics in these frequency bands. The ultrathin profile and flexible nature of the sensors enable intimate contact with contoured surfaces such as human skin, reducing motion artifacts and improving signal quality compared to conventional rigid electrodes. The direct integration with adhesive materials eliminates the need for additional attachment mechanisms, simplifying device application and improving user comfort.

For mechanical sensing applications, the AP process enables the fabrication of strain gauges with tailored gauge factors and geometries. The strain sensors are produced using the same fundamental process as the electrical sensors, with the photomask pattern modified to create the desired strain-sensitive geometry. The resulting sensors exhibit reliable performance under axial loading, maintaining their electrical connections and sensing properties throughout multiple strain cycles. While the gauge factor of gold-based strain sensors (approximately 0.79) may be lower than some specialized strain gauge materials, the primary advantage lies in the manufacturing simplicity and integration capabilities of the AP process.

The exemplary embodiment demonstrates the successful fabrication of both electrical and mechanical sensors on rigid donor substrates (silicon and glass wafers) as well as flexible donor substrates (Kapton film). The use of flexible donor substrates enables compatibility with roll-to-roll manufacturing processes, as demonstrated by successful peel-off operations using consumer-grade lint rollers and flexible tape substrates. This compatibility with established industrial manufacturing techniques represents a significant advantage for large-scale production and cost reduction.

The AP process exhibits remarkable robustness to different metallization techniques, with both electron beam evaporation and sputter coating producing functional sensors with appropriate optimization of the donor substrate properties. Electron beam evaporated sensors typically exhibit smoother surface topography with minimal wrinkling, while sputtered sensors may show some surface waviness due to the plasma interaction during deposition. However, both types maintain their electrical interconnects and sensing properties, demonstrating the versatility of the AP process across different fabrication environments.

The exemplary embodiment further demonstrates the dense packing capability of the AP process, with multiple sensors fabricated on the same working area of the donor substrate without requiring individual dicing or handling. This efficient use of substrate area contributes to improved manufacturing economics and reduced material waste compared to conventional approaches that require individual sensor handling after release from the donor substrate.

### Additional Exemplary Embodiments

The present invention encompasses numerous additional exemplary embodiments that extend the basic AP process to various applications, materials, and manufacturing scenarios, demonstrating the broad applicability and adaptability of the disclosed methodology. These additional embodiments address specific requirements for different sensor types, application domains, and manufacturing constraints, while maintaining the core principles of inverted fabrication and controlled weak adhesion.

In one additional exemplary embodiment, the AP process is adapted for the fabrication of silver (Ag)-based electrical sensors, which offer distinct advantages for certain biosignal acquisition applications. Silver and silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) electrodes are particularly well-suited for DC-coupled signal acquisition due to their stable electrochemical properties and low noise characteristics. The fabrication process follows the same inverted sequence as the gold-based sensors, with the Au layer replaced by Ag and appropriate adjustments made to the deposition parameters and etch chemistry. The weak adhesion between Ag and PDMS is exploited in the same manner as the Au-PDMS system, enabling direct peel-off transfer to adhesive substrates. This embodiment demonstrates the material versatility of the AP process, showing that it can be readily adapted to different noble metals with similar interfacial adhesion characteristics.

Another additional exemplary embodiment focuses on platinum (Pt)-based sensors, which are valued for their long-term stability and biocompatibility in vivo applications, particularly in neural stimulation and recording. The AP process is modified to accommodate Pt's different deposition characteristics and adhesion properties, while maintaining the fundamental inverted fabrication sequence. The resulting Pt-based sensors exhibit the same direct transfer capability to adhesive substrates, enabling the production of implantable or chronic monitoring devices with simplified manufacturing processes. This embodiment highlights the potential of the AP process for advanced medical applications requiring high biocompatibility and long-term stability.

In a further additional exemplary embodiment, the AP process is extended to incorporate active electronic components alongside passive sensor elements. This hybrid approach enables the fabrication of integrated sensor systems that combine sensing elements with signal conditioning, amplification, or wireless transmission capabilities. The process involves selective fabrication of active components on rigid islands within the flexible substrate, followed by the AP process for the passive interconnects and sensor elements. The weak adhesion interface is maintained only in the regions containing the passive elements, while the active components remain firmly attached to the donor substrate. This selective adhesion approach enables the production of sophisticated flexible electronic systems with both active and passive components, addressing the growing demand for integrated wearable electronics.

Another additional exemplary embodiment addresses the specific requirements of neonatal and pediatric applications, where gentle adhesion to sensitive skin is paramount. In this embodiment, the AP process is modified to produce sensors with reduced peel-off forces, enabling transfer to weaker adhesive materials that are less likely to cause skin irritation or damage. This is achieved by tailoring the PDMS formulation and surface treatment to further reduce the interfacial adhesion between the sensor and donor substrate. The resulting sensors can be transferred to medical-grade adhesives specifically designed for sensitive skin applications, expanding the utility of the AP process to vulnerable patient populations.

In yet another additional exemplary embodiment, the AP process is adapted for environmental and industrial sensing applications, where different material requirements and operating conditions must be considered. For example, sensors designed for harsh chemical environments may utilize alternative polymer backings with enhanced chemical resistance, while maintaining the same inverted fabrication sequence. The process can be modified to incorporate specialized sensing materials such as conductive polymers, carbon nanotubes, or graphene-based materials, depending on the specific sensing modality required. This embodiment demonstrates the flexibility of the AP process to accommodate diverse material systems while maintaining the core manufacturing advantages.

A further additional exemplary embodiment focuses on the integration of the AP process with existing industrial manufacturing infrastructure, particularly roll-to-roll processing systems. In this embodiment, flexible donor substrates such as PET films or metal foils are used instead of rigid wafers, enabling continuous processing through standard web-handling equipment. The AP process is modified to accommodate the continuous nature of roll-to-roll processing, with synchronized deposition, patterning, and peel-off operations. This embodiment demonstrates the potential for high-volume, low-cost production of flexible sensors using established industrial manufacturing techniques, significantly reducing the barrier to widespread adoption.

Another additional exemplary embodiment addresses the fabrication of multi-layer sensor systems, where multiple functional layers are stacked to create more complex sensing capabilities. The AP process is extended to enable sequential fabrication of multiple sensor layers, with each layer transferred to the previous layer through controlled peel-off operations. This approach enables the creation of three-dimensional sensor architectures with integrated functionality, such as combined electrical and mechanical sensing, or multi-modal biosignal acquisition. The controlled weak adhesion principle is maintained throughout the multi-layer fabrication process, ensuring reliable transfer between layers while maintaining device integrity.

In a further additional exemplary embodiment, the AP process is adapted for the fabrication of stretchable electronics through the incorporation of serpentine or mesh-like conductor geometries. The inverted fabrication sequence is modified to create these specialized geometries during the metal deposition and patterning steps, enabling the production of sensors that can accommodate large strains without compromising electrical connectivity. The weak adhesion interface is maintained throughout the stretchable regions, allowing for direct transfer to stretchable adhesive substrates. This embodiment addresses the growing demand for truly stretchable electronics that can conform to highly dynamic surfaces such as moving joints or expanding organs.

Another additional exemplary embodiment focuses on the fabrication of disposable diagnostic sensors for point-of-care testing applications. In this embodiment, the AP process is optimized for single-use applications, with emphasis on cost reduction and simplified manufacturing. The sensors are designed to integrate with simple readout systems, such as smartphone-based detection or basic electronic interfaces, enabling widespread deployment in resource-limited settings. The direct transfer capability to adhesive substrates simplifies the final assembly process, reducing the need for complex packaging and integration steps.

A further additional exemplary embodiment addresses the fabrication of large-area sensor arrays for applications such as tactile sensing or distributed environmental monitoring. The AP process is scaled to accommodate larger donor substrates and more complex patterning requirements, while maintaining the fundamental advantages of reduced processing steps and direct transfer capability. The dense packing capability of the AP process is particularly advantageous for large-area arrays, enabling efficient use of substrate area and reduced manufacturing costs. This embodiment demonstrates the scalability of the AP process to meet the requirements of emerging applications requiring extensive sensor coverage.

In yet another additional exemplary embodiment, the AP process is modified to incorporate energy harvesting or storage components alongside the sensing elements. This integrated approach enables the fabrication of self-powered sensor systems that can operate independently of external power sources. The process involves the selective fabrication of energy harvesting components such as piezoelectric or thermoelectric generators, or energy storage components such as thin-film batteries or supercapacitors, using the same inverted fabrication sequence. The weak adhesion interface is maintained only in the regions containing the sensor elements, while the energy components remain firmly attached to enable reliable operation. This embodiment addresses the growing demand for autonomous wearable electronics that can operate continuously without requiring frequent battery replacement or recharging.

These additional exemplary embodiments collectively demonstrate the versatility, adaptability, and broad applicability of the AP process across diverse application domains, material systems, and manufacturing requirements. The fundamental principles of inverted fabrication and controlled weak adhesion provide a robust foundation that can be readily modified and extended to meet specific application needs while maintaining the core manufacturing advantages of reduced processing steps, direct transfer capability, and compatibility with established industrial processes.
