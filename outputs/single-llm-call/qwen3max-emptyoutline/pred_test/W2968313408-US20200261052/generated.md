# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD) is a well-established, noninvasive diagnostic modality that enables the measurement of cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV) through the major intracranial arteries. Since its introduction, TCD has become a critical tool in the evaluation of cerebrovascular health, particularly in acute neurological settings such as stroke. The technique relies on the Doppler effect to detect the velocity of red blood cells moving through cerebral arteries, typically accessed via acoustic windows in the temporal bone. The resulting CBFV signal manifests as a pulsatile waveform whose morphology is tightly coupled to the dynamics of the cardiac cycle and the integrity of the cerebral vascular tree. Each waveform cycle generally comprises a rapid systolic upstroke followed by a series of characteristic peaks—commonly denoted as P1, P2, and P3—before returning to a diastolic minimum. These morphological features reflect the interplay between forward cardiac ejection, vascular compliance, and downstream resistance, and their alteration can serve as an early indicator of pathological conditions affecting cerebral perfusion.

Among the most clinically significant applications of TCD is the detection of large vessel occlusion (LVO), a condition involving partial or complete blockage of major cerebral arteries such as the middle cerebral artery (MCA) or internal carotid artery (ICA). LVO is a leading cause of severe ischemic stroke, which in turn represents a primary source of long-term disability and mortality in developed nations. Timely identification of LVO is crucial, as it dictates eligibility for advanced interventions such as endovascular thrombectomy, which must be performed within a narrow therapeutic window to preserve viable brain tissue. Traditional TCD assessment for LVO has relied heavily on qualitative interpretation of waveform morphology, most notably through the Thrombolysis in Brain Ischemia (TIBI) grading system. This five-point scale (grades 0–5) classifies waveforms based on features such as the presence of identifiable systolic peaks, the timing of peak velocity, and the overall contour of the waveform. Grade 5 denotes normal flow, while grades 0 and 1 indicate absent or minimal flow, respectively. Intermediate grades describe blunted (grade 2), dampened (grade 3), or stenotic (grade 4) flow patterns, each with distinct morphological signatures.

Despite its clinical utility, the TIBI system suffers from significant limitations that hinder its broader adoption, particularly in prehospital or resource-limited settings. Chief among these is its reliance on subjective visual assessment by trained specialists, which introduces variability in interpretation and limits reproducibility. Moreover, the categorical nature of TIBI may not fully capture the continuous spectrum of morphological variation present in real-world TCD data. While objective metrics such as the pulsatility index have been proposed to quantify waveform characteristics, they often lack the granularity needed to differentiate between subtle but clinically relevant morphological subtypes. Consequently, there remains a pressing need for an automated, objective, and data-driven framework capable of categorizing TCD waveforms in a manner that aligns with—and potentially extends beyond—established clinical paradigms like TIBI.

Recent advances in machine learning offer promising avenues for addressing this gap. Supervised learning approaches have demonstrated success in classifying TCD signals for specific pathologies, including stenosis and vasospasm. However, such methods require large, labeled datasets, which are difficult to obtain due to the scarcity of expert annotations and the heterogeneity of clinical presentations. Unsupervised learning, by contrast, does not rely on pre-existing labels and instead seeks to uncover inherent structure within the data. Spectral clustering, in particular, has emerged as a powerful technique for identifying complex, non-convex groupings in high-dimensional spaces, making it well-suited for analyzing the nuanced morphological variations observed in TCD waveforms. When combined with robust feature extraction and principled criteria for determining the optimal number of clusters—such as the gap statistic—unsupervised methods can reveal natural partitions in the data that may correspond to clinically meaningful categories.

Prior attempts to automate TCD interpretation have often focused on global waveform descriptors or frequency-domain features, which may overlook critical time-domain morphological details. Additionally, many studies have restricted their analysis to binary classification tasks (e.g., LVO vs. non-LVO), thereby neglecting the rich typology of flow abnormalities that exist along the continuum of cerebrovascular disease. The present invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel methodology for the unsupervised categorization of TCD waveforms based on a minimal set of morphologically informative features derived directly from the time-domain signal. These features—onset, canopy, and peaks—are specifically designed to encode aspects of waveform morphology that are central to TIBI grading while remaining computable even in the presence of severe pathology where standard peak structures may be absent or distorted. By applying spectral clustering to this feature space, the invention identifies distinct waveform archetypes that not only recapitulate known TIBI categories but also reveal previously underappreciated morphological subtypes with strong associations to LVO status.

This approach represents a paradigm shift from rule-based, expert-dependent classification to a data-driven discovery framework that is both objective and scalable. It enables the generation of standardized morphological templates—referred to herein as cluster archetypes—that can serve as reference standards for automated waveform comparison. Furthermore, because the clustering is performed without prior knowledge of clinical labels, the resulting categories are free from the biases inherent in existing diagnostic systems and may uncover novel phenotypes that could inform future refinements to stroke classification. The invention thus provides a foundational tool for the development of next-generation TCD interpretation systems capable of supporting rapid, accurate, and accessible stroke triage in diverse clinical environments.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for the automated, objective categorization of transcranial Doppler (TCD) cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV) waveforms using unsupervised machine learning. The invention is predicated on the recognition that the morphological features of TCD waveforms contain clinically significant information regarding the presence and nature of large vessel occlusion (LVO) and other cerebrovascular pathologies, but that current methods for interpreting these features are subjective, inconsistent, and limited in scope. To overcome these limitations, the invention introduces a novel computational framework that transforms raw TCD waveforms into a low-dimensional feature space capturing key aspects of waveform morphology, applies spectral clustering to identify natural groupings within this space, and generates representative waveform archetypes for each cluster that can be used for automated classification and clinical interpretation.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a method for processing TCD waveform data comprising the steps of: (a) acquiring a plurality of CBFV waveforms from a subject population including individuals with confirmed LVO and control subjects; (b) preprocessing each waveform to ensure temporal and amplitude normalization, thereby removing confounding effects of heart rate and velocity scale; (c) extracting three morphological features from each normalized waveform—onset, canopy, and peaks—wherein the onset feature quantifies the normalized time at which maximum velocity occurs, the canopy feature measures the duration of the systolic complex as a proportion of the cardiac cycle, and the peaks feature computes a weighted count of true and pseudo-peaks/troughs within the systolic complex; (d) standardizing the extracted features across all waveforms using z-score normalization to produce a uniform feature space; (e) applying spectral clustering to partition the feature space into a predetermined number of clusters, wherein the optimal number of clusters is determined using the gap statistic criterion; and (f) generating a cluster archetype for each identified cluster by averaging the waveforms that are most representative of the cluster’s morphological characteristics.

In another aspect, the invention provides a system for implementing the above method, comprising a signal acquisition module configured to receive TCD waveform data, a preprocessing module for normalizing the waveforms in time and amplitude, a feature extraction module programmed to compute the onset, canopy, and peaks features, a clustering module that executes spectral clustering with gap statistic-based cluster number selection, and an archetype generation module that produces representative waveform templates for each cluster. The system may be implemented in software running on a general-purpose computer or embedded within a dedicated TCD diagnostic device.

The invention further encompasses a database of cluster archetypes derived from the application of the disclosed method to a diverse cohort of subjects, including those with acute LVO and healthy controls. These archetypes serve as reference standards for the automated classification of new TCD waveforms, enabling rapid determination of whether a given waveform aligns with a morphology strongly associated with LVO or with normal or benign variants. In particular, the invention identifies four distinct waveform types: Type I, characterized by early peak velocity, wide systolic complex, and strong peaks, predominantly observed in control subjects and corresponding to normal (TIBI grade 5) flow; Type II, featuring late peak velocity but otherwise normal morphology, also primarily found in controls and possibly reflecting variations in peripheral resistance; Type III, exhibiting early peak velocity but narrow systolic complex and weak peaks, almost exclusively associated with LVO and potentially representing a stenotic flow pattern; and Type IV, marked by late peak velocity, wide systolic complex, and weak peaks, strongly linked to LVO and analogous to blunted (TIBI grade 2) flow.

By providing an objective, reproducible, and data-driven means of categorizing TCD waveforms, the invention facilitates more accurate and timely diagnosis of LVO, supports improved triage decisions in stroke care, and lays the groundwork for the integration of TCD into automated clinical decision support systems. Moreover, the unsupervised nature of the approach ensures that the discovered categories are not constrained by preconceived diagnostic labels, thereby enabling the identification of novel morphological phenotypes that may refine our understanding of cerebrovascular pathophysiology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a comprehensive methodology for the unsupervised categorization of transcranial Doppler (TCD) cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV) waveforms, with specific application to the detection and characterization of large vessel occlusion (LVO) in acute ischemic stroke. The invention overcomes the limitations of existing subjective and semi-quantitative approaches by leveraging machine learning techniques to objectively identify morphologically distinct waveform types directly from empirical data. The following detailed description outlines the components, processes, and technical innovations that constitute the invention, presented in a manner sufficient to enable any person skilled in the art to practice the claimed subject matter.

The invention begins with the acquisition of TCD waveform data from a heterogeneous subject population. In a preferred embodiment, this population includes three groups: (1) patients with computed tomography angiography (CTA)-confirmed LVO involving the proximal extracranial or terminal intracranial segments of the internal carotid artery (ICA) or the M1/M2 branches of the middle cerebral artery (MCA); (2) in-hospital control (IHC) subjects presenting with stroke-like symptoms but without evidence of LVO on CTA; and (3) out-of-hospital control (OHC) volunteers without acute neurological symptoms or known cerebrovascular disease. TCD examinations are performed using standard 2 MHz handheld probes and commercially available Doppler systems, with recordings obtained transtemporally from both left and right MCA at depths between 45–60 mm to ensure consistent vessel sampling. Each recording session captures at least 30 seconds of continuous waveform data, yielding a minimum of 15 individual cardiac cycles per depth for subsequent analysis.

Following acquisition, the raw waveform data undergoes a rigorous preprocessing pipeline designed to enhance signal quality and ensure comparability across subjects. First, individual cardiac cycles (beats) are extracted from each 30-second recording using an automated beat identification algorithm that detects the onset of systolic upstroke based on derivative-based criteria. Beats exhibiting excessive artifact or poor signal-to-noise ratio are automatically rejected using iterated interquartile range (IQR) exclusion, with cross-correlation and beat duration serving as primary quality metrics. Only recordings containing at least one bilateral pair of acceptable MCA scans (i.e., both left and right hemispheres) are retained for analysis. Next, the accepted beats from each depth-specific recording are aligned at the systolic onset and padded to a common length using endpoint replication, after which they are averaged to produce a single representative average beat waveform per recording. This averaging process reduces noise while preserving the underlying morphological structure of the waveform.

To eliminate confounding effects of inter-subject variability in heart rate and absolute flow velocity, each average beat waveform is subjected to dual normalization. Temporal normalization is achieved by resampling the waveform to a fixed number of time points—preferably 100 samples—using cubic spline interpolation, effectively standardizing the cardiac cycle duration across all waveforms. Amplitude normalization is performed by subtracting the diastolic minimum from each sample and then dividing by the resultant systolic-diastolic range, thereby scaling all waveforms to the unit interval [0, 1] along the velocity axis. Additionally, to mitigate high-frequency noise unrelated to hemodynamic phenomena, all waveforms are smoothed via convolution with a 90-millisecond Hanning window. For consistency across datasets acquired at different sampling rates, OHC waveforms originally sampled at 400 Hz are resampled to 125 Hz to match the native resolution of hospital-acquired data.

With the waveforms preprocessed and normalized, the invention proceeds to extract a minimal set of morphological features that capture the essential aspects of TCD waveform shape relevant to LVO detection. These features—termed onset, canopy, and peaks—are specifically engineered to be robust to pathological distortions and computable even when standard peak structures (P1, P2, P3) are absent. The onset feature, defined as the normalized time at which the maximum velocity occurs within the cardiac cycle, encodes information about the timing of peak flow acceleration, which is known to be delayed in blunted flow patterns. Mathematically, for a normalized waveform x(t) defined over t ∈ [0, 1], the onset is given by argmaxₜ x(t). The canopy feature quantifies the duration of the systolic complex, defined as the proportion of the cardiac cycle during which the velocity exceeds 25% of the systolic-diastolic range. This threshold was selected empirically to encompass the primary systolic upstroke and associated peaks while excluding diastolic tail artifacts. Formally, canopy = |{t : x(t) ≥ 0.25}|, where |·| denotes cardinality. The peaks feature provides a nuanced measure of waveform complexity by counting both true peaks/troughs (where the first derivative changes sign) and pseudo-peaks/troughs (where the derivative magnitude falls below a critical threshold of 0.01). True peaks are assigned a weight of 1.0, while pseudo-peaks are weighted linearly based on how close their derivative magnitude is to zero, with weights ranging from 0 to 1. The total peaks value is the sum of all weights, yielding a continuous measure of morphological richness that avoids the brittleness of discrete peak counting in noisy or pathological signals.

These three features collectively form a three-dimensional feature vector for each waveform, which is then standardized across the entire dataset using z-score normalization. That is, for each feature dimension, the mean and standard deviation are computed across all waveforms, and each individual value is transformed by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation. This step ensures that no single feature dominates the clustering process due to differences in scale or variance.

The standardized feature vectors are then subjected to spectral clustering, a graph-based unsupervised learning algorithm that excels at identifying non-convex and irregularly shaped clusters. In the preferred implementation, the scikit-learn SpectralClustering module is employed with default parameters, including a radial basis function (RBF) kernel for affinity computation. A critical aspect of the invention is the use of the gap statistic to determine the optimal number of clusters objectively. The gap statistic compares the observed within-cluster dispersion (log Wₖ) to its expectation under a null reference distribution generated by uniform sampling in the principal component space of the data. Specifically, for candidate cluster numbers k ranging from 2 to 7, the gap statistic Gₖ is computed as E[log Wₖ] − log Wₖ, where E[·] denotes the expectation estimated from 1000 bootstrap samples. The optimal k is selected as the smallest integer satisfying Gₖ ≥ Gₖ₊₁ − sₖ₊₁, where sₖ is the standard deviation of log Wₖ adjusted for simulation error. This criterion effectively identifies the point at which additional clusters yield diminishing returns in terms of reducing within-cluster dispersion.

Application of this methodology to a dataset comprising 996 waveforms from 106 subjects (33 LVO, 33 IHC, 40 OHC) yields four distinct clusters, designated Type I through Type IV. For each cluster, a representative archetype is generated to visualize the characteristic morphology. This is accomplished by computing the pairwise squared Euclidean distances between all member waveforms, ranking each waveform by its average distance to others in the cluster, and selecting the five closest waveforms for averaging. The resulting archetypes provide canonical templates that encapsulate the essential morphological traits of each cluster.

Type I, the largest cluster (n=400), is characterized by early onset (peak velocity occurring in the first third of the cardiac cycle), wide canopy (systolic complex spanning approximately 60% of the cycle), and strong peaks (high peaks feature value). This morphology closely resembles normal TCD waveforms and corresponds to TIBI grade 5. Notably, 82% of Type I waveforms originate from control subjects (52% IHC, 30% OHC), with only 18% from LVO patients. Type II (n=257) shares the wide canopy and strong peaks of Type I but exhibits late onset (peak velocity in the second half of the cycle). This pattern may reflect altered wave reflection dynamics due to changes in peripheral resistance and is also predominantly found in controls (80%), suggesting it represents a benign variant rather than pathology. Type III (n=83) is the most distinctive and clinically significant: it features early onset like Type I but has a narrow canopy (systolic complex occupying less than 40% of the cycle) and very weak peaks, indicating a near-complete loss of secondary morphological structure. Strikingly, 95% of Type III waveforms come from LVO subjects, strongly implicating this morphology in occlusive disease. Preliminary evidence suggests Type III may correspond to TIBI grade 4 (stenotic flow), though further validation is required. Type IV (n=256) displays late onset, wide canopy, and weak peaks—a combination that matches the classic description of blunted flow (TIBI grade 2). Consistent with this, 60% of Type IV waveforms are from LVO patients, though the substantial control representation (40%) indicates this pattern may also occur in non-occlusive conditions.

The invention thus provides a robust, automated framework for TCD waveform categorization that transcends the limitations of subjective grading systems. By grounding classification in data-driven discovery rather than predefined rules, the method reveals both expected and novel morphological phenotypes with clear clinical correlations. The resulting cluster archetypes serve as objective reference standards that can be integrated into diagnostic algorithms, enabling real-time waveform classification during TCD exams. Furthermore, the modular design of the invention—comprising independent preprocessing, feature extraction, clustering, and archetype generation stages—allows for straightforward adaptation to new datasets, incorporation of additional features, or replacement of clustering algorithms as the field evolves. In summary, the invention represents a significant advance in the objective interpretation of TCD signals, with direct implications for improving stroke diagnosis, triage, and outcomes.
