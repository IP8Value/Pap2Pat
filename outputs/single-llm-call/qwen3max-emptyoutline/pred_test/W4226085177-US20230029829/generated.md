# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of information retrieval has undergone a profound transformation in recent decades, evolving from static, document-centric systems to dynamic, interactive paradigms that seek to understand and respond to the nuanced information needs of users. Traditional ad-hoc retrieval systems, which operate on a one-shot query-response model, are increasingly inadequate in the face of complex, ambiguous, or underspecified user intents. In such scenarios, a single query often fails to capture the full scope of the user’s underlying information need, leading to suboptimal retrieval results and user frustration. This limitation has spurred the development of conversational search systems—intelligent interfaces that engage users in multi-turn dialogues to iteratively refine understanding and deliver more relevant information.

Conversational search represents a synthesis of several research domains, including exploratory search, interactive information retrieval, and natural language generation. A key innovation within this paradigm is the concept of mixed-initiative interaction, wherein the system not only responds to user queries but also proactively seeks clarification by posing questions of its own. These clarification questions serve to disambiguate user intent, narrow broad topics, or resolve ambiguities that cannot be resolved through passive analysis of the initial query alone. For instance, a user query such as “all men are created equal” may refer to historical, philosophical, legal, or literary contexts; without further context, a retrieval system risks returning irrelevant or overly general results. By asking, “Would you like to learn more about the Declaration of Independence?” or “Are you interested in Thomas Jefferson’s role in drafting this phrase?”, the system can guide the user toward a more precise information goal.

Two primary approaches have emerged for generating or selecting such clarification questions: generation and selection. In the generation approach, systems employ rule-based templates or neural generative models—such as sequence-to-sequence architectures or large language models—to produce novel clarification questions tailored to the current conversation context. While powerful and flexible, generative methods often suffer from issues of fluency, relevance, and factual grounding, particularly when operating without access to a knowledge base or document corpus. Moreover, generated questions may introduce noise, redundancy, or even hallucinated content that misleads the user.

In contrast, the selection approach operates within a constrained space of pre-existing clarification questions, typically drawn from historical query logs, dialogue corpora, or curated question banks. The system’s task is not to invent new questions but to select the most appropriate one from a candidate pool based on the current conversation context. This approach offers several advantages: it ensures linguistic quality and factual plausibility (since questions are derived from real interactions), reduces computational complexity, and facilitates integration with existing retrieval infrastructures. The selection paradigm has been notably advanced through initiatives such as the Clarifying Questions for Open-Domain Dialogue Systems (ClariQ) challenge, which established standardized benchmarks and datasets for evaluating clarification question selection in open-domain conversational search.

Despite these advances, existing selection methods exhibit significant limitations. Many prior systems rely solely on the surface-level similarity between the conversation context and candidate clarification questions, often using embedding-based models like BERT to compute relevance scores. While effective to a degree, such approaches neglect the rich semantic content embedded in the documents that ultimately ground the conversation. In content-grounded conversational search—where dialogues culminate in the retrieval of one or more relevant documents—the passages within those documents contain critical signals about potential user intents and the types of clarifications that would be most informative. For example, if a retrieved passage discusses both the historical origins of a phrase and its modern legal interpretations, the system should be able to generate or select clarification questions that reflect these distinct dimensions. However, conventional selection methods that ignore passage content fail to leverage this contextual richness, resulting in less targeted and less effective clarification strategies.

Furthermore, existing systems often treat the conversation context as a flat sequence of tokens, without adequately accounting for the discourse structure of multi-turn dialogues. Utterances in a conversation carry varying degrees of importance: later turns typically reflect refined or corrected user intents, while earlier turns may contain initial, possibly inaccurate, formulations. Effective clarification question selection must therefore weigh utterances differentially, prioritizing recent and contextually salient contributions. Additionally, the integration of passage-level information with conversational context remains underexplored. While some systems use retrieved documents to inform final answer selection, few exploit them during the clarification phase to anticipate which questions will best resolve ambiguity in light of the available evidence.

These gaps highlight the need for a more sophisticated, content-aware architecture for clarification question selection—one that jointly models the conversation history, the semantic content of retrieved passages, and the relationships between them. Such a system would not only improve the relevance and utility of clarification questions but also enhance the overall efficiency and user satisfaction of conversational search experiences. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel dual-model re-ranking framework that leverages deep learning to fuse conversational and passage-based signals, thereby enabling more accurate and contextually grounded selection of clarification questions in both open-domain and task-oriented settings.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for selecting clarification questions in a conversational search environment, where interactions between a user and an automated agent are grounded in a corpus of documents and culminate in the retrieval of one or more relevant documents. The core innovation lies in a dual-model re-ranking architecture that integrates both conversational context and passage content to identify the most appropriate clarification question from a pre-defined pool of candidates. This approach significantly improves the precision and relevance of selected clarification questions compared to existing methods that rely solely on conversational similarity or document-agnostic retrieval.

The method begins with the receipt of a conversation context, defined as the sequence of utterances exchanged between the user and the agent up to, but not including, the point at which a clarification question is to be posed. Using this context, the system first retrieves a set of top-k relevant passages from a document index. This retrieval process employs a two-stage mechanism: initial document-level retrieval using an enhanced word-weighting scheme that accounts for the segmented nature of dialogue utterances, followed by passage-level extraction via a sliding window over the retrieved documents. Each passage is scored based on a combination of term coverage relative to the conversation context and the relevance score of its parent document, with utterance-specific weighting applied to emphasize more recent and contextually significant contributions.

Subsequently, the system uses these retrieved passages to fetch candidate clarification questions from a dedicated clarification-questions index. For each passage, a query is formed by concatenating the passage content with the full conversation context, and this composite query is used to retrieve a list of candidate clarification questions. This step ensures that the candidate pool is not only relevant to the conversation but also aligned with the semantic content of the supporting documents.

The heart of the invention resides in the re-ranking phase, which employs two fine-tuned BERT-based models to evaluate and rank the candidate clarification questions. The first model, referred to as BERT-C-cq, learns a direct association between the conversation context and candidate clarification questions. It is trained using a triplet loss framework, where positive examples consist of actual clarification questions observed in training conversations, and negative examples are randomly sampled from the clarification pool. The second model, BERT-C-P-cq, extends this by incorporating passage content into the representation: it processes a combined input of the conversation context, a separator token, and a retrieved passage, thereby learning a joint embedding that captures the interplay between dialogue, document evidence, and clarification intent. Both models are fine-tuned on domain-specific data and respect the token length constraints of the BERT architecture by truncating inputs to the most recent utterances that fit within the 512-character limit.

Final ranking of candidate clarification questions is achieved through a simple yet effective score fusion technique, specifically Comb-SUM, which linearly combines the relevance scores output by the two BERT models. This fusion leverages the complementary strengths of each model: BERT-C-cq excels in scenarios where the conversation context is sufficiently informative on its own, while BERT-C-P-cq provides critical disambiguation power when passage content reveals latent aspects of user intent not evident from the dialogue alone. The result is a robust, context-aware selection mechanism that consistently outperforms baseline retrieval and single-model approaches.

The system is designed to operate in diverse environments, including open-domain web search and specialized task-oriented domains such as customer support. In the latter, additional preprocessing rules are applied to automatically identify and filter clarification questions from noisy human-to-human conversation logs, ensuring that only content-relevant, non-chit-chat questions are considered. Experimental evaluation on both the ClariQ benchmark and an internal customer support dataset demonstrates substantial improvements in Recall@30—ranging from 12% to 87% over base retrieval—validating the efficacy of the proposed architecture. The invention thus provides a scalable, high-performance solution for enhancing conversational search systems through intelligent, content-grounded clarification question selection.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a computer-implemented method and system for selecting clarification questions in a conversational search setting, where the ultimate goal of the interaction is the retrieval of one or more relevant documents from a background corpus. The system operates by analyzing the conversation history between a user and an automated agent, retrieving supporting passages from a document collection, and using a dual deep learning model architecture to re-rank candidate clarification questions based on their relevance to both the conversation and the retrieved content. The following detailed description outlines the components, processes, and operational logic of the system.

The system architecture comprises two primary indices: a Documents index and a Clarification-questions index. The Documents index contains a corpus of documents, each of which may be associated with one or more historical conversations that led to its retrieval. For efficient indexing and retrieval, each document is represented using three fields: a “text” field containing the raw document content, an “anchor” field containing concatenated text from all dialogue utterances that historically linked to the document, and a combined “anchor and text” field that merges the two. The Clarification-questions index, in contrast, stores a pool of candidate clarification questions, typically derived from historical query logs, dialogue transcripts, or manually curated sources. These questions are assumed to be high-quality, grammatically correct, and semantically meaningful, thereby avoiding the pitfalls of generative approaches.

At runtime, the system receives a conversation context C_j, defined as the sequence of utterances {c_0, c_1, ..., c_{j-1}} exchanged up to the point where a clarification question is to be generated. The first step in the process is passage retrieval. This is performed in two stages. In the first stage, the system constructs a verbose query from the entire conversation context and submits it to the Documents index. To account for the segmented nature of dialogue, the system employs an utterance-biased extension of the Fixed-Point (FP) word weighting method. Specifically, initial FP weights are computed for each term in the conversation, and these weights are aggregated at the utterance level to produce an utterance score. This utterance score is then propagated back to the constituent terms, effectively boosting the weight of words appearing in more relevant or recent utterances. The resulting weighted query is used to retrieve the top-k documents from the index, with the choice of field (“text” vs. “anchor and text”) depending on the dataset characteristics—for instance, using the “text” field in open-domain settings where anchor data is sparse, and the combined field in task-oriented domains with rich dialogue-document linkages.

In the second stage, candidate passages are extracted from the top-k retrieved documents using a sliding window of fixed size (e.g., 512 characters) with overlap to ensure continuity. Each passage p is assigned an initial score based on its term coverage of the conversation context. This coverage is computed as a weighted sum over utterances, where the weight for each utterance is discounted to give greater importance to later turns. Within each utterance, term coverage is evaluated using a combination of global inverse document frequency (idf) and scaled term frequency (tf), with multiple tf scaling schemes applied—including BM25-based scoring and minimum tf between the utterance and passage. The final passage score is a linear combination (e.g., with λ = 0.5) of this initial coverage score and the relevance score of the parent document, both normalized to ensure comparability.

Once a set of relevant passages has been retrieved, the system proceeds to fetch candidate clarification questions. For each passage P, a composite query is formed by concatenating the passage content with the full conversation context C_j. This query is then submitted to the Clarification-questions index to retrieve a list of candidate clarification questions associated with that passage. This step ensures that the candidate pool is not only responsive to the user’s stated needs but also informed by the evidentiary content of the supporting documents.

The core innovation of the system lies in the re-ranking of these candidate clarification questions using two fine-tuned BERT models. The first model, BERT-C-cq, is trained to assess the relevance of a clarification question cq to a conversation context C_j in isolation. It is fine-tuned using a triplet loss objective, where each training instance consists of a triple (C_j, cq^+, cq^-). Here, cq^+ is the ground-truth clarification question observed in a training conversation at position j, and cq^- is a randomly sampled negative example from the clarification pool. During inference, the model computes a relevance score for each candidate cq_i by processing the pair (C_j, cq_i) as input.

The second model, BERT-C-P-cq, extends this by incorporating passage content. It is trained on triples of the form (C_j [SEP] P, cq^+, cq^-), where P is a passage retrieved for conversation C_j, and [SEP] is BERT’s standard separator token. This model learns to associate clarification questions with the joint context of conversation and passage, capturing nuances that arise only when document evidence is considered. Due to BERT’s token limit (512 tokens), both the conversation context and passage are truncated to fit within the model’s capacity. Specifically, the conversation is represented by the most recent m utterances whose total character length does not exceed 512, and the passage is limited to a 512-character window. This ensures that the most salient and recent information is preserved while adhering to architectural constraints.

During inference, each candidate clarification question cq_i is evaluated by both models. For BERT-C-cq, the input is (C_j, cq_i); for BERT-C-P-cq, the input is (C_j [SEP] P, cq_i), where P is the passage that yielded cq_i during the retrieval phase. The final relevance score for cq_i is computed as the sum of the scores from both models—a technique known as Comb-SUM fusion. This simple additive fusion has been shown to be highly effective in combining complementary ranking signals, and it avoids the need for complex learned fusion mechanisms that may overfit to specific datasets.

The system is designed to be adaptable to different domains. In open-domain settings such as the ClariQ benchmark, the method operates on clean, crowd-sourced dialogues with explicit clarification turns. In contrast, in task-oriented domains like customer support, the input data may consist of noisy human-to-human logs containing chit-chat, off-topic remarks, and non-question utterances. To handle such noise, the system applies a series of filtering rules to automatically identify valid clarification questions. First, only sentences containing a question mark are considered. Second, the presence of interrogative words (e.g., “what,” “how,” “where”) is checked, and only the span from the first such word to the question mark is retained. If no interrogative words are found, a syntactic parser (e.g., AllenNLP’s constituency parser) is used to identify sentences with question-like clause types (e.g., SQ or SBARQ in the Penn Treebank tagset). Finally, to ensure content relevance, each candidate question is paired with the user’s subsequent response and used as a query to retrieve passages from the Documents index; only those questions that return a passage from the conversation’s final document in the top-3 results are retained. This four-step filtering process effectively isolates genuine, document-grounded clarification questions from the noise of real-world logs.

The system is implemented using standard information retrieval and deep learning frameworks. Document indexing is performed using Apache Lucene with an English language analyzer and BM25 similarity. BERT models are implemented using the Hugging Face Transformers library, with bert-base-uncased as the base architecture. Fine-tuning is conducted with a learning rate of 2e-5 over three epochs, and inference is accelerated using GPU hardware (e.g., 32GB V100), enabling re-ranking of up to 1000 candidates per conversation in 1–2 seconds.

### Experimental Results

The efficacy of the proposed method was rigorously evaluated on two distinct datasets representing different conversational search scenarios: the ClariQ benchmark for open-domain information seeking and an internal customer support dataset for task-oriented technical assistance. Both datasets were processed according to the domain-specific adaptations described above, and performance was measured using Recall@30 (R@30)—the proportion of test instances where the ground-truth clarification question appears in the top 30 ranked candidates—as the primary metric, consistent with the ClariQ challenge guidelines.

On the ClariQ dev set, the base information retrieval system (IR-Base), which retrieves clarification questions using a simple BM25 query over the conversation context, achieved an R@30 of 0.692. The BERT-C-cq model, which re-ranks candidates based solely on conversation context, improved this to 0.770—a relative gain of 11.3%. The BERT-C-P-cq model, which incorporates passage content, achieved a comparable R@30 of 0.768, demonstrating that passage-informed ranking is nearly as effective as context-only ranking in this relatively clean dataset. However, when the scores from both models were fused using Comb-SUM (BERT-fusion), performance increased to 0.791, representing a statistically significant improvement over either model alone. This gain underscores the complementary nature of the two signals: while the conversation context captures user intent directly, the passage content provides external validation and disambiguation, especially in cases where the initial query is vague or polysemous.

A qualitative analysis of individual queries further illustrates this complementarity. For the query “all men are created equal,” BERT-C-P-cq successfully identified nine correct clarification questions in the top 30 (including “Would you like to learn more about the Declaration of Independence?” and “Would you like to learn more about Thomas Jefferson?”), whereas BERT-C-cq identified only three. This is because the retrieved passages explicitly mentioned both the Declaration and Jefferson, enabling the passage-aware model to make more informed selections. In contrast, for queries with clear and unambiguous context, BERT-C-cq sometimes outperformed BERT-C-P-cq, indicating that passage noise can occasionally degrade performance when document retrieval is imperfect.

On the Support dataset, which features noisy, real-world customer support logs, the improvements were even more pronounced. IR-Base achieved only R@30 = 0.294, reflecting the difficulty of matching clarification questions in a domain with high lexical and structural variability. BERT-C-cq raised this to 0.538 (an 82.9% relative improvement), demonstrating the power of deep contextual embeddings in handling informal language. BERT-C-P-cq achieved 0.541, and the fused model reached 0.555—again showing that combining both signals yields the best results. The larger gains in this domain highlight the importance of robust preprocessing (e.g., the four-step filtering rules) and the value of passage grounding in disambiguating technical queries that may have multiple valid interpretations.

When evaluated on the official ClariQ test set, the BERT-fusion model achieved fourth place overall and second among independent teams, trailing only a system that exploited dataset-specific artifacts (e.g., domain separation between train and test topics). Crucially, the present invention treats all clarification questions equally, making no assumptions about topic distribution, and thus represents a more generalizable and deployable solution.

In summary, the experimental results confirm that the dual-model, passage-aware re-ranking architecture significantly enhances clarification question selection across diverse domains. By fusing conversational and document-based signals, the system achieves state-of-the-art performance without relying on dataset-specific heuristics, thereby offering a robust foundation for real-world conversational search applications.
