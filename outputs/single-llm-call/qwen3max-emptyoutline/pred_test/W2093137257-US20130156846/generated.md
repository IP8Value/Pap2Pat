# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

Protein biosynthesis, or translation, is a fundamental biological process by which the genetic information encoded in messenger RNA (mRNA) is converted into functional polypeptide chains composed of amino acid residues. This process is orchestrated with remarkable fidelity through the coordinated action of ribosomes, transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and a family of enzymes known as aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (aaRSs). Each aaRS specifically recognizes one of the twenty canonical proteinogenic amino acids and catalyzes its attachment to the corresponding cognate tRNA, thereby ensuring that the correct amino acid is incorporated at each codon position during elongation of the nascent polypeptide chain. Despite this high degree of specificity, translational errors do occur, albeit at low frequency—typically estimated between 1 in 10³ to 1 in 10⁴ amino acid incorporations. These rare misincorporation events can result from structural similarities among certain amino acids that allow non-cognate substrates to be mistakenly recognized and activated by an aaRS, leading to the formation of mischarged tRNAs. In some cases, proofreading mechanisms intrinsic to certain aaRSs serve to hydrolyze incorrectly formed aminoacyl-AMP intermediates or mischarged tRNAs before they are delivered to the ribosome, thereby enhancing overall translational accuracy.

However, the cellular machinery responsible for protein synthesis is not inherently restricted to the canonical set of twenty amino acids. Hundreds of non-proteinogenic amino acids exist in nature, many of which are structurally analogous to one or more of the standard amino acids. Such analogues may originate from natural biosynthetic pathways in plants and microorganisms, arise through oxidative damage to endogenous amino acids, or be introduced exogenously via environmental exposure or dietary intake. When present in sufficient intracellular concentrations, these non-canonical amino acids can compete with their canonical counterparts for recognition by aaRSs, potentially resulting in their erroneous incorporation into nascent polypeptides. This phenomenon, referred to herein as “misincorporation,” has been documented for several non-proteinogenic amino acids, including canavanine and 3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine (L-DOPA), both of which have been shown to substitute for arginine and tyrosine, respectively, in newly synthesized proteins. The consequences of such misincorporation can be profound, ranging from subtle alterations in protein conformation and function to catastrophic failures in protein folding, aggregation, and ultimately, cellular toxicity.

One particularly concerning example of a non-proteinogenic amino acid implicated in neurodegenerative pathology is β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA). BMAA is a neurotoxic compound produced by various species of cyanobacteria and has been detected in diverse ecological niches, including aquatic environments, terrestrial flora such as cycads, and organisms occupying higher trophic levels that consume BMAA-containing biomass. Epidemiological studies have linked chronic human exposure to BMAA with an increased incidence of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis/Parkinsonism dementia complex (ALS/PDC), especially among indigenous populations in Guam who historically consumed flour derived from cycad seeds. Subsequent analyses revealed that BMAA was predominantly found in a protein-bound form within these food sources, suggesting that it had been incorporated into polypeptide chains rather than merely adsorbed or sequestered passively. Further support for this hypothesis came from autoradiographic studies in mice following systemic administration of tritiated BMAA ([³H]-BMAA), which demonstrated tissue distribution patterns consistent with active protein synthesis and retention of radioactivity after rigorous acid extraction procedures designed to remove non-covalently associated compounds.

Despite mounting circumstantial evidence, direct biochemical confirmation of BMAA’s capacity to undergo ribosomal incorporation into human proteins remained elusive until recently. Moreover, the molecular mechanism underlying such misincorporation—including identification of the specific canonical amino acid displaced by BMAA—had not been definitively established. Understanding this mechanism is critical not only for elucidating the pathophysiological basis of BMAA-induced neurotoxicity but also for developing targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at preventing or mitigating its deleterious effects. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by providing conclusive experimental evidence that BMAA is misincorporated into human proteins in a protein synthesis-dependent manner, specifically substituting for L-serine during translation. Furthermore, the invention discloses that co-administration of L-serine effectively inhibits BMAA misincorporation, thereby preventing downstream pathological consequences such as protein misfolding, aggregation, and apoptosis. These findings establish a novel paradigm for understanding how environmental toxins can subvert the fidelity of the translational apparatus and offer a rational strategy for pharmacological intervention in diseases associated with aberrant protein homeostasis.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and compositions for inhibiting the misincorporation of β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA) into human proteins by administering L-serine. It has been discovered that BMAA, a non-proteinogenic amino acid produced by cyanobacteria and implicated in neurodegenerative disorders such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and Parkinsonism dementia complex (PDC), is erroneously incorporated into nascent polypeptide chains during protein synthesis in human cells. This misincorporation occurs through a ribosome-mediated, protein synthesis-dependent mechanism wherein BMAA substitutes for the canonical amino acid L-serine due to structural and physicochemical similarities that enable recognition by seryl-tRNA synthetase (SerRS). The incorporation of BMAA into proteins leads to widespread protein misfolding, aggregation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and ultimately, apoptotic cell death—pathological hallmarks commonly observed in neurodegenerative proteinopathies.

Crucially, the invention demonstrates that the misincorporation of BMAA can be competitively inhibited by increasing the extracellular and intracellular concentrations of L-serine. Experimental data show that co-incubation of human fibroblasts, neuroblastoma cells, and primary endothelial cells with equimolar or excess L-serine significantly reduces the amount of BMAA incorporated into cellular proteins in a dose-dependent manner. In contrast, D-serine—an enantiomer that cannot be utilized in mammalian protein synthesis—exerts no protective effect, confirming that the inhibition is mediated through competition at the level of aminoacylation by SerRS. Moreover, when all twenty canonical amino acids are present in culture medium, BMAA incorporation is markedly suppressed; however, omission of L-serine alone from this mixture restores high levels of BMAA incorporation, further underscoring the specificity of serine as the displaced residue.

The invention further establishes that BMAA, once incorporated into proteins, becomes covalently linked via peptide bonds and cannot be removed by denaturing agents such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) or reducing agents like dithiothreitol (DTT). Complete release of BMAA requires either acid hydrolysis or proteolytic digestion with pronase, confirming its integration into the primary amino acid sequence. Using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), the presence of BMAA in hydrolyzed cellular proteins was unequivocally verified through matching retention times, characteristic product ion transitions (e.g., m/z 459 → 171, 289, 119), and ion ratio consistency with authenticated synthetic BMAA standards. A linear correlation between administered BMAA concentration and recovered BMAA from hydrolyzed proteins (R² = 0.99) further validates the quantitative reliability of this incorporation process.

Additionally, the invention reveals that BMAA-induced protein misfolding manifests as intracellular autofluorescence—a well-established indicator of protein aggregation—and triggers caspase-independent apoptosis in neuronal and non-neuronal human cell lines. Critically, both autofluorescence and apoptosis are abrogated when cells are co-treated with L-serine or when protein synthesis is pharmacologically inhibited by cycloheximide, directly linking cytotoxicity to the act of BMAA incorporation rather than to off-target signaling or membrane receptor activation. These findings collectively demonstrate that BMAA functions as a molecular mimic of L-serine and that its neurotoxic effects stem from translational infidelity.

Accordingly, the invention encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising L-serine or pharmaceutically acceptable salts thereof, formulated for systemic or localized administration to subjects at risk of or suffering from conditions associated with BMAA exposure. Such conditions include, but are not limited to, ALS, Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, and other tauopathies or synucleinopathies where environmental BMAA exposure is suspected. The invention also includes diagnostic methods for detecting BMAA incorporation into proteins using LC-MS/MS or radiolabeling techniques, as well as kits for assessing susceptibility to BMAA toxicity based on cellular serine availability or SerRS expression levels. By targeting the root cause of BMAA-induced proteotoxicity—namely, its misincorporation in place of serine—the present invention offers a mechanistically grounded, safe, and readily implementable therapeutic approach with significant potential to modify disease progression in environmentally triggered neurodegenerative disorders.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is predicated on the unexpected discovery that β-N-methylamino-L-alanine (BMAA), a non-proteinogenic amino acid of environmental origin, is misincorporated into human proteins during active translation by substituting for the canonical amino acid L-serine. This misincorporation is not a passive adsorption or post-translational modification but a ribosome-dependent process that requires ongoing protein synthesis and results in the covalent integration of BMAA into the polypeptide backbone. The structural resemblance between BMAA and L-serine—both featuring a small, polar side chain with a terminal hydroxyl or methylamino group—enables BMAA to be recognized, albeit erroneously, by seryl-tRNA synthetase (SerRS), the enzyme responsible for charging tRNA^Ser with its cognate amino acid. Once attached to tRNA^Ser, BMAA is delivered to the ribosomal A-site and incorporated into growing polypeptide chains in response to serine codons (UCU, UCC, UCA, UCG, AGU, AGC), thereby introducing a non-native residue at positions normally occupied by serine.

This translational error has profound consequences for protein structure and function. Serine residues are frequently involved in critical biological processes, including phosphorylation-dependent signaling cascades, hydrogen bonding networks that stabilize secondary structures, and catalytic sites of enzymes. Substitution with BMAA—which lacks a true hydroxyl group and instead bears a methylamino moiety—disrupts these interactions, leading to misfolding, loss of solubility, and aggregation of affected proteins. Over time, the accumulation of such aberrant proteins overwhelms cellular quality control mechanisms, including the ubiquitin-proteasome system and autophagy, resulting in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and activation of apoptotic pathways. These pathological features mirror those observed in numerous neurodegenerative diseases, suggesting that BMAA misincorporation may represent a previously underappreciated environmental trigger for proteinopathies.

The invention provides robust experimental validation of this mechanism through multiple orthogonal approaches. First, time-course studies in human MRC-5 lung fibroblasts exposed to [³H]-BMAA demonstrated a progressive increase in radiolabel associated with the protein fraction over 16 hours, indicating active uptake and retention. This incorporation was abolished by co-treatment with cycloheximide (CHX), a potent inhibitor of eukaryotic translation elongation, confirming dependence on de novo protein synthesis. Identical results were obtained in SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells and primary human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), demonstrating that the phenomenon is not cell-type restricted but rather a general property of human cells capable of protein synthesis.

Second, competitive inhibition assays revealed that among all twenty canonical L-amino acids, only L-serine significantly reduced BMAA incorporation into proteins in a concentration-dependent manner. At 250 µM, L-serine decreased [³H]-BMAA incorporation by over 70%, whereas D-serine—structurally identical but stereochemically incompatible with mammalian translation—had no effect. Furthermore, when cells were cultured in medium containing all twenty amino acids at physiological concentrations (400 µM each), BMAA incorporation was minimal; however, selective omission of L-serine from this mixture restored high-level incorporation, proving that serine is the specific competitor whose absence permits BMAA entry into the translational machinery.

Third, biochemical characterization confirmed that BMAA is covalently bound within proteins. Radiolabeled proteins isolated from [³H]-BMAA-treated SH-SY5Y cells resisted extraction by SDS and DTT, agents that disrupt non-covalent interactions and disulfide bonds, respectively. Only harsh treatments that cleave peptide bonds—such as 6 M hydrochloric acid at 110°C for 16 hours or prolonged incubation with the broad-spectrum protease pronase—released detectable radioactivity, establishing that BMAA is integrated into the primary sequence via amide linkages.

Fourth, liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) provided definitive chemical identification of BMAA in hydrolyzed cellular proteins. Using a validated method involving derivatization with 6-aminoquinolyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate (AQC) and monitoring of six specific SRM transitions (e.g., m/z 459 → 171, CE 38 eV), the analyte from cell lysates exhibited identical retention time (≈5.0 min), fragmentation pattern, and ion ratios as an authentic BMAA standard. A linear calibration curve (R² = 0.99) across a range of BMAA doses confirmed quantitative recovery, ruling out artifactual detection.

Fifth, functional consequences of misincorporation were assessed. Chronic exposure to BMAA (300 µM for 96 hours) induced intense perinuclear and cytosolic autofluorescence in MRC-5 cells—a hallmark of protein aggregation previously associated with misfolded polypeptides containing non-canonical residues. This phenotype was completely prevented by co-administration of equimolar L-serine. Concurrently, BMAA triggered apoptosis, as evidenced by increased Annexin V binding to externalized phosphatidylserine, without inducing necrosis (no LDH release or propidium iodide uptake). Both apoptosis and autofluorescence were suppressed by CHX, confirming their dependence on new protein synthesis and thus on BMAA incorporation itself.

These findings collectively establish a causal chain: BMAA mimics L-serine → is mischarged onto tRNA^Ser by SerRS → is incorporated into proteins during translation → causes misfolding and aggregation → induces ER stress and apoptosis → contributes to neurodegeneration. The invention leverages this mechanistic insight to propose L-serine supplementation as a therapeutic strategy. By saturating SerRS with its natural substrate, L-serine outcompetes BMAA for tRNA charging, thereby preserving translational fidelity and preventing proteotoxicity. Given that L-serine is a naturally occurring, nutritionally available amino acid with a well-established safety profile in humans—even at high doses used in clinical trials for other neurological conditions—the invention represents a low-risk, high-impact intervention for populations exposed to BMAA through contaminated water, seafood, or dietary supplements.

Moreover, the invention extends to diagnostic applications. The LC-MS/MS method described herein enables sensitive (LOD = 48 fmol) and specific detection of protein-bound BMAA in biological samples, facilitating biomonitoring in at-risk individuals. Similarly, radiolabeling protocols using [³H]-BMAA can be adapted for high-throughput screening of compounds that modulate BMAA incorporation, aiding drug discovery efforts. The invention also contemplates genetic or epigenetic factors that may influence susceptibility—for instance, polymorphisms in SerRS genes or variations in serine biosynthesis pathways (e.g., PHGDH, PSAT1)—which could inform personalized prevention strategies.

In summary, the detailed description herein provides a comprehensive framework for understanding, detecting, and counteracting BMAA-induced translational infidelity. By identifying L-serine as the key competitive inhibitor and demonstrating its efficacy in blocking downstream pathology, the invention opens new avenues for the prevention and treatment of environmentally linked neurodegenerative diseases.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Demonstration of Protein Synthesis-Dependent Incorporation of BMAA into Human Cells**

Human MRC-5 lung fibroblasts (passage 14–19) were seeded at 3×10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 4 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were then washed and incubated in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) containing 10% FBS and 31.25 nM [³H]-BMAA (80 Ci/mmol). At 2, 4, and 16 hours, cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 10% FBS, lysed by three freeze-thaw cycles in 0.1% Triton X-100, and total protein quantified via BCA assay. Radiolabel in lysates was measured by liquid scintillation counting (LSC), revealing a time-dependent increase in [³H]-BMAA association with cellular material. To isolate protein-bound radioactivity, lysates were subjected to trichloroacetic acid (TCA) precipitation (10% final concentration), washed thrice with cold TCA, and dissolved in neat formic acid. LSC of TCA-precipitable fractions confirmed progressive incorporation of [³H]-BMAA into proteins over 16 hours.

To test dependence on active translation, parallel cultures of MRC-5, SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma (passage 30–32), and primary HUVECs were treated with [³H]-BMAA (31.25 nM) ± 2 µg/mL cycloheximide (CHX) for 16 hours. CHX reduced [³H]-BMAA incorporation into proteins by 82 ± 5% in MRC-5, 79 ± 6% in SH-SY5Y, and 85 ± 4% in HUVECs (all p < 0.001 vs. control). As a positive control, [³H]-leucine (41 nM) incorporation was similarly inhibited by CHX (84 ± 3% reduction), confirming effective blockade of protein synthesis. These results demonstrate that BMAA incorporation is not due to non-specific binding but requires ongoing ribosomal activity.

**Example 2: Identification of L-Serine as the Specific Competitor Inhibiting BMAA Incorporation**

MRC-5 cells were incubated with [³H]-BMAA (31.25 nM) in HBSS ± individual L-amino acids (250 µM each) for 16 hours. Among all twenty canonical amino acids tested, only L-serine significantly reduced protein-associated radioactivity (73 ± 4% inhibition, p < 0.001). Dose-response analysis showed that L-serine inhibited incorporation in a concentration-dependent manner (IC₅₀ ≈ 80 µM), with near-complete blockade at 250 µM. In contrast, D-serine (250 µM) had no effect (3 ± 2% inhibition, p = NS), confirming stereospecificity.

In a complementary experiment, cells were cultured in HBSS containing: (i) no amino acids (“ALL” condition); (ii) all 20 amino acids at 400 µM each (“NONE”); or (iii) 19 amino acids excluding L-serine (“L-SERINE”). BMAA incorporation was highest in “ALL,” reduced by 89% in “NONE,” and partially restored (58% of “ALL”) in “L-SERINE” (p < 0.01 vs. “NONE”), proving that L-serine is the critical limiting factor governing BMAA misincorporation.

**Example 3: Biochemical Confirmation of Covalent Integration of BMAA into Proteins**

SH-SY5Y cells were treated with [³H]-BMAA (31.25 nM) for 24 hours, and proteins were isolated by TCA precipitation. Aliquots were subjected to: (i) buffer alone; (ii) 1 mM DTT + 2% SDS; (iii) 12 M HCl (110°C, 16 h); or (iv) pronase (2 mg/mL, 37°C, 48 h). Only acid hydrolysis and pronase treatment released significant radioactivity (92 ± 3% and 88 ± 4% of total, respectively), while DTT/SDS released <5%. This confirms that BMAA is covalently embedded within the polypeptide chain and not merely associated via weak interactions.

**Example 4: LC-MS/MS Validation of BMAA in Hydrolyzed Cellular Proteins**

MRC-5 cells were exposed to 0, 50, 100, or 200 µM unlabeled BMAA for 24 hours. Proteins were TCA-precipitated, hydrolyzed in 6 M HCl, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS following AQC derivatization. BMAA was identified by six SRM transitions with retention time 4.98 ± 0.05 min, matching the standard. Peak areas correlated linearly with dose (y = 3.19×10¹²x + 1.92×10⁴, R² = 0.99). Ion ratios (e.g., m/z 459→289 / m/z 459→171 = 30.7 ± 2.4%) matched reference values, excluding isobaric interferences from DAB or AEG.

**Example 5: Prevention of BMAA-Induced Protein Aggregation and Apoptosis by L-Serine**

MRC-5 cells were treated for 96 hours with: (i) vehicle; (ii) 300 µM BMAA; or (iii) 300 µM BMAA + 300 µM L-serine. BMAA induced bright autofluorescence (excitation 365 nm, emission 450 nm) in perinuclear regions, indicative of protein aggregates. Co-treatment with L-serine abolished this signal. Apoptosis, measured by Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry, increased from 4.2% (control) to 28.7% (BMAA) but was reduced to 6.1% with L-serine co-administration (p < 0.001). CHX (2 µg/mL) similarly suppressed apoptosis (5.8%), confirming dependence on new protein synthesis.

These examples collectively validate the core claims of the invention: BMAA is misincorporated for serine during translation, causing proteotoxicity that is preventable by L-serine supplementation.
