# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of metabolic diagnostics and therapeutics, particularly concerning the identification, monitoring, and treatment of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes mellitus. More specifically, the invention provides methods for diagnosing and assessing the severity of insulin resistance in a human subject by measuring the concentration of 1-methylnicotinamide (MNA) in a biological sample, such as plasma or serum, and correlating this concentration with the expression level of nicotinamide-N-methyltransferase (NNMT) in adipose tissue. The invention further encompasses therapeutic strategies that involve the modulation of NNMT activity—particularly its inhibition—as a means to ameliorate insulin resistance and improve glucose homeostasis. The disclosed methods are grounded in the discovery that elevated NNMT expression in visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue is strongly associated with systemic insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, and that circulating MNA levels serve as a reliable biomarker of this pathological state, especially in individuals with established metabolic dysfunction. This invention thus bridges the gap between molecular enzymology, clinical endocrinology, and precision medicine by offering a novel diagnostic axis centered on the NNMT–MNA pathway and a corresponding therapeutic target for metabolic disease intervention.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Technical Problems Underlying the Present Invention

Insulin resistance represents a fundamental pathophysiological defect underlying the development of type 2 diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease. Despite its clinical significance, the precise molecular mechanisms driving insulin resistance in humans remain incompletely understood, and current diagnostic tools often lack sensitivity in early-stage disease or fail to reflect tissue-specific metabolic dysregulation. Existing biomarkers such as fasting glucose, HbA1c, and HOMA-IR provide systemic assessments but do not capture the dynamic interplay between adipose tissue metabolism and whole-body insulin sensitivity. Moreover, while interventions such as lifestyle modification, pharmacotherapy, and bariatric surgery can improve insulin sensitivity, there is no reliable method to predict individual responsiveness or to monitor the molecular efficacy of these interventions in real time. This gap in clinical practice stems from a limited understanding of the metabolic pathways that directly link adipose tissue dysfunction to systemic insulin resistance.

One emerging area of interest involves the metabolism of nicotinamide, the amide form of vitamin B3, which serves as a precursor for the essential coenzymes NAD+ and NADP+. These coenzymes are critical for cellular redox reactions, mitochondrial function, DNA repair, and epigenetic regulation. However, prolonged high-dose administration of nicotinamide or its derivative niacin has been paradoxically associated with reduced insulin sensitivity in both normoglycemic and prediabetic individuals, suggesting that disturbances in nicotinamide metabolism may contribute to metabolic dysfunction. The enzyme nicotinamide-N-methyltransferase (NNMT) catalyzes the methylation of nicotinamide using S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) as the methyl donor, producing 1-methylnicotinamide (MNA) and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH). While NNMT is expressed in multiple tissues, including liver, kidney, and muscle, recent evidence indicates that its expression in adipose tissue is selectively upregulated in obesity and diabetes in rodent models. Furthermore, genetic or pharmacological reduction of NNMT in mice protects against diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance, implicating adipose NNMT as a potential driver of metabolic disease.

However, translating these findings to humans has been hindered by several technical and conceptual challenges. First, it was unclear whether NNMT expression is similarly elevated in human adipose tissue in the context of insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes. Second, although urinary MNA levels have been reported to be higher in diabetic patients and animal models, the relationship between circulating (plasma or serum) MNA concentrations and tissue-specific NNMT activity—particularly in adipose depots—had not been established in human cohorts. Third, it was unknown whether plasma MNA could serve as a non-invasive biomarker that reflects the degree of insulin resistance or responds dynamically to therapeutic interventions. Finally, even if such a correlation existed, it remained uncertain whether the association was confounded by factors such as body mass index (BMI), age, sex, or concomitant medications, thereby limiting its clinical utility.

Prior studies attempting to link MNA to glucose metabolism yielded inconsistent results. For example, one small study observed elevated plasma MNA only after a nicotinamide challenge in diabetic patients but found no difference in baseline levels, leading to the hypothesis that impaired MNA clearance—not increased production—was responsible. However, this study did not assess NNMT expression in relevant tissues and lacked detailed phenotyping for insulin resistance. Additionally, the contribution of different organs—liver versus adipose tissue—to systemic MNA production in health and disease was unknown. Given that the liver is a major site of NNMT expression, it was plausible that hepatic NNMT, rather than adipose NNMT, dictated circulating MNA levels. Without direct measurements of adipose tissue NNMT in well-phenotyped human cohorts, the role of adipose-specific NNMT in human insulin resistance could not be confirmed.

These unresolved questions represent significant technical problems that have impeded the development of NNMT-targeted diagnostics and therapeutics. The absence of a validated, non-invasive biomarker for adipose tissue NNMT activity has prevented the stratification of patients who might benefit from NNMT inhibition. Moreover, without evidence that NNMT expression in human adipose tissue correlates with the gold standard measure of insulin sensitivity—the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp—it was difficult to justify NNMT as a clinically relevant therapeutic target. The present invention addresses these critical gaps by demonstrating, for the first time in humans, that NNMT mRNA expression is significantly elevated in both subcutaneous and omental adipose tissue in individuals with type 2 diabetes and insulin resistance, that this elevation is independent of BMI, and that plasma MNA concentrations correlate strongly with adipose NNMT expression—but only in metabolically compromised individuals. Furthermore, the invention shows that interventions known to improve insulin sensitivity, such as exercise and bariatric surgery, lead to a concomitant reduction in both adipose NNMT expression and plasma MNA levels, thereby establishing the NNMT–MNA axis as a dynamic, modifiable pathway in human metabolic disease. These findings resolve the prior uncertainties and provide a robust foundation for the development of MNA-based diagnostics and NNMT inhibitors as novel therapeutics for insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution to the technical problems outlined above by establishing a novel diagnostic and therapeutic paradigm centered on the enzyme nicotinamide-N-methyltransferase (NNMT) and its metabolite 1-methylnicotinamide (MNA). The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that NNMT expression is significantly upregulated in human adipose tissue—both subcutaneous and omental—in individuals with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, and that this upregulation correlates directly with the severity of systemic insulin resistance as measured by the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp. Critically, the invention demonstrates that plasma MNA concentration serves as a reliable, non-invasive biomarker of adipose tissue NNMT activity, but only in individuals with established metabolic dysfunction, such as those with type 2 diabetes or high BMI. This conditional correlation resolves prior inconsistencies in the literature and defines a specific clinical context in which MNA measurement is diagnostically meaningful.

The invention further discloses that therapeutic interventions known to improve insulin sensitivity—including structured exercise programs and bariatric surgery—result in a significant reduction in both adipose tissue NNMT expression and circulating MNA levels. This dynamic responsiveness confirms that the NNMT–MNA pathway is not merely a static correlate of disease but an active, modifiable component of the metabolic dysregulation underlying insulin resistance. As such, the invention provides a method for monitoring the efficacy of metabolic interventions by tracking changes in plasma MNA over time.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for diagnosing insulin resistance in a human subject, comprising the steps of: (a) obtaining a biological sample from the subject, wherein the biological sample is plasma or serum; (b) measuring the concentration of 1-methylnicotinamide (MNA) in the biological sample; and (c) comparing the measured MNA concentration to a reference value, wherein an elevated MNA concentration relative to the reference value indicates the presence of insulin resistance, particularly in subjects with type 2 diabetes or a BMI above 39 kg/m². In another aspect, the invention provides a method for assessing the severity of insulin resistance, comprising correlating the plasma MNA concentration with the glucose infusion rate (GIR) during a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, wherein lower GIR values correspond to higher MNA levels.

In a further aspect, the invention provides a method for selecting a patient for treatment with an NNMT inhibitor, comprising identifying a patient with elevated plasma MNA levels and/or elevated adipose tissue NNMT expression, wherein such a patient is deemed likely to respond to NNMT inhibition. The invention also encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising an NNMT inhibitor for use in the treatment of insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes. Additionally, the invention includes kits for the diagnosis of insulin resistance, comprising reagents for the quantification of MNA in plasma or serum, along with instructions for interpreting the results in the context of the patient’s metabolic status.

By linking adipose tissue NNMT expression to systemic insulin resistance through the measurable biomarker MNA, and by demonstrating the reversibility of this pathway with effective interventions, the present invention establishes a new framework for the precision management of metabolic disease. It enables clinicians to identify at-risk individuals, stratify patients based on molecular pathology, monitor treatment response, and ultimately deploy targeted therapies that address the root cause of insulin resistance at the enzymatic level.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this invention, the following terms are defined as follows:

“Insulin resistance” refers to a physiological condition in which cells fail to respond normally to the hormone insulin, resulting in impaired glucose uptake and elevated blood glucose levels. Insulin resistance is quantitatively assessed by the glucose infusion rate (GIR) during a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp, with a GIR below 50 μmol·kg⁻¹·min⁻¹ being indicative of insulin resistance.

“Type 2 diabetes mellitus” is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from a combination of insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency. Diagnosis is based on established criteria, such as fasting plasma glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L, 2-hour plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), or HbA1c ≥6.5%.

“Adipose tissue” encompasses both subcutaneous adipose tissue (scWAT), located beneath the skin, and visceral or omental adipose tissue (omWAT), located within the abdominal cavity surrounding internal organs. Both depots are metabolically active and contribute to systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, though visceral fat is generally more strongly associated with metabolic risk.

“Nicotinamide-N-methyltransferase (NNMT)” is a cytosolic enzyme that catalyzes the transfer of a methyl group from S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) to nicotinamide, producing 1-methylnicotinamide (MNA) and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH). NNMT expression is measured at the mRNA level using quantitative real-time RT-PCR, with results expressed in arbitrary units relative to housekeeping genes.

“1-Methylnicotinamide (MNA)” is the primary metabolite of nicotinamide produced by NNMT activity. MNA concentration is measured in plasma or serum using liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), and is reported in nanomolar (nM) or micromolar (μM) units.

“Biological sample” refers to any bodily fluid or tissue from which MNA can be quantified or NNMT expression assessed. In the context of this invention, the preferred biological sample for MNA measurement is plasma or serum, while adipose tissue biopsies are used for NNMT mRNA analysis.

“Reference value” denotes a threshold or range of MNA concentrations derived from a population of metabolically healthy individuals, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI where appropriate. An MNA concentration exceeding the upper limit of the reference range in a subject with type 2 diabetes or high BMI is considered indicative of elevated adipose NNMT activity and insulin resistance.

“NNMT inhibitor” is any compound that reduces the enzymatic activity of NNMT, either by competitive, non-competitive, or allosteric mechanisms. Such inhibitors may be small molecules, peptides, antisense oligonucleotides, or RNA interference constructs designed to suppress NNMT expression or function.

### EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is embodied in several interrelated aspects that collectively enable the diagnosis, monitoring, and treatment of insulin resistance through the NNMT–MNA pathway. In a first embodiment, the invention provides a diagnostic method for insulin resistance in a human subject. This method involves the collection of a fasting blood sample, isolation of plasma or serum, and precise quantification of MNA concentration using LC-MS/MS. The measured MNA level is then interpreted in the context of the subject’s clinical profile: if the subject has been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes or has a BMI greater than 39 kg/m², an elevated MNA concentration (e.g., above the 90th percentile of a healthy reference population) is indicative of increased adipose tissue NNMT expression and, by extension, significant insulin resistance. This diagnostic approach is particularly valuable because it reflects tissue-specific metabolic activity that is not captured by conventional markers like fasting glucose or HbA1c.

In a second embodiment, the invention offers a method for assessing the severity of insulin resistance. Here, the plasma MNA concentration is correlated with the glucose infusion rate (GIR) obtained during a hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp. In insulin-resistant individuals, a strong inverse correlation exists between MNA levels and GIR, such that higher MNA corresponds to lower insulin sensitivity. This allows clinicians to use a simple blood test to estimate the degree of insulin resistance, reducing the need for invasive and time-consuming clamp procedures in routine monitoring.

A third embodiment pertains to patient stratification for targeted therapy. Individuals with elevated plasma MNA and/or confirmed high adipose NNMT expression are identified as candidates for treatment with an NNMT inhibitor. This precision medicine approach ensures that therapeutic resources are directed toward patients most likely to benefit, thereby improving efficacy and reducing unnecessary exposure to experimental drugs.

A fourth embodiment involves the use of MNA as a pharmacodynamic biomarker to monitor response to metabolic interventions. Whether the intervention is lifestyle-based (e.g., exercise), surgical (e.g., bariatric surgery), or pharmacological (e.g., NNMT inhibition), serial measurements of plasma MNA can track changes in adipose tissue NNMT activity over time. A decline in MNA following intervention correlates with improved insulin sensitivity and provides objective evidence of biological response, even before changes in weight or glycemic control become apparent.

In a fifth embodiment, the invention encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising a therapeutically effective amount of an NNMT inhibitor, formulated for oral, intravenous, or subcutaneous administration. These compositions may include additional excipients, stabilizers, or delivery systems to enhance bioavailability and tissue targeting, particularly to adipose depots. The inhibitors may be selected from known chemical libraries or developed de novo based on the crystal structure of human NNMT.

Finally, the invention includes diagnostic kits containing all necessary reagents and instructions for performing the MNA assay in a clinical laboratory setting. Such kits may include internal standards for LC-MS/MS, calibration curves, and software algorithms to adjust for covariates like renal function or medication use, ensuring accurate and reproducible results across diverse patient populations.

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the practical application and validation of the invention across three distinct human cohorts, each designed to address a specific aspect of the NNMT–MNA–insulin resistance relationship.

**Example 1: Cross-Sectional Cohort Demonstrates Elevated Adipose NNMT and Correlation with MNA in Type 2 Diabetes**

A cross-sectional study was conducted in 199 individuals undergoing abdominal surgery, including 88 with type 2 diabetes and 111 without. Paired biopsies of subcutaneous and omental adipose tissue were obtained, and NNMT mRNA expression was quantified by TaqMan RT-PCR. Plasma MNA was measured by LC-MS/MS. Results showed that NNMT expression was approximately twofold higher in both adipose depots in diabetic patients compared to non-diabetic controls, regardless of sex. Notably, this elevation was independent of BMI, as no correlation was found between NNMT expression and BMI across the cohort. In a subset of 85 individuals who underwent hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamps, omental NNMT expression was 3.5-fold higher in insulin-resistant subjects (GIR <50) than in insulin-sensitive ones. Crucially, plasma MNA correlated strongly with adipose NNMT expression (r = 0.6, p < 0.001) only in diabetic individuals, not in non-diabetics. This demonstrates that MNA is a context-dependent biomarker, valid specifically in the setting of metabolic disease.

**Example 2: Exercise Intervention Confirms Reversibility of NNMT Upregulation**

Sixty individuals—20 each with normal glucose tolerance (NGT), impaired glucose tolerance (IGT), and type 2 diabetes—participated in a 12-week supervised exercise program. Subcutaneous adipose biopsies were taken before and after intervention. Baseline NNMT expression was significantly higher in IGT and diabetic groups (2.7- and 2.9-fold, respectively) compared to NGT. Following exercise, NNMT expression decreased by 21% in IGT and 16% in diabetics, but remained unchanged in NGT. Improvements in GIR strongly correlated with reductions in NNMT expression (r = 0.70 post-exercise), confirming that enhanced insulin sensitivity is associated with downregulation of adipose NNMT. This example validates the dynamic nature of the pathway and supports the use of MNA as a monitoring tool for lifestyle interventions.

**Example 3: Bariatric Surgery Cohort Links Weight Loss, Metabolic Improvement, and NNMT Suppression**

Fifty-five morbidly obese patients underwent a two-step bariatric surgery (sleeve gastrectomy followed by Roux-en-Y gastric bypass 12 months later). Adipose tissue and plasma samples were collected at both surgeries. Between procedures, mean BMI dropped from 53.6 to 36.7 kg/m², and the prevalence of type 2 diabetes fell from 56% to 7%. NNMT expression decreased by 50% in subcutaneous and 41% in omental fat, accompanied by a 24% reduction in plasma MNA (p = 0.001). The parallel decline in MNA and NNMT, alongside dramatic metabolic improvements, provides compelling evidence that the NNMT–MNA axis is a central node in human insulin resistance and that its suppression is a key mechanism of surgical efficacy.

Together, these examples substantiate the invention’s claims and provide a robust evidentiary basis for its clinical translation.
