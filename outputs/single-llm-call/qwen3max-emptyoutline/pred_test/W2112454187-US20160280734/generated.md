# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of automated chemical synthesis, and more particularly to an automated radiosynthesizer system for the production of positron-emission tomography (PET) radiopharmaceuticals. The invention provides a modular, computer-controlled platform capable of performing complex multi-step syntheses involving high temperatures, high pressures, volatile solvents, and corrosive reagents, which are commonly encountered in the preparation of 18F-labeled PET tracers beyond the widely used [18F]FDG. The system is specifically engineered to overcome limitations inherent in conventional automated radiosynthesizers, which often restrict reaction conditions due to permanent fluidic connections between reaction vessels and fixed tubing or valve assemblies that cannot withstand elevated pressures or aggressive chemical environments. The invention further pertains to a disposable cassette-based architecture that enables rapid interchangeability between different tracer syntheses without requiring hardware reconfiguration, cleaning, or cross-contamination mitigation procedures. Additionally, the invention encompasses integrated robotic subsystems for precise reagent handling, dynamic gas and vacuum delivery, real-time visual monitoring, and programmable thermal control, all coordinated through a unified software interface that organizes synthesis protocols into intuitive “unit operations.” This technical field intersects with nuclear medicine, radiochemistry, automation engineering, fluidics, thermal management, and regulatory compliance for both preclinical research and clinical-grade radiopharmaceutical manufacturing under current good manufacturing practice (cGMP) standards.

## BACKGROUND

Positron-emission tomography (PET) has become an indispensable tool in modern medical diagnostics, enabling non-invasive visualization of biochemical processes in living organisms for applications such as early disease detection, cancer staging, and evaluation of therapeutic response. Central to PET imaging is the use of radiolabeled molecular probes, or tracers, which accumulate in target tissues based on specific biological mechanisms. Among these, 2-[18F]fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose ([18F]FDG) remains the most extensively utilized PET tracer due to its favorable production characteristics—including a manageable half-life of approximately 110 minutes for fluorine-18—and its broad applicability in oncology, neurology, and cardiology. The widespread adoption of [18F]FDG has driven significant advancements in automated radiosynthesis technology, resulting in commercial synthesizers that reliably produce this tracer with minimal human intervention, thereby reducing radiation exposure to personnel, lowering production costs, and facilitating decentralized manufacturing at hospital-based cyclotron facilities.

However, the clinical and research potential of PET extends far beyond [18F]FDG. Numerous next-generation tracers—such as nucleoside analogs like 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-β-d-arabinofuranosylcytosine (d-[18F]FAC) and 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-5-methyl-β-l-arabinofuranosyluracil (l-[18F]FMAU)—offer unique capabilities for imaging cellular proliferation, monitoring gene expression in reporter systems, and assessing chemotherapy efficacy. These tracers often require synthetic routes that involve harsh reaction conditions, including elevated temperatures (frequently exceeding 150°C), high internal pressures generated by volatile organic solvents such as acetonitrile or dimethyl sulfoxide, and the use of moisture-sensitive or corrosive reagents like trimethylsilyl compounds or strong acids. Unfortunately, conventional automated radiosynthesizers are ill-suited for such demanding chemistries. Most existing platforms rely on fixed fluidic pathways wherein reaction vessels are permanently connected via tubing and valves to reagent reservoirs, gas lines, and purification modules. Under high-temperature or high-pressure conditions, these static connections become points of failure: seals degrade, valves leak, and tubing may rupture, leading to inconsistent yields, safety hazards, and instrument damage. Moreover, the inability to isolate the reaction environment from the rest of the fluidic network limits the maximum permissible pressure, forcing chemists to compromise reaction parameters—such as using lower temperatures, alternative solvents, or extended reaction times—which can significantly reduce radiochemical yield, purity, or specific activity.

Several attempts have been made to adapt commercial synthesizers for challenging tracers, but these typically involve extensive hardware modifications, custom plumbing, or manual interventions that undermine the core benefits of automation: reproducibility, reliability, and operator safety. Furthermore, transitioning a novel tracer from laboratory-scale development to routine production often requires complete re-engineering of the synthesis setup, creating a bottleneck in the translation of promising radiopharmaceuticals from bench to bedside. Another critical limitation of traditional systems is the requirement for thorough cleaning between syntheses to prevent cross-contamination, a time-consuming and error-prone process that is particularly problematic when switching between tracers with vastly different chemical profiles. This cleaning necessity also introduces variability due to residual solvent or reagent carryover, potentially affecting product quality and regulatory compliance.

In response to these challenges, prior art has explored alternative approaches, including the development of modular platforms with movable reaction components. One such approach involved sealing a reaction vessel against an inert stopper during high-pressure steps to isolate it from vulnerable tubing and valves, thereby preserving system integrity. While effective in principle, early implementations lacked full automation, robust thermal control, integrated reagent handling, or user-friendly software interfaces necessary for widespread adoption. There remained a clear unmet need for a radiosynthesizer that could seamlessly accommodate both routine and complex syntheses—spanning from simple one-pot reactions to multi-reactor sequences with intermediate purifications—without sacrificing performance, safety, or ease of use. Such a system would need to support disposable fluidic pathways to eliminate cleaning, incorporate active cooling for precise temperature modulation, provide real-time visual feedback for process validation, and offer flexible reagent delivery mechanisms capable of handling air- and moisture-sensitive compounds. Critically, it must also be scalable from tracer development in academic laboratories to compliant production in clinical settings governed by stringent regulatory frameworks such as 21 CFR Part 212 or USP <823>. The present invention addresses these deficiencies by introducing a novel architecture centered on dynamically reconfigurable fluid paths enabled by mechanical motion, disposable cassettes containing all wetted components, and a coordinated suite of robotic and thermal subsystems under unified software control.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides an automated radiosynthesizer system specifically designed for the reliable, flexible, and safe production of a wide range of positron-emission tomography (PET) radiopharmaceuticals, including those requiring high-temperature, high-pressure, or chemically aggressive reaction conditions that are incompatible with conventional automated synthesizers. The system overcomes the limitations of fixed fluidic architectures by employing a unique combination of movable reaction vessels, disposable cassettes, and a reagent and gas handling robot to dynamically reconfigure fluid pathways during synthesis. At the core of the invention is a modular platform comprising three independently controlled reactor assemblies, each capable of precise thermal regulation from ambient temperature up to at least 185°C with active liquid cooling, and equipped with magnetic stirring and real-time visual monitoring via integrated cameras. Each reactor houses a standard glass reaction vial within a spring-loaded chuck that ensures optimal thermal contact and can be pneumatically actuated to move horizontally among multiple predefined positions beneath a disposable cassette and vertically to seal the vial against a chemically inert gasket affixed to the cassette’s underside. This sealing mechanism isolates the reaction volume from external tubing and valves during pressurized steps, enabling reactions to proceed at elevated temperatures and pressures without risk of leakage or component degradation.

The disposable cassette serves as the primary fluidic manifold and reagent storage unit, containing pre-assembled stainless steel needles, PTFE-coated silicone gaskets, chemically resistant stopcock valves, and internal tubing configured to support a variety of unit operations including radioisotope trapping and elution, reagent addition, sealed reactions, solvent evaporation, and cartridge-based purification. Each cassette accommodates up to eleven inverted reagent vials, which are accessed by a two-axis robotic gripper that retrieves vials from storage positions and delivers them to designated addition sites where they are pierced by dual needles—one for fluid transfer and one for inert gas pressurization. An integrated gas supplier robot, mounted on a separate z-axis actuator, provides on-demand inert gas and vacuum to dedicated ports on the cassette top, eliminating the need for multiple permanent gas connections and enhancing system reliability. The use of disposable cassettes eliminates cross-contamination between syntheses, removes the need for post-run cleaning, and allows rapid transition from tracer development to routine production simply by swapping cassettes and selecting a corresponding software protocol.

The entire system is orchestrated by a centralized control architecture comprising a Linux-based server interfaced with a programmable logic controller (PLC) that coordinates all mechanical, thermal, pneumatic, and fluidic subsystems. Synthesis protocols are constructed using a drag-and-drop software interface that organizes chemistry into intuitive “unit operations” such as Add, React, Evaporate, Transfer, and Purify, enabling users to design complex multi-step sequences without low-level programming. The system supports independent operation of each of the three reactors, allowing parallel or sequential processing with intermediate purification via Sep-Pak cartridges integrated into the cassette fluid path. Validation studies demonstrate successful synthesis of challenging tracers such as d-[18F]FAC and l-[18F]FMAU with decay-corrected radiochemical yields and synthesis times comparable to or better than literature reports, and products meeting >99% radiochemical purity. The system has also been used to produce a diverse array of other tracers—including [18F]FDG, [18F]FLT, [18F]fallypride, [18F]FHBG, and [18F]SFB—by merely exchanging cassettes and software programs, confirming its versatility. Furthermore, tracers produced on the system have passed all required quality assurance tests for clinical use, and ongoing software enhancements aim to achieve full compliance with cGMP regulations for deployment in clinical radiopharmacies. In summary, the invention represents a paradigm shift in radiosynthesizer design, offering unprecedented flexibility, robustness, and user-friendliness for both research and regulated production environments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is embodied in an automated radiosynthesizer system referred to herein as the ELIXYS platform, which integrates mechanical motion, disposable fluidics, robotic reagent handling, and advanced thermal control to enable the synthesis of a broad spectrum of PET radiopharmaceuticals under demanding chemical conditions. The following detailed description outlines the structural and functional components of the system, their interrelationships, and their coordinated operation during representative synthesis protocols.

The hardware architecture of the ELIXYS system comprises three principal subsystems: a set of three reactor assemblies, a reagent and gas handling robot, and a family of disposable cassettes. These components operate in concert under the supervision of a centralized control system housed in a separate enclosure, ensuring precise execution of user-defined synthesis protocols while maintaining operator safety and product integrity. Each reactor assembly is designed to accommodate a standard 5-mL glass V-vial, which serves as the primary reaction vessel throughout the synthesis. The vial is secured within a three-segment spring-loaded chuck that applies uniform radial pressure to ensure excellent thermal contact between the glass surface and the surrounding heating elements. Each segment of the chuck incorporates a 100-watt cartridge heater and a K-type thermocouple, enabling independent temperature monitoring and feedback-controlled heating. Although individual thermocouple readings are available, the system typically computes the average of the three measurements to represent the overall reactor temperature, a simplification justified by empirical observations of highly consistent thermal response across segments. The maximum operational temperature of the reactor is currently rated at 185°C, though ongoing investigations into alternative sealing materials and mechanical fittings aim to extend this limit further to accommodate even more aggressive reaction conditions.

Active cooling is achieved through a closed-loop liquid cooling system that circulates a propylene/ethylene glycol-water mixture through channels integrated into each reactor block. A dedicated coolant pump drives the fluid through all three reactors in series before passing it through a radiator equipped with three 140-mm fans, enabling rapid quenching of reactions after high-temperature steps. This active cooling capability is critical for controlling exothermic reactions, minimizing side-product formation, and expediting synthesis cycles. Complementing the thermal control system is a magnetic stirring mechanism consisting of a DC motor-mounted magnet positioned beneath the reactor, which rotates a removable stir bar inside the reaction vial to ensure homogeneous mixing during reagent addition and reaction phases. To facilitate process development and quality assurance, a high-resolution camera is mounted behind each reactor, providing real-time visual feedback on liquid levels during evaporations, confirmation of reagent transfers, observation of reaction progression (e.g., color changes or precipitate formation), and post-purification inspection of eluate clarity.

The reactor assembly is further equipped with precision motion capabilities that distinguish the present invention from conventional synthesizers. Each reactor is mounted on a linear servomotor that enables horizontal translation along the y-axis among multiple predefined positions beneath a disposable cassette. In each position, the reactor can be pneumatically raised or lowered via a cylinder actuator to engage or disengage the reaction vial with a corresponding sealing zone on the underside of the cassette. Hall effect sensors provide positional feedback to confirm the vertical state (raised or lowered) of the reactor, while the linear actuator’s encoder ensures accurate horizontal placement. This dynamic reconfiguration of the reaction vessel’s location relative to the cassette enables the system to switch between distinct fluidic configurations tailored to specific unit operations. For instance, in a “reagent addition” position, internal tubing within the cassette aligns with the vial opening to permit fluid inflow; in a “sealed reaction” position, no tubing is present, allowing the vial to be fully isolated upon sealing against the gasket, thereby containing internal pressure without exposing external components to stress. Similarly, dedicated positions exist for solvent evaporation (with access to vacuum and inert gas ports) and product transfer (with connection to purification cartridges). This motion-based fluidic reconfiguration eliminates the need for permanent tubing connections to the reaction vessel—a key innovation that circumvents the pressure limitations and failure modes endemic to fixed-path synthesizers.

The disposable cassette constitutes the second major component of the system and serves as the complete fluidic and reagent interface for a given synthesis. Molded from polyurethane for chemical resistance and dimensional stability, each cassette integrates all wetted parts—including stainless steel needles, PTFE-coated silicone gaskets, three-way stopcock valves, and internal tubing—into a single, pre-assembled unit that slides into the synthesizer along precision rails and locks into place via clamps. The cassette contains eleven inverted storage positions for standard 13-mm crimp-sealed vials (maximum 3 mL capacity), each held upside-down to facilitate bottom-piercing during reagent delivery. Three of these positions are designated as “addition sites,” each equipped with dual upward-pointing needles: one connected to the internal fluid path for reagent transfer, and another linked to an inert gas port on the cassette top for vial pressurization. Two of these addition sites are used for general reagent delivery to the reaction vessel, while the third is reserved for eluent addition during radioisotope elution from trapping cartridges.

Integrated into the cassette’s fluid architecture are three chemically inert stopcock valves that enable dynamic reconfiguration of flow paths for radioisotope processing and product purification. The first two valves work in tandem to manage anion exchange cartridges (e.g., QMA cartridges for [18F]fluoride trapping). During the trapping phase, incoming [18F]fluoride solution—delivered either directly from a cyclotron target or from a source vial pressurized by the system—is directed through the cartridge, with the [18O]H2O effluent collected in a built-in recovery vial. Upon switching the stopcocks, the system can then deliver eluent from the dedicated addition site through the same cartridge to release the trapped [18F]fluoride into the reaction vessel. Multiple rinse and elution cycles may be performed to maximize recovery efficiency. The third stopcock valve governs the purification of crude reaction products via solid-phase extraction cartridges (e.g., silica or C18 Sep-Pak cartridges). A dip tube, constructed from Teflon® tubing (typically 1/8-inch OD to accommodate precipitates), extends from the reaction vessel sealing zone into the cassette and connects upstream of the purification cartridge. Downstream of the cartridge, the stopcock selects between a waste collection vial (for trapping impurities and washing) and an external output line that can be routed to the next cassette’s reaction vessel or to an HPLC injection valve for final purification. Cartridge mounting clips are provided on the cassette exterior for convenient installation and replacement.

The third major subsystem is the reagent and gas handling robot, a pneumatically actuated, three-axis manipulator responsible for all reagent vial movements and gas/vacuum delivery. The robot consists of two primary end-effectors mounted on a shared x-y gantry: a vial gripper and a gas supplier. The vial gripper, powered by a pneumatic cylinder in the z-axis, retrieves reagent vials from their inverted storage positions by grasping the crimped cap and transporting them to the appropriate addition site. Once positioned, the gripper lowers the vial onto the dual needles, simultaneously piercing the septum and establishing fluid and gas connections. Pressurization of the vial with inert gas (typically nitrogen or argon) then drives the complete transfer of its contents into the reaction vessel or cartridge path. Characterization studies have shown that approximately 120 ± 20 μL of liquid remains in the vial post-transfer due to dead volume effects; this loss is compensated by loading excess reagent during cassette preparation. After delivery, the gripper lifts the empty vial and returns it to its original storage slot, maintaining inventory tracking and preventing misplacement.

The gas supplier, mounted adjacent to the gripper on a second z-axis pneumatic actuator, features two concentric ports: a lower inert gas inlet and an upper vacuum port mounted on a spring-loaded mechanism to ensure simultaneous sealing when both functions are required. The supplier lowers to engage corresponding rubber-gasketed ports on the cassette top, forming gas-tight seals without the need for manual connections. Inert gas is used not only for reagent transfer but also to assist solvent evaporation by sweeping vapor from the reaction headspace, while vacuum—drawn through an in-line cold trap cooled with dry ice/methanol—is applied during evaporation to accelerate solvent removal. An in-line check valve near the gas delivery point prevents backflow of volatile reagents into the supply line, preserving system integrity. Hall effect sensors on both z-axis actuators ensure that the gripper and gas supplier are fully retracted before any x-y motion occurs, preventing collisions, and also detect missing vials by monitoring actuator resistance during gripping attempts.

All subsystems are coordinated by a centralized control system comprising a Linux server interfaced via Ethernet to a programmable logic controller (PLC). The PLC, in turn, communicates with motor controllers, pneumatic solenoid banks, temperature modules, and radioactivity detectors through a series of expansion units. Five motor controllers manage the robotic motions: two pulse motor controllers drive the x- and y-axes of the reagent/gas robot, while three linear servomotor controllers govern the y-axis movement of each reactor. Pneumatic functions—including reactor elevation, stopcock rotation, gripper actuation, and gas supplier movement—are powered by a high-pressure inert gas line (regulated to ~60 psig), while a separate low-pressure line (3–15 psig) supplies gas for fluid transfers and evaporation. Solid-state relays modulate power to the cartridge heaters based on thermocouple feedback, and the cooling pump is activated automatically during cooldown phases. Video streams from the reactor cameras are encoded by a dedicated video server and made available to the user interface for remote monitoring.

Synthesis protocols are constructed using a graphical drag-and-drop software environment that abstracts complex sequences into standardized “unit operations.” These include Radioisotope Handling (for trapping and eluting [18F]fluoride), Reagent Addition (for delivering stored solutions), Reaction (for heating/stirring under sealed conditions), Evaporation (for solvent removal under heat, gas, and vacuum), and Transfer/Purification (for moving products through cartridges). Each unit operation is parameterized by user-defined values such as temperature, time, pressure, and volume, and the software automatically computes safety margins—for example, multiplying empirically determined evaporation times by a factor of 1.2 to ensure completeness. The system supports independent programming of each reactor, enabling sophisticated workflows such as concurrent synthesis of different tracers or sequential multi-step reactions with intermediate purification. Validation experiments have demonstrated robust performance in synthesizing d-[18F]FAC and l-[18F]FMAU, with decay-corrected yields of 25–30%, synthesis times of ~70 minutes, and radiochemical purity exceeding 99%. Seal integrity tests confirmed less than 1.5% solvent loss during 1-hour reactions at 165°C, and thermal profiling showed heating rates comparable to oil baths with superior cooling kinetics due to active liquid cooling. The disposable cassette approach has enabled rapid deployment of additional tracers—including [18F]FDG, [18F]FLT, and [18F]SFB—without hardware changes, and clinical-grade batches have passed all required quality tests, paving the way for cGMP-compliant production.
