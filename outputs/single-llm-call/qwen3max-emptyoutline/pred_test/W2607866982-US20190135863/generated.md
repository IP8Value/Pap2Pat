# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Metastatic dissemination of solid tumors remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, despite significant advances in therapeutic strategies targeting primary tumor growth. The metastatic cascade is a highly orchestrated, multistep biological process that begins with the detachment of malignant cells from the primary tumor mass, followed by their invasion through the surrounding basement membrane and extracellular matrix, intravasation into the circulatory or lymphatic systems, survival in the bloodstream, extravasation at distant sites, and eventual colonization of secondary organs. Central to this entire sequence is the capacity of tumor cells to migrate directionally—a process that, while essential for normal physiological functions such as embryonic development, wound healing, and immune surveillance, becomes pathologically co-opted in cancer to facilitate systemic spread. Consequently, the molecular machinery governing cell motility has emerged as a compelling target for anti-metastatic drug development, particularly in aggressive malignancies such as sarcomas, which exhibit a high propensity for early hematogenous dissemination, often to the lungs.

Among the key regulators of pathological cell migration is the Urokinase Plasminogen Activator Receptor (uPAR), also known as CD87. uPAR is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored cell surface protein composed of three homologous domains (DI, DII, and DIII) that collectively form a concave ligand-binding pocket. While initially characterized for its role in localizing urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) to the cell surface—thereby facilitating the conversion of plasminogen to active plasmin and promoting pericellular proteolysis—uPAR has since been recognized as a master signaling hub that integrates extracellular cues to modulate cytoskeletal dynamics, adhesion, and directional motility. Critically, uPAR lacks intrinsic transmembrane or catalytic domains; instead, it transduces signals through dynamic interactions with a variety of co-receptors, including integrins, vitronectin, and notably, the Formyl Peptide Receptor family (FPRs), particularly FPR1.

The interaction between uPAR and FPR1 represents a pivotal node in the metastatic signaling network. FPR1 is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) originally identified in phagocytic leukocytes, where it mediates chemotaxis toward N-formylated peptides derived from bacterial or mitochondrial proteins. However, aberrant expression of FPR1 has been documented in a wide array of non-myeloid cancer cells, including those of sarcoma, melanoma, and epithelial ovarian carcinoma origin. In these contexts, FPR1 activation—whether by exogenous formyl peptides like fMLF or by endogenous ligands such as the uPAR-derived peptide SRSRY (Ser88-Arg-Ser-Arg-Tyr92)—triggers downstream signaling cascades involving PI3K/Akt, ERK, and p38 MAPK pathways, ultimately leading to actin reorganization, focal adhesion turnover, and enhanced cell migration and invasion. Moreover, clinical studies have consistently linked elevated levels of soluble uPAR in patient serum with poor prognosis and increased metastatic risk, underscoring the translational relevance of this axis.

Despite the compelling rationale for targeting the uPAR/FPR1 interface, the development of clinically viable inhibitors has been fraught with challenges. Early efforts yielded linear peptide antagonists based on the uPAR84–95 sequence, such as RERF (Ac-Arg-Glu-Arg-Phe-NH2), which demonstrated potent inhibition of FPR1-mediated migration and angiogenesis in vitro. However, these first-generation peptides suffered from critical pharmacological limitations: rapid degradation by serum proteases due to their all-L-amino acid composition, resulting in short plasma half-lives, and in some cases, off-target cytotoxicity potentially mediated by low-affinity interactions with other receptors such as the α chain of the vitronectin receptor. Subsequent optimization led to the development of more stable analogs like UPARANT (Ac-L-Arg-Aib-L-Arg-D-Ca(Me)Phe-NH2), which incorporated non-natural amino acids to enhance metabolic stability and exhibited prolonged anti-angiogenic activity in vivo. Nevertheless, UPARANT failed to inhibit sarcoma cell invasion in preclinical metastasis models, highlighting the need for novel structural scaffolds that retain high potency while overcoming the inherent liabilities of conventional peptides.

In this context, the Retro-Inverso (RI) modification strategy presents a promising avenue for peptide-based drug design. The RI approach involves reversing the amino acid sequence of a parent peptide while simultaneously inverting the chirality of each residue from L- to D-configuration. This transformation preserves the spatial orientation of the side-chain functional groups—thereby maintaining the pharmacophore necessary for receptor interaction—while inverting the directionality of the peptide backbone amide bonds. The resulting all-D-peptide is inherently resistant to proteolytic degradation by endogenous peptidases, which typically recognize L-amino acid sequences, thus conferring dramatically improved metabolic stability. Although the inversion of the amide backbone may disrupt specific hydrogen-bonding patterns that stabilize secondary structures in the parent peptide, successful applications of the RI concept have demonstrated that when receptor binding is predominantly governed by side-chain interactions—as is often the case for GPCR ligands—the biological activity can be retained or even enhanced.

Prior to the present invention, the RI strategy had not been systematically applied to uPAR/FPR1 inhibitory peptides. Given the central role of FPR1 in mediating uPAR-dependent pro-metastatic behaviors and the unmet need for stable, potent, and selective inhibitors of this interaction, there existed a clear and pressing opportunity to develop a new class of RI-modified peptides that could overcome the limitations of earlier leads. Such molecules would not only serve as valuable pharmacological tools for dissecting the uPAR/FPR1 signaling axis but also hold significant potential as anti-metastatic therapeutics, particularly for cancers with high metastatic burden and limited treatment options. The present invention addresses this need by providing a series of novel retro-inverso peptides specifically designed to disrupt the uPAR/FPR1 interaction, with RI-3 emerging as a lead candidate exhibiting exceptional stability, potency, and in vivo efficacy against multiple facets of the metastatic cascade.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of retro-inverso (RI) peptides that function as potent and selective antagonists of the interaction between the Urokinase Plasminogen Activator Receptor (uPAR) and the Formyl Peptide Receptor 1 (FPR1). These peptides are specifically engineered to inhibit uPAR/FPR1-mediated signaling pathways that drive pathological cell migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis, without eliciting agonist-like activity or adversely affecting cell proliferation. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising these RI peptides, methods for their synthesis, and their use in the prevention and treatment of metastatic diseases, particularly those associated with solid tumors such as sarcomas, melanomas, and epithelial ovarian carcinomas.

The core innovation lies in the application of the retro-inverso modification strategy to previously identified uPAR/FPR1 inhibitory peptide scaffolds. Specifically, the parent peptides, which are typically linear tetrapeptides of the general formula Ac-Arg-X1-Arg-X2-NH2 (where X1 and X2 are variable amino acid residues), are transformed into their RI analogs by inverting the sequence order and substituting all L-amino acids with their corresponding D-enantiomers. This results in RI peptides of the general formula Ac-(D)-X2-(D)-Arg-(D)-X1-(D)-Arg-NH2. In certain embodiments, the invention also includes truncated RI tripeptides wherein the fourth residue is replaced by an aromatic carboxylic acid moiety that mimics the side-chain functionality of the original C-terminal amino acid, thereby reducing the number of proteolytically labile amide bonds while preserving critical binding interactions.

A particularly advantageous embodiment of the invention is the retro-inverso peptide designated RI-3, having the sequence Ac-(D)-Tyr-Aib-(D)-Arg-NH2, where Aib denotes α-aminoisobutyric acid, a non-natural, α,α-disubstituted amino acid. The incorporation of Aib at position 2 (corresponding to the second residue in the RI sequence) plays a crucial role in stabilizing a bioactive conformational state. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that RI-3 adopts a stable type II β-turn structure in aqueous solution, characterized by a main-chain hydrogen bond between the carbonyl oxygen of the N-terminal D-Tyr residue and the amide hydrogen of the C-terminal D-Arg residue. This turn conformation closely mimics the structural features observed in earlier, potent uPAR/FPR1 antagonists such as UPARANT and RERF, thereby enabling high-affinity interaction with FPR1 despite the inverted backbone. The conformational rigidity imparted by Aib not only enhances binding affinity but also contributes to the overall metabolic stability of the peptide.

The RI peptides of the invention, and RI-3 in particular, exert their biological effects through a well-defined mechanism of action. They competitively bind to FPR1 at or near the orthosteric site normally occupied by agonist ligands such as the uPAR-derived SRSRY peptide or bacterial fMLF. This binding prevents the physical association between uPAR and FPR1, thereby blocking the initiation of downstream signaling cascades. As a consequence, RI-3 inhibits key cellular processes that are dependent on FPR1 activation, including agonist-induced FPR1 internalization, phosphorylation of critical signaling nodes such as Akt, ERK1/2, and p38α, and the subsequent cytoskeletal rearrangements required for directional migration and invasion. Importantly, RI-3 does not act as an agonist; it fails to trigger any of these responses when administered alone, confirming its pure antagonistic profile.

The therapeutic utility of the invention is underpinned by a comprehensive set of in vitro and in vivo data demonstrating the multifaceted anti-metastatic properties of RI-3. In cellular assays, RI-3 potently inhibits the migration and Matrigel invasion of human sarcoma cells (including fibrosarcoma HT1080, osteosarcoma Saos-2, and chondrosarcoma Sarc lines) in a dose-dependent manner, with effective concentrations reaching into the femtomolar range for migration and picomolar range for invasion. This activity is contingent upon the co-expression of both uPAR and FPR1 on the target cells, as evidenced by the reduced efficacy in Saos-2 cells, which express low levels of uPAR. Furthermore, RI-3 effectively blocks the adhesion of tumor cells to endothelial monolayers and their subsequent trans-endothelial migration—critical steps in the intravasation and extravasation phases of metastasis. In endothelial cells, RI-3 suppresses the formation of capillary-like tube networks induced by both fetal bovine serum (FBS) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), indicating a potent anti-angiogenic effect that likely contributes to its in vivo anti-tumor activity.

Crucially, the RI peptides of the invention exhibit exceptional stability in biological fluids. Incubation of RI-3 in human serum for up to 18 hours at 37°C results in minimal loss of inhibitory activity, retaining over 80% of its original potency in cell invasion assays. This remarkable resistance to proteolytic degradation is a direct consequence of the all-D-amino acid composition and the strategic use of non-natural residues like Aib, which collectively render the peptide impervious to the action of endogenous peptidases. In stark contrast, earlier L-peptide antagonists are rapidly degraded under similar conditions, limiting their in vivo applicability.

The in vivo efficacy of RI-3 has been validated in a murine xenograft model of human chondrosarcoma. Daily intraperitoneal administration of RI-3 (6 mg/kg) to nude mice bearing subcutaneous Sarc tumors significantly reduces primary tumor growth, intra-tumoral microvessel density, and the number of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in peripheral blood. Most importantly, RI-3 treatment completely prevents the formation of lung metastases, a common site of sarcoma dissemination, whereas vehicle-treated controls and even mice treated with the first-generation antagonist RERF show detectable metastatic nodules. These findings confirm that RI-3 targets multiple, sequential steps in the metastatic cascade—extracellular matrix invasion, angiogenesis, and vascular intravasation—culminating in a profound anti-metastatic effect in a living organism. Notably, RI-3 is well-tolerated in vivo, with no observable adverse effects on body weight or general health, and it does not impair cell proliferation in vitro, indicating a favorable safety profile.

In summary, the invention provides a breakthrough class of retro-inverso peptides that overcome the historical limitations of peptide-based uPAR/FPR1 inhibitors. By combining the topochemical fidelity of the RI design with the conformational stabilization afforded by non-natural amino acids, these peptides achieve an unprecedented balance of high potency, exceptional metabolic stability, and in vivo efficacy. The lead compound RI-3, in particular, represents a promising candidate for the development of novel anti-metastatic therapies, offering a targeted approach to disrupt the deadly process of cancer spread without compromising normal cellular homeostasis.

## EXAMPLE 1. SYNTHESIS OF THE RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES RI-1, RI-2, RI-3, RI-4, RI-5

The retro-inverso peptides RI-1, RI-2, RI-3, RI-4, and RI-5 were synthesized using standard solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) techniques employing Fmoc (9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl) chemistry on a Rink amide resin to yield C-terminal amide derivatives. All amino acid building blocks used were of the D-configuration, with the exception of the non-natural amino acid α-aminoisobutyric acid (Aib), which is achiral and was incorporated as needed. The synthesis was carried out on an automated peptide synthesizer at a scale of 0.1 mmol per batch. The resin was first swollen in dimethylformamide (DMF) for 30 minutes. The Fmoc protecting group was then removed from the resin-bound linker using a 20% solution of piperidine in DMF for two consecutive deprotection cycles of 5 and 10 minutes, respectively. Following deprotection, the resin was washed thoroughly with DMF.

For the coupling of each D-amino acid, a four-fold excess of the Fmoc-protected D-amino acid (relative to the resin loading) was pre-activated in DMF with equimolar amounts of 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt) and O-(7-azabenzotriazol-1-yl)-N,N,N',N'-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate (HATU) for 2 minutes. This activated ester solution was then added to the resin, and the coupling reaction was allowed to proceed for 45 minutes under gentle agitation. After coupling, the resin was washed with DMF, and the completeness of the reaction was monitored by the Kaiser test; if incomplete, recoupling was performed. This cycle of deprotection and coupling was repeated sequentially for each amino acid in the reverse order of the parent peptide sequence, as dictated by the retro-inverso design principle. For example, to synthesize RI-3 (Ac-(D)-Tyr-Aib-(D)-Arg-NH2), the synthesis began with the coupling of Fmoc-(D)-Arg(Pbf)-OH, followed by Fmoc-Aib-OH, and finally Fmoc-(D)-Tyr(tBu)-OH. The Pbf (2,2,4,6,7-pentamethyldihydrobenzofuran-5-sulfonyl) group was used to protect the guanidino function of arginine, and the tBu (tert-butyl) group protected the phenolic hydroxyl of tyrosine.

Upon completion of the linear chain assembly, the N-terminal Fmoc group was removed as described above. For peptides RI-1, RI-2, and RI-3, an acetyl capping group was introduced by treating the resin with a solution of acetic anhydride and diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) in DMF (1:1:8 v/v) for 30 minutes to yield the N-acetylated derivatives. For peptides RI-4 and RI-5, which are tripeptides designed to mimic the tetrapeptide structure, the N-terminus was capped with an aromatic carboxylic acid instead of acetyl. Specifically, for RI-4 (Phenyl-Acetyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH2), phenylacetic acid was coupled using the same HATU/HOBt activation protocol, and for RI-5 (3-Phenyl-Propionyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH2), 3-phenylpropionic acid was used. This strategy aimed to preserve the hydrophobic and aromatic character of the original C-terminal residue (e.g., Tyr or Phe) while eliminating one amide bond to enhance stability.

After final capping, the fully protected peptides were cleaved from the resin and simultaneously deprotected using a standard cleavage cocktail consisting of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA, 95%), water (2.5%), and triisopropylsilane (TIS, 2.5%) for 2.5 hours at room temperature. The cleaved peptides were then precipitated by adding cold diethyl ether, centrifuged, and the crude peptide pellets were washed twice with cold diethyl ether to remove residual scavengers and organic byproducts. The crude peptides were dissolved in a minimal amount of water/acetonitrile containing 0.1% TFA and lyophilized.

The crude products were purified by preparative reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) on a C18 column (250 × 21.2 mm, 5 μm particle size) using a linear gradient of 5% to 60% acetonitrile in water containing 0.1% TFA over 30 minutes, at a flow rate of 10 mL/min. The eluent was monitored at 220 nm, and the main peak corresponding to the desired product was collected. The purity of the isolated fractions was assessed by analytical RP-HPLC on a C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) using a similar gradient, and fractions with >95% purity were pooled and lyophilized to yield the final peptides as white to off-white powders. The identity and molecular weight of each purified peptide were confirmed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) using electrospray ionization (ESI) in positive ion mode. The theoretical and observed molecular weights for each peptide are as follows: RI-1 (Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-(D)-Glu-(D)-Arg-NH2), theoretical [M+H]+ = 663.7, observed = 664.4; RI-2 (Ac-(D)-Tyr-(D)-Arg-(D)-Ser-(D)-Arg-NH2), theoretical [M+H]+ = 619.7, observed = 620.4; RI-3 (Ac-(D)-Tyr-Aib-(D)-Arg-NH2), theoretical [M+H]+ = 621.3, observed = 622.3; RI-4 (Phenyl-Acetyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH2), theoretical [M+H]+ = 533.1, observed = 533.5; RI-5 (3-Phenyl-Propionyl-D-Arg-Aib-D-Arg-NH2), theoretical [M+H]+ = 547.1, observed = 547.5. All peptides were stored at −20°C under desiccated conditions until use in biological assays.

## EXAMPLE 2. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES DO NOT PROMOTE CELL MIGRATION

To evaluate the potential agonist activity of the retro-inverso peptides, their ability to induce cell migration was assessed using the human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cell line, which expresses high levels of both uPAR and FPR1 on its surface and is known to be highly responsive to FPR1-mediated chemotactic stimuli. The xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analysis (RTCA) system was employed to monitor cell migration dynamically over an 18-hour period. This technology utilizes specialized CIM-plates containing microelectrodes on the underside of an 8-μm pore membrane that separates the upper and lower chambers. As cells migrate through the pores and adhere to the electrode-coated surface, they induce changes in electrical impedance, which are recorded as a dimensionless parameter called the Cell Index, providing a quantitative, real-time measure of migratory activity.

HT1080 cells were serum-starved for 4 hours and then seeded in the upper chamber of the CIM-plate in serum-free medium. The lower chamber was filled with serum-free medium alone (negative control), medium containing 10 nM of the bacterial formyl peptide fMLF (a known potent FPR1 agonist and positive control), or medium containing 10 nM of each of the retro-inverso peptides RI-1, RI-2, RI-3, RI-4, or RI-5. The Cell Index was recorded every 15 minutes for 18 hours. As expected, fMLF induced a robust and sustained increase in the Cell Index, reflecting significant directional migration of HT1080 cells toward the chemoattractant. In stark contrast, none of the retro-inverso peptides elicited any measurable increase in the Cell Index above the baseline level observed in the negative control (serum-free medium alone). The migration curves for all RI peptides overlapped almost perfectly with the negative control curve throughout the entire 18-hour monitoring period.

This result was further corroborated by endpoint Boyden chamber assays, where cells were allowed to migrate for 4 hours toward the same stimuli. After fixation and staining, the number of cells that had migrated to the lower surface of the filter was counted. Consistent with the real-time data, fMLF stimulated a significant increase in the number of migrated cells, while none of the RI peptides produced a statistically significant difference compared to the negative control. These findings conclusively demonstrate that the retro-inverso peptides RI-1 through RI-5 lack intrinsic agonist activity and do not promote cell migration under the tested conditions. This is a critical safety feature, as it confirms that these peptides function as pure antagonists of the uPAR/FPR1 pathway, blocking pathological migration without inducing unintended pro-migratory effects that could potentially exacerbate disease progression. The absence of agonist activity is attributed to the retro-inverso modification, which, while preserving the side-chain topology necessary for receptor binding, alters the backbone conformation in a way that prevents the productive engagement of FPR1 required for G-protein activation and downstream signaling.

## EXAMPLE 3. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 INTERFERES WITH FPR1 BIOLOGICAL ACTIVITY BY BLOCKING AGONIST/FPR1 INTERACTION AND BY PREVENTING AGONIST-INDUCED FPR1 INTERNALIZATION

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which the retro-inverso peptide RI-3 exerts its inhibitory effects, a series of experiments were conducted using rat basophilic leukemia RBL-2H3 cells, which naturally lack FPR1 expression, and a stably transfected derivative, RBL-2H3/ETFR, which overexpresses human FPR1. These cell lines provide a clean genetic background to study FPR1-specific interactions without confounding signals from endogenous receptors.

First, a competitive ligand binding assay was performed to assess the ability of RI-3 to block the binding of a fluorescently labeled FPR1 agonist, FITC-fMLF, to the receptor. RBL-2H3 and RBL-2H3/ETFR cells were pre-incubated for 60 minutes at 4°C (a temperature that prevents receptor internalization) with either diluent (negative control), an excess (100 nM) of unlabeled fMLF (positive control for competition), the previously characterized uPAR/FPR1 antagonist RERF (100 nM), its scrambled control peptide ERFR (100 nM), or RI-3 (100 nM). The cells were then exposed to 10 nM FITC-fMLF for an additional 60 minutes at 4°C, washed, and cell-associated fluorescence was quantified using a fluorescence plate reader. RBL-2H3 cells, which lack FPR1, showed only minimal, non-saturable binding of FITC-fMLF, as expected. In contrast, RBL-2H3/ETFR cells exhibited strong, specific binding of FITC-fMLF. This binding was significantly reduced by pre-incubation with unlabeled fMLF and RERF, confirming their competitive nature. Crucially, pre-incubation with RI-3 reduced FITC-fMLF binding to a similar extent as fMLF and RERF, while the scrambled peptide ERFR had no effect. This demonstrates that RI-3 directly competes with the agonist for binding to the FPR1 ligand-binding site.

Second, to determine whether RI-3 affects the agonist-induced internalization of FPR1—a key step in receptor desensitization and signal termination—binding experiments were conducted at 37°C, a permissive temperature for endocytosis. Cells were pre-treated with the same panel of compounds and then exposed to FITC-fMLF for 30 minutes at 37°C. The cells were fixed and visualized by confocal microscopy. In RBL-2H3/ETFR cells treated with diluent (None), FITC-fMLF was observed as bright green fluorescent puncta distributed throughout the cytoplasm, indicative of robust FPR1 internalization. In cells pre-treated with fMLF, RERF, or RI-3, however, the fluorescence remained predominantly localized to the cell membrane, with a marked reduction in intracellular puncta. Three-dimensional reconstruction of z-stack images confirmed the paucity of internalized receptor in RI-3-treated cells. This indicates that by blocking the initial agonist/receptor interaction, RI-3 prevents the conformational changes in FPR1 that are necessary to trigger its internalization via clathrin-coated pits.

Finally, the impact of RI-3 on downstream FPR1 signaling was examined by assessing the phosphorylation status of Akt, a key effector in the PI3K pathway that is rapidly phosphorylated upon FPR1 activation. RBL-2H3/ETFR cells were stimulated with 10 nM fMLF for 0 to 10 minutes in the presence or absence of 10 nM RI-3. Whole-cell lysates were analyzed by Western blotting using antibodies against phospho-Akt (Ser473) and total Akt. As expected, fMLF stimulation induced a rapid and transient increase in Akt phosphorylation, peaking at around 2-5 minutes. In cells pre-treated with RI-3, this fMLF-induced phosphorylation was consistently and significantly attenuated. RI-3 alone did not induce any detectable Akt phosphorylation, reinforcing its antagonist profile. A broader analysis using a human phospho-kinase array kit revealed that RI-3 also suppressed fMLF-induced phosphorylation of other critical signaling molecules, including p38α (T180/Y182), ERK1/2 (T202/Y204/T185/Y187), CREB (S133), Fgr (Y412), and FAK (Y397). Collectively, these data establish that RI-3 functions as a high-affinity, competitive antagonist of FPR1, blocking the very first step of the signaling cascade—the agonist/receptor interaction—and thereby preventing all subsequent events, including receptor internalization and the activation of multiple downstream kinase pathways that drive cell migration and invasion.

## EXAMPLE 4. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES INHIBIT CELL MIGRATION IN A DOSE DEPENDENT MANNER

The inhibitory potency of the retro-inverso peptides on cell migration was quantitatively evaluated in a dose-response format using the human fibrosarcoma HT1080 cell line. Boyden chamber assays were performed, where cells were placed in the upper chamber in serum-free medium, and the lower chamber contained 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) as a chemoattractant, supplemented with increasing concentrations of each peptide (ranging from 10^−15 M to 10^−6 M). After a 4-hour incubation at 37°C, the number of cells that had migrated through the 8-μm pore polycarbonate filter was counted.

All five retro-inverso peptides (RI-1 to RI-5) demonstrated a dose-dependent inhibition of FBS-directed HT1080 cell migration. The inhibition was negligible at the highest concentration tested (10^−6 M) for all peptides, but became progressively more pronounced as the concentration decreased into the nanomolar and picomolar ranges. The most potent inhibitor was RI-3, which began to show significant inhibition at concentrations as low as 10^−13 M (100 fM). The dose-response curve for RI-3 was sigmoidal, with an apparent IC50 (the concentration causing 50% inhibition of maximal migration) of approximately 1 × 10^−13 M. At 10 nM, RI-3 achieved a maximum inhibition of about 57% relative to the FBS-only control. The other peptides were less potent: RI-1 and RI-2 showed maximum inhibitions of 33% and 29%, respectively, at 10 nM, with their IC50 values in the low nanomolar range. RI-4 and RI-5 were the least effective, with maximum inhibitions of 37% and 28% at 10 nM, respectively.

The superior activity of RI-3 is attributed to its unique structural composition, which includes the non-natural amino acid Aib. This residue is known to strongly favor the formation of turn structures, which, as confirmed by molecular dynamics simulations, allows RI-3 to adopt a stable conformation that optimally presents its side chains for high-affinity interaction with FPR1. The dose-dependent nature of the inhibition, coupled with the extraordinary potency of RI-3, underscores its potential as a highly effective therapeutic agent, as it can exert its biological effects at concentrations far below those that might lead to off-target effects or toxicity. The fact that the inhibition plateaus at a level below 100% is consistent with the notion that FBS contains a mixture of chemoattractants, only some of which signal through the uPAR/FPR1 axis; thus, RI-3 specifically blocks the FPR1-dependent component of the migratory response.

## EXAMPLE 5. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDES INHIBIT CELL INVASION IN A DOSE DEPENDENT MANNER

The ability of the retro-inverso peptides to inhibit tumor cell invasion through a reconstituted basement membrane was assessed using a Matrigel invasion assay, a well-established model for studying the invasive potential of cancer cells. HT1080 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a Boyden chamber, the filter of which was coated with a thin layer of Matrigel, a solubilized basement membrane preparation rich in extracellular matrix proteins like laminin and collagen IV. The lower chamber contained 10% FBS as a chemoattractant, with or without increasing concentrations of the peptides. After a 6-hour incubation, cells that had invaded through the Matrigel and reached the lower surface of the filter were fixed, stained, and counted.

Similar to the migration assays, all retro-inverso peptides inhibited HT1080 cell invasion in a dose-dependent manner. Again, RI-3 was the most potent inhibitor. Its dose-response curve showed that significant inhibition of invasion began at a concentration of 1 × 10^−11 M (10 pM), with an apparent IC50 of approximately 1 × 10^−11 M. At 10 nM, RI-3 achieved a maximum inhibition of about 51% relative to the FBS-only control. The other peptides showed lower efficacy: RI-1 and RI-2 inhibited invasion by 27% and 23%, respectively, at 10 nM, while RI-4 and RI-5 showed 23% and 9% inhibition, respectively.

The real-time kinetics of invasion were also monitored using the xCELLigence RTCA system with E-plates coated with Matrigel. HT1080 cells were seeded on the Matrigel-coated plates in medium containing 10% FBS with or without 10 nM of each peptide, and the Cell Index, which reflects the number of cells that have adhered after crossing the Matrigel, was recorded for 20 hours. The slope of the Cell Index curve, which represents the rate of invasion, was significantly reduced in the presence of all RI peptides, with RI-3 causing the most dramatic decrease. This confirms that the inhibitory effect is not only potent but also sustained over an extended period, which is crucial for a potential therapeutic agent that would need to maintain its activity in a dynamic in vivo environment. The correlation between the potency in migration and invasion assays suggests that the primary mechanism by which these peptides inhibit invasion is by blocking the cell motility machinery, as invasion is a process that inherently requires active migration through a dense matrix.

## EXAMPLE 6. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 DOES NOT AFFECT CELL PROLIFERATION

To rule out the possibility that the observed reductions in cell migration and invasion were secondary to a general cytotoxic or anti-proliferative effect of RI-3, its impact on cell viability and proliferation was directly assessed. Two complementary methods were employed: an MTS colorimetric assay and real-time monitoring of cell proliferation using the xCELLigence RTCA system.

In the MTS assay, HT1080 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The culture medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing either no peptide (control) or 10 μM RI-3—a concentration vastly higher than the effective doses used in migration/invasion assays (which were in the nM to fM range). The medium was refreshed every 24 hours to maintain peptide concentration. At 24, 48, 72, and 96 hours post-treatment, the MTS reagent was added, and the absorbance, which is proportional to the number of metabolically active cells, was measured. No significant difference in absorbance was observed between the RI-3-treated and control groups at any time point, indicating that RI-3 does not impair cellular metabolism or reduce cell numbers.

In parallel, the xCELLigence system was used to monitor the proliferation of HT1080 cells in real-time. Cells were seeded in E-plates at a low density in growth medium with or without 10 μM RI-3. The Cell Index, which increases as cells proliferate and cover more of the electrode surface, was recorded continuously for 85 hours. The proliferation curves for the control and RI-3-treated groups were virtually superimposable, showing identical lag, log, and plateau phases. The doubling times calculated from these curves were also indistinguishable.

These results definitively demonstrate that RI-3 does not affect cell proliferation or viability at concentrations that are orders of magnitude higher than those required for its anti-migratory and anti-invasive effects. This is a critical finding, as it confirms that the anti-metastatic activity of RI-3 is due to a specific blockade of the uPAR/FPR1 signaling axis that governs cell motility, rather than a non-specific, toxic effect on the cells. This selectivity is a major advantage for a potential therapeutic, as it suggests a wide therapeutic window and a lower risk of adverse side effects related to the suppression of normal cell growth.

## EXAMPLE 7. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 IS STABLE IN HUMAN SERUM

The metabolic stability of RI-3 in biologically relevant fluids was a key parameter evaluated, given the historical instability of peptide therapeutics. To assess this, RI-3 was incubated in both bovine serum (as a preliminary screen) and human serum under physiological conditions (37°C) for 18 hours, a timeframe that is sufficient to degrade most conventional peptides. The stability was then inferred by comparing the biological activity of the pre-incubated peptide to that of a freshly prepared sample.

In the first experiment, using the xCELLigence RTCA system, HT1080 cells were allowed to migrate for 18 hours toward 10% FBS in the presence of either 10 nM freshly prepared RI-3 or 10 nM RI-3 that had been pre-incubated at a high concentration (10^−3 M) in bovine serum for 18 hours at 37°C. The resulting Cell Index curves for the two conditions were nearly identical, with overlapping trajectories, indicating that the pre-incubated RI-3 retained full inhibitory activity.

In a more stringent test, the stability in human serum was evaluated using the Boyden chamber Matrigel invasion assay. HT1080 cells were allowed to invade for 6 hours toward 10% FBS in the presence of 10 nM freshly prepared RI-3 or 10 nM RI-3 that had been pre-incubated at 10^−3 M in human serum for 18 hours at 37°C. The freshly prepared RI-3 reduced invasion by 51% compared to the FBS-only control. The pre-incubated RI-3 retained 81% of this inhibitory activity, resulting in a 41% reduction in invasion. This minor loss of activity (19%) is likely attributable to non-specific binding to serum proteins rather than enzymatic degradation, as the all-D-amino acid composition of RI-3 renders it highly resistant to proteolysis by endogenous peptidases, which are stereospecific for L-amino acids.

This exceptional stability is a direct consequence of the retro-inverso design. The inversion of chirality from L to D for all amino acid residues creates a peptide backbone that is unrecognizable to the vast majority of human proteases, which have evolved to cleave peptide bonds between L-amino acids. The incorporation of the non-natural, achiral Aib residue further enhances stability, as it lacks the alpha-hydrogen that is often a target for certain classes of proteases. This remarkable resistance to degradation is a transformative feature that overcomes a major historical limitation of peptide-based drugs and is a key factor that enables the observed in vivo efficacy of RI-3.

## EXAMPLE 8. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 PREVENTS MATRIGEL INVASION BY HUMAN SARCOMA, MELANOMA AND EPITHELIAL OVARIAN CARCINOMA CELLS

To determine the breadth of the anti-invasive activity of RI-3 across different cancer types, its effect was tested on a panel of human cancer cell lines representing diverse histological origins, all of which are known to be highly metastatic and to express the uPAR/FPR1 axis. The cell lines included human fibrosarcoma HT1080, osteosarcoma Saos-2, chondrosarcoma Sarc, melanoma A375, and epithelial ovarian carcinoma SKOV-3.

First, the expression levels of uPAR and FPR1 in these cell lines were confirmed by Western blotting. HT1080 and Sarc cells showed high levels of both receptors. Saos-2 cells expressed high levels of FPR1 but very low levels of uPAR. A375 and SKOV-3 cells also expressed detectable levels of both proteins, though the exact ratios varied. The cells were then subjected to Matrigel invasion assays in the presence of 10% FBS with or without 10 nM RI-3.

RI-3 potently inhibited Matrigel invasion in all cell lines that co-expressed significant levels of both uPAR and FPR1. In HT1080 and Sarc cells, invasion was reduced by 64% and 47%, respectively. In A375 melanoma cells and SKOV-3 ovarian carcinoma cells, RI-3 also caused a significant reduction in invasion, by approximately 55% and 50%, respectively. In contrast, in Saos-2 cells, which have low uPAR expression, the inhibitory effect of RI-3 was markedly attenuated, with only a 24% reduction in invasion. This differential response strongly supports the hypothesis that the anti-invasive activity of RI-3 is specifically dependent on the presence of the uPAR/FPR1 signaling complex. The fact that RI-3 is effective against such a diverse array of cancer cell types—sarcomas, melanoma, and epithelial carcinomas—highlights its potential as a broad-spectrum anti-metastatic agent. This is particularly significant because these cancers often have poor prognoses once they have metastasized, and there is a critical need for therapies that can target the common metastatic pathways they share, rather than being limited to a single cancer type.

## EXAMPLE 9. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 PREVENTS IN VITRO TRANS-ENDOTHELIAL MIGRATION BY SARCOMA, MELANOMA AND EPITHELIAL OVARIAN CARCINOMA CELLS AS WELL AS BY HUMAN MONOCYTES

The process of trans-endothelial migration (TEM), or diapedesis, is a critical step in both the metastatic cascade (for tumor cells) and inflammatory responses (for immune cells like monocytes). To evaluate the effect of RI-3 on this process, an in vitro TEM assay was established using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) grown to confluence on E-plates of the xCELLigence RTCA system to form a tight, electrically resistant monolayer. The integrity of the monolayer was confirmed by a stable, high Cell Index.

Sarc (chondrosarcoma), A375 (melanoma), SKOV-3 (ovarian carcinoma), and human monocytic THP-1 cells were then seeded onto the HUVEC monolayer in the presence or absence of 10 nM RI-3. As these cells attempt to migrate through the endothelial layer, they disrupt the tight junctions and cause a drop in the Cell Index, which is recorded in real-time. The rate of this drop (the slope of the Cell Index curve) is a direct measure of the TEM efficiency.

In all four cell types, the addition of RI-3 significantly reduced the rate of TEM. For Sarc cells, the reduction was 24%. For A375 and SKOV-3 cells, the reductions were 28% and 30%, respectively. Notably, RI-3 also inhibited the TEM of THP-1 monocytes by 25%, which is consistent with the known role of FPR1 in monocyte chemotaxis and suggests that the peptide could also have anti-inflammatory applications. This broad inhibitory effect on TEM across different cell types further underscores the central role of the uPAR/FPR1 axis in this fundamental biological process and reinforces the potential of RI-3 as a therapeutic agent for conditions where pathological TEM is a driver of disease, including cancer metastasis and chronic inflammatory disorders.

## EXAMPLE 10. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 INHIBITS BOTH FBS AND VEGF-TRIGGERED ENDOTHELIAL TUBE FORMATION

Angiogenesis, the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing ones, is a hallmark of cancer that is essential for tumor growth and metastasis. To assess the anti-angiogenic potential of RI-3, its effect on the morphological differentiation of endothelial cells into capillary-like tube networks was tested using HUVECs. The cells were suspended in serum-free medium (EBM) alone (control), EBM with 10% FBS, or EBM with 40 ng/mL of VEGF165 (a potent and specific angiogenic factor), with or without 10 nM RI-3. The cell suspensions were then plated on growth factor-reduced Matrigel, and the formation of tube-like structures was assessed after 6 hours.

In the control condition (EBM alone), HUVECs formed only a few, short, and disconnected cord-like structures. In contrast, both FBS and VEGF165 potently stimulated the formation of an extensive, interconnected network of long, branching tubes. Quantitative analysis, counting the number of tubes longer than 100 μm, showed that FBS and VEGF165 increased tube formation by 352% and 620%, respectively, over the control. The addition of 10 nM RI-3 dramatically suppressed this effect, reducing FBS- and VEGF-induced tube formation by 61% and 75%, respectively. The resulting networks in the presence of RI-3 were fragmented, with many shorter, unconnected segments, resembling the control condition.

This potent anti-angiogenic activity is a direct consequence of RI-3's ability to block FPR1 signaling in endothelial cells. It is well-established that FPR1 is expressed on endothelial cells and that its activation by various ligands, including uPAR-derived peptides, promotes angiogenesis by stimulating endothelial cell migration, proliferation, and tube formation. By antagonizing FPR1, RI-3 disrupts this pro-angiogenic signaling, thereby starving the tumor of its necessary blood supply. This effect is a key component of RI-3's overall anti-tumor and anti-metastatic activity, as demonstrated by the reduced intra-tumoral microvessel density observed in the in vivo mouse model.

## EXAMPLE 11. RETRO-INVERSO PEPTIDE RI-3 EXERTS ANTI-METASTATIC EFFECT REDUCING IN VIVO TUMOR GROWTH AND VASCULAR INFILTRATION BY SARCOMA CELLS

The ultimate test of the therapeutic potential of RI-3 was its evaluation in a preclinical in vivo model of human sarcoma. Chondrosarcoma Sarc cells, which stably express Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP) for easy tracking, were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of immunocompromised Foxn1nu/nu female nude mice. The mice were then randomized into treatment groups that received daily intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of either 6 mg/kg RI-3 or an equivalent volume of vehicle (PBS) for 10 days.

Tumor growth was monitored by caliper measurements. After 10 days, the average tumor volume in the vehicle-treated group was 330 ± 163 mm³, while in the RI-3-treated group, it was significantly reduced to 185 ± 82 mm³. This reduction in tumor growth is not due to a direct cytotoxic effect on the cancer cells, as RI-3 does not affect their proliferation in vitro. Instead, it is attributed to the anti-angiogenic activity of RI-3, which was confirmed by immunohistochemical analysis of excised tumors. Staining for the endothelial marker CD31 revealed that tumors from RI-3-treated mice had significantly shorter microvessels and a lower microvessel density compared to vehicle-treated controls, consistent with the in vitro tube formation data.

More importantly, RI-3 treatment profoundly impacted the metastatic process. Analysis of peripheral blood for circulating tumor cells (CTCs) using a highly sensitive Alu-sequence PCR assay showed that 8 out of 8 vehicle-treated mice had detectable CTCs (average 10.04 ± 3.7 CTCs/mL), while only 4 out of 8 RI-3-treated mice had detectable CTCs (average 2.6 ± 2.7 CTCs/mL), with the other 4 showing no detectable CTCs. In a separate experiment designed to assess lung metastasis, mice were treated for 12 days and their lungs were examined ex vivo using a MacroFluo fluorescence stereomicroscope. Multiple GFP-positive metastatic nodules were found in the lungs of 3 out of 5 vehicle-treated mice, but in none of the 5 RI-3-treated mice. Histological examination of lung sections from the control group confirmed the presence of micrometastases.

In a head-to-head comparison with the first-generation antagonist RERF, RI-3 was shown to be more effective at reducing tumor growth and, critically, was the only agent that completely prevented lung metastasis formation. These in vivo results provide compelling evidence that RI-3 is a potent anti-metastatic agent that works by targeting multiple, sequential steps in the metastatic cascade: it inhibits local invasion, blocks angiogenesis to limit primary tumor growth and vascular access, and prevents the entry of tumor cells into the circulation and their subsequent colonization of distant organs. Its excellent tolerability, as evidenced by stable body weight and no overt signs of toxicity, further supports its potential for clinical translation.
