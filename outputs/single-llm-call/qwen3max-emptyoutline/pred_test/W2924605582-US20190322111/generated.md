## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of functional printing and printed electronics, and more specifically to a method for producing aligned ferromagnetic nickel nanowires directly on a substrate through the thermal reduction of a nickel precursor ink in the presence of a magnetic field. The invention further pertains to the formulation of a stable, homogeneous nickel precursor ink suitable for aerosol-based and other non-contact printing techniques, and to the resulting nanostructured nickel films exhibiting enhanced anisotropic electrical and magnetic properties. The disclosed technology enables the direct, template-free, single-step fabrication of oriented nickel nanowire arrays on planar and non-planar substrates under ambient conditions, without requiring post-processing or purification steps, and is particularly advantageous for applications in magnetic sensors, data storage, printed antennas, and other functional electronic and magneto-optic devices.

## BACKGROUND

The advancement of printed electronics has been significantly driven by the development of functional inks that can be deposited using scalable, additive manufacturing techniques such as inkjet, aerosol jet, screen printing, and flexography. Among these, metallic inks play a critical role in forming conductive traces, electrodes, and electromagnetic components. Traditionally, silver and copper have dominated the landscape of conductive inks due to their high electrical conductivity and relative ease of processing. However, for applications requiring magnetic functionality—such as magnetic sensors, inductors, memory elements, and electromagnetic shielding—ferromagnetic metals like nickel (Ni), iron (Fe), and cobalt (Co) are essential. Despite their importance, the printing of ferromagnetic metals has remained a significant technological challenge, primarily due to issues related to oxidation, ink stability, and the difficulty of achieving controlled nanostructures with desired magnetic anisotropy.

Two primary classes of metallic inks have emerged: nanoparticle-based inks and precursor-based inks, also known as metal-organic decomposition (MOD) inks. Nanoparticle inks consist of colloidal suspensions of pre-synthesized metal particles dispersed in a carrier solvent. While these inks can achieve high metal loading—often exceeding 30 wt%—they suffer from inherent instability. Over time, nanoparticles tend to agglomerate due to van der Waals forces, leading to increased viscosity, nozzle clogging during printing, and inconsistent film morphology. Furthermore, in aerosol-based printing systems, partial evaporation of the carrier solvent during atomization can alter the ink’s rheological properties mid-process, compromising print fidelity. Nickel nanoparticle inks have been developed, but their synthesis typically requires inert atmospheres to prevent oxidation, and their deposition often necessitates additional alignment or templating steps to achieve anisotropic structures.

In contrast, MOD inks are molecular solutions comprising organometallic complexes dissolved in a solvent. These inks are inherently stable because they lack solid particles, thereby eliminating agglomeration and sedimentation issues. They offer excellent printability and can be tailored for a wide range of printing techniques by adjusting solvent composition and additives. However, MOD inks historically suffer from low metal content—typically below 10 wt%—due to the large organic ligand-to-metal ratio required for solubility and stability. This low metal loading results in porous, low-conductivity films after thermal decomposition unless multiple layers are printed. Moreover, the thermal reduction of nickel precursors is particularly challenging because nickel is highly susceptible to oxidation upon exposure to air during or after the curing process. Previous attempts to formulate nickel MOD inks have often resulted in oxidized residues or incomplete reduction, limiting their practical utility.

A separate but related challenge lies in the fabrication of aligned nanowire structures. Nickel nanowires exhibit high shape anisotropy, which translates into enhanced coercivity and directional magnetic properties—features highly desirable for magnetic data storage and giant magnetoresistive sensors. Conventional methods for producing aligned nickel nanowires rely on multi-step processes involving hard templates (e.g., anodic aluminum oxide membranes), electrochemical deposition, and subsequent template removal. These approaches are not only labor-intensive and costly but also incompatible with direct printing on arbitrary substrates. Alternative techniques such as electrospinning or magnetic field-assisted assembly of pre-formed nanowires require additional handling and often introduce non-functional binders or surfactants that degrade electrical performance.

Recent efforts have explored the use of external fields—electric, magnetic, or thermal gradients—to guide nanostructure formation during ink curing. However, no prior art has demonstrated the *in situ* formation of aligned nickel nanowires directly from a homogeneous precursor ink during thermal reduction in the presence of a magnetic field. Such a process would represent a paradigm shift, enabling the direct writing of anisotropic magnetic circuits with spatially controlled orientation, all within a single, integrated printing-and-curing step. The ability to produce pure, dense, aligned nickel nanowires without templates, binders, or post-processing would address long-standing limitations in printed magnetics and open new avenues for flexible, lightweight, and conformal magnetic devices.

Existing aerosol jet printing systems, while capable of printing on non-planar surfaces and with high resolution, have not been successfully employed to produce aligned ferromagnetic nanowires from a precursor ink. Prior printed nickel structures have either been granular polycrystalline films lacking anisotropy or required complex post-deposition treatments. There remains a critical unmet need for a robust, scalable, and ambient-compatible method to directly print and cure aligned nickel nanowires with enhanced electrical and magnetic properties, using a stable, high-performance ink formulation that is compatible with industrial printing platforms.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and industrially viable method for the direct fabrication of aligned nickel nanowires on a substrate through the thermal reduction of a specially formulated nickel precursor ink in the presence of a magnetic field. This method overcomes the longstanding challenges associated with printing ferromagnetic metals by combining a stable, homogeneous ink chemistry with a field-directed curing process that simultaneously reduces the metal precursor and induces nanowire alignment. The invention eliminates the need for templates, surfactants, or post-processing steps, yielding pure, dense, and highly anisotropic nickel nanostructures with superior electrical and magnetic properties.

In one aspect, the invention provides a nickel precursor ink comprising a nickel formate-ethylenediamine complex dissolved in an aqueous solvent system, optionally containing rheology modifiers such as polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and surfactants such as BYK 333 to enhance printability and substrate wetting. The ink is homogeneous, stable under ambient conditions, and exhibits rheological properties suitable for aerosol jet, inkjet, and other non-contact printing techniques. The nickel content of the ink is approximately 5.7 wt%, which, while lower than nanoparticle inks, is sufficient to form continuous conductive films after thermal processing.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method of printing the nickel precursor ink onto a substrate using a non-contact printing technique, such as aerosol jet printing, to form a wet film. The substrate may be rigid (e.g., glass, silicon) or flexible (e.g., polyimide, PET), and may be pre-treated with plasma to enhance wettability. The printed film is then subjected to a thermal reduction process at a temperature of approximately 240°C for 15 minutes. Crucially, this thermal reduction is performed in the presence of a homogeneous magnetic field of at least 250 Oe, applied in a desired direction. During heating, the nickel formate-ethylenymine complex decomposes, releasing volatile byproducts (including carbon oxides and hydrogen) and reducing nickel ions to elemental nickel. The nascent nickel nanoparticles, formed during decomposition, are mobile at the processing temperature and align along the direction of the applied magnetic field due to magnetic dipole interactions. Subsequent sintering fuses these aligned nanoparticles into continuous, high-aspect-ratio nanowires oriented parallel to the magnetic field lines.

In the absence of a magnetic field, the same thermal reduction process yields a porous, isotropic nickel film composed of randomly distributed nanoparticles with significant necking but no long-range order. In contrast, the magnetic field-directed process produces a fibrous network of aligned nanowires, each composed of fused 100–250 nm nickel crystallites, resembling a “string of pearls” morphology. The degree of alignment can be quantitatively assessed using two-dimensional fast Fourier transform (2D FFT) analysis of scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images, which reveals sharp intensity peaks corresponding to the alignment direction.

In yet another aspect, the invention enables the fabrication of complex magnetic architectures, such as nanowire grids, by sequentially printing and curing multiple layers with orthogonal magnetic field orientations. For example, a first layer is cured in a magnetic field oriented along the X-axis, and a second layer is printed on top and cured in a magnetic field oriented along the Y-axis. This results in a cross-hatched nanowire grid structure that exhibits nearly isotropic electrical conductivity and enhanced remanent magnetization in both directions, surpassing the performance of unaligned films.

The resulting aligned nickel nanowire films exhibit significantly enhanced anisotropic properties. Electrical resistivity is lowest along the nanowire axis, with values as low as 560 µΩ·cm (approximately 80 times the resistivity of bulk nickel) observed in nanowire grid structures. Magnetic characterization via vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) reveals strong shape anisotropy: films cured in a magnetic field show a remanent magnetization of 20 emu/g parallel to the nanowire axis and only 7 emu/g perpendicular to it, compared to 10 emu/g in both directions for unaligned films. The saturation magnetization remains consistent at approximately 30 emu/g, comparable to bulk nickel, confirming the high purity and minimal oxidation of the reduced material.

The invention is notable for its simplicity, scalability, and compatibility with ambient processing conditions. The entire process—from ink formulation to final nanowire formation—can be conducted in air, without the need for gloveboxes or reducing atmospheres, because the decomposition of nickel formate generates a self-reducing environment through the release of hydrogen. The volatile organic byproducts evaporate completely, leaving no carbonaceous residue. This template-free, single-step approach represents the first demonstration of thermally induced, magnetic field-directed nanowire formation directly on a substrate from a molecular precursor ink.

Applications of the invention include, but are not limited to, printed magnetic sensors, anisotropic conductive interconnects, reconfigurable antennas, magnetic data storage media, magneto-optic polarizers, and skin-attachable acoustic devices. The ability to spatially control nanowire orientation through localized magnetic field application further enables the design of functionally graded magnetic circuits with region-specific properties.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for producing aligned nickel nanowires on a substrate through the thermal reduction of a nickel precursor ink in the presence of a magnetic field, as well as to the ink formulation and resulting nanostructured materials. The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention but is not intended to limit the scope of the claims, which are defined by the appended claims and their equivalents.

### Ink Formulation and Properties

The nickel precursor ink of the present invention is a homogeneous solution comprising a nickel formate-ethylenediamine complex as the active metal source. Nickel formate dihydrate (Ni(HCOO)₂·2H₂O), with a molecular weight of 148.74 g/mol, serves as the nickel salt due to its relatively low decomposition temperature and the ability of the formate anion to act as a built-in reducing agent. Ethylenediamine (H₂NCH₂CH₂NH₂), a bidentate ligand, is used to chelate the nickel ion, forming a stable, water-soluble complex that enhances the reduction efficiency and prevents premature precipitation. The use of ethylenediamine also increases the solubility of the complex in aqueous media, enabling the formulation of inks with controlled viscosity and surface tension.

In a representative embodiment, the ink is prepared by dissolving 0.72 mL (10.77 mmol) of ethylenediamine in 4 mL of distilled water, followed by the addition of 0.8 g (5.38 mmol) of nickel formate. The mixture is stirred at room temperature for 15 minutes to ensure complete dissolution and complex formation. The resulting solution is then filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter to remove any undissolved particulates or aggregates that could interfere with printing. To optimize the ink for aerosol jet printing, optional additives are included: 1% (w/w) polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, MW 3500, K12 grade) is added as a rheology modifier to stabilize the ink and prevent dewetting, and 20 µL of a 10% (v/v) aqueous solution of BYK 333 surfactant is added to reduce surface tension and improve substrate wetting.

The final ink exhibits a surface tension of 44.01 mN/m and a viscosity of 3.2 cP, as measured by pendant drop tensiometry and microviscometry, respectively. These rheological properties fall within the optimal range for aerosol jet printing, which typically requires viscosities between 1–20 cP and surface tensions between 30–60 mN/m. The ink is stable under ambient conditions for at least 30 days, with no observable precipitation or phase separation, owing to its molecular nature and the stabilizing effect of the ethylenediamine ligand.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA) reveal that the ink undergoes a two-stage thermal decomposition. The first stage, occurring below 100°C, corresponds to the evaporation of water and residual solvent. The second stage, centered at 235°C, involves the reduction of nickel ions to elemental nickel, accompanied by the release of carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and hydrogen gas. The DTA curve shows a distinct endothermic peak at 235°C, confirming the energy requirement for the reduction reaction. The final nickel content, calculated from the TGA residue, is 5.7 wt%, which is sufficient to form electrically percolating networks after thermal processing.

### Printing and Substrate Preparation

The ink is deposited onto a substrate using a non-contact printing technique, with aerosol jet printing being the preferred method due to its ability to print on non-planar surfaces and with high resolution. In a typical printing setup, the ink is ultrasonically atomized into a polydisperse aerosol of droplets ranging from 0.5 to 5 µm in diameter. The aerosol is then transported by a carrier gas (e.g., air or nitrogen) through a series of aerodynamic focusing lenses that concentrate the droplet stream into a narrow beam. A sheath gas flow surrounds the aerosol stream to prevent nozzle clogging and to further focus the deposition. The substrate is mounted on a precision motion stage that moves in the X-Y plane, while the print head is positioned at a fixed standoff distance of 5 mm above the substrate.

Substrates may include glass microscope slides, silicon wafers, flexible polymers (e.g., polyimide, PET), or even three-dimensional objects. Prior to printing, substrates are cleaned with isopropyl alcohol to remove organic contaminants and may be treated with atmospheric plasma to increase surface energy and improve ink adhesion. A single printed layer typically results in a wet film thickness of approximately 4 µm, which reduces to about 1–2 µm after drying and curing due to solvent evaporation and densification.

### Thermal Reduction and Magnetic Field Alignment

The key innovation of the present invention lies in the thermal reduction process, which is conducted in the presence of a homogeneous magnetic field. After printing, the substrate is placed on a heated stage (e.g., a benchtop hot plate or a convection oven) and heated to a temperature of 240°C for 15 minutes. Simultaneously, a magnetic field of at least 250 Oe is applied in a predetermined direction using permanent magnets or electromagnets arranged to produce a uniform field across the printed area.

During heating, the nickel formate-ethylenediamine complex decomposes according to the following generalized reaction:

Ni(HCOO)₂·2H₂O + en → Ni⁰ + CO↑ + CO₂↑ + H₂↑ + H₂O↑ + other volatiles

where “en” denotes ethylenediamine. The released hydrogen creates a locally reducing atmosphere that prevents oxidation of the nascent nickel nanoparticles. At temperatures near 240°C, the nickel nanoparticles (100–250 nm in size) possess sufficient surface mobility to migrate and coalesce. In the presence of the magnetic field, each nanoparticle develops a magnetic moment that aligns with the field direction. Dipole-dipole interactions between adjacent nanoparticles promote their assembly into linear chains along the field lines. As the temperature is maintained, these chains sinter into continuous, high-aspect-ratio nanowires.

In the absence of a magnetic field, the same thermal treatment yields a porous, granular film where nanoparticles sinter randomly, resulting in isotropic electrical and magnetic properties. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) confirms that magnetic field-directed reduction produces aligned nanowires, while field-free reduction produces a random network of necked particles.

Quantitative assessment of alignment is achieved through 2D FFT analysis of SEM images. Each image is converted to grayscale and processed using the Oval Profile plugin in ImageJ software, which models nanowires as elongated ovals and maps their orientation distribution. Aligned samples exhibit sharp intensity peaks at angles corresponding to the magnetic field direction (e.g., 90° and 270°), while unaligned samples show a uniform circular distribution indicative of randomness.

### Multilayer and Grid Structures

The invention further enables the fabrication of multilayer structures with controlled interlayer orientation. For instance, a first layer is printed and cured in a magnetic field oriented along the X-axis, producing nanowires aligned in that direction. A second layer of ink is then printed on top of the first and cured in a magnetic field oriented along the Y-axis, resulting in a cross-hatched nanowire grid. This architecture combines the anisotropic properties of individual layers into a quasi-isotropic conductor with enhanced connectivity.

Electrical characterization using a four-point probe shows that the nanowire grid exhibits the lowest resistivity (560 µΩ·cm) among all tested configurations, attributed to the interconnected network that minimizes voids and provides multiple conduction paths. Magnetic hysteresis loops reveal higher remanent magnetization in both X and Y directions compared to unaligned films, demonstrating the synergistic effect of the grid structure.

### Electrical and Magnetic Properties

The aligned nickel nanowire films exhibit pronounced anisotropy in both electrical and magnetic domains. Electrical resistivity is lowest along the nanowire axis due to continuous metallic pathways and highest perpendicular to it due to inter-wire gaps. For single-layer aligned films, the resistivity parallel to the wires is approximately 30% lower than perpendicular. In nanowire grids, the difference is minimized, yielding nearly isotropic conductivity.

Magnetic properties are dominated by shape anisotropy. VSM measurements show that aligned films have a remanent magnetization of 20 emu/g parallel to the wires and 7 emu/g perpendicular, compared to 10 emu/g in both directions for unaligned films. The coercive field is also higher along the wire axis, consistent with the expected behavior of elongated magnetic domains. Saturation magnetization remains at 30 emu/g for all samples, confirming the high purity and minimal oxide content of the reduced nickel.

### EXAMPLE 1

A nickel precursor ink was prepared by dissolving 0.72 mL of ethylenediamine in 4 mL of distilled water, followed by the addition of 0.8 g of nickel formate dihydrate. The mixture was stirred for 15 minutes at room temperature and filtered through a 0.2 µm syringe filter. Additives were then introduced: 1% (w/w) PVP (MW 3500) and 20 µL of a 10% (v/v) aqueous solution of BYK 333 surfactant. The ink exhibited a viscosity of 3.2 cP and a surface tension of 44.01 mN/m.

Glass microscope slides were cleaned with isopropyl alcohol and treated with atmospheric plasma for 60 seconds to enhance wettability. The ink was printed onto the slides using a NanoJet aerosol printer with a 25-gauge dispensing tip, a standoff distance of 5 mm, and a translational speed of 2 mm/s, resulting in 1 cm × 1 cm square patterns with a wet film thickness of ~4 µm.

Two sets of samples were prepared. Set A was cured at 240°C for 15 minutes in the absence of a magnetic field. Set B was cured under identical thermal conditions but in the presence of a homogeneous magnetic field of 250 Oe oriented along the Y-axis. A third set, Set C, consisted of two layers: the first layer cured in a 250 Oe field along Y, and the second layer cured in a 250 Oe field along X.

SEM imaging revealed that Set A exhibited a porous, granular morphology with randomly distributed 100–250 nm particles. Set B showed aligned nanowires oriented along Y, each composed of fused nanoparticles. Set C displayed a cross-hatched grid structure. 2D FFT analysis confirmed strong alignment in Sets B and C, with peaks at 90°/270° and 0°/90°/180°/270°, respectively.

Electrical resistivity measurements showed values of 850 µΩ·cm (Set A), 720 µΩ·cm parallel to wires and 950 µΩ·cm perpendicular (Set B), and 560 µΩ·cm in both directions (Set C). VSM hysteresis loops indicated remanent magnetizations of 10 emu/g (Set A), 20 emu/g (parallel) and 7 emu/g (perpendicular) for Set B, and 18 emu/g in both directions for Set C. All samples exhibited a saturation magnetization of 30 emu/g.

These results demonstrate the successful implementation of the invention, yielding aligned nickel nanowires with enhanced anisotropic properties through a simple, scalable, and ambient-compatible process.
