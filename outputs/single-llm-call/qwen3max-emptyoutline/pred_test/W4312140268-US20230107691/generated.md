# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of biometric monitoring has undergone significant transformation in recent decades, driven by the increasing availability of digital health technologies (DHTs) that enable the collection of physiological data from individuals in both clinical and everyday settings. These technologies, which include wearable devices, implantable sensors, and lab-on-a-chip nanosensor platforms, have expanded the scope of physiological measurement beyond traditional clinical environments. However, despite the proliferation of such devices, healthcare systems remain largely reactive, intervening only after disease is diagnosed rather than proactively preventing its onset or progression. This reactive paradigm limits the potential for early intervention and assumes a uniformity in disease trajectories and treatment responses that does not reflect the biological diversity of human populations. Consequently, there is a pressing need for continuous, accurate, and non-invasive monitoring solutions that can bridge the gap between consumer-grade convenience and medical-grade precision.

Cardiovascular monitoring represents a critical domain within biometric surveillance, as cardiovascular diseases are among the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Traditional methods for assessing cardiac function, such as electrocardiography (ECG), provide high-fidelity measurements of electrical activity associated with heartbeats and are widely regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing arrhythmias and other cardiac conditions. However, ECG systems are typically confined to clinical or hospital settings due to their size, cost, and requirement for trained personnel. In contrast, consumer wearable devices—such as smartwatches and activity trackers—have attempted to bring cardiac monitoring into daily life through techniques like photoplethysmography (PPG) and simplified ECG. While these devices offer convenience and accessibility, they often suffer from limitations in accuracy, particularly in beat-to-beat interval measurements, with error rates as high as 10% in heart rate estimation alone. Moreover, design constraints related to power consumption, memory capacity, and data storage further restrict their ability to deliver continuous, high-resolution physiological data suitable for clinical decision-making.

The ear presents a uniquely advantageous anatomical site for cardiovascular monitoring. Positioned in close proximity to major vascular structures—including the carotid artery and internal jugular vein—the ear canal is subject to subtle mechanical and acoustic perturbations generated by hemodynamic activity. These perturbations manifest as low-frequency pressure fluctuations, often in the infrasonic range (below 20 Hz), which are imperceptible to human hearing but detectable with sensitive acoustic transducers. Importantly, when the ear canal is occluded by an earbud or similar device, the enclosed volume acts as a resonant cavity that amplifies these infrasonic signals, enhancing their detectability without interference from audible-range audio playback. This physical principle enables the coexistence of high-fidelity biosignal acquisition and conventional audio functionality within a single in-ear form factor—a feature not achievable with most existing wearable platforms.

Despite these advantages, prior art has not fully exploited the ear’s potential for continuous, medical-grade cardiovascular monitoring. Existing in-ear devices primarily focus on audio delivery or basic motion tracking, with limited integration of physiological sensing capabilities. Some research prototypes have explored the use of microphones in earbuds to capture heart sounds or pulse-related vibrations, but these efforts have generally lacked the signal processing sophistication, validation against clinical standards, or robustness under real-world conditions necessary for reliable medical application. Furthermore, no known system has demonstrated the ability to accurately measure interbeat intervals (IBIs) and heart rate variability (HRV)—key indicators of autonomic nervous system function and cardiac health—across diverse physiological states, including both regular sinus rhythm and irregular rhythms such as atrial fibrillation (AF).

Atrial fibrillation, the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, poses a significant public health challenge due to its association with stroke, heart failure, and cognitive decline. Early and accurate detection of AF is crucial for initiating appropriate anticoagulation therapy and reducing adverse outcomes. However, paroxysmal AF—episodes that occur intermittently and may be asymptomatic—remains difficult to diagnose with conventional intermittent ECG monitoring. Continuous, long-term monitoring solutions are therefore essential, yet current wearable technologies often fail to provide the sensitivity and specificity required for reliable AF detection, particularly in ambulatory settings where motion artifacts and environmental noise can degrade signal quality.

In light of these limitations, there exists a clear and unmet need for a biometric monitoring system that combines the comfort, wearability, and ubiquity of consumer earbuds with the precision, reliability, and clinical validity of medical-grade cardiac monitoring. Such a system must be capable of capturing high-fidelity infrasonic signals related to vascular hemodynamics, processing these signals in real time to extract clinically relevant metrics such as IBI, HR, and HRV, and distinguishing between normal and pathological cardiac rhythms—including AF—with performance comparable to ECG. Additionally, the system should support seamless integration with cloud-based infrastructure for continuous data streaming, storage, and advanced analytics, thereby enabling scalable, population-level health monitoring and early disease detection. The present invention addresses these needs by introducing a novel method and apparatus for in-ear infrasonic hemodynography (IH), which leverages the unique acoustic properties of the occluded ear canal to achieve continuous, accurate, and non-invasive cardiovascular monitoring.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and system for in-ear infrasonic hemodynography (IH) that enables continuous, accurate, and non-invasive monitoring of cardiovascular activity through the detection and analysis of low-frequency acoustic biosignals generated by vascular hemodynamics. The system comprises a pair of earbuds, each containing at least one passive acoustic sensor configured to detect infrasonic pressure fluctuations within the occluded ear canal, and a controller unit in communication with the earbuds for signal acquisition, processing, and transmission. The earbuds are designed to maintain a sealed acoustic environment when inserted into the ear, thereby amplifying infrasonic biosignals while allowing concurrent playback of audible-range audio without interference. The invention further includes a signal processing pipeline that transforms raw acoustic data into clinically meaningful cardiovascular metrics, including interbeat intervals (IBIs), heart rate (HR), heart rate variability (HRV), and cardiac rhythm classification.

In one embodiment, the IH system captures biosignals in the frequency range of 0 to 20 Hz, corresponding to the infrasonic spectrum, which are produced by mechanical vibrations associated with cardiac contractions, valve movements, and arterial pulse wave propagation. These signals are distinct from audible sounds and are not perceptible to the user, ensuring that biosignal acquisition does not compromise audio experience. The acoustic sensors are passive, requiring no active transmission components, which minimizes power consumption and extends battery life. Data from the sensors is transmitted via a wired or wireless connection—such as Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE)—to a controller, which may be integrated into a neckband, clip, or mobile device, and subsequently relayed to a cloud-based infrastructure for secure storage and advanced analytics.

The invention further encompasses a multi-level signal processing pipeline that operates in real time to extract vital signs from the raw IH data. This pipeline includes: (1) raw data acquisition from left and right earbuds; (2) calibration and filtering to remove audible audio components and environmental noise; (3) data quality assessment using a machine learning-based classifier to distinguish cardiac signals from motion artifacts; (4) detection of cardiac cycle events through adaptive peak identification in the IH waveform; and (5) computation of cardiovascular metrics such as IBI, HR, and HRV, with fusion of data from both ears to enhance reliability. The system is capable of detecting prominent features in the IH waveform that correspond to specific hemodynamic events, such as aortic valve opening and closing, offering insights beyond electrical activity measured by ECG.

A key aspect of the invention is its demonstrated high correlation with gold-standard ECG measurements across diverse physiological conditions. In clinical studies involving subjects in sinus rhythm (SR) and atrial fibrillation (AF), the IH system achieved Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.988 for IBI and 0.994 for HR in SR subjects, with mean differences of 0.05 ms and 0.03 bpm, respectively. These results were consistent across varying breathing patterns—including normal breathing, music-listening, and resonant breathing—demonstrating robustness to physiological perturbations and external auditory stimuli. In AF subjects, characterized by highly irregular IBIs, the system maintained a high correlation (r = 0.99) with ECG over an extended IBI range (400–1600 ms), confirming its applicability to pathological cardiac rhythms.

Moreover, the invention enables accurate discrimination between SR and AF through a machine learning-based classification algorithm. A random forest classifier, trained on over 200,000 segments of publicly available ECG data from the PhysioNet database, was applied without modification to both ECG and IH data from the study cohort. The algorithm achieved sensitivity and specificity of at least 0.99 for AF detection in both modalities, with only minor misclassifications observed in the IH data (five SR segments misclassified as AF, four AF segments as SR). This demonstrates that the temporal structure of IBI sequences extracted from IH signals contains sufficient information to support clinical-grade rhythm classification, comparable to ECG.

The IH system is further distinguished by its potential for long-term, ambulatory monitoring. The earbud form factor ensures high user compliance due to comfort and familiarity, supporting both daytime and nighttime use. The integration with cloud infrastructure allows for continuous data streaming without local storage limitations, enabling real-time analytics, trend detection, and closed-loop biofeedback applications. Future implementations may incorporate motion sensors to enhance artifact rejection and expand arrhythmia detection capabilities to include a broader spectrum of cardiac conditions.

In summary, the present invention provides a transformative approach to cardiovascular monitoring by harnessing the acoustic properties of the occluded ear canal to capture high-fidelity infrasonic biosignals. It bridges the gap between consumer wearables and medical devices, offering a platform that is simultaneously convenient, accurate, and clinically validated. The system supports comprehensive assessment of cardiac function, including beat-to-beat dynamics, autonomic regulation, and arrhythmia detection, thereby enabling proactive, personalized, and preventive healthcare strategies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a system and method for in-ear infrasonic hemodynography (IH), which utilizes acoustic sensors embedded within earbuds to detect and analyze low-frequency pressure fluctuations in the occluded ear canal that are generated by cardiovascular activity. The following detailed description sets forth the preferred embodiments of the invention, including the structural components, operational principles, signal processing methodologies, and clinical validation results that collectively enable continuous, accurate, and non-invasive cardiovascular monitoring.

The core hardware architecture of the IH system consists of a pair of earbuds, each housing at least one passive acoustic sensor, and a controller unit that manages data acquisition, processing, and communication. Each earbud is designed to fit securely within the concha and ear canal of the user, creating an airtight seal that isolates the internal cavity from external environmental noise. This seal is critical to the system’s operation, as it transforms the ear canal into a closed acoustic volume that significantly amplifies infrasonic biosignals. According to the principles of acoustics, the dynamic pressure \( P_\Delta \) within a sealed cavity is inversely proportional to its volume \( V \), as described by the relationship \( P_\Delta \propto 1/V \) under constant temperature conditions. Given that the average volume of the human ear canal is approximately 2 cubic centimeters when occluded, compared to an open-canal effective volume on the order of hundreds of cubic centimeters, this occlusion can amplify infrasonic pressure signals by up to 40 decibels. This amplification brings otherwise imperceptible hemodynamic vibrations into the measurable range of commercially available microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) microphones, which serve as the passive acoustic sensors in the preferred embodiment.

The acoustic sensors are positioned within the front cavity of each earbud, adjacent to the speaker driver, in a configuration analogous to the feedback microphone used in active noise cancellation (ANC) systems. This placement is advantageous because it is already standard in many consumer earbuds, facilitating seamless integration of IH technology into existing manufacturing processes. The sensors are passive in nature, meaning they do not emit any energy but instead transduce ambient pressure variations into electrical signals. This design minimizes power consumption, a critical consideration for wearable devices intended for extended use. In the prototype described herein, the sensors operate with a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz, providing sufficient temporal resolution to capture the rapid dynamics of cardiac-related infrasound.

Each earbud is connected to a controller unit, which in the initial prototype is housed in a neckband-style enclosure containing a printed circuit board assembly (PCBA), a rechargeable battery, and a Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) radio module. The connection between the earbuds and the controller may be implemented via flexible cables, as in the prototype, or through fully wireless intra-aural communication in future embodiments. The controller serves as the central hub for signal conditioning, analog-to-digital conversion, and data transmission. During operation, the raw analog signals from the left and right acoustic sensors are digitized and synchronized using a common system clock, ensuring precise temporal alignment between channels. This synchronization is essential for subsequent signal fusion and artifact rejection algorithms.

In the clinical validation setup, the controller was connected via a USB cable to a laptop computer, which acted as a data acquisition (DAQ) device to simultaneously record IH and ECG signals. The ECG was obtained using a medical-grade three-lead system (GE Transport Pro), with electrodes placed on the left chest, right chest, and left leg to produce a Lead-II configuration. Both IH and ECG signals were sampled at 1000 Hz and time-synchronized at the hardware level, enabling direct comparison of cardiac events. In the commercial implementation, however, the controller communicates directly with a mobile device via BLE, which then transmits the encrypted data to a cloud infrastructure using a secure messaging protocol such as Message Queue Telemetry Transport (MQTT). This cloud-based architecture eliminates local storage and computational constraints, allowing for continuous, real-time data collection and analysis without interruption for battery recharging or memory management.

The signal processing pipeline of the IH system is structured into five hierarchical levels, each performing a distinct function in the transformation of raw acoustic data into clinically actionable insights. Level 1 involves the acquisition of raw time-series data from the left and right earbuds. At this stage, the signal may contain a mixture of cardiac biosignals, motion artifacts (e.g., from walking, head movement, or jaw clenching), and residual audio components. Despite the frequency separation between infrasound (<20 Hz) and audible sound (20 Hz–20 kHz), strong bass frequencies or transient audio spikes can introduce low-frequency harmonics that contaminate the biosignal band. Level 2 addresses this issue through calibration and filtering. A digital filter, such as a low-pass Butterworth filter with a cutoff at 20 Hz, removes audible components, while adaptive noise cancellation algorithms compensate for any crosstalk induced by the speaker driver. Empirical testing has shown that even at maximum audio volume, the IH signal remains recoverable after this processing stage.

Level 3 implements a data quality assessment module based on a proprietary neural network classifier. This module evaluates one-second windows of filtered IH data to determine whether they contain high-quality cardiac signals or are corrupted by motion artifacts. The classifier is a multi-layer perceptron trained on a dataset of over 10,000 manually annotated cardiac cycles, with features extracted in the time domain including statistical measures (mean, variance, skewness, quantiles) and morphological characteristics (peak width, amplitude, waveform shape). The output is a probability score indicating the likelihood of a clean cardiac signal. Only segments exceeding a predefined threshold (e.g., 0.9) are passed to subsequent levels, ensuring that downstream metrics are derived from reliable data. This step is particularly important for ambulatory use, where motion artifacts are prevalent.

At Level 4, the system identifies cardiac cycle events by detecting peaks in the IH waveform. Unlike the sharp R-peaks of ECG, the IH waveform exhibits a broader, delayed peak that corresponds to the mechanical consequences of ventricular contraction—specifically, the propagation of the pressure pulse through the aortic arch and into the carotid artery, which transmits vibrations to the ear canal. In clinical studies, this peak was found to lag the ECG R-peak by approximately 158 milliseconds, with an onset delay of about 84 milliseconds. Peak detection is performed using an adaptive threshold algorithm that adjusts to signal amplitude variations, ensuring robustness across different users and physiological states. Additional features, such as dicrotic notches or secondary peaks potentially corresponding to aortic valve closure, may also be identified for advanced hemodynamic analysis.

Finally, Level 5 computes cardiovascular metrics from the detected events. Interbeat intervals (IBIs) are calculated as the time difference between consecutive IH peaks, forming a tachogram that reflects beat-to-beat heart rate dynamics. Heart rate (HR) is derived as the inverse of the IBI, averaged over user-defined windows (e.g., 20 seconds). Heart rate variability (HRV) is quantified using standard time-domain measures such as the standard deviation of IBIs (SDNN) or the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD). To enhance reliability, data from both earbuds are fused, with preference given to the channel exhibiting higher quality scores from Level 3. Respiratory rate can also be estimated from the periodic modulation of IBIs during breathing exercises, leveraging the phenomenon of respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA).

The clinical performance of the IH system was validated in two prospective studies approved by institutional review boards. The first study enrolled 25 healthy subjects in sinus rhythm (SR), aged 20 to 77 years, who underwent simultaneous IH and ECG recording during various breathing maneuvers. The second study included 15 patients with confirmed atrial fibrillation (AF), aged 45 to 90 years, providing a cohort with naturally occurring large IBI variations. Across 3744 IBI pairs in SR subjects, the IH system demonstrated a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.988 with ECG, with a mean difference of 0.05 ms and a standard deviation of 21.1 ms. For heart rate, the correlation was 0.994, with a mean difference of 0.03 bpm and a standard deviation of 1.0 bpm. These results were consistent across normal breathing, music-listening, and resonant breathing conditions, as confirmed by one-way ANOVA (p > 0.05 for both IBI and HR), indicating insensitivity to external auditory stimuli and induced physiological changes.

In AF subjects, the system maintained high fidelity over an extended IBI range (400–1600 ms), achieving a correlation of 0.99 with ECG. Furthermore, a machine learning model trained on external ECG data from the PhysioNet database—comprising 196,514 SR and 11,054 AF segments—was applied to classify 30-second IH segments from the study cohort. The model, a random forest classifier using 17 IBI-derived features (including Poincaré plot dispersion and pNN50), achieved 99% sensitivity and specificity for AF detection in IH data, matching ECG performance. Only nine misclassifications occurred out of 1064 total segments, demonstrating the clinical viability of IH for arrhythmia screening.

In conclusion, the preferred embodiments of the invention provide a robust, validated platform for continuous cardiovascular monitoring that leverages the unique acoustic properties of the occluded ear canal. By integrating passive infrasonic sensing, advanced signal processing, and cloud-based analytics into a familiar earbud form factor, the system offers a practical solution for preventive cardiology, remote patient monitoring, and early disease detection.
