## BACKGROUND

Additive manufacturing (AM), commonly referred to as three-dimensional (3D) printing, has emerged as a transformative technology for fabricating complex structures with unprecedented geometric freedom and material control. Among the various AM techniques, Direct Ink Writing (DIW) has gained significant attention due to its ability to deposit functional materials in a layer-by-layer fashion with high spatial resolution. DIW enables the fabrication of architected materials—composites whose internal microstructure is deliberately designed to achieve tailored mechanical, thermal, or electrical properties. This capability is particularly valuable in advanced engineering applications such as aerospace, defense, and energy systems, where performance demands often exceed the capabilities of conventional manufacturing methods.

A central challenge in the development of high-performance additively manufactured composites lies in the integration of reinforcing phases—such as fibers—into polymeric matrices while maintaining precise control over their orientation, distribution, and volume fraction. Traditional composite manufacturing processes, including compression molding, resin transfer molding, and filament winding, rely on continuous fiber reinforcements that are pre-aligned in woven or unidirectional configurations. While these methods yield excellent mechanical properties, they are inherently limited in geometric complexity and require extensive tooling, post-processing, and manual labor. In contrast, short-fiber-reinforced composites offer greater processability but suffer from random fiber orientation, which significantly reduces their effective stiffness, strength, and anisotropic functionality compared to continuous fiber counterparts.

The incorporation of high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers into thermoset resins via DIW presents a promising route to bridge this performance gap. However, achieving successful extrusion of such highly filled inks without nozzle clogging, phase separation, or loss of structural fidelity remains technically challenging. Conventional epoxy resins used in thermoset composites are typically low-viscosity Newtonian fluids that cannot effectively suspend or transport discrete carbon fibers during extrusion. As a result, fibers tend to sediment or aggregate, leading to non-uniform distributions and poor printability. Moreover, even when extrusion is achieved, the resulting printed filaments often exhibit minimal fiber alignment, thereby failing to capitalize on the intrinsic anisotropy of carbon fibers.

Prior attempts to address these limitations have included the use of thermoplastic matrices, such as acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS), in fused deposition modeling (FDM) processes. While such approaches have demonstrated fiber alignment at relatively high volume fractions (up to 25 vol%), the resulting composites are constrained by the inferior thermal and mechanical properties of thermoplastics, which are unsuitable for high-performance applications requiring elevated temperature resistance, dimensional stability, and long-term durability. Aromatic thermoset resins—such as bisphenol-F epoxy (BPFE)—offer superior performance characteristics but pose significant rheological challenges for DIW processing due to their low initial viscosity and lack of shear-thinning behavior.

To overcome these obstacles, researchers have explored the modification of resin rheology through the addition of colloidal additives, such as fumed silica, to impart thixotropic and shear-thinning properties. These modifications enable the resin to behave as a structured fluid that flows under applied shear stress during extrusion yet rapidly recovers its solid-like character upon deposition, thereby supporting the formation of freestanding 3D architectures. However, the synergistic interaction between such rheology modifiers, high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers, and the curing chemistry of the thermoset matrix has not been systematically optimized for DIW-based composite fabrication.

Furthermore, existing DIW methodologies lack predictive models capable of correlating ink formulation, nozzle geometry, and printing parameters with the degree of fiber alignment and final composite performance. Empirical trial-and-error approaches dominate current practice, limiting scalability and reproducibility. Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations and discrete particle modeling offer potential pathways to rational design, but their application to multi-phase, non-Newtonian systems containing rigid, high-aspect-ratio particles remains computationally intensive and methodologically immature.

In parallel, the curing strategy employed in DIW of thermoset composites must balance print window longevity with rapid post-deposition solidification. Conventional two-part epoxy systems, which require mixing of resin and hardener immediately prior to printing, introduce logistical complexity and risk premature gelation during extended print jobs. Latent curing systems, wherein polymerization is triggered only upon exposure to elevated temperatures, provide a more practical solution. However, the selection of appropriate catalysts, their concentration, and the thermal cure profile must be carefully engineered to ensure complete network formation without compromising ink stability or interlayer adhesion.

Despite these advances, no prior art has successfully demonstrated the DIW-based additive manufacturing of aromatic thermoset composites containing high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers at volume fractions exceeding 1 vol% with controlled, shear-induced fiber alignment and tunable orthotropic properties. Existing reports either utilize thermoplastic matrices, employ low fiber loadings, or fail to achieve sufficient alignment to realize performance gains over randomly oriented composites. There remains a critical unmet need for an integrated materials-process-systems approach that enables the reliable, high-resolution 3D printing of structurally ordered, high-performance carbon fiber/thermoset composites with programmable anisotropy in mechanical, thermal, and electrical domains.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method and system for the additive manufacturing of meso-structurally ordered carbon fiber-reinforced thermoset composites exhibiting unprecedented orthotropic physical properties. This is achieved through the synergistic integration of a specially formulated shear-thinning, thixotropic carbon fiber/epoxy ink, a direct ink writing (DIW) extrusion process that induces in-situ fiber alignment via controlled shear flow, and a latent thermal cure chemistry that enables stable ink storage, flexible printing timelines, and rapid post-deposition crosslinking.

The ink composition comprises an oligomerized bisphenol-F diglycidyl ether (BPFE) resin modified with colloidal fumed silica nanoparticles to impart non-Newtonian rheological behavior, combined with discrete high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers at volume fractions ranging from 3 to 8 vol%, though higher loadings are feasible with optimized nozzle geometries. The silica modifier transforms the otherwise Newtonian resin into a thixotropic fluid that exhibits pronounced shear-thinning, allowing it to flow under extrusion pressure while maintaining sufficient static viscosity to prevent fiber settling and enable self-supporting filament deposition. Critically, the increased viscous drag provided by the silica-modified resin ensures effective coupling between the fluid matrix and the dispersed carbon fibers, enabling the fibers to be carried cohesively through the print head and aligned along the extrusion direction under shear.

During DIW, the ink is extruded through a micro-nozzle with an inner diameter as small as 250 μm, generating high shear rates that induce preferential alignment of the carbon fibers parallel to the filament axis. This shear alignment is further enhanced by optimizing nozzle geometry to minimize abrupt expansions or contractions that could disrupt flow uniformity or cause fiber clumping. The resulting printed filaments exhibit a high degree of axial fiber orientation—estimated at 80–90% alignment—compared to the isotropic distribution (<33% effective alignment in any given direction) typical of conventionally molded short-fiber composites.

The cured composite architecture is formed through a latent thermal homopolymerization mechanism initiated by a Lewis acid generator, such as a boron trifluoride salt, present at low concentrations (e.g., 0.1 wt%). This catalyst remains inactive at ambient temperatures, conferring exceptional ink shelf life (>5 days at 20°C), but rapidly activates upon heating above 70°C to catalyze the ring-opening polymerization of epoxide groups, forming a densely crosslinked aromatic thermoset network. Full cure is achieved through a mild thermal cycle (e.g., 80°C for 12 hours followed by 120°C for 24 hours), yielding composites with mechanical properties comparable to or exceeding those of amine-cured BPFE systems and commercial cyanate ester resins.

The resulting additively manufactured carbon fiber-reinforced composites (AMCFRCs) demonstrate remarkable orthotropy. In tensile testing, unidirectionally printed AMCFRCs with only 3 vol% carbon fibers exhibit a Young’s modulus 6% higher than randomly pressed parts containing 8 vol% fibers, and 8 vol% AMCFRCs show a 37% increase in modulus over their random counterparts. This performance enhancement is directly attributable to the high degree of fiber alignment, which maximizes load transfer efficiency along the print direction. Surprisingly, compressive strength is also improved—not only in the axial direction but also transversely and through-thickness—indicating that the regular, filamentary microstructure enhances overall structural integrity beyond simple rule-of-mixtures predictions.

Moreover, the invention enables programmable anisotropy in electrical conductivity. Four-point probe measurements on uniaxially printed AMCFRCs reveal surface conductivities of 1.1 S cm⁻¹ parallel to the fiber alignment, versus only 3.7 × 10⁻⁵ S cm⁻¹ perpendicular—a difference of over four orders of magnitude. This extreme directional dependence allows for the design of components with spatially varying electrical pathways, thermal management channels, or mechanical reinforcement zones within a single monolithic structure.

The invention further includes computational modeling frameworks to predict and optimize fiber alignment during extrusion. High-fidelity numerical simulations based on the Distributed Lagrange Multiplier (DLM) method explicitly resolve individual fiber dynamics within a non-Newtonian fluid matrix, capturing interactions with walls and neighboring fibers. Complementary global CFD analyses using commercial software assess bulk flow patterns in various nozzle designs to identify regions prone to stagnation, recirculation, or expansion-induced fiber aggregation. Together, these tools facilitate rational design of print parameters and hardware to maximize alignment fidelity and process reliability.

In summary, the present invention represents a paradigm shift in the additive manufacturing of high-performance composites by enabling the 3D printing of aromatic thermoset matrices reinforced with aligned, high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers at industrially relevant volume fractions. It overcomes longstanding limitations in printability, fiber control, and property anisotropy, opening new avenues for lightweight, multifunctional structures in aerospace, automotive, electronics, and energy applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a method for producing additively manufactured carbon fiber-reinforced composites (AMCFRCs) with controlled meso-scale architecture and tunable orthotropic properties through direct ink writing (DIW) of a specially formulated carbon fiber/epoxy ink. The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, including ink composition, preparation protocol, printing methodology, curing strategy, and resulting material characteristics. It will be understood by those skilled in the art that the invention is not limited to the specific embodiments described herein but encompasses all variations and equivalents falling within the scope of the appended claims.

The ink used in the present invention comprises three primary components: (1) an oligomerized bisphenol-F diglycidyl ether (BPFE) resin, (2) colloidal fumed silica nanoparticles, and (3) discrete high-aspect-ratio carbon fibers. The BPFE resin serves as the thermoset matrix precursor and is selected for its aromatic backbone, which imparts high glass transition temperature, thermal stability, and mechanical rigidity upon curing. In one embodiment, the BPFE resin is partially oligomerized to increase its molecular weight and reduce volatility, while retaining sufficient epoxide functionality for crosslinking. The resin is used in its neat form without diluents or reactive modifiers that could compromise final network density.

The colloidal fumed silica, such as Cabosil TS-530, is added to the resin at a concentration of approximately 15 wt% relative to the resin mass. This additive functions as a rheology modifier, transforming the low-viscosity Newtonian resin into a thixotropic, shear-thinning fluid suitable for DIW processing. The silica particles, having a high specific surface area and hydrophobic surface treatment, form a percolating network within the resin through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals interactions. This network imparts yield stress behavior, enabling the ink to resist flow under low stresses (preventing sagging or slumping after deposition) while readily yielding under the higher shear stresses encountered during extrusion. Rheological characterization confirms that the silica-modified resin follows a Carreau fluid model, with zero-shear viscosity (μ₀) of approximately 8000 Pa·s, infinite-shear viscosity (μ∞) of 2.2 Pa·s, and empirical constants a = 45 and n = 0.1.

Discrete carbon fibers are incorporated into the silica-modified resin at volume fractions ranging from 3 to 8 vol%, though the invention contemplates loadings up to 20 vol% with advanced nozzle designs. The fibers are derived from high-tensile-strength (HTS) grade carbon fiber tow, such as Toho Tenax HTS40, which is chopped to lengths of 300–610 μm to achieve aspect ratios (length-to-diameter) greater than 50. The fiber diameter is approximately 6–7 μm, consistent with standard aerospace-grade carbon fibers. Prior to incorporation, the fibers may be subjected to surface treatments (e.g., oxidative or plasma etching) to enhance interfacial adhesion with the epoxy matrix, though such treatments are not essential for achieving alignment or basic mechanical reinforcement.

The ink is prepared through a multi-step mixing and degassing protocol to ensure homogeneous dispersion and eliminate entrapped air. In a representative procedure, 10 g of BPFE resin is weighed into a polypropylene mixing cup, and 0.1 wt% of a latent thermal cure catalyst—such as a boron trifluoride-amine complex—is added. Subsequently, 15 wt% fumed silica is introduced, and the mixture is homogenized using an off-axis centrifugal mixer operating at 2500 rpm for 1 minute. The resulting paste is then vacuum degassed at room temperature for 20 minutes to remove air bubbles introduced during mixing. Following degassing, the desired volume fraction of chopped carbon fibers is added, and the mixture is subjected to a second mixing cycle at 2500 rpm for 1 minute, followed by an extended vacuum degassing step lasting 12 hours to ensure complete removal of voids and stabilization of the fiber dispersion.

The prepared ink is loaded into a syringe barrel equipped with a luer-lock fitting and attached to a precision dispensing nozzle. Nozzle geometries include smooth-flow tapered tips with exit diameters of 250, 400, or 610 μm, or straight-walled stainless steel capillaries of similar dimensions. The choice of nozzle diameter influences both the minimum feature size and the shear rate experienced by the ink during extrusion; smaller diameters generate higher shear, promoting greater fiber alignment but increasing the risk of clogging at high fiber loadings. An air-powered fluid dispenser applies controlled pressure (typically 20–100 psi) to drive extrusion, with pressure modulated in real-time to maintain consistent flow rates across varying print speeds and path geometries.

Printing is performed on a three-axis computer-controlled positioning stage with sub-10 μm positional accuracy. The substrate is typically a fluoropolymer-coated glass slide, which provides moderate adhesion to facilitate layer stacking while allowing easy demolding after cure. The nozzle height is maintained at approximately 0.75 times the nozzle exit diameter to ensure intimate contact between successive layers without excessive flattening of the deposited filament. Print paths are generated using CAD/CAM software to define complex 3D architectures, including lattice structures, curved shells, and functionally graded compositions. In one embodiment, a di-parabolic rocket nozzle geometry is fabricated to demonstrate the capability to produce aerodynamically shaped, load-bearing components with spatially varying fiber orientations.

A key innovation of the present invention lies in the in-situ shear alignment of carbon fibers during extrusion. As the ink passes through the converging section of the nozzle, it experiences a steep velocity gradient, resulting in high shear rates that orient the high-aspect-ratio fibers along the flow direction. Optical microscopy of cross-sectioned filaments confirms that fibers are predominantly aligned parallel to the filament axis, with minimal transverse or out-of-plane components. This alignment is preserved upon deposition due to the rapid structural recovery of the thixotropic ink, which “freezes” the fiber orientation before significant relaxation can occur. The degree of alignment is further enhanced by using nozzles with monotonic taper profiles that avoid abrupt expansions, which could induce flow instabilities or fiber buckling.

Following printing, the green part—comprising uncured resin and aligned fibers—is transferred to a forced-convection oven for thermal curing. The cure cycle exploits the latent nature of the catalyst: at ambient temperature, the ink remains stable for days, but upon heating to 80°C, the catalyst decomposes to release a strong Lewis acid that initiates homopolymerization of the epoxide groups. This reaction proceeds without the need for a separate hardener, simplifying formulation and eliminating stoichiometric constraints. A typical cure schedule involves 12 hours at 80°C to achieve gelation and partial crosslinking, followed by a 24-hour post-cure at 120°C to drive the reaction to completion and maximize network density. Dielectric relaxometry and rheological monitoring confirm that full conversion is achieved within this timeframe, with no residual mobility detectable in the cured network.

The resulting AMCFRC exhibits a fully dense, void-free microstructure with excellent fiber-matrix adhesion. Mechanical testing reveals exceptional orthotropic behavior. Tensile specimens printed unidirectionally and tested parallel to the print direction show Young’s moduli of 3.2 GPa at 3 vol% fiber loading and 4.1 GPa at 8 vol%, compared to 3.0 GPa for a randomly pressed 8 vol% control. This 37% improvement at equivalent loading underscores the efficacy of alignment. Compressive testing further demonstrates that the ordered filamentary architecture enhances performance not only axially but also transversely, likely due to reduced stress concentrations and more uniform load distribution compared to random composites.

Electrical characterization highlights the functional anisotropy enabled by the invention. Four-point probe measurements on a 3 vol% AMCFRC printed in a hexagonal close-packed pattern yield in-plane conductivity of 1.1 S cm⁻¹ parallel to fibers and 3.7 × 10⁻⁵ S cm⁻¹ perpendicular—a contrast sufficient to enable directional current routing or electromagnetic shielding in specific planes. Thermal conductivity is expected to follow similar trends, though not explicitly measured herein.

Computational modeling supports process optimization. High-resolution DLM simulations tracking individual 6 μm diameter × 300 μm length fibers in an 8 vol% suspension through a 400 μm nozzle reproduce the experimental observation of wall-initiated alignment progressing inward with time, consistent with a parabolic velocity profile. Global CFD analyses identify flow deceleration zones in non-monotonic nozzles as potential sites for fiber accumulation, guiding future hardware design toward continuously converging geometries.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a robust, scalable platform for 3D printing high-performance thermoset composites with programmable microstructure and multifunctional anisotropy, overcoming fundamental limitations of prior art in both materials formulation and process control.
