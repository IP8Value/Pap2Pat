# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The regulation of the eukaryotic cell cycle is a fundamental biological process that ensures accurate DNA replication and cellular division. Central to this regulation are cyclins and their associated cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), which orchestrate progression through distinct phases of the cell cycle by phosphorylating key substrates involved in DNA synthesis, chromatin remodeling, and mitotic entry. Among these regulatory proteins, cyclin D1 plays a pivotal role in driving the transition from the G1 phase to the S phase of the cell cycle. Cyclin D1 forms active complexes with CDK4 or CDK6, thereby promoting phosphorylation of the retinoblastoma protein (Rb) and subsequent release of E2F transcription factors, which activate genes necessary for DNA replication. Given its critical function in cell cycle progression, dysregulation of cyclin D1 expression or stability is frequently observed in a wide spectrum of human malignancies, including breast, colon, esophageal, and hematological cancers. Overexpression of cyclin D1 is not only a common feature of tumorigenesis but also correlates with poor prognosis and resistance to therapy, underscoring its significance as a potential therapeutic target.

While the transcriptional upregulation of cyclin D1 in response to mitogenic signals—particularly through the Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling cascade—is well established, the mechanisms governing its post-translational turnover remain incompletely understood. It has long been recognized that cyclin D1 is a short-lived protein subject to rapid degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. This degradation is initiated by phosphorylation at a conserved threonine residue, Thr286, located near the C-terminus of the protein. Phosphorylation at this site triggers nuclear export of cyclin D1 and targets it for polyubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal destruction. Historically, glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β) was proposed as the primary kinase responsible for Thr286 phosphorylation. However, recent evidence has cast doubt on the universality of this mechanism, particularly in cancer cells where GSK3β activity may be suppressed or uncoupled from cyclin D1 regulation. Alternative kinases, such as p38 SAPK2 under stress conditions, have also been implicated, suggesting context-dependent regulation of cyclin D1 stability.

Despite these advances, the identity of the E3 ubiquitin ligase complex that specifically recognizes phosphorylated cyclin D1 and mediates its ubiquitination has remained elusive. The SCF (SKP1–CUL1–F-box protein) family of E3 ligases represents a major class of ubiquitin ligases that target phosphorylated substrates for degradation in a highly selective manner. These complexes consist of four core components: SKP1, which acts as an adaptor; CUL1 (or, in some cases, CUL7), which serves as a scaffold; RBX1 (RING-box protein 1), which recruits the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme; and an F-box protein, which confers substrate specificity through direct binding to phosphorylated motifs on target proteins. Although several F-box proteins, including SKP2 and FBXW7, have been investigated for their potential roles in cyclin D1 regulation, genetic and biochemical evidence has failed to consistently support their involvement across diverse cellular contexts. For instance, cyclin D1 does not accumulate in SKP2-knockout mouse embryonic fibroblasts, and FBXW7 primarily targets other oncoproteins such as c-Myc and Notch rather than cyclin D1. This gap in knowledge has hindered the development of targeted strategies to modulate cyclin D1 stability for therapeutic benefit.

Compounding this uncertainty is the observation that cyclin D1 exhibits distinct degradation kinetics in normal versus cancer cells. In normal proliferating cells, cyclin D1 levels peak at the G1–S transition and remain relatively stable during S phase. In contrast, many cancer cell lines display a dramatic decline in cyclin D1 protein levels specifically during S phase, despite ongoing transcription and translation. This phase-specific destabilization suggests the existence of a cancer-selective regulatory mechanism that actively promotes cyclin D1 turnover during DNA replication—a process that may be essential for maintaining genomic integrity or enabling uncontrolled proliferation. Understanding the molecular basis of this differential regulation is therefore critical not only for elucidating fundamental aspects of cell cycle control but also for identifying novel vulnerabilities in cancer cells that could be exploited therapeutously.

The present invention addresses these unresolved questions by identifying a previously unrecognized E3 ubiquitin ligase complex centered on the F-box protein FBXW8 (F-box and WD repeat domain-containing protein 8) that specifically recognizes cyclin D1 phosphorylated at Thr286 and mediates its degradation in a cell cycle– and cancer context–dependent manner. Furthermore, the invention establishes that the MAPK signaling pathway—not GSK3β—is the dominant kinase responsible for Thr286 phosphorylation in multiple cancer cell types, acting through a conserved docking (D-) domain within cyclin D1. This dual regulatory axis—MAPK-mediated phosphorylation coupled with FBXW8-dependent ubiquitination—operates predominantly during S phase in the cytoplasm of cancer cells and is essential for sustained proliferation. These findings redefine the paradigm of cyclin D1 regulation and provide a foundation for the development of precision therapeutics targeting the FBXW8–cyclin D1 interaction or the upstream MAPK–FBXW8 signaling node in cancers characterized by aberrant cyclin D1 expression.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive mechanistic framework for the regulated degradation of cyclin D1 in cancer cells, revealing a novel E3 ubiquitin ligase complex and its upstream signaling regulator. Specifically, the invention discloses that cyclin D1 is selectively destabilized during the S phase of the cell cycle in a broad range of cancer cells—but not in normal cells—through a phosphorylation-dependent ubiquitination pathway mediated by the F-box protein FBXW8. This degradation occurs predominantly in the cytoplasm and is essential for the continued proliferation of cancer cells. The invention further identifies the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade, particularly ERK1/2, as the primary kinase responsible for phosphorylating cyclin D1 at threonine 286 (Thr286), a modification that is prerequisite for recognition by FBXW8. This phosphorylation event is facilitated by a conserved kinase docking (D-) domain located within amino acids 179–193 of cyclin D1, which directly interacts with ERK/MAPK to ensure efficient and specific phosphorylation.

In one aspect, the invention provides isolated nucleic acid molecules encoding FBXW8, CUL1, CUL7, SKP1, or cyclin D1, including variants and mutants thereof, such as the Thr286-to-alanine (T286A) mutant of cyclin D1 or the D-domain deletion (ΔD) mutant. Also provided are expression vectors comprising these nucleic acids, host cells transformed therewith, and methods for producing the encoded polypeptides. In another aspect, the invention encompasses fusion proteins comprising FBXW8 or cyclin D1 linked to detectable or purification tags, which are useful for biochemical and cellular assays.

The invention further provides methods for modulating cyclin D1 stability in a cell by targeting components of the FBXW8-mediated degradation pathway. Such methods include administering agents that inhibit the interaction between FBXW8 and phosphorylated cyclin D1, suppress FBXW8 expression (e.g., via siRNA or antisense oligonucleotides), or disrupt the assembly of the SCF-like complex comprising SKP1, CUL7 (or CUL1), RBX1, and FBXW8. Conversely, the invention also contemplates enhancing cyclin D1 degradation by promoting FBXW8 activity or expression, which may be beneficial in cancers driven by cyclin D1 overexpression.

Additionally, the invention discloses diagnostic and prognostic methods based on the detection of FBXW8 expression levels, cyclin D1 phosphorylation status at Thr286, or the subcellular colocalization of FBXW8 and cyclin D1 in tumor samples. High FBXW8 activity coupled with elevated phospho-Thr286 cyclin D1 may indicate a dependency on this degradation pathway for tumor growth, thereby identifying patients who would benefit from therapies targeting this axis.

In yet another embodiment, the invention provides kits for assessing cyclin D1 ubiquitination or degradation in vitro or in cell-based systems, comprising purified or recombinant FBXW8, SKP1, CUL1, CUL7, RBX1, E1, E2 (e.g., UbcH5C), ubiquitin, ATP, and optionally active ERK2 kinase. These kits enable the screening of compounds that modulate FBXW8-mediated cyclin D1 ubiquitination.

Critically, the invention demonstrates that inhibition of FBXW8—through genetic knockdown, dominant-negative mutants, or pharmacological means—leads to accumulation of phosphorylated cyclin D1 in the cytoplasm, mislocalization of CDK1 (and likely CDK4/6), loss of nuclear CDK activity, and consequent growth arrest in cancer cells. Importantly, this phenotype can be rescued by expression of a nuclear-localized, non-degradable cyclin D1 T286A mutant, confirming that the essential function of FBXW8 is to clear cyclin D1 from the cytoplasm during S phase to permit proper cell cycle progression. Thus, the FBXW8–cyclin D1 axis represents a cancer-selective vulnerability with significant therapeutic potential.

## DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “cyclin D1” refers to the human cyclin D1 protein (UniProt ID P24385) and its orthologs from other mammalian species, including but not limited to mouse, rat, and primate cyclin D1. It encompasses full-length cyclin D1 as well as biologically active fragments, variants, and isoforms that retain the ability to bind CDK4/6 and/or be regulated by phosphorylation at Thr286. The term includes naturally occurring polymorphic variants and engineered mutants, such as the T286A substitution or D-domain deletions.

“Phosphorylation at Thr286” means the covalent addition of a phosphate group to the hydroxyl side chain of threonine residue 286 of human cyclin D1 (corresponding to Thr280 in cyclin D2 and Thr283 in cyclin D3). This modification is a prerequisite for nuclear export and ubiquitin-mediated degradation of cyclin D1 in the context of the present invention.

“FBXW8” refers to F-box and WD repeat domain-containing protein 8 (also known as Fbx29 or Fbxw8), a member of the F-box protein family that contains an N-terminal F-box domain for binding SKP1 and C-terminal WD40 repeats for substrate recognition. Human FBXW8 (UniProt ID Q969H0) and its orthologs are included.

“MAPK” or “ERK/MAPK” denotes the mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade, specifically the Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK signaling pathway, and includes ERK1 (MAPK3) and ERK2 (MAPK1). The term encompasses active, phosphorylated forms (pERK) as well as upstream activators such as MEK1/2.

“SCF complex” refers to the Skp1–Cul1–F-box protein E3 ubiquitin ligase complex. “SCF-like complex” denotes analogous complexes that substitute CUL7 for CUL1, such as the SKP1–CUL7–FBXW8–RBX1 complex described herein.

“Ubiquitin-proteasome pathway” means the cellular machinery responsible for tagging proteins with polyubiquitin chains and degrading them via the 26S proteasome. This includes E1 (ubiquitin-activating), E2 (ubiquitin-conjugating), and E3 (ubiquitin ligase) enzymes.

“Cancer cell” refers to any malignant or transformed cell line or primary tumor cell that exhibits uncontrolled proliferation, evasion of growth suppression, and other hallmarks of cancer, as opposed to normal, non-transformed cells.

“Destabilized” or “degraded” in the context of cyclin D1 means a reduction in protein half-life due to increased rates of ubiquitination and proteasomal destruction, as measured by pulse-chase analysis or accumulation upon proteasome inhibition.

“D-domain” or “docking domain” refers to a conserved motif in MAPK substrates that facilitates high-affinity interaction with ERK/MAPK, typically characterized by clusters of basic and hydrophobic residues. In cyclin D1, this domain spans amino acids 179–193.

“Covalently modified proteins” include cyclin D1 or other components that have been post-translationally modified by phosphorylation, ubiquitination, or fusion to affinity or epitope tags (e.g., HA, Flag, V5, GST).

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### FBXW8 Protein (F-BOX WD-40 Domain Protein 8)

The present invention identifies FBXW8 as a critical E3 ubiquitin ligase component that specifically targets cyclin D1 for degradation in cancer cells. FBXW8 is a member of the F-box protein family, characterized by an N-terminal F-box domain that mediates interaction with the adaptor protein SKP1 and C-terminal WD40 repeat domains that confer substrate specificity. Unlike other F-box proteins such as FBXW7 or SKP2, which have been previously investigated in the context of cyclin regulation, FBXW8 exhibits a unique and selective interaction with cyclin D1 that is strictly dependent on phosphorylation at Thr286. The invention provides isolated and purified FBXW8 polypeptides, including full-length human FBXW8 (comprising 592 amino acids) and functional fragments thereof that retain either the F-box domain (for complex assembly) or the WD40 repeats (for substrate binding). Nucleic acid molecules encoding FBXW8, including cDNA and genomic sequences, are also encompassed, as are expression vectors for recombinant production in bacterial, yeast, insect, or mammalian host systems. The invention further contemplates mutant forms of FBXW8, such as the F-box deletion mutant (ΔF), which retains substrate-binding capacity but cannot assemble into functional E3 ligase complexes, thereby acting as a dominant-negative inhibitor of cyclin D1 degradation. Antibodies specific to FBXW8, whether polyclonal or monoclonal, are useful for immunoprecipitation, immunoblotting, immunofluorescence, and diagnostic applications. The functional characterization of FBXW8 as a cyclin D1-specific E3 ligase component represents a foundational discovery that enables the development of targeted therapeutics aimed at modulating cyclin D1 stability in cancer.

### MAPK (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase)

The invention establishes that the MAPK signaling pathway, specifically ERK1/2, is the principal kinase responsible for phosphorylating cyclin D1 at Thr286 in cancer cells. This finding challenges the prevailing model that attributes this modification primarily to GSK3β and instead positions sustained MAPK activity—a hallmark of many cancers—as the driver of cyclin D1 turnover during S phase. The invention discloses that ERK/MAPK recognizes cyclin D1 through a conserved D-domain (amino acids 179–193), which is essential for efficient phosphorylation. Deletion or mutation of this domain abrogates both ERK binding and Thr286 phosphorylation, confirming its necessity. The invention provides methods for modulating cyclin D1 stability by targeting MAPK activity, including the use of MEK inhibitors (e.g., U0126, trametinib) or ERK inhibitors, which stabilize cyclin D1 and induce growth arrest in FBXW8-dependent cancer cells. Recombinant ERK2 protein, either wild-type or kinase-dead, is useful for in vitro kinase and ubiquitination assays. The invention also encompasses phospho-specific antibodies that selectively recognize cyclin D1 phosphorylated at Thr286, enabling detection of MAPK pathway activity in tumor samples. The dual role of MAPK—in both inducing cyclin D1 transcription and promoting its degradation—reveals a sophisticated feedback mechanism that ensures transient cyclin D1 activity during G1 and its timely removal during S phase, a process that is co-opted in cancer for sustained proliferation.

### CULLIN 1 (CUL1)

CUL1 is a core scaffold component of canonical SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase complexes. The invention demonstrates that CUL1 participates in the FBXW8-mediated degradation of cyclin D1, forming a complex with SKP1, FBXW8, and RBX1. Immunoprecipitation experiments confirm that endogenous cyclin D1 associates with CUL1 in cancer cells, and siRNA-mediated knockdown of CUL1 leads to cyclin D1 accumulation and stabilization. The invention provides isolated CUL1 polypeptides, nucleic acids encoding CUL1, and expression vectors for functional studies. CUL1 is part of a redundant or complementary degradation pathway alongside CUL7, as depletion of either cullin extends cyclin D1 half-life, albeit to different extents. This suggests that FBXW8 can utilize either CUL1- or CUL7-based scaffolds to mediate cyclin D1 ubiquitination, providing robustness to the regulatory system. Targeting CUL1 neddylation or its interaction with SKP1 represents an alternative strategy to inhibit cyclin D1 degradation, although such approaches may lack specificity due to the involvement of CUL1 in numerous other SCF complexes.

### Cullin 7 (CUL7)

CUL7 is a less characterized cullin family member that forms SCF-like complexes with SKP1 and specific F-box proteins, including FBXW8. The invention reveals that CUL7 is a key scaffold in the FBXW8–cyclin D1 degradation axis in cancer cells. Co-immunoprecipitation data show that cyclin D1 associates with CUL7, and siRNA knockdown of CUL7 results in significant cyclin D1 stabilization, comparable to FBXW8 depletion. Pulse-chase analysis confirms that CUL7 loss extends cyclin D1 half-life from approximately 28 minutes to nearly 59 minutes in HCT 116 cells. The invention provides purified CUL7 protein, encoding nucleic acids, and tools for disrupting the CUL7–SKP1–FBXW8 interaction. Given that CUL7-based complexes may be more selectively dedicated to cyclin D1 regulation than CUL1-based ones, CUL7 represents a potentially more specific therapeutic target. Mutations or dysregulation of CUL7 in certain cancers could therefore serve as biomarkers for FBXW8 pathway dependency.

### SKP1 (S-Phase Kinase-associated Protein 1)

SKP1 serves as the central adaptor that links F-box proteins like FBXW8 to cullin scaffolds (CUL1 or CUL7). The invention confirms that SKP1 is indispensable for cyclin D1 degradation, as its siRNA-mediated depletion causes robust cyclin D1 accumulation in cancer cells. SKP1 bridges the substrate-recognition module (FBXW8) with the catalytic core (cullin–RBX1), enabling efficient transfer of ubiquitin to cyclin D1. The invention encompasses SKP1 polypeptides, nucleic acids, and expression constructs, as well as methods for inhibiting SKP1–FBXW8 interaction using peptide mimetics or small molecules. Because SKP1 is shared among many SCF complexes, global inhibition may have off-target effects; however, localized disruption of the SKP1–FBXW8 interface could offer selectivity. SKP1 levels or post-translational modifications may also serve as indicators of FBXW8 pathway activity in tumors.

### Cyclin D1

Cyclin D1 is the central substrate of the degradation pathway disclosed herein. The invention provides detailed characterization of its regulation, including the identification of Thr286 as the critical phospho-degron and the D-domain as the MAPK docking site. Isolated cyclin D1 polypeptides, including wild-type, T286A, and ΔD mutants, are useful for structural, biochemical, and cellular studies. Nucleic acids encoding these variants, along with expression vectors and host cells, are provided. The invention also discloses that cyclin D1 must be exported to the cytoplasm during S phase to encounter FBXW8, and that failure to degrade it leads to cytoplasmic sequestration of CDKs and growth arrest. Therapeutic strategies based on stabilizing or destabilizing cyclin D1—depending on the cancer context—are enabled by this work. For example, in tumors addicted to cyclin D1, promoting its degradation via FBXW8 activation could be beneficial, whereas in tumors where cyclin D1 degradation drives proliferation, inhibiting FBXW8 may be advantageous.

### Nucleic Acid Molecules, Polypeptide Production Methods, Expression Vectors, Fusion Proteins

The invention includes isolated nucleic acid molecules encoding FBXW8, CUL1, CUL7, SKP1, cyclin D1, and their variants. These may be genomic DNA, cDNA, or synthetic genes optimized for expression. Expression vectors—such as pcDNA3, pCMV, pET, or retroviral/lentiviral constructs—are provided for transient or stable expression in prokaryotic or eukaryotic systems. Methods for producing the encoded polypeptides involve transforming host cells (e.g., E. coli, HEK293, Sf9) and purifying the proteins using affinity tags (GST, His6, Flag, HA). Fusion proteins, such as GST–cyclin D1 for kinase assays or V5–FBXW8 for immunoprecipitation, are explicitly contemplated and are instrumental in the functional assays described. Site-directed mutagenesis protocols for generating T286A, ΔD, or ΔF mutants are standard and well within the skill of the art.

### Covalently Modified Proteins

The invention involves proteins that are covalently modified by phosphorylation (e.g., phospho-Thr286 cyclin D1), ubiquitination (polyubiquitinated cyclin D1), or fusion to tags (e.g., HA–cyclin D1, Flag–ERK2). These modifications are critical for function, detection, or purification. Phospho-specific antibodies enable monitoring of pathway activity, while ubiquitin pulldowns reveal degradation competence. Tagged proteins facilitate complex isolation and reconstitution. The invention also includes chemically synthesized phosphopeptides corresponding to the Thr286 region, useful for antibody generation or competitive inhibition studies.

### Components for Polyubiquitination Assays

The invention provides complete systems for reconstituting cyclin D1 polyubiquitination in vitro. These include purified or recombinant E1 (UBA1), E2 (UbcH5C), ubiquitin, ATP, and the E3 ligase complex (SKP1, CUL1 or CUL7, RBX1, and FBXW8). Active ERK2 is included to phosphorylate cyclin D1 substrate prior to or during the reaction. Assay buffers, controls (e.g., T286A cyclin D1), and detection reagents (anti-ubiquitin or anti-cyclin D1 antibodies) are part of the kit. These components enable high-throughput screening of inhibitors or activators of FBXW8-mediated cyclin D1 degradation.

## EXAMPLES

### Cyclin D1 Protein is Destabilized Specifically in S Phase in Cancer Cells

Comparative analysis of cyclin D1 expression dynamics in synchronized normal and cancer cell lines revealed a striking divergence in protein stability during S phase. Normal human fibroblasts (WI-38), murine fibroblasts (NIH 3T3), and colon epithelial cells (CCD841 CoN) exhibited a gradual increase in cyclin D1 levels following serum stimulation, peaking at the G1–S transition and remaining relatively constant throughout S phase. In stark contrast, colon carcinoma lines (HCT 116, SW480) and glioblastoma cells (T98G) displayed a pronounced decline in cyclin D1 protein abundance specifically during S phase, despite comparable transcriptional induction. Pulse-chase metabolic labeling with ³⁵S-methionine/cysteine confirmed that the half-life of cyclin D1 in HCT 116 cells decreased from 27.5 minutes in G1 to 11.8 minutes in S phase, whereas no significant change was observed in NIH 3T3 cells. This cancer-selective destabilization indicates the engagement of an active degradation mechanism during DNA replication, which is absent or suppressed in normal proliferating cells. The temporal specificity of this process suggests coordination with S-phase events, such as origin firing or replication fork progression, and implies that persistent cyclin D1 activity beyond G1 may be detrimental to cancer cell fitness.

### Cyclin D1 Protein is Degraded During the S Phase Through the Ubiquitin-proteasome Pathway in Cancer Cells

Treatment of synchronized HCT 116 cells with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 resulted in a marked accumulation of cyclin D1 specifically during S phase, with minimal effect in G1, confirming proteasome dependence. Immunoprecipitation of cyclin D1 from cells co-expressing HA-tagged ubiquitin revealed high-molecular-weight smears detectable by anti-HA immunoblotting, indicative of polyubiquitin conjugation. This signal was enhanced by MG132 and absent in control immunoprecipitations, verifying specificity. Subcellular fractionation demonstrated that polyubiquitinated cyclin D1 species were predominantly localized to the cytoplasmic compartment during S phase, consistent with the known nuclear export of phosphorylated cyclin D1. Inhibition of nuclear export with leptomycin B did not rescue nuclear ubiquitination, reinforcing that degradation occurs exclusively in the cytoplasm. These findings establish that cyclin D1 is targeted for proteasomal destruction via polyubiquitination in the cytoplasm of cancer cells during S phase, a process that is tightly coupled to cell cycle progression and oncogenic signaling.

### Expression of Cyclin D1 Protein in the Nucleus Decreases Through the G1-S Transition in Tumor Cells

Immunofluorescence and biochemical fractionation of synchronized HCT 116 cells showed that cyclin D1 accumulates in the nucleus during late G1, coinciding with Rb phosphorylation and E2F activation. As cells enter S phase, nuclear cyclin D1 levels diminish concomitantly with an increase in cytoplasmic pools. This redistribution is dependent on Thr286 phosphorylation, as the T286A mutant remains nuclear throughout the cell cycle. The temporal correlation between nuclear export and onset of degradation indicates that cytoplasmic translocation is a prerequisite for FBXW8-mediated ubiquitination. The spatial segregation of cyclin D1 from its nuclear substrates (e.g., CDK4/6–Rb complexes) during S phase may serve to terminate G1-promoting signals and prevent re-replication, a safeguard that is hijacked in cancer to enable continuous cycling.

### Phosphorylation of Cyclin D1 Protein is Mediated by the Same Mechanism as Cyclin D1 Gene Expression, Via the MAPK Signaling Pathway

Bioinformatic analysis identified a high-confidence D-domain (¹⁷⁹KRKLDRFFKELRQDL¹⁹³) in cyclin D1, homologous to those in established ERK substrates. In vitro kinase assays demonstrated that purified ERK2 phosphorylates wild-type GST–cyclin D1 but not T286A or ΔD mutants. Co-immunoprecipitation in cells confirmed ERK2 binding to wild-type but not ΔD cyclin D1. Pharmacological inhibition of MEK with U0126 abolished Thr286 phosphorylation and stabilized cyclin D1, extending its half-life more than twofold. These data collectively prove that the same MAPK cascade that induces cyclin D1 transcription also terminates its activity via phosphorylation-dependent degradation, creating a self-limiting loop that is amplified in cancer due to constitutive MAPK signaling.

### Example 5

Genetic and functional validation confirmed FBXW8 as the E3 ligase for cyclin D1. Overexpression of FBXW8 reduced endogenous cyclin D1 without affecting cyclin E, while a dominant-negative ΔF mutant caused cyclin D1 accumulation. siRNA knockdown of FBXW8, CUL1, or CUL7 each stabilized cyclin D1 and inhibited cancer cell proliferation. Critically, this growth arrest was rescued by inducible expression of nuclear-localized cyclin D1 T286A, proving that the essential function of FBXW8 is to clear cytoplasmic cyclin D1 during S phase. Without this clearance, cyclin D1 sequesters CDK1 in the cytoplasm, abolishing nuclear CDK activity required for S-phase progression. Thus, the FBXW8–cyclin D1 axis is not merely a degradation pathway but a linchpin of cancer cell cycle control, representing a compelling target for therapeutic intervention.
