# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

High-frequency (HF) radar systems have been employed for oceanographic observations since the 1960s, primarily to map surface currents and monitor sea state. These systems, typically installed on coastlines and transmitting vertically polarized radio waves, exploit the high conductivity of seawater to propagate signals beyond the optical or microwave horizon. Over the past several decades, HF radar networks have expanded significantly, with over 350 operational stations globally, providing continuous, real-time measurements of surface current velocities and wave characteristics. Despite their widespread use in ocean monitoring, the potential of HF radars for tsunami detection remained largely theoretical until recent catastrophic events underscored the urgent need for improved local early warning capabilities.

The concept of using HF radar to detect tsunamis was first proposed by Barrick in 1979, who theorized that the orbital velocity associated with an approaching tsunami could be discerned within the radar-measured surface current field. However, due to the limited global distribution of HF radar systems prior to the 1990s, this idea was not pursued operationally. The devastating 2004 Banda Aceh earthquake and tsunami, which claimed approximately 250,000 lives, catalyzed renewed interest in alternative tsunami detection methodologies. Although no HF radars were positioned to observe that event, the tragedy prompted systematic efforts to quantify the radar response to tsunamis and to develop robust detection algorithms.

It was not until the 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami in Japan that sufficient HF radar coverage existed around the Pacific Rim to capture comprehensive observational data. This event provided the first opportunity to validate theoretical models and refine empirical detection techniques using real-world measurements. Subsequent analyses confirmed that the Japan tsunami was clearly observed by multiple HF radar systems in Japan, the United States, and Chile. Additional, albeit weaker, tsunami events—including the 2012 Indonesia tsunami and the 2013 US East Coast meteotsunami—have also been successfully detected by HF radars, further enriching the observational database and reinforcing the reliability of the methodology.

Current operational tsunami detection relies heavily on two primary sensor types: seismic networks and deep-ocean pressure sensors. Seismic detection provides the earliest possible indication of a potential tsunami-generating event through the identification of undersea earthquakes. However, not all subsea earthquakes produce tsunamis, and the magnitude of an earthquake alone is insufficient to accurately forecast the intensity or even the occurrence of a resulting tsunami. Consequently, seismic alerts often trigger unnecessary warnings, leading to public desensitization and economic disruption.

The only operational sensors that directly detect and measure tsunami intensity are the Deep-ocean Assessment and Reporting of Tsunamis (DART™) buoys, developed and deployed by the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) following the 2004 disaster. These systems consist of bottom pressure recorders linked to surface buoys that transmit real-time data on tsunami wave height as it passes overhead in the deep ocean. While invaluable for basin-wide forecasting, DART systems have inherent limitations. Their deep-ocean placement means they may not detect tsunamis generated in regions distant from buoy locations or those that do not propagate through monitored areas. Moreover, numerical models that assimilate DART data to predict coastal impacts often produce coarse forecasts that fail to capture critical local variations—referred to as the "near-field" effects—that determine the actual hazard at specific coastal locations.

HF radars operate precisely within this near-field region, typically observing surface currents up to 50 kilometers offshore, where tsunami orbital velocities become amplified due to decreasing water depth. Unlike DART buoys that measure wave height, HF radars measure the horizontal orbital velocity of the tsunami wave, which increases more rapidly than wave height as the tsunami approaches the coast. This characteristic makes HF radar an exceptionally sensitive indicator of an approaching tsunami in the critical pre-impact zone. The ability to provide localized, real-time detection and warning in the near-field region addresses a significant gap in the existing tsunami warning infrastructure.

Despite these advantages, the practical implementation of HF radar for tsunami warning faces several challenges. The tsunami signal must be distinguished from complex background ocean currents, which can include tides, wind-driven flows, and geostrophic circulation. Additionally, external radio frequency interference and internal signal processing artifacts, such as range aliasing, can produce false signatures that mimic tsunami behavior. Effective detection therefore requires sophisticated signal processing algorithms capable of identifying the unique spatiotemporal patterns associated with tsunami-induced currents while minimizing false alarms.

Recent collaborative efforts between organizations such as Codar Ocean Sensors and NOAA have focused on developing and validating empirical detection algorithms, evaluating site suitability for tsunami monitoring, and integrating HF radar data into operational warning systems. These developments have culminated in the recognition of HF radar as a valuable component of the US Integrated Ocean Observing System (IOOS) for tsunami warning, with ongoing work to transition the technology to operational tsunami warning centers in Hawaii and Alaska. The present invention builds upon this foundation by providing a comprehensive, robust, and automated method for tsunami detection using HF radar observations that addresses the limitations of prior approaches and enables reliable, real-time warning capabilities.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for the detection and warning of approaching tsunamis using high-frequency (HF) coastal radar observations. The invention leverages the unique capability of HF radars to measure surface current velocities in the near-field coastal region, where tsunami orbital velocities become sufficiently amplified to exceed detection thresholds. Unlike conventional tsunami sensors that measure wave height, the present invention exploits the fact that tsunami orbital velocity increases more rapidly than wave height as the tsunami propagates into shallower water, providing an early and sensitive indicator of an approaching tsunami.

The core of the invention is an empirical pattern recognition algorithm that analyzes time series of radar-derived current velocities to identify the distinctive spatiotemporal signature of a tsunami. The method begins by processing raw radar spectra to obtain radial current velocities at high temporal resolution, without the averaging typically applied in standard ocean current mapping. These radial velocities are then resolved into components perpendicular and parallel to depth contours and averaged over rectangular area bands approximately 2 kilometers wide and oriented parallel to bathymetric features. This band-averaging approach enhances the signal-to-noise ratio while preserving the spatial coherence characteristic of tsunami-induced currents.

The detection algorithm operates by computing a quantitative factor, referred to herein as the q-factor, which evaluates three key characteristics of the band velocity time series: (1) the magnitude of velocity changes over consecutive time intervals within individual bands; (2) the temporal coincidence of maximum or minimum velocities across adjacent bands; and (3) the spatial coherence of velocity changes across three or more adjacent bands. Each of these criteria contributes incrementally to the q-factor, with positive values indicating onshore-directed flow (tsunami crest) and negative values indicating offshore-directed flow (tsunami trough). A tsunami detection is declared when the q-factor exceeds a predetermined threshold value that has been optimized to balance the probability of detection against the false alarm rate for a given radar site.

The invention further provides a method for evaluating the suitability of coastal radar sites for tsunami detection and for estimating the warning time available for tsunamis of varying intensities. This evaluation method utilizes simulated tsunami velocities generated by superimposing reference tsunami velocity signatures onto measured background current velocities at the test site. The reference velocities are adjusted according to local bathymetry using Green's Law relationships, which describe how tsunami height and orbital velocity scale with water depth. By systematically varying the amplitude of the simulated tsunami and applying the detection algorithm, the minimum tsunami velocity and corresponding height required for detection can be determined as a function of distance from shore. This information enables the estimation of warning time based on the propagation characteristics of the tsunami across the continental shelf.

To address the challenge of false alarms, the invention incorporates two complementary validation strategies. The first strategy involves cross-correlation of q-factor alerts from multiple adjacent HF radar sites. Given that a genuine tsunami will produce coherent signals across neighboring radar coverage areas with predictable arrival time differences based on local bathymetry and wave propagation physics, simultaneous or near-simultaneous detections at multiple sites significantly increase confidence in the alert. The second strategy correlates q-factor alerts with seismic event notifications from global earthquake monitoring networks. By establishing a threat window based on the predicted tsunami arrival time from a potentially tsunamigenic earthquake, q-factor peaks occurring within this window can be prioritized for further evaluation and potential warning issuance.

The invention is applicable to both seismically generated tsunamis and meteotsunamis, which are produced by atmospheric pressure disturbances. Although their generation mechanisms differ, both types of events produce shallow-water waves that exhibit identical propagation characteristics and detection signatures in the near-field region. The method has been validated against observational data from multiple real tsunami events, including the 2011 Tohoku Japan tsunami, the 2012 Indonesia tsunami, and the 2013 US East Coast meteotsunami, demonstrating consistent detection capabilities across diverse geographic and bathymetric conditions.

Key advantages of the present invention include its ability to provide localized, real-time tsunami detection in the critical near-field region where conventional deep-ocean sensors provide limited information; its sensitivity to relatively small tsunami events that may not trigger alerts from other systems; and its integration with existing HF radar infrastructure, enabling rapid deployment without significant additional hardware investment. The invention represents a significant advancement in tsunami warning capabilities, particularly for coastal communities that require precise, location-specific information about impending tsunami impacts.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive method and system for detecting and warning of approaching tsunamis using high-frequency (HF) coastal radar observations. The detailed description that follows outlines the technical foundations, operational procedures, and implementation strategies that enable reliable tsunami detection while addressing the practical challenges associated with real-world deployment.

The method begins with the acquisition and processing of HF radar data to extract high-resolution surface current velocity measurements. Standard HF radar systems, such as the SeaSonde® radars manufactured by Codar Ocean Sensors, operate by transmitting radio waves in the 3-30 MHz frequency range and receiving backscattered signals from ocean surface waves with half the transmitted wavelength. The Doppler shift of these backscattered signals relative to the expected Bragg frequency provides a direct measurement of the radial component of surface current velocity along the line of sight from the radar. In conventional ocean current mapping applications, multiple radar spectra are averaged over time intervals of 15-60 minutes to improve signal quality and reduce noise. However, for tsunami detection, this temporal averaging would obscure the relatively rapid velocity changes associated with tsunami periods of 20-50 minutes. Therefore, the present invention processes unaveraged radar spectra at the highest available temporal resolution, typically 1-4 minute intervals, to preserve the dynamic characteristics of the tsunami signal.

The raw radial velocity measurements from individual radar sites are then processed to enhance the tsunami signature while suppressing background noise and interference. This processing involves several key steps. First, the radar coverage area is divided into rectangular area bands approximately 2 kilometers wide, oriented parallel to the local bathymetric contours. This band orientation is critical because tsunami-induced currents tend to flow perpendicular to depth contours as the wave refracts in response to changing water depth. Within each band, the radial velocity vectors are resolved into components perpendicular and parallel to the depth contours. The perpendicular component contains the primary tsunami signal, while the parallel component primarily reflects background currents and can be used for noise characterization. The resolved velocity components are then averaged over each band to produce band-averaged velocity time series, referred to herein as "band velocities." This spatial averaging significantly improves the signal-to-noise ratio while preserving the spatial coherence that distinguishes tsunami signals from random background fluctuations.

The core of the detection algorithm is the computation of a quantitative detection metric, designated as the q-factor, which evaluates the likelihood that observed velocity patterns represent a genuine tsunami. The q-factor is computed through a multi-step process that assesses three distinct characteristics of the band velocity time series. In the first step, the algorithm examines each individual band for significant velocity changes over consecutive time intervals. Specifically, if the velocity in a band increases or decreases by more than a predetermined threshold amount over two consecutive time intervals, the q-factor for that band is incremented or decremented accordingly. This step captures the fundamental characteristic of tsunami-induced currents: their oscillatory nature with significant velocity changes over short time periods.

The second step evaluates the temporal coherence of velocity extrema across adjacent bands. If the maximum or minimum velocities in consecutive bands occur within a predetermined time window (typically 5-10 minutes), the q-factor is further modified to reflect this spatial correlation. This step is particularly important because genuine tsunami signals exhibit strong spatial coherence across the radar coverage area, with velocity peaks and troughs occurring nearly simultaneously across multiple bands as the tsunami wavefront passes through the observation region.

The third step assesses the spatial extent of coherent velocity changes by examining three or more adjacent bands simultaneously. If significant velocity changes occur in the same direction across three or more adjacent bands over two consecutive time intervals, the q-factor receives an additional increment or decrement. This step helps distinguish tsunami signals from localized phenomena that might affect only one or two bands, such as ship wakes or localized current instabilities.

The final q-factor value represents a composite assessment of these three criteria, with positive values indicating onshore-directed flow (corresponding to a tsunami crest) and negative values indicating offshore-directed flow (corresponding to a tsunami trough). A tsunami detection is declared when the q-factor exceeds a predetermined threshold value. This threshold is not universal but is instead optimized for each radar site based on extensive analysis of background current conditions and historical false alarm rates. The optimization process involves analyzing extended datasets of normal operating conditions to establish a statistical baseline for q-factor values in the absence of tsunamis, then selecting a threshold that provides an acceptable balance between detection probability and false alarm rate.

The invention further provides a comprehensive method for evaluating the suitability of coastal radar sites for tsunami detection and for estimating the warning time available for tsunamis of varying intensities. This evaluation method is particularly important because not all coastal locations are equally suitable for tsunami monitoring with HF radar. Factors such as offshore bathymetry, background current variability, and radio frequency interference can significantly affect detection performance. The evaluation method utilizes simulated tsunami velocities generated by superimposing reference tsunami velocity signatures onto measured background current velocities at the test site.

The reference tsunami velocity signatures are obtained from actual tsunami observations at well-characterized radar sites, such as the Kinaoshi radar (A088) during the 2011 Tohoku Japan tsunami. These reference velocities are stored in a database and serve as templates for simulating tsunami events at other locations. Before superimposition, the reference velocities are adjusted according to the local bathymetry of the test site using Green's Law relationships. Green's Law describes how tsunami height and orbital velocity scale with water depth in the shallow-water approximation, where tsunami height varies with depth to the power of -1/4 and orbital velocity varies with depth to the power of -3/4. This adjustment accounts for the amplification of tsunami signals as they propagate into shallower water, ensuring that the simulated velocities accurately reflect the expected behavior at the test site.

The superimposition process is governed by the following equation:

V_Sim = V_Site + F × V_Ref × (Depth_Site / Depth_Ref)^(-3/4)

where V_Sim is the simulated velocity, V_Site is the measured background velocity at the test site, V_Ref is the reference tsunami velocity, Depth_Site and Depth_Ref are the average water depths across the corresponding bands at the test and reference sites, respectively, and F is a multiplicative scaling factor that controls the amplitude of the simulated tsunami.

The evaluation procedure involves systematically varying the scaling factor F and applying the detection algorithm to the resulting simulated velocity time series. For each value of F, the algorithm computes q-factors and determines whether a detection would occur. The minimum value of F that produces a detection (designated F_detect) is identified through this iterative process. Using the Green's Law relationships, F_detect can then be converted to the corresponding tsunami velocity (V_detect) and height (H_detect) required for detection at the test site:

V_detect = F_detect × V_Ref × (Depth_Site / Depth_Ref)^(-3/4)

H_detect = F_detect × H_Ref × (Depth_Site / Depth_Ref)^(-1/4)

where H_Ref is the reference tsunami height corresponding to V_Ref.

This evaluation method provides critical information for operational planning, including the minimum tsunami size that can be detected at various distances from shore and the corresponding warning time available. Warning time is estimated based on the tsunami propagation characteristics across the continental shelf, which can be calculated using the phase velocity relationship v_ph = √(g × d), where g is gravitational acceleration and d is water depth. By integrating this relationship across the actual bathymetry profile, the time required for a tsunami to travel from the detection range to the shoreline can be accurately estimated.

To address the persistent challenge of false alarms, the invention incorporates two complementary validation strategies that significantly enhance detection reliability. The first strategy involves cross-correlation of q-factor alerts from multiple adjacent HF radar sites. Given that a genuine tsunami will produce coherent signals across neighboring radar coverage areas, with predictable arrival time differences based on local bathymetry and wave propagation physics, simultaneous or near-simultaneous detections at multiple sites provide strong evidence of a real event. The expected arrival time difference between adjacent radar sites can be calculated based on the local bathymetry and the assumption that tsunami waves refract to become perpendicular to the coastline as they approach shore. Typically, for radar sites separated by 30-50 kilometers along a relatively straight coastline, the arrival time difference should be less than 15 minutes. Alerts that occur within this expected time window at multiple sites are assigned high confidence, while isolated alerts without corresponding detections at neighboring sites are flagged as potential false alarms.

The second validation strategy correlates q-factor alerts with seismic event notifications from global earthquake monitoring networks. Several international services, such as the United States Geological Survey (USGS) and the Global Centroid Moment Tensor (GCMT) project, provide real-time notifications of significant earthquakes within 2-5 minutes of occurrence. These notifications include the earthquake's location, time, magnitude, and depth. While not all subsea earthquakes generate tsunamis, earthquakes with magnitudes greater than 5.0 and epicenters located in known tsunamigenic regions can be considered potential tsunami sources. For such events, a threat window can be established based on the predicted tsunami arrival time at the radar site, calculated using the phase velocity integration method described earlier. The threat window typically extends ±30 minutes around the predicted arrival time to account for uncertainties in the source mechanism and propagation path. Q-factor peaks occurring within this threat window are assigned higher priority for evaluation and potential warning issuance, while alerts occurring outside the threat window are treated with greater skepticism.

The invention is specifically designed to handle both seismically generated tsunamis and meteotsunamis, which are produced by atmospheric pressure disturbances. Although their generation mechanisms differ fundamentally—seismic tsunamis result from massive displacement of water due to tectonic activity, while meteotsunamis are generated by atmospheric pressure anomalies traveling across the sea surface—their propagation characteristics in the near-field region are identical. Both types of events produce shallow-water waves that obey the same fundamental equations of motion and exhibit the same scaling relationships with water depth. Consequently, the detection algorithm and evaluation methods described herein are equally applicable to both tsunami types, as demonstrated by successful detections of the 2013 US East Coast meteotsunami at multiple New Jersey radar sites.

The operational implementation of the invention involves continuous background monitoring of HF radar data streams, with the detection algorithm running in real-time or near-real-time mode. When a q-factor threshold is exceeded, the system automatically generates an alert that includes the detection time, estimated tsunami characteristics, and confidence level based on the validation strategies described above. This alert can be transmitted to tsunami warning centers, emergency management agencies, and other relevant stakeholders through established communication channels. The system also maintains detailed logs of all detections and near-misses for post-event analysis and algorithm refinement.

The invention has been extensively validated against observational data from multiple real tsunami events, demonstrating consistent detection capabilities across diverse geographic and bathymetric conditions. For example, during the 2011 Tohoku Japan tsunami, the algorithm successfully detected the tsunami at radar sites in Japan, the United States, and Chile, with detection times ranging from 15 to 40 minutes before coastal impact as measured by tide gauges. Similarly, the 2013 US East Coast meteotsunami was detected at New Jersey radar sites approximately 47 minutes before coastal impact, providing valuable warning time that was not available from conventional tsunami monitoring systems.

The technical specifications and operational parameters of the invention can be adapted to different HF radar systems and coastal environments. While the examples provided herein primarily reference SeaSonde® radars operating in the 12-42 MHz frequency range, the fundamental principles and algorithms are applicable to other HF radar systems, including WERA (WavE RAdar) systems, with appropriate adjustments for system-specific characteristics such as range resolution, angular resolution, and signal processing methods. The invention represents a significant advancement in tsunami warning capabilities, particularly for coastal communities that require precise, location-specific information about impending tsunami impacts, and provides a robust framework for integrating HF radar observations into comprehensive tsunami warning systems.
