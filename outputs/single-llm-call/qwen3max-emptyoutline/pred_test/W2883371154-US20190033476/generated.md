# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of geophysical risk assessment and early warning systems, and more specifically to methods and systems for determining enhanced global earthquake risk following large-magnitude seismic events. The invention provides a novel framework for identifying statistically significant increases in the probability of subsequent earthquakes occurring at teleseismic distances—up to and including antipodal regions—within a defined temporal window after a major earthquake. This framework enables the generation and dissemination of actionable earthquake alerts based on empirical evidence of remote triggering phenomena that extend beyond conventional aftershock zones and timeframes. The invention further encompasses computer-implemented systems configured to execute these methods in real time using global seismic catalogs, thereby facilitating improved short-term forecasting of seismic hazards on a planetary scale.

## BACKGROUND

Earthquake forecasting has traditionally been grounded in the assumption that seismic events are spatially and temporally independent outside of well-defined aftershock sequences governed by empirical laws such as Omori’s law and the Gutenberg-Richter relation. Under this paradigm, the occurrence of an earthquake is considered to influence only nearby faults within a limited spatial extent—typically defined by the rupture length of the mainshock—and over a decaying temporal window extending days to months. Consequently, operational earthquake early warning systems have focused almost exclusively on local or regional hazard mitigation, with little consideration given to potential global interdependencies between large seismic events separated by thousands of kilometers and occurring hours or days apart.

Despite this prevailing orthodoxy, anecdotal observations and isolated case studies have long suggested the possibility of remote triggering, wherein seismic waves from a large earthquake might induce fault slip at distant locations. Notable examples include the 1992 Landers earthquake in California, which was associated with increased geyser activity and small seismicity at Yellowstone National Park approximately 1,200 km away, and the 2004 Sumatra-Andaman earthquake, whose surface waves were correlated with triggered seismicity in Tibet. Similarly, the 2011 Tohoku earthquake in Japan was linked to changes in fault behavior along the San Andreas system in California. However, these observations have largely involved low-magnitude events (typically ≤M4), and the scientific community has remained skeptical about the existence of statistically robust, systematic triggering of significant earthquakes (≥M5.0) at teleseismic distances.

This skepticism stems from both theoretical and empirical challenges. Mechanistically, the dynamic stresses imparted by passing seismic waves diminish rapidly with distance, often falling below the threshold required to overcome static friction on tectonically loaded faults—unless those faults are already critically stressed near the end of their seismic cycle. Moreover, comprehensive statistical analyses of global earthquake catalogs have historically failed to detect consistent patterns of remote triggering beyond random background rates. For instance, a widely cited study examining 205 M>7 earthquakes found no significant increase in M>5 events at ranges greater than 1,000 km within 16 to 100 hours post-mainshock. Another analysis of 260 Mw>7 events concluded that fewer than 3% exhibited any evidence of triggering, and even then only for minor seismicity within 24 hours.

These null findings have reinforced the null hypothesis that global seismicity follows a Poisson process—i.e., events occur independently in space and time—with deviations attributable solely to local clustering processes like aftershocks. As a result, current risk models do not incorporate cross-regional dependencies, and emergency response protocols remain siloed by geography. Yet this approach may overlook a subtle but consequential phenomenon: the delayed, non-local amplification of seismic hazard following the world’s largest earthquakes.

Recent methodological advances now enable a more sensitive interrogation of this question. By rigorously declustering global seismic catalogs to remove aftershock contamination, normalizing observation windows, and applying binomial statistical tests to arc-distance binned data, it becomes possible to detect small but systematic deviations from baseline seismicity rates. Crucially, such analyses must account for the spherical geometry of the Earth, treating each mainshock as a local origin and measuring subsequent events in terms of great-circle (arc) distance. Furthermore, by distinguishing between prospective analyses (looking forward from source events) and retrospective analyses (looking backward from potential triggered events), researchers can triangulate evidence for a global forcing function that modulates seismic risk in predictable spatial and temporal patterns.

The absence of a practical system leveraging such insights represents a critical gap in disaster preparedness. While individual nations maintain robust seismic monitoring networks, there exists no integrated global alerting mechanism that synthesizes evidence of remote triggering to issue probabilistic warnings for regions far removed from the epicenter of a major quake. The present invention addresses this deficiency by providing a scientifically validated, computationally tractable method for identifying periods of elevated global earthquake risk and disseminating timely alerts to at-risk populations, infrastructure operators, and emergency management agencies worldwide.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and systems for determining enhanced earthquake risk at teleseismic distances following large-magnitude seismic events and for generating and disseminating corresponding earthquake alerts. In one aspect, the invention includes a computer-implemented method for determining enhancement of earthquake risk, comprising: receiving seismic data indicative of a source earthquake having a magnitude of at least 6.0; defining a three-day temporal window beginning at the occurrence time of the source earthquake; computing, for each candidate earthquake occurring within the three-day temporal window and having a magnitude of at least 5.0, an arc distance from the epicenter of the source earthquake; aggregating the candidate earthquakes into spatial bins based on arc distance, excluding bins within a predefined aftershock exclusion zone; comparing the observed count of candidate earthquakes in each spatial bin to a baseline count derived from a historical control group of non-source time periods; calculating a statistical p-value for each spatial bin using a binomial distribution model, wherein the p-value represents the probability under a null hypothesis of independent seismicity that the observed count would occur by chance; and identifying one or more spatial bins exhibiting statistically significant enhancement of earthquake risk when the p-value is below a predetermined significance threshold.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for generating and disseminating an earthquake alert, comprising: executing the aforementioned risk enhancement determination method; upon identifying at least one spatial bin with statistically significant risk enhancement, defining a high-risk region corresponding to the identified spatial bin on the Earth’s surface; generating an alert message specifying the high-risk region, the temporal validity of the alert, and the magnitude of risk enhancement relative to baseline; and transmitting the alert message to one or more recipient devices via a communication network.

In yet another aspect, the invention encompasses a system for providing earthquake alerts, comprising: a seismic data ingestion module configured to receive real-time seismic event data from one or more global seismic networks; a declustering module configured to filter aftershocks and foreshocks from the seismic event data using spatial and temporal windowing criteria based on event magnitude; a risk analysis engine configured to perform prospective and retrospective statistical analyses of post-mainshock seismicity using arc-distance binning and binomial hypothesis testing; an alert generation module configured to produce structured alert messages upon detection of statistically significant risk enhancement; and a dissemination interface configured to distribute the alert messages to authorized recipients via multiple communication channels.

The invention further includes a generalized computer environment comprising one or more processors, memory storing executable instructions, and input/output interfaces, wherein the instructions, when executed, cause the processor to implement any of the aforementioned methods. Additionally, the invention contemplates general considerations regarding normalization of relative rates across test sets of varying sample sizes, handling of overlapping source events, and integration with existing seismic monitoring infrastructures.

Collectively, these aspects enable the first operational system capable of detecting and communicating evidence-based, short-term increases in global earthquake risk resulting from remote triggering phenomena, thereby advancing the state of the art in seismic hazard mitigation.

### I. Terms

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed herein unless otherwise indicated by context:

“Source earthquake” refers to a seismic event of sufficient magnitude—typically at least 6.0 on the moment magnitude scale (Mw)—that is hypothesized to potentially trigger subsequent earthquakes at teleseismic distances. The source earthquake serves as the temporal and spatial origin for prospective risk analysis.

“Candidate earthquake” denotes any seismic event occurring within a defined temporal window following a source earthquake and meeting a minimum magnitude threshold—typically at least 5.0—that is evaluated for potential triggering association.

“Arc distance” means the angular separation between two points on the surface of a sphere, measured along the shortest path on the great circle connecting them, and expressed in degrees. Arc distance is used to quantify the spatial relationship between a source earthquake and candidate earthquakes in a geocentric reference frame.

“Aftershock exclusion zone” refers to a region surrounding the epicenter of a source earthquake, defined as three times the estimated rupture length of the fault, within which candidate earthquakes are excluded from analysis to prevent contamination by conventional aftershock sequences.

“Baseline count” signifies the expected number of earthquakes in a given spatial bin and temporal window under the null hypothesis of independent, Poisson-distributed seismicity, derived from a historical control group of non-source time periods sharing the same geographic origin.

“Relative rate” is the ratio of the observed count of candidate earthquakes in a spatial bin to the corresponding baseline count, serving as a measure of seismicity enhancement or suppression relative to historical norms.

“Statistical p-value” represents the probability, under the null hypothesis, of observing a count of earthquakes equal to or greater than the actual observed count in a given spatial bin, calculated using a binomial distribution model with mid-p correction to account for discrete count data.

“High-risk region” denotes a geographic area on the Earth’s surface corresponding to one or more spatial bins exhibiting statistically significant enhancement of earthquake risk, typically defined as a circular cap centered on the antipodal point of the source earthquake with an angular radius of 30 degrees.

“Temporal window” refers to a defined period following a source earthquake during which candidate earthquakes are monitored for triggering effects; in the preferred embodiment, this window spans three days (72 hours).

“Declustering” is the process of removing aftershocks and foreshocks from a seismic catalog using magnitude-dependent spatial and temporal windowing criteria, such that the remaining events approximate a Poisson process suitable for statistical hypothesis testing.

“Prospective analysis” describes a methodological approach wherein seismicity is examined forward in time from source earthquakes to detect increases in subsequent event rates.

“Retrospective analysis” refers to an inverse methodological approach wherein potential triggered events are examined backward in time to identify preceding source earthquakes.

“Global forcing function” is a conceptual model representing the spatial and temporal pattern of seismicity modulation induced by large earthquakes, characterized by maxima near 45 and 150 degrees arc distance and a minimum near 90 degrees, with peak impact in the antipodal region.

### II. Method for Determining Enhancement of Earthquake Risk

The present invention provides a rigorous, data-driven method for determining whether a large-magnitude earthquake enhances the risk of subsequent significant seismic events at teleseismic distances. This method operates by statistically comparing observed post-mainshock seismicity against a well-characterized baseline derived from decades of historical earthquake data, thereby isolating signal from noise in a manner that accounts for the Earth’s spherical geometry and the stochastic nature of seismic processes.

The method begins with the identification of a source earthquake, defined as any seismic event with a magnitude of at least 6.0 as reported by authoritative global seismic networks such as the United States Geological Survey (USGS). Upon detection of such an event, the system establishes a three-day temporal window commencing at the precise origin time of the source earthquake. This window duration is empirically determined based on extensive analysis showing that statistically significant triggering effects manifest predominantly within this interval, with peak enhancement occurring beyond the first 24 hours—a finding that distinguishes the present approach from prior art which often restricted analysis to shorter timeframes.

Within this temporal window, the system retrieves all candidate earthquakes—defined as events with magnitude ≥5.0—from a continuously updated global seismic catalog. Each candidate earthquake is then processed to compute its arc distance from the epicenter of the source earthquake. Arc distance is calculated using standard spherical trigonometry formulas that account for the latitude and longitude of both events, yielding an angular measure in degrees that reflects true great-circle separation on the Earth’s surface. This geometric transformation is essential because it enables consistent spatial binning regardless of the absolute location of the source earthquake, thereby facilitating global comparability.

To avoid conflating remote triggering with conventional aftershock processes, the method implements a multi-layered declustering protocol. First, a preliminary declustering step is applied using the Gardner-Knopoff windowing technique, which removes events falling within magnitude-dependent spatial and temporal proximity to any mainshock. Specifically, for each earthquake in the catalog, a spatial window (in kilometers) is computed as 10^(0.1238*M + 0.983) and a temporal window (in days) as 10^(0.032*M + 2.7389) for M≥6.5 or 10^(0.5409*M − 0.547) for smaller magnitudes, where M is the event magnitude. Any event occurring within both the spatial and temporal windows of a larger event is flagged as an aftershock or foreshock and excluded from further analysis.

Second, an additional aftershock exclusion zone is enforced during the binning phase. This zone is defined as three times the estimated rupture length of the source earthquake’s fault, with rupture length derived from empirical relationships between moment magnitude and rupture dimensions. For the largest events (M≥8.0), this exclusion zone extends up to 25 degrees of arc distance, necessitating that the first analyzed spatial bin be centered at 30 degrees. For smaller source events (M<7.5), the exclusion zone is less extensive, and the first bin is centered at 10 degrees. This dual-layer filtering ensures that the observed counts reflect genuine remote triggering rather than residual aftershock contamination.

The declustered candidate earthquakes are then aggregated into spatial bins of 10 degrees width, with bins offset every 5 degrees to enhance resolution through overlapping coverage. This binning strategy balances statistical power with spatial specificity, allowing detection of localized enhancements while mitigating the effects of sparse data in any single bin.

Concurrently, the system constructs a baseline count for each spatial bin by analyzing a historical control group. This control group comprises 5,355 non-overlapping three-day time periods sampled uniformly from the archival record spanning 1973 to 2016, all referenced to the same geographic origin as the source earthquake. For each control period, the number of declustered earthquakes ≥M5.0 falling within each spatial bin is tallied. The total counts across all control periods are summed and divided by the number of control periods to yield an expected baseline count per three-day window for each bin.

With both observed and baseline counts in hand, the system performs a binomial hypothesis test for each spatial bin. Under the null hypothesis of independent seismicity, the probability p of observing an earthquake in the test window versus any single control window is 1/5,356 (since there are 5,355 controls plus one test period). The observed count x in the test window and the total count n = x + baseline serve as inputs to the binomial cumulative distribution function. The p-value is computed as 1 minus the cumulative probability of observing up to x−0.5 events (mid-p correction), yielding the probability that the null hypothesis could produce a count as extreme as or more extreme than the observed value.

Bins with p-values below a predetermined significance threshold—typically 0.05, though more stringent thresholds like 0.01 may be used for high-confidence alerts—are flagged as exhibiting statistically significant enhancement of earthquake risk. The magnitude of enhancement is quantified by the relative rate (observed/baseline), which in validated cases often exceeds 1.5× and can reach 2× or higher in the antipodal region (140–175 degrees arc distance).

This method is further refined through normalization techniques that enable comparison across test sets of different sample sizes. When aggregating results from multiple source earthquakes of similar magnitude, relative rates are adjusted to a common sample size (e.g., 100 for Experiment 1, 1,200 for Experiment 2) while preserving the original p-values, ensuring that apparent differences in enhancement are not artifacts of varying statistical power.

By integrating these procedural elements—precise temporal windowing, rigorous declustering, spherical spatial binning, robust baseline modeling, and statistically sound hypothesis testing—the method provides an unprecedented capability to detect and quantify remote earthquake triggering with high confidence, forming the analytical foundation for the alert generation system described herein.

### III. Method for Generating and Disseminating an Earthquake Alert

Building upon the risk enhancement determination method, the present invention further provides a systematic approach for generating and disseminating actionable earthquake alerts to relevant stakeholders. This method transforms statistical findings into operational intelligence, enabling timely risk mitigation measures in regions identified as experiencing elevated seismic hazard due to remote triggering.

The alert generation process initiates automatically upon the detection of one or more spatial bins exhibiting statistically significant earthquake risk enhancement, as determined by the method described in Section II. Specifically, when the p-value for any bin falls below the significance threshold (e.g., 0.05) and the corresponding relative rate exceeds a minimum enhancement factor (e.g., 1.2× baseline), the system triggers an alert workflow.

The first step in this workflow is the definition of a high-risk region on the Earth’s surface. Based on empirical findings demonstrating that the maximum impact of remote triggering occurs within approximately 30 degrees of the antipodal point of the source earthquake, the high-risk region is delineated as a circular cap centered on the antipode with an angular radius of 30 degrees. This geometric construct captures the zone of highest relative rate enhancement observed in global analyses, particularly for source events of magnitude M≥8.0. In cases where multiple spatial bins show significant enhancement, the high-risk region may be expanded or subdivided to reflect the actual pattern of risk distribution—for example, if enhancement is observed both near 45 degrees and in the antipodal zone, separate alert regions may be defined.

Once the high-risk region is established, the system generates a structured alert message containing several key elements: (1) identification of the source earthquake, including its magnitude, epicenter coordinates, depth, and origin time; (2) specification of the high-risk region, including its geographic boundaries and center coordinates; (3) temporal validity of the alert, corresponding to the remainder of the three-day window following the source event; (4) quantitative assessment of risk enhancement, expressed as both a relative rate (e.g., “2.1× baseline”) and a statistical confidence level (e.g., “p < 0.01”); and (5) recommended actions for at-risk entities, such as heightened monitoring of critical infrastructure, review of emergency response plans, or public advisories depending on the severity and location of the risk.

The alert message is formatted according to standardized protocols compatible with existing emergency communication systems, such as the Common Alerting Protocol (CAP), ensuring interoperability with national and international disaster management frameworks. Metadata tags are included to facilitate automated processing by downstream systems, including fields for event type (“remote-triggered seismic risk”), certainty (“observed”), and urgency (“expected”).

Dissemination of the alert occurs through multiple redundant communication channels to maximize reach and reliability. Primary channels include satellite-based messaging systems (e.g., Global Maritime Distress and Safety System), internet-based alerting platforms (e.g., email, SMS, mobile push notifications), and integration with governmental emergency alert networks (e.g., Wireless Emergency Alerts in the United States). Recipients are pre-registered based on geographic jurisdiction, operational responsibility, or user subscription, and may include national geological surveys, civil protection agencies, utility operators, transportation authorities, and the general public in affected regions.

To prevent alert fatigue and ensure credibility, the system incorporates several validation checks before dissemination. These include confirmation that the source earthquake meets minimum magnitude criteria, verification that the enhancement signal persists across multiple overlapping spatial bins, and cross-validation with retrospective analysis (Experiment 2) when feasible. Additionally, alerts are tiered by severity: moderate enhancements (1.2–1.5×) may trigger internal notifications to scientific partners, while high enhancements (>1.5×) with strong statistical support (p < 0.01) activate public-facing alerts.

The temporal dynamics of risk enhancement are also factored into the alert lifecycle. As demonstrated in temporal pattern analyses, the relative rate of seismicity evolves over the three-day window, often peaking between 24–72 hours post-mainshock and sometimes followed by a quiescent period. Accordingly, the system may issue follow-up alerts to update risk assessments as new data accumulate, or to cancel alerts if subsequent observations fail to corroborate initial findings.

By converting complex statistical outputs into clear, actionable guidance delivered through resilient communication pathways, this method bridges the gap between scientific discovery and practical risk reduction, offering a novel tool for global seismic resilience.

### IV. System for Providing Earthquake Alerts

The present invention is implemented within a specialized computing system designed to execute the methods described herein with high reliability, speed, and scalability. This system integrates real-time data ingestion, advanced statistical processing, and multi-channel alert dissemination into a cohesive architecture optimized for global seismic risk monitoring.

At the core of the system is a seismic data ingestion module responsible for acquiring and preprocessing raw earthquake reports from authoritative sources. Primary data feeds include the USGS Comprehensive Catalog (ComCat), the International Seismological Centre (ISC) bulletin, and regional networks such as the European-Mediterranean Seismological Centre (EMSC). Data are received via secure APIs or file transfer protocols at intervals as frequent as every minute, ensuring near-real-time awareness of global seismic activity. Each incoming event record is parsed to extract essential parameters: origin time, latitude, longitude, depth, magnitude type and value, and associated uncertainty estimates.

Coupled with the ingestion module is a declustering engine that applies the multi-stage filtering protocol described earlier. This engine maintains an in-memory representation of the global seismic catalog, dynamically updating it with new events while applying spatial and temporal windowing rules to identify and exclude aftershocks and foreshocks. The declustering logic is implemented as a set of magnitude-dependent functions that compute exclusion radii and durations on-the-fly, with results cached for efficiency. The output is a clean, Poisson-like catalog suitable for statistical analysis.

The risk analysis engine constitutes the analytical heart of the system. It comprises two parallel processing pipelines implementing Experiment 1 (prospective analysis) and Experiment 2 (retrospective analysis). The prospective pipeline activates whenever a qualifying source earthquake (M≥6.0) is detected. It spawns a dedicated analysis thread that: (1) defines the three-day observation window; (2) queries the declustered catalog for candidate events (M≥5.0) within that window; (3) computes arc distances from the source epicenter; (4) bins events into 10-degree spatial intervals with 5-degree offsets; (5) retrieves precomputed baseline statistics for the source location; and (6) executes binomial hypothesis tests for each bin. The retrospective pipeline operates continuously in the background, scanning for clusters of candidate events that might indicate triggering, then searching backward for potential source earthquakes.

Both pipelines leverage a shared statistical library that implements the binomial p-value calculation with mid-p correction, along with utilities for relative rate normalization and collective p-value aggregation (e.g., combining evidence across multiple bins using Fisher’s method). Computational efficiency is achieved through vectorized operations and parallelization across spatial bins.

When either pipeline detects statistically significant risk enhancement, it invokes the alert generation module. This module orchestrates the creation of alert messages by: (1) determining the high-risk region geometry based on enhancement patterns; (2) populating message templates with event-specific data; (3) applying severity-based filtering rules; and (4) formatting outputs in standard protocols like CAP. The module also logs all alert decisions for audit and performance evaluation.

The dissemination interface manages outbound communications through a pluggable architecture supporting multiple transport mechanisms. Each channel (e.g., SMS gateway, email server, satellite modem) is abstracted as a service adapter that handles protocol-specific encoding and delivery. A queuing system ensures reliable message transmission even during network outages, with automatic retries and failure notifications. Recipient lists are maintained in a relational database with fine-grained access controls, allowing jurisdiction-specific targeting.

The entire system is monitored by a health-check subsystem that tracks data latency, processing throughput, and alert delivery success rates. Anomalous conditions trigger automated diagnostics and operator notifications. Security is enforced through encrypted communications, role-based access control, and regular vulnerability assessments.

Deployed on cloud infrastructure with auto-scaling capabilities, the system can handle spikes in seismic activity without degradation in performance. Its modular design facilitates integration with external platforms such as tsunami warning centers or infrastructure monitoring systems, enabling a holistic approach to geohazard response.

### V. A Generalized Computer Environment

The methods and systems of the present invention are implemented within a generalized computer environment comprising standard hardware and software components adapted to execute the specialized seismic risk analysis workflows described herein. This environment ensures portability, maintainability, and compatibility with existing computational infrastructures while providing the performance necessary for real-time operation.

The foundational hardware includes one or more central processing units (CPUs) with multi-core architectures capable of parallel task execution, sufficient random-access memory (RAM) to hold in-memory representations of global seismic catalogs and intermediate analysis results (typically 64 GB or more), and high-speed storage subsystems (solid-state drives or NVMe arrays) for rapid data access. Network interfaces support gigabit Ethernet or faster connections to ensure low-latency data ingestion from global seismic networks. For enhanced computational throughput, the system may optionally incorporate graphics processing units (GPUs) or field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) to accelerate statistical calculations, though the core algorithms are designed to run efficiently on CPU-only systems.

The operating system is a POSIX-compliant platform such as Linux or Unix, chosen for its stability, security features, and support for scientific computing libraries. The software stack is built primarily in Python, leveraging its rich ecosystem for data science (NumPy, SciPy, Pandas) and geospatial analysis (ObsPy, Cartopy). Critical performance-sensitive components—such as arc distance computation and binomial p-value calculation—are implemented in compiled languages like C or Cython and exposed to Python via extension modules. Database interactions utilize SQLAlchemy for ORM abstraction, with PostgreSQL serving as the primary relational backend due to its robustness and geospatial extensions (PostGIS).

Memory management is optimized through lazy loading and caching strategies. Global baseline statistics, which are computationally expensive to generate but change infrequently, are precomputed offline and stored in compressed binary formats for rapid retrieval. Real-time event data are buffered in ring buffers to minimize disk I/O during peak ingestion periods. The system employs a microservices architecture, with each functional module (ingestion, declustering, analysis, alerting) running as an independent process or container, enabling isolated scaling and fault tolerance.

Execution is orchestrated by a workflow management system such as Apache Airflow or Luigi, which schedules analysis tasks based on event triggers and enforces dependency constraints. Logging and monitoring are handled by the ELK stack (Elasticsearch, Logstash, Kibana) or equivalent, providing real-time visibility into system performance and alert history. Configuration parameters—such as magnitude thresholds, significance levels, and recipient lists—are stored in version-controlled YAML files, allowing reproducible deployments across development, testing, and production environments.

Security is implemented at multiple layers: transport layer security (TLS) encrypts all external communications; API endpoints require OAuth2 authentication; database access is restricted via virtual private clouds (VPCs) and firewall rules; and regular penetration testing validates defenses against cyber threats. Backup and disaster recovery procedures ensure data integrity through daily snapshots and offsite replication.

This generalized environment strikes a balance between specialization and flexibility, enabling deployment on-premises at geological survey offices, in commercial cloud platforms (AWS, Azure, GCP), or in hybrid configurations tailored to institutional requirements. Its adherence to open standards and modular design ensures longevity and adaptability as seismic monitoring technologies evolve.

### VI. General Considerations

Several important considerations underpin the practical implementation and interpretation of the invention’s methods and systems. First, the normalization of relative rates across test sets of varying sample sizes is essential for valid cross-magnitude comparisons. As demonstrated in the research, larger source events (M≥8.0) exhibit stronger triggering signals, but this effect could be confounded by differences in statistical power if not properly normalized. The invention addresses this by adjusting bin counts proportionally to a common sample size while preserving original p-values, ensuring that observed differences reflect true physical phenomena rather than sampling artifacts.

Second, the handling of overlapping source events—where multiple large earthquakes occur within the same three-day window—requires careful treatment to avoid double-counting or misattribution of triggered events. The system resolves such scenarios by computing the union of high-risk regions from all active sources and weighting contributions based on source magnitude and temporal proximity. In cases where antipodal zones overlap, the intersection area is flagged as having compounded risk, as evidenced by historical examples where subsequent events clustered precisely within overlapping caps.

Third, integration with existing seismic monitoring infrastructures presents both opportunities and challenges. While the invention complements rather than replaces local early warning systems, coordination is necessary to avoid conflicting alerts. Protocols should be established for hierarchical alerting, where global remote-triggering alerts serve as supplementary context to regional ground-motion-based warnings. Additionally, false alarm mitigation strategies—such as requiring corroboration from both prospective and retrospective analyses—enhance system credibility.

Fourth, the temporal evolution of risk enhancement implies that static alerts may become outdated. Future enhancements could incorporate machine learning models trained on historical triggering patterns to forecast the trajectory of relative rates over the full three-day window, enabling dynamic alert updates. Similarly, incorporating real-time strainmeter or GPS data might refine the identification of critically stressed faults susceptible to triggering.

Finally, ethical and legal considerations surround the dissemination of probabilistic risk information. Clear communication of uncertainty—expressing risk as relative rates with confidence intervals rather than deterministic predictions—is crucial to prevent panic or complacency. Liability frameworks should distinguish between scientific alerts (intended for expert interpretation) and public warnings (requiring higher certainty thresholds), with appropriate disclaimers regarding the inherent limitations of earthquake forecasting.

Collectively, these considerations ensure that the invention is deployed responsibly, maximizing its potential to save lives and reduce economic losses while maintaining scientific rigor and public trust.
