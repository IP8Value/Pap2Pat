# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The evolution of wireless communication systems has been driven by the dual imperatives of increasing spectral efficiency and ensuring equitable resource allocation among users. In the context of 3GPP Long-Term Evolution (LTE) networks, which operate under a packet-switched architecture, the downlink packet scheduler assumes a pivotal role in determining overall system performance. The LTE standard specifies ambitious targets, including peak data rates of 100 Mbps in the downlink and 50 Mbps in the uplink, enhanced throughput at cell boundaries, improved spectral efficiency, and support for scalable bandwidths. Achieving these objectives necessitates sophisticated scheduling mechanisms that balance competing demands such as throughput maximization, user fairness, quality of service (QoS) guarantees, and power efficiency.

Conventional packet scheduling algorithms have primarily focused on optimizing either throughput or fairness, often at the expense of other critical performance metrics. The Round Robin (RR) algorithm, for instance, ensures strict temporal fairness by cyclically allocating resources to all active users, but it fails to exploit channel variations and consequently yields suboptimal throughput. Conversely, the Maximum Throughput (MT) algorithm prioritizes users with the best instantaneous channel conditions, thereby maximizing aggregate system throughput; however, this approach leads to severe unfairness, as users located near cell edges or experiencing poor channel states are systematically starved of resources. The Proportional Fair (PF) algorithm attempts to strike a balance between these extremes by allocating resources based on the ratio of instantaneous data rate to historical average throughput, thereby providing a degree of both throughput efficiency and fairness. Despite these advancements, existing schedulers remain fundamentally limited by their exclusive reliance on channel quality and throughput-related metrics, neglecting the critical dimension of transmit power consumption.

In modern cellular networks, particularly those operating under stringent energy constraints or dense deployment scenarios, power efficiency has emerged as a paramount concern. Base stations, especially in macro-cellular deployments, constitute significant contributors to the overall energy footprint of mobile networks. Moreover, in heterogeneous networks comprising small cells, power limitations are even more acute due to smaller form factors and limited backhaul capabilities. Traditional scheduling algorithms do not explicitly account for the energy cost associated with transmitting a given number of bits to a user equipment (UE). This omission becomes particularly problematic in mixed-traffic environments where real-time (RT) services—such as voice or video streaming—coexist with non-real-time (NRT) best-effort data traffic. RT traffic typically requires guaranteed bit rates and is highly sensitive to delay, necessitating priority treatment, while NRT traffic can opportunistically utilize residual resources. However, without a mechanism to evaluate the power efficiency of potential transmissions, conventional schedulers may allocate resources to UEs that require disproportionately high transmit power per bit, thereby depleting the available power budget inefficiently and limiting the capacity available for other users.

The inefficiency of current approaches is further exacerbated in multi-cell environments where inter-cell interference and variable path losses significantly affect the required transmit power. A UE located at the cell edge may report a low Channel Quality Indicator (CQI), prompting a conservative Modulation and Coding Scheme (MCS) selection. Under an MT scheduler, such a UE would rarely be scheduled, yet when it is, the eNB must expend substantial power to overcome path loss and interference. In contrast, a centrally located UE with excellent CQI might be scheduled frequently with minimal power expenditure per bit. However, neither scenario is evaluated from the perspective of power-per-bit efficiency. Consequently, there exists a fundamental gap in the state of the art: no known scheduling algorithm systematically incorporates the ratio of transmit power to the number of transmittable bits as a primary scheduling metric.

This deficiency is particularly acute given that LTE systems employ Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access (OFDMA) in the downlink, which enables fine-grained frequency-domain resource allocation through Physical Resource Blocks (PRBs). Each PRB can be independently assigned to different UEs based on their channel conditions across the bandwidth. While this flexibility allows for frequency-selective scheduling, the absence of a power-aware metric means that the scheduler cannot identify which UE-PRB combinations offer the most energy-efficient transmission opportunities. Furthermore, the two-phase scheduling architecture commonly adopted in LTE—comprising a Time Domain Packet Scheduler (TDPS) that selects a Scheduling Candidate Set (SCS) and a Frequency Domain Packet Scheduler (FDPS) that allocates PRBs to the SCS—has not been leveraged to embed power efficiency considerations at both stages.

Prior art has explored various adaptations for specific traffic types: margin adaptation for constant bit rate (CBR) RT traffic to minimize power under rate constraints, and rate adaptation for best-effort NRT traffic to maximize throughput under power constraints. However, these approaches are applied separately and do not provide a unified framework for joint optimization in mixed-traffic scenarios. Additionally, while some studies have considered power minimization in resource allocation, they typically do so in the context of static optimization problems rather than dynamic, real-time packet scheduling under stochastic traffic arrivals and time-varying channels. There remains a pressing need for a scheduling algorithm that dynamically evaluates, for each candidate UE-PRB pair, the efficiency of power utilization—defined as the inverse of transmit power per bit—and uses this metric to guide both time-domain candidate selection and frequency-domain resource assignment.

The present invention addresses these longstanding deficiencies by introducing a novel Minimum Transmit Power-based (MP) packet scheduling algorithm that fundamentally reorients the scheduling objective toward power efficiency without sacrificing throughput or fairness. By leveraging readily available CQI reports and employing link adaptation to determine feasible MCS levels, the MP scheduler computes, for each UE and PRB, the minimum transmit power required to successfully deliver a certain number of bits. It then prioritizes allocations that minimize the ratio of this power to the number of bits transmitted, effectively identifying the most energy-efficient transmission opportunities. This approach not only conserves transmit power but also enhances spectral efficiency by enabling more users to be served within the same power budget, particularly benefiting cell-edge users who would otherwise be neglected by throughput-centric schedulers. The following sections detail the structure, operation, and advantages of this innovative scheduling methodology.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel Minimum Transmit Power-based (MP) packet scheduling algorithm for use in wireless communication systems, particularly in 3GPP Long-Term Evolution (LTE) networks employing Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access (OFDMA) in the downlink. The core innovation lies in the formulation and application of a new scheduling metric that prioritizes the allocation of Physical Resource Blocks (PRBs) to User Equipments (UEs) based on the ratio of the minimum required transmit power to the number of transmittable bits, thereby achieving superior power efficiency, enhanced system throughput, and improved user fairness compared to conventional scheduling algorithms.

In accordance with the invention, the MP scheduler operates within the standard two-phase packet scheduling architecture of LTE, comprising a Time Domain Packet Scheduler (TDPS) and a Frequency Domain Packet Scheduler (FDPS). During the TDPS phase, a Scheduling Candidate Set (SCS) is selected from the pool of active UEs. Unlike conventional TDPS implementations that rely solely on metrics such as buffer occupancy, delay, or historical throughput, the TDPS according to the present invention incorporates the proposed power-per-bit efficiency metric into its candidate selection process. Specifically, for each UE, the scheduler computes an estimate of the minimum transmit power required to deliver data over potential PRBs, normalized by the corresponding number of bits that can be transmitted using the appropriate Modulation and Coding Scheme (MCS) level determined via link adaptation based on Channel Quality Indicator (CQI) reports. UEs exhibiting higher power efficiency—that is, lower transmit power per bit—are preferentially included in the SCS.

Subsequently, during the FDPS phase, PRBs are allocated to the UEs in the SCS. The FDPS according to the present invention assigns each available PRB to the UE that minimizes the ratio of required transmit power to the number of bits transmittable over that PRB. This is mathematically expressed by selecting the UE *s* and PRB *n* that maximize the scheduling metric *M(s, n)*, defined as the excess channel gain normalized by the received power per bit. The excess channel gain is computed as the difference between the actual channel power *g<sub>s,n</sub>* and the minimum channel gain *g<sub>min</sub>(b<sub>s,n</sub>)* required to support the transmission of *b<sub>s,n</sub>* bits. The received power per bit is derived from the noise variance and the target bit error rate associated with the selected MCS level. By prioritizing allocations with higher excess channel gain relative to the power cost per bit, the MP scheduler ensures that each PRB is utilized in the most energy-efficient manner possible.

A key technical advantage of the invention is its ability to simultaneously improve both system throughput and fairness, resolving the traditional trade-off inherent in prior art schedulers. Conventional Maximum Throughput (MT) algorithms achieve high aggregate throughput but exhibit poor fairness, as they consistently favor UEs with excellent channel conditions, typically located near the base station. In contrast, the MP scheduler, by considering power efficiency, allocates resources to UEs that can achieve high data rates with minimal power expenditure—even if their absolute channel quality is moderate—as well as to cell-edge UEs whose channel conditions, while poor, may still support efficient transmission when evaluated on a per-bit power basis. This results in a more equitable distribution of resources without compromising overall system capacity. Simulation results demonstrate that the MP-MP configuration—where both TDPS and FDPS employ the MP algorithm—achieves up to 18% higher average user throughput compared to MT-MT under saturated network conditions and provides a 70% throughput gain at the cell boundary relative to Round Robin (RR) scheduling.

Furthermore, the invention offers significant power savings. By minimizing the transmit power required per bit, the MP scheduler enables the same level of cell throughput to be achieved with substantially lower total transmit power. For instance, simulations show that the MP-MP algorithm can sustain an average cell throughput of over 10 Mbps with a transmit power of 30 dBm, whereas the MT-MT algorithm requires approximately 38 dBm to achieve the same throughput—a reduction of 8 dBm. This power efficiency is particularly valuable in energy-constrained deployments and contributes to reduced operational costs and environmental impact.

The invention is especially effective in mixed-traffic environments comprising both real-time (RT) and non-real-time (NRT) services. RT traffic, characterized by constant bit rate requirements and strict delay constraints, is assigned higher priority and allocated resources first. The MP scheduler ensures that these allocations are performed in a power-efficient manner, preserving residual power for NRT traffic. NRT traffic, being best-effort, then utilizes the remaining power budget to maximize throughput, again guided by the power-per-bit metric. This hierarchical yet efficiency-aware approach ensures QoS compliance for RT services while optimizing the utilization of residual resources for NRT services.

Importantly, the computational complexity of the MP scheduler is manageable and suitable for real-time implementation. The scheduling metric can be simplified through precomputation of key parameters such as the minimum channel gain *g<sub>min</sub>(b)* and the function *ω(b)* for all feasible values of *b*, which correspond to the number of bits supported by each MCS level. During runtime, the scheduler only needs to evaluate a simple expression involving the measured channel power and these precomputed values, avoiding complex iterative calculations.

In summary, the present invention introduces a paradigm shift in packet scheduling by embedding power efficiency as a first-order design criterion. The resulting MP algorithm delivers concurrent improvements in throughput, fairness, and energy consumption, making it ideally suited for next-generation wireless networks where spectral and power resources are increasingly scarce and must be utilized with maximum efficiency.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a Minimum Transmit Power-based (MP) packet scheduling algorithm designed for deployment in 3GPP Long-Term Evolution (LTE) wireless communication systems. The following detailed description sets forth exemplary embodiments of the invention, including its architectural integration, operational principles, mathematical formulation, and performance characteristics. It will be understood by those skilled in the art that the invention is not limited to the specific embodiments described herein but encompasses all modifications, equivalents, and alternatives falling within the spirit and scope of the appended claims.

In an exemplary embodiment, the MP scheduler is implemented within the evolved Node B (eNB) of an LTE network and operates in accordance with the standard two-phase packet scheduling framework. This framework comprises a Time Domain Packet Scheduler (TDPS) and a Frequency Domain Packet Scheduler (FDPS), which together manage the allocation of downlink resources to User Equipments (UEs) over successive Transmission Time Intervals (TTIs), each of duration one millisecond. The eNB is assumed to maintain Layer 2 data buffers for each active UE, receive periodic Channel Quality Indicator (CQI) reports from UEs, and employ link adaptation to select appropriate Modulation and Coding Scheme (MCS) levels for data transmission. The physical layer utilizes Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiple Access (OFDMA), wherein the available bandwidth is partitioned into discrete Physical Resource Blocks (PRBs), each consisting of multiple subcarriers over a fixed time duration. In a typical 5 MHz LTE deployment, 25 PRBs are available per TTI.

The operation of the MP scheduler begins with traffic classification. Upon arrival at the eNB, incoming packets are classified by a traffic classifier into distinct categories based on their Quality of Service (QoS) requirements. In the exemplary embodiment, two primary traffic types are considered: real-time (RT) traffic and non-real-time (NRT) traffic. RT traffic, exemplified by voice or video streaming applications, exhibits constant bit rate (CBR) characteristics and is subject to stringent delay constraints. NRT traffic, such as web browsing or file downloads, follows a best-effort (BE) model with no guaranteed bit rate and greater tolerance for delay. The classifier assigns RT traffic a higher scheduling priority than NRT traffic and maintains separate queues for each type. This prioritization ensures that RT traffic is serviced first during each TTI, in compliance with its QoS requirements.

For each traffic type, the TDPS phase is executed to determine a Scheduling Candidate Set (SCS). The SCS comprises a subset of UEs that are eligible for resource allocation in the subsequent FDPS phase. The purpose of this two-stage approach is to reduce computational complexity by limiting the number of UEs that must be considered during the more granular PRB allocation process. In conventional systems, the TDPS may select candidates based on metrics such as buffer occupancy, packet delay, or historical throughput. However, in the present invention, the TDPS employs a novel power-efficiency metric derived from the ratio of minimum required transmit power to the number of transmittable bits.

Specifically, for each UE *s* in the active set, the TDPS computes, for each PRB *n*, the minimum transmit power *P<sub>s,n</sub>* required to successfully transmit *b<sub>s,n</sub>* bits, where *b<sub>s,n</sub>* is determined by the MCS level selected via link adaptation based on the CQI reported by UE *s* for PRB *n*. The link adaptation process maps the CQI to an MCS index, which in turn defines the modulation order, coding rate, and thus the number of bits that can be conveyed per PRB. The minimum transmit power is calculated using the Shannon-Hartley theorem adapted for practical coding schemes, yielding:

*P<sub>s,n</sub> = f(b<sub>s,n</sub>) / g<sub>s,n</sub>*,

where *g<sub>s,n</sub>* is the channel power gain for UE *s* on PRB *n*, and *f(b<sub>s,n</sub>)* is a function representing the minimum received signal power required to support *b<sub>s,n</sub>* bits with a target bit error rate. The function *f(b)* is dependent on the noise variance *σ²* and the specific characteristics of the employed coding and modulation scheme, and can be precomputed for all possible values of *b* corresponding to the LTE MCS table.

The TDPS then evaluates the power efficiency of each UE as the inverse of the average transmit power per bit across all PRBs, or alternatively, as the maximum value of *b<sub>s,n</sub> / P<sub>s,n</sub>* over *n*. UEs with higher power efficiency—i.e., those capable of transmitting more bits per unit of power—are preferentially selected for inclusion in the SCS. The size of the SCS may be fixed or dynamically adjusted based on system load, but it is typically much smaller than the total number of active UEs to ensure computational tractability.

Following the TDPS phase, the FDPS allocates the available PRBs to the UEs in the SCS. In the exemplary embodiment, PRB allocation proceeds iteratively: at each step, the scheduler selects the UE-PRB pair that maximizes the MP scheduling metric *M(s, n)*, defined as:

*M(s, n) = Δ<sub>s,n</sub> / (f(b<sub>s,n</sub>) / b<sub>s,n</sub>)*,

where *Δ<sub>s,n</sub> = g<sub>s,n</sub> – g<sub>min</sub>(b<sub>s,n</sub>)* is the excess channel gain, and *g<sub>min</sub>(b<sub>s,n</sub>) = f(b<sub>s,n</sub>) / P<sub>max</sub>* is the minimum channel gain required to support *b<sub>s,n</sub>* bits given the maximum allowable transmit power *P<sub>max</sub>* per PRB. The term *f(b<sub>s,n</sub>) / b<sub>s,n</sub>* represents the received power per bit, which increases nonlinearly with *b<sub>s,n</sub>* due to the exponential relationship between required signal-to-noise ratio and spectral efficiency.

The metric *M(s, n)* thus captures two essential aspects of power-efficient scheduling: (1) the margin by which the actual channel gain exceeds the minimum required for successful transmission (*Δ<sub>s,n</sub>*), and (2) the cost of transmission in terms of power per bit. A high value of *M(s, n)* indicates that the UE can transmit a substantial number of bits with a large safety margin and low power cost per bit. The FDPS assigns the PRB *n* to the UE *s* that maximizes *M(s, n)*, removes *n* from the pool of available PRBs, and repeats the process until all PRBs are allocated or the transmit power budget is exhausted.

To facilitate real-time implementation, the computation of *M(s, n)* is optimized through precomputation. Specifically, for each possible value of *b* (corresponding to each MCS level), the values of *g<sub>min</sub>(b)* and *ω(b) = b / f(b)* are calculated offline and stored in lookup tables. During runtime, the scheduler retrieves *b<sub>s,n</sub>* from the link adaptation module, accesses the precomputed *g<sub>min</sub>(b<sub>s,n</sub>)* and *ω(b<sub>s,n</sub>)*, and computes:

*M(s, n) = (g<sub>s,n</sub> – g<sub>min</sub>(b<sub>s,n</sub>)) · ω(b<sub>s,n</sub>)*.

This simplification reduces the per-iteration computational burden to a few arithmetic operations, making the MP scheduler feasible for deployment in commercial eNBs.

In mixed-traffic scenarios, the MP scheduler operates in a hierarchical manner. First, the RT traffic queue is processed: the TDPS selects RT UEs for the SCS based on power efficiency, and the FDPS allocates PRBs to these UEs until their guaranteed bit rate (GBR) requirements are met or the RT queue is emptied. Only after RT traffic has been fully accommodated does the scheduler proceed to NRT traffic, applying the same MP metric to allocate any remaining PRBs and transmit power. This ensures QoS compliance for delay-sensitive services while maximizing the power-efficient utilization of residual resources for best-effort traffic.

The advantages of the MP scheduler are manifold. By prioritizing power-efficient transmissions, it conserves the eNB’s transmit power budget, enabling more UEs to be served within the same power constraint. This is particularly beneficial for cell-edge UEs, which, despite having lower absolute channel gains, may still exhibit favorable power-per-bit ratios when assigned lower MCS levels. Consequently, the MP scheduler achieves a more balanced throughput distribution across the cell, improving fairness without sacrificing aggregate throughput. Simulation studies conducted under 3GPP-compliant LTE parameters confirm that the MP-MP configuration (MP algorithm used in both TDPS and FDPS) outperforms conventional MT, RR, and PF schedulers in terms of average user throughput, cell-edge throughput, and fairness index. For example, with 25 UEs per cell, MP-MP yields an 18% higher average user throughput than MT-MT and a 70% higher cell-edge throughput than RR-RR. Moreover, it achieves a given cell throughput level with up to 8 dBm less transmit power than MT-MT.

It will be appreciated that the invention is not limited to the specific two-phase architecture described. In alternative embodiments, the MP metric may be applied in a single-phase scheduler that jointly considers time and frequency domain allocation. Furthermore, while the exemplary embodiment focuses on downlink scheduling in LTE, the principles of the invention are equally applicable to uplink scheduling, other OFDMA-based systems such as 5G NR, and even non-OFDMA systems where power-efficient resource allocation is desired.

In conclusion, the Minimum Transmit Power-based packet scheduling algorithm represents a significant advancement in wireless resource management. By introducing a scheduling metric that explicitly accounts for the energy cost of data transmission, the invention enables simultaneous improvements in throughput, fairness, and power efficiency—addressing critical challenges in modern and future wireless networks.
