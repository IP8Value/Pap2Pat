# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of medical diagnostics and post-operative monitoring, and more particularly to systems, methods, and devices for the early detection and prediction of surgical site infections (SSIs) through continuous, non-invasive, multi-modal sensing of physiological biomarkers at or near a surgical wound site. The invention encompasses a wearable bio-signal acquisition system configured to monitor tissue oxygen saturation (StO₂), peri-wound temperature, and bioimpedance in real time, coupled with an ensemble machine learning model trained to interpret these signals and generate predictive alerts regarding the onset or presence of superficial incisional SSIs. The invention is especially directed toward applications in both inpatient and outpatient settings, where timely identification of infection can significantly reduce morbidity, mortality, hospital readmissions, and healthcare expenditures. The system is designed for use following a broad range of surgical procedures—including but not limited to cardiothoracic, orthopedic, gynecologic, vascular, transplant, and head and neck surgeries—and is particularly suited for deployment in scenarios where patients are discharged prior to the manifestation of clinical signs of infection. By leveraging digital biomarkers derived from continuous physiological monitoring, the invention provides a novel, objective, and scalable approach to post-surgical surveillance that overcomes the limitations of conventional, intermittent, and subjective clinical assessment methods.

## BACKGROUND

Surgical site infections (SSIs) represent one of the most prevalent and consequential complications following surgical intervention, affecting hundreds of thousands of patients annually across global healthcare systems. Despite decades of advances in sterile technique, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and perioperative care protocols, SSIs remain a persistent clinical challenge with profound implications for patient outcomes and healthcare economics. SSIs are broadly categorized into three types: superficial-incisional (involving only the skin and subcutaneous tissue), deep-incisional (extending into deeper soft tissues such as muscle and fascia), and organ/space infections (affecting any part of the anatomy opened or manipulated during surgery). Among these, superficial-incisional SSIs are the most frequently observed, accounting for more than half of all reported cases across diverse surgical specialties. The clinical burden of SSIs is substantial: in the United States alone, an SSI is associated with an average prolongation of hospital stay by nearly ten days and a two- to eleven-fold increase in the risk of mortality. Moreover, the economic impact is staggering, with aggregate annual costs exceeding $10 billion and individual case expenses averaging approximately $25,000—figures that include extended hospitalization, additional diagnostic testing, prolonged antibiotic therapy, and potential reoperation.

A critical and growing concern in modern surgical care is the increasing shift toward outpatient and short-stay procedures, driven by cost containment initiatives and technological advancements in minimally invasive surgery. As a result, an estimated 69% of all SSIs now manifest after the patient has been discharged from the hospital. This trend presents a formidable obstacle to effective infection surveillance, as traditional post-discharge monitoring relies heavily on passive, infrequent, and often unreliable mechanisms such as patient self-reporting via telephone interviews, mailed questionnaires, or scheduled outpatient clinic visits. These approaches suffer from significant limitations, including poor patient compliance, subjective interpretation of symptoms, delayed reporting, and the absence of objective physiological data. Consequently, SSIs are frequently diagnosed only after they have progressed to an advanced stage, at which point therapeutic interventions are less effective and complications more severe. Indeed, studies have demonstrated that a delay of just 45 hours in the initiation of appropriate treatment for an SSI can increase the odds of infection-related death by nearly fourfold.

The pathophysiology of wound infection involves complex and dynamic alterations in local tissue physiology that precede the emergence of overt clinical signs such as erythema, swelling, purulence, or fever. During normal healing, the wound microenvironment maintains a tightly regulated balance of oxygen tension, pH, temperature, and cellular activity to support tissue repair and microbial defense. In contrast, the presence of pathogenic organisms—most commonly *Staphylococcus aureus*, including both methicillin-susceptible (MSSA) and resistant (MRSA) strains—triggers a robust inflammatory response characterized by increased metabolic demand, leukocyte infiltration, vascular permeability, and local heat production. These processes lead to measurable deviations in key physiological parameters: tissue oxygen saturation may initially rise due to hyperemia before declining as bacterial metabolism consumes available oxygen; peri-wound temperature increases as a result of localized inflammation; and bioimpedance—the opposition of biological tissue to alternating electrical current—decreases due to changes in extracellular fluid composition, edema, and cellular necrosis. Critically, these biophysical changes occur hours to days before visible symptoms appear, creating a window of opportunity for early intervention.

In recent years, researchers have explored the use of sensor-based technologies to capture these subtle physiological shifts. For instance, near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) has been employed to assess subcutaneous oxygen partial pressure, with one study demonstrating its ability to predict SSI after colorectal surgery with moderate sensitivity and specificity. Similarly, smart wound dressings integrating pH and temperature sensors have shown promise in detecting bacterial proliferation in vitro. However, these prior approaches are typically unimodal, capturing only a single aspect of the wound’s physiological state, and thus lack the robustness required for reliable clinical prediction. Furthermore, many existing systems are either too bulky, power-intensive, or insufficiently validated in relevant biological models to support widespread adoption. There remains a pressing need for an integrated, multi-parameter sensing platform capable of continuous, real-time monitoring of the surgical site, combined with advanced analytical methods that can synthesize heterogeneous data streams into actionable clinical insights.

Machine learning offers a powerful framework for interpreting complex, high-dimensional biosignal data. Ensemble methods—which combine predictions from multiple base models—have proven particularly effective in biomedical applications due to their ability to reduce variance, mitigate overfitting, and improve generalization. Techniques such as bagging (bootstrap aggregating) and stacking (meta-learning) enhance predictive performance by leveraging the strengths of diverse algorithms while compensating for individual weaknesses. When applied to imbalanced datasets—a common scenario in medical diagnostics where positive cases (e.g., infections) are far fewer than negatives—specialized strategies like class balancing and cost-sensitive learning become essential to avoid biased models that favor the majority class. Despite these methodological advances, no prior art has successfully integrated a multi-modal biosensor array with a rigorously validated ensemble machine learning architecture specifically designed for the early prediction of SSIs in a physiologically relevant animal model that closely mimics human wound healing.

The porcine model has emerged as the gold standard for preclinical wound research due to striking anatomical, immunological, and physiological similarities between pig and human skin. Both species exhibit a thick epidermis, comparable dermal structure with dense elastic fibers, similar epidermal turnover rates, and sparse hair coverage. Importantly, studies have shown that findings from porcine wound healing experiments demonstrate 78% concordance with human clinical outcomes—significantly higher than rodent or in vitro models. This high translational fidelity makes the pig an ideal testbed for evaluating novel diagnostic technologies intended for eventual human use. Prior attempts to develop SSI prediction tools have largely relied on retrospective analysis of electronic health records or static imaging modalities, which fail to capture the dynamic, time-evolving nature of the infection process. What is missing from the current landscape is a prospective, longitudinal, and multi-parametric approach that leverages the porcine model to validate a wearable sensing system capable of generating continuous digital biomarkers predictive of infection onset.

Accordingly, there exists a clear and unmet need for a comprehensive solution that bridges the gap between physiological sensing and intelligent analytics to enable proactive, rather than reactive, management of post-surgical wounds. Such a system must be non-invasive, comfortable for extended wear, capable of operating in both hospital and home environments, and powered by a predictive algorithm that delivers high sensitivity without sacrificing specificity. The present invention addresses these requirements through a novel integration of hardware and software innovations, offering a paradigm shift in post-operative care that promises to reduce the incidence of late-diagnosed SSIs, improve patient recovery trajectories, and alleviate the enormous clinical and financial burdens associated with this preventable complication.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for the continuous, non-invasive monitoring and early prediction of surgical site infections (SSIs), particularly superficial-incisional SSIs, through the integration of a multi-modal biosensor apparatus and an advanced ensemble machine learning model. The core of the invention resides in a wearable bio-signal acquisition device configured to simultaneously measure three key physiological parameters at the peri-wound site: tissue oxygen saturation (StO₂) via near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS), local temperature using a high-precision digital sensor, and bioimpedance through application of high-frequency alternating currents. These measurements are performed continuously over an extended post-operative period—typically spanning from pre-surgery baseline establishment through at least seven days post-incision—thereby generating a rich, time-series dataset reflective of the dynamic wound healing process.

The biosensor apparatus, referred to herein as the CrelySENSE module, is engineered for robustness, miniaturization, and biocompatibility. It comprises a printed circuit board (PCB) integrating a custom NIRS subsystem featuring dual-wavelength light-emitting diodes (LEDs) emitting at 730 nm and 850 nm, a large-area silicon photodiode for optical signal detection, a digital temperature sensor (e.g., TMP117), a bioimpedance measurement unit capable of signal generation up to 200 kHz (e.g., AD5941), and an inertial measurement unit (IMU) containing a 3D accelerometer and gyroscope to detect and compensate for motion artifacts. The entire assembly is encapsulated in medical-grade silicone molding, resulting in a lightweight, waterproof, and flexible form factor suitable for secure attachment to the skin adjacent to a surgical incision. Data acquisition occurs in regular burst-mode intervals—such as one-minute sampling bursts every ten minutes—at a high sampling frequency (e.g., 50 Hz), ensuring temporal resolution sufficient to capture rapid physiological fluctuations while conserving power for extended operation.

The CrelySENSE modules interface wirelessly or via tethered connection with a central bio-signal acquisition hub, termed the CrelyPRO unit, which aggregates data from multiple sensor sites, performs preliminary signal conditioning, and stores the information locally or transmits it to a remote server for further analysis. The CrelyPRO is built around a low-power microcontroller platform (e.g., Feather nRF52840) equipped with real-time clock functionality and powered by a rechargeable lithium-polymer battery, enabling autonomous operation in both clinical and ambulatory settings.

Coupled with this hardware platform is a sophisticated machine learning pipeline designed to transform raw biosignals into clinically meaningful predictions. The analytical framework employs a bagged, stacked, and balanced ensemble logistic regression model specifically trained to detect the presence of a current SSI and to forecast the likelihood of infection onset 24 and 48 hours in advance of clinical diagnosis. The model development process begins with rigorous preprocessing of the raw sensor data, including filtering to remove noise and motion-induced artifacts, followed by extraction of a comprehensive feature set—comprising statistical, temporal, and spectral descriptors such as slopes, percentiles, variances, and frequency-domain metrics—from sliding time windows across the recording period. To address the inherent class imbalance between infected and non-infected wound sites, the training dataset undergoes strategic up-sampling of the minority (infected) class, ensuring that the model learns discriminative patterns without being biased toward the more numerous negative cases.

The ensemble architecture combines multiple base classifiers through bootstrap aggregation (bagging) to reduce variance and improve stability, and then integrates their outputs via a meta-learner (stacking) to produce a final probabilistic prediction. Model validation is performed using stringent leave-one-out cross-validation to maximize the utilization of limited experimental data while minimizing the risk of overfitting. Performance is evaluated using clinically relevant metrics including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, with AUC serving as the primary indicator of discriminative capacity.

Validation of the invention was conducted in a controlled porcine model of superficial-incisional SSI, wherein full-thickness incisions were created on the dorsum of skeletally mature pigs and selectively inoculated with Methicillin-Susceptible *Staphylococcus aureus* (MSSA). Continuous monitoring from three days pre-surgery through seven days post-surgery revealed statistically significant divergences in StO₂, temperature, and bioimpedance trajectories between infected and non-infected wounds, with StO₂ peaking approximately 25 hours post-inoculation, temperature elevations becoming significant between 24–72 hours, and bioimpedance reductions manifesting between 72–120 hours. Crucially, cross-correlation analyses demonstrated that these biosignal changes preceded clinical diagnosis by veterinary experts by 24 to 31 hours, confirming the system’s predictive capability.

The trained ensemble model achieved an AUC of 0.80 for predicting SSI 24 hours in advance of clinical diagnosis, with corresponding accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of 78%, 81%, and 76%, respectively—performance levels indicative of excellent clinical utility. Even at 48 hours in advance, the model maintained acceptable discriminability (AUC = 0.74), underscoring the feasibility of very early warning. These results establish that the multi-modal biosignal signature captured by the invention contains sufficient information to enable timely, objective, and automated detection of developing infection, well before traditional assessment methods would trigger clinical concern.

In summary, the invention provides a complete end-to-end solution for post-surgical infection surveillance that overcomes the limitations of current practice through continuous physiological monitoring and intelligent data interpretation. By delivering early, accurate, and actionable alerts, the system empowers clinicians to initiate targeted interventions—such as localized or systemic antibiotics—at the optimal therapeutic window, thereby improving patient outcomes, reducing hospital readmissions, and lowering healthcare costs. The modular design of the hardware and the adaptability of the machine learning framework further allow for future expansion to include additional biosensors (e.g., pH, perfusion, respiration rate) and integration with electronic health records, paving the way for a new standard of care in post-operative monitoring.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention is described in detail below with reference to its various components, operational principles, and experimental validation. It should be understood that the specific embodiments disclosed herein are provided for illustrative purposes and do not limit the scope of the invention, which encompasses all equivalents and modifications falling within the spirit and claims of the appended patent claims.

The invention comprises two principal subsystems: (1) a multi-modal wearable biosensor apparatus for continuous acquisition of physiological signals from the peri-wound region, and (2) a machine learning-based analytical engine for processing these signals and generating predictive alerts regarding the presence or imminent onset of surgical site infection. Each subsystem is described in turn, followed by a detailed account of the experimental methodology used to validate the system in a porcine model of superficial-incisional SSI.

**I. Multi-Modal Biosensor Apparatus**

The biosensor apparatus, designated as the CrelySENSE module, is a compact, self-contained device engineered for long-term, non-invasive attachment to the skin adjacent to a surgical incision. Its physical dimensions are approximately 10 cm × 6 cm × 3 cm, with a total mass of 121 grams, making it sufficiently lightweight to avoid discomfort or interference with patient mobility. The housing is constructed from medical-grade silicone via molding techniques, providing a waterproof, biocompatible, and flexible enclosure that conforms to body contours and withstands exposure to bodily fluids, cleaning agents, and mechanical stress.

Embedded within the silicone casing is a multi-layer printed circuit board (PCB) that integrates several specialized sensing subsystems:

- **Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS) Subsystem**: This component is responsible for measuring tissue oxygen saturation (StO₂), defined as the percentage of oxygenated hemoglobin relative to total hemoglobin in the microvasculature of the peri-wound tissue. The NIRS subsystem employs an analog front-end integrated circuit (e.g., Texas Instruments AFE4420) that drives two surface-mount technology (SMT) light-emitting diodes (LEDs)—one emitting red light at 730 nm and the other infrared light at 850 nm (e.g., Marubeni SMT730D/850D). These wavelengths are selected based on their differential absorption characteristics by oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin, enabling calculation of oxygen saturation via modified Beer-Lambert law principles. The emitted light penetrates the skin and underlying tissue, and the backscattered photons are captured by a large-active-area silicon photodiode (e.g., Vishay VEMD5060X01, 7.5 mm²) positioned at a fixed source-detector separation distance of 50 mm. This geometry optimizes the penetration depth to target the dermal and subcutaneous layers relevant to superficial wound healing while minimizing contamination from deeper or superficial structures. The analog photocurrent generated by the photodiode is converted to digital values by the AFE4420 and sampled at 50 Hz during active acquisition bursts.

- **Temperature Sensing Subsystem**: A high-accuracy digital temperature sensor (e.g., Texas Instruments TMP117) is mounted on the PCB in direct thermal contact with the skin-facing surface of the module. This sensor provides continuous measurement of local skin temperature with a resolution of 0.0078°C and an accuracy of ±0.1°C over the clinical range of 0°C to +65°C. Temperature deviations from baseline are indicative of localized inflammatory responses associated with infection.

- **Bioimpedance (BioZ) Subsystem**: Tissue bioimpedance is measured using a precision analog front-end (e.g., Analog Devices AD5941), which generates a sinusoidal excitation current at frequencies up to 200 kHz and measures the resulting voltage drop across the tissue. The impedance magnitude and phase are computed in real time, with changes in extracellular fluid volume, ion concentration, and cellular integrity—hallmarks of infection—manifesting as decreases in impedance magnitude. Electrodes for current injection and voltage sensing are integrated into the skin-contact surface of the module, arranged in a tetrapolar configuration to minimize the influence of electrode-skin interface impedance.

- **Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)**: To mitigate motion artifacts that could corrupt biosignal integrity, the module includes a 6-axis IMU (e.g., STMicroelectronics LSM6DS33) combining a 3D accelerometer and 3D gyroscope. This sensor captures movement dynamics at high frequency, enabling post-processing algorithms to identify and exclude data segments contaminated by excessive motion.

Data acquisition is orchestrated by an onboard microcontroller that implements a power-efficient burst-mode sampling protocol. Specifically, the system activates all sensors for a one-minute interval every ten minutes, during which data is sampled at 50 Hz per channel. This strategy balances the need for high temporal resolution with battery longevity, enabling uninterrupted operation for over a week on a single charge. Raw sensor data is temporarily buffered in on-chip memory before being transmitted to the central acquisition hub.

The CrelySENSE modules interface with the CrelyPRO bio-signal acquisition hub, a palm-sized device that serves as the data aggregation and communication center. The CrelyPRO is built around the Adafruit Feather nRF52840 platform, which features an ARM Cortex-M4 processor with Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) 5.0 connectivity, allowing wireless transmission of sensor data to smartphones, tablets, or cloud servers. For applications requiring higher reliability or longer range, a wired USB or serial interface may alternatively be used. The hub incorporates a PCF8523 real-time clock (RTC) to maintain precise time synchronization across all connected sensors, critical for aligning multi-site measurements and correlating events with clinical timelines. Power is supplied by a 3.7V lithium-polymer battery with a capacity sufficient for seven days of continuous operation. Onboard flash memory provides local storage redundancy in case of communication failures.

**II. Machine Learning Analytical Engine**

The raw biosignals collected by the hardware platform are processed through a multi-stage machine learning pipeline designed to extract predictive features and generate probabilistic infection forecasts. The pipeline operates as follows:

1. **Signal Preprocessing**: Raw time-series data from each sensor modality undergoes filtering to remove high-frequency noise (e.g., using a low-pass Butterworth filter with cutoff at 10 Hz) and motion artifact correction. Motion artifacts are identified using IMU-derived acceleration and angular velocity thresholds; affected segments are either interpolated from neighboring clean data or excluded from feature extraction.

2. **Feature Engineering**: From the cleaned signals, a comprehensive set of 90 features is extracted using a moving window approach (e.g., 30-minute windows with 50% overlap). Features include:
   - Temporal statistics: mean, median, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis
   - Trend indicators: linear regression slope, first-order difference
   - Distributional metrics: 10th, 25th, 75th, 90th percentiles
   - Frequency-domain descriptors: power spectral density peaks, dominant frequency
   - Cross-modality interactions: correlation coefficients between StO₂ and temperature, etc.
   These features collectively capture both the absolute levels and dynamic evolution of each biosignal over time.

3. **Class Balancing**: Given the expected rarity of infection events in real-world deployment, the training dataset is inherently imbalanced. To prevent the model from biasing toward the majority (non-infected) class, synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) or random up-sampling is applied to the infected instances, ensuring equal representation during training.

4. **Ensemble Model Construction**: A bagged, stacked, and balanced ensemble logistic regression model is trained on the engineered features. Bagging involves training multiple logistic regression models on bootstrapped subsets of the training data, reducing variance and improving robustness. Stacking then combines the predictions of these base models using a meta-logistic regressor that learns optimal weighting based on validation performance. This hierarchical architecture enhances generalization and mitigates overfitting.

5. **Prediction and Alerting**: At inference time, the trained ensemble processes real-time feature vectors and outputs a probability score indicating the likelihood of current infection or infection within the next 24 or 48 hours. Thresholds are calibrated to achieve desired sensitivity-specificity tradeoffs (e.g., >80% sensitivity for early warning), with scores exceeding the threshold triggering clinician alerts via mobile or web interfaces.

**III. Experimental Validation in Porcine Model**

The invention was validated in a controlled study involving two skeletally mature domestic pigs (*Sus scrofa domestica*), each weighing 80–90 kg, in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines. The animals underwent a seven-day acclimatization period wearing a custom-fitted jacket that provided access to 14 dorsal test sites while protecting sensors and wounds from external perturbation.

Three days prior to surgery (Day -3), the lateral trunk was shaved and disinfected, and two CrelySENSE modules were sutured 2 cm lateral to each of the 14 planned incision sites. Baseline biosignal recordings commenced immediately and continued through Day 7 post-surgery. On Day 0, thirteen 8-cm full-thickness incisions were created under general anesthesia, with seven randomly assigned sites inoculated with 100 µL of 10⁹ CFU/mL MSSA (ATCC 29213); six served as sham (surgical trauma only) controls, and one remained untouched as a true control.

Daily clinical assessments by blinded veterinarians using CDC criteria assigned infection scores, while skin biopsies (Days 2–7) provided histopathological confirmation of infection (e.g., polymorphonuclear cell infiltration). Biosignal data was continuously recorded, and vital signs (heart rate, respiratory rate, SpO₂, core temperature) were monitored daily.

Statistical analysis revealed significant differences between infected and non-infected wounds:
- StO₂ in infected wounds peaked at ~25 h post-inoculation, differing significantly from controls from 12–50 h (p < 0.05).
- Peri-wound temperature elevation in infected wounds was significant from 24–72 h.
- Bioimpedance reduction in infected wounds was significant from 72–120 h.

Cross-correlation showed StO₂, temperature, and BioZ changes preceded clinical diagnosis by 25 h, 31 h, and 24 h, respectively (all p < 0.001).

The ensemble model achieved:
- Current SSI detection: AUC = 0.77, accuracy = 64%, sensitivity = 82%, specificity = 59%
- 24-h prediction: AUC = 0.80, accuracy = 78%, sensitivity = 81%, specificity = 76%
- 48-h prediction: AUC = 0.74, accuracy = 69%, sensitivity = 77%, specificity = 65%

DeLong’s test confirmed significantly better performance at 24 h vs. 48 h prediction (p < 0.001), validating the system’s utility for early warning.

In conclusion, the detailed embodiment demonstrates that the invention enables reliable, early detection of SSIs through continuous multi-modal biosensing and intelligent analytics, establishing a foundation for transformative improvements in post-surgical care.
