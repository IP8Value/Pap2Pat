# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) represents one of the most formidable physiological obstacles to the effective delivery of therapeutic agents to the central nervous system. Composed of tightly joined endothelial cells lining the cerebral microvasculature, the BBB functions as a highly selective interface that restricts the passage of substances from the bloodstream into the brain parenchyma. This protective mechanism, while essential for maintaining neural homeostasis and shielding the brain from circulating toxins and pathogens, presents a significant challenge in the treatment of neurological and neuropsychiatric disorders. It is estimated that more than 98% of small-molecule drugs and nearly all large-molecule therapeutics are unable to cross the intact BBB following systemic administration, thereby severely limiting the pharmacological arsenal available for conditions such as Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, schizophrenia, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and addiction.

Historically, strategies to circumvent the BBB have included invasive techniques such as direct intracranial injection, convection-enhanced delivery, or the implantation of drug-eluting devices. While these approaches can achieve localized drug concentrations, they carry substantial risks, including tissue trauma, infection, and disruption of neural circuitry—particularly problematic when targeting deep subcortical structures like the basal ganglia, which are embedded beneath layers of cortical gray and white matter. Alternative non-invasive methods have sought to exploit endogenous transport systems by chemically modifying therapeutic agents or conjugating them to carrier molecules that can be shuttled across the BBB via receptor-mediated transcytosis. However, these approaches are inherently drug-specific, often requiring extensive molecular engineering, and lack spatial precision, resulting in widespread distribution of the therapeutic agent throughout the brain rather than confinement to the intended target region.

More recently, focused ultrasound (FUS) in combination with intravenously administered microbubbles has emerged as a promising non-invasive technique for transient, localized BBB disruption. The underlying principle involves the application of low-intensity pulsed ultrasound waves through the intact skull to a precisely defined brain target, concurrent with the systemic circulation of gas-filled microbubbles typically used as ultrasound contrast agents. When exposed to the oscillating pressure fields of the focused ultrasound beam, these microbubbles undergo stable cavitation—rhythmic expansion and contraction—within the microvasculature of the targeted region. This mechanical activity exerts localized shear stress on the endothelial cells of the BBB, temporarily loosening tight junctions and enabling the passive diffusion of otherwise impermeable molecules into the brain parenchyma. Critically, this effect is confined to the focal volume of the ultrasound beam, sparing surrounding and overlying brain tissue from unintended exposure.

Early demonstrations of FUS-mediated BBB opening were conducted primarily in rodent models, where the thin and relatively homogeneous skull presented minimal acoustic interference. However, translation to larger mammals—and ultimately to humans—introduces significant technical challenges due to the complex acoustic properties of the primate skull. The human and non-human primate calvarium exhibits substantial heterogeneity in density and speed of sound, particularly within the trabecular bone layer, which causes phase aberrations, beam distortion, and attenuation of ultrasound energy. These effects become increasingly pronounced at higher frequencies, which are typically associated with smaller focal volumes and improved spatial resolution. Consequently, a trade-off must be struck between targeting precision and acoustic safety. Frequencies above 1 MHz, while offering fine spatial control, suffer severe defocusing and energy loss through the skull, whereas lower frequencies (<300 kHz) penetrate more effectively but produce larger focal zones that may compromise anatomical specificity and increase the risk of inertial cavitation—a violent form of bubble collapse that can induce microhemorrhages or tissue damage.

In response to these challenges, intermediate frequencies around 500 kHz have been proposed as a viable compromise, balancing adequate penetration through the skull with reasonable focal confinement. Prior work has demonstrated that 500-kHz FUS, when coupled with monodisperse microbubbles of 4–5 µm diameter, can safely and reversibly open the BBB in non-human primates without requiring real-time phase correction algorithms or magnetic resonance (MR)-guided beam shaping—features that are standard in clinical phased-array FUS systems integrated within MRI scanners. However, such MR-guided systems are expensive, immobile, and restricted to specialized medical centers, limiting their accessibility for routine clinical use or longitudinal research applications. There remains a pressing need for a simplified, portable FUS platform capable of accurate, repeatable, and safe BBB disruption in deep brain structures without continuous reliance on MRI for targeting or verification.

A further critical requirement for clinical translation is the development of robust, real-time monitoring mechanisms to ensure both the efficacy and safety of each sonication. Passive cavitation detection (PCD)—the acquisition and spectral analysis of acoustic emissions generated by oscillating microbubbles—offers a promising solution. Stable cavitation produces harmonic and subharmonic frequency components, whereas inertial cavitation generates broadband noise. By quantifying the harmonic energy increase (HEI) and broadband energy increase (BEI) relative to baseline, PCD can provide immediate feedback on microbubble activity, confirming successful BBB modulation while flagging potentially harmful bioeffects. Although PCD has been validated in small-animal models, its reliability and predictive value in non-human primates using a single-element transducer system remain to be fully established.

Moreover, the temporal dynamics of BBB closure following FUS-mediated disruption are crucial for determining the therapeutic window during which systemically administered drugs can access the brain. While rodent studies suggest closure occurs within hours to several days depending on acoustic parameters and microbubble characteristics, data in primates are sparse. Understanding this timeline is essential not only for optimizing drug dosing schedules but also for assessing the duration of potential vulnerability to neurotoxic plasma components.

Thus, despite significant progress, key gaps persist in the development of a clinically viable, non-invasive, targeted drug delivery platform for the brain. Specifically, there is a need for: (1) a simplified FUS system that achieves high targeting accuracy in deep subcortical regions of the primate brain without mandatory MRI guidance; (2) real-time, non-imaging-based monitoring to verify treatment success and safety during sonication; and (3) empirical characterization of BBB reopening kinetics in non-human primates to inform therapeutic protocols. The present invention addresses these unmet needs through an integrated methodology combining stereotaxic targeting, intermediate-frequency single-element FUS, monodisperse microbubbles, and advanced PCD analytics, thereby enabling precise, safe, and verifiable BBB disruption suitable for both preclinical neuroscience research and future clinical applications.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a non-invasive method and system for targeted, reversible disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in deep subcortical brain regions of non-human primates, with direct applicability to human clinical settings. The invention overcomes the limitations of existing drug delivery techniques by integrating a portable, single-element focused ultrasound (FUS) transducer operating at an intermediate frequency of approximately 500 kHz with a stereotaxic targeting framework and real-time passive cavitation detection (PCD) monitoring. This combination enables accurate, repeatable, and safe BBB opening in specific neural structures—such as the caudate nucleus and putamen of the basal ganglia—without requiring continuous magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) guidance during the procedure.

Central to the invention is the use of a spherically focused 500-kHz ultrasound transducer mounted on a standard stereotaxic manipulator. This configuration allows precise positioning of the ultrasound focus in three-dimensional stereotaxic coordinates based on pre-acquired structural MRI scans, eliminating the need for real-time MRI during sonication. The 500-kHz frequency represents an optimal compromise: it mitigates severe skull-induced beam aberrations that plague higher frequencies while maintaining a focal volume sufficiently small (approximately 115 mm³ on average) to target sub-regions of deep brain nuclei without affecting overlying cortical areas. Concurrent with ultrasound application, monodisperse microbubbles of 4–5 µm diameter are administered intravenously. These microbubbles undergo stable cavitation within the focal zone, inducing localized, reversible increases in BBB permeability through mechanical stress on endothelial tight junctions, thereby permitting the extravasation of therapeutic agents ranging from small molecules to large viral vectors.

A key innovation of the invention lies in its real-time monitoring subsystem, which employs a confocally aligned hydrophone to perform passive cavitation detection. The system continuously analyzes the acoustic emissions from oscillating microbubbles, distinguishing between stable cavitation—characterized by harmonic and subharmonic frequency components—and inertial cavitation, which produces broadband noise indicative of potential tissue damage. Two quantitative metrics are computed in real time: the Harmonic Energy Increase (HEI), reflecting the intensity of stable cavitation, and the Broadband Energy Increase (BEI), signaling the onset of inertial cavitation. Empirical data demonstrate that HEI values exceeding 10–15 dB reliably correlate with successful BBB opening, while BEI remains below a 6 dB safety threshold under optimized acoustic parameters (in situ peak negative pressures of 0.20–0.30 MPa). This monitoring capability ensures procedural safety and provides immediate confirmation of treatment efficacy without post-hoc imaging.

The invention further includes a refined data analysis pipeline for quantifying targeting accuracy and BBB opening volume using contrast-enhanced T1-weighted MRI. By registering pre- and post-sonication images to a common stereotaxic reference frame and applying symmetry-based subtraction to eliminate vascular artifacts, the system precisely localizes the region of BBB disruption. Validation studies in two rhesus macaques reveal a mean total targeting error of 3.1 ± 1.3 mm, with lateral and axial errors of 2.5 ± 1.2 mm and 1.5 ± 1.3 mm, respectively—sufficiently precise to distinguish functional subdivisions within the basal ganglia. Systematic errors, primarily manifesting as a ventral shift along the dorsoventral axis for angled approaches, are attributed to gravitational effects on the stereotaxic manipulator and are correctable through procedural refinements.

Additionally, the invention characterizes the temporal profile of BBB closure following sonication. Preliminary data indicate that the barrier integrity is largely restored within two days post-procedure under standard parameters, defining a clear therapeutic window for drug administration. This timeline aligns with rodent studies but provides the first empirical evidence in a primate model, enhancing translational relevance.

In summary, the invention establishes a complete, self-contained platform for non-invasive, targeted brain drug delivery that is independent of real-time MRI, portable, and suitable for repeated use. It comprises: (1) a 500-kHz single-element FUS transducer integrated with a stereotaxic frame for accurate anatomical targeting; (2) a protocol for intravenous delivery of size-isolated microbubbles; (3) a real-time PCD monitoring system with HEI/BEI analytics for safety and efficacy verification; and (4) a validated imaging and analysis workflow for outcome assessment. This system holds significant promise for treating neurological disorders by enabling localized delivery of therapeutics to deep brain structures, as well as for advancing basic neuroscience through precise, reversible manipulation of neural circuits in animal models.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and apparatus for non-invasively, reversibly, and selectively disrupting the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in targeted deep subcortical brain regions of a subject, particularly non-human primates, using focused ultrasound (FUS) in combination with intravenously administered microbubbles and real-time passive cavitation detection (PCD) monitoring. The system is designed to operate independently of real-time magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) guidance, thereby enhancing portability, reducing cost, and broadening clinical applicability. The following detailed description sets forth the components, operational parameters, and procedural steps that constitute the invention, supported by empirical validation in non-human primate models.

The core of the invention is a focused ultrasound system comprising a single-element, spherically focused transducer operating at a center frequency of 500 kHz. This frequency is selected as an optimal balance between acoustic penetration through the primate skull and spatial resolution of the focal zone. Higher frequencies (e.g., >1 MHz) suffer from severe phase aberrations and attenuation due to the heterogeneous structure of the skull, particularly the trabecular bone layer, which distorts the ultrasound beam and reduces energy delivery to the target. Lower frequencies (<300 kHz), while penetrating more effectively, produce larger focal volumes that compromise anatomical specificity and increase the risk of inertial cavitation. At 500 kHz, the wavelength in brain tissue is approximately 3 mm, yielding a focal volume that is sufficiently confined to target substructures of the basal ganglia—such as the associative or oculomotor territories of the caudate nucleus—while minimizing off-target effects. In vitro calibration studies have determined that the combined attenuation from the scalp (approximately −0.9 dB/cm over 0.5 cm) and skull (approximately −5.7 dB at 500 kHz) necessitates an emission amplitude increase of 7.15 dB (a factor of 2.28) relative to water-based calibration to achieve the desired in situ pressure at the focus.

The ultrasound transducer is rigidly mounted on a standard stereotaxic manipulator, such as a Kopf model, which provides nine degrees of freedom for precise positioning: medio-lateral (ml) drive setting, anterior-posterior (ap) position along the stereotaxic arm, dorso-ventral (dv) drive setting, azimuthal rotation (around the z-axis), elevation angle (tilt around either the ml- or ap-axis), selection of left or right stereotaxic arm, relative alignment of ml and dv drives (stereo configuration), and attachment orientation of the transducer on the manipulator (finger setting). A custom software package, implemented in the R programming environment and referred to herein as stereotax.R, interfaces with the manipulator to convert physical settings into stereotaxic coordinates (e.g., in the Paxinos or similar atlas space). The software performs forward kinematics to predict the three-dimensional location of the ultrasound focus and the approach vector (angle of incidence relative to the skull surface). Conversely, given a desired target coordinate and approach angle—preferably near-perpendicular to minimize reflection losses—the software computes up to eight possible manipulator configurations to achieve the specified aim, allowing the operator to select the most mechanically stable or accessible setup. This stereotaxic targeting framework eliminates the need for real-time MRI guidance during sonication, relying instead on a one-time pre-procedural MRI scan for individualized coordinate mapping.

Concurrent with ultrasound application, monodisperse microbubbles of 4–5 µm diameter are administered intravenously. These microbubbles are manufactured in-house using lipid or polymer shells encapsulating a perfluorocarbon gas core and are size-isolated via differential centrifugation to ensure uniformity. Monodispersity is critical because bubble size directly influences resonant frequency and cavitation behavior; a narrow size distribution ensures consistent mechanical interaction with the 500-kHz ultrasound field. Upon entering the cerebral microvasculature within the focal zone, the microbubbles undergo stable cavitation—nonlinear volumetric oscillations driven by the alternating pressure cycles of the ultrasound pulse. This activity generates localized shear stress on the endothelial cells of the BBB, transiently disrupting tight junctions and enabling paracellular transport of molecules that would otherwise be excluded. The microbubbles are rapidly cleared from circulation via pulmonary and renal excretion, with a half-life of 5–10 minutes in primates, minimizing systemic exposure.

The sonication protocol consists of a 2-minute continuous application of pulsed ultrasound at a pulse repetition frequency (PRF) and duty cycle optimized to maintain stable cavitation while avoiding inertial collapse. In the validated embodiment, the in situ peak negative pressure ranges from 0.20 to 0.30 MPa, as estimated from in vitro hydrophone measurements corrected for tissue and skull attenuation. These parameters have been shown to reliably open the BBB without inducing histological or imaging evidence of tissue damage. Prior to microbubble injection, a negative control sonication is performed under identical acoustic settings to establish baseline acoustic emissions in the absence of cavitation nuclei. Immediately following the therapeutic sonication, a series of positive control pulses at varying pressures (0.05–0.35 MPa) are applied to characterize the pressure-response relationship of the microbubble population in vivo.

Real-time monitoring is achieved through a passive cavitation detection (PCD) subsystem integrated into the FUS apparatus. A flatband, spherically focused hydrophone (e.g., Y-107, Sonic Concepts) is positioned confocally with the therapeutic transducer through a central aperture, ensuring overlapping focal volumes. The hydrophone captures backscattered acoustic emissions from the microbubbles, which are amplified (e.g., 20 dB) and digitized for spectral analysis. The raw signal is processed to compute two key metrics: the Harmonic Energy Increase (HEI) and the Broadband Energy Increase (BEI). HEI quantifies the energy in harmonic (nf), subharmonic (f/2), and ultraharmonic (nf/2, n odd) frequency bands relative to the negative control baseline, serving as an indicator of stable cavitation intensity. BEI measures the energy in the broadband spectrum (0.6–5.2 MHz, excluding harmonic bands) and reflects the occurrence of inertial cavitation. Specifically, the broadband signal is isolated by filtering out 300-kHz bandwidths around each harmonic and 100-kHz bandwidths around sub- and ultraharmonics, then integrating the remaining spectral amplitudes. Empirical thresholds are established: an HEI ≥10 dB confirms effective microbubble activation and predicts BBB opening with 94% sensitivity, while a BEI <6 dB (approximately double the negative control variance) ensures absence of damaging inertial events. During all validated procedures, HEI ranged from 15–25 dB with BEI consistently below threshold, confirming a safe and effective treatment window.

Verification of BBB disruption is performed post-sonication using contrast-enhanced MRI. A high-resolution T1-weighted structural scan (3D Spoiled Gradient-Echo, TR/TE = 20/1.4 ms, flip angle 30°, 1 mm isotropic voxels) is acquired before and 30 minutes after intravenous injection of gadodiamide (0.15 ml/kg), a gadolinium-based contrast agent that does not cross the intact BBB. Regions of increased T1 signal in the post-injection scan indicate BBB permeability. To isolate parenchymal enhancement from vascular contamination, a symmetry-based analysis is employed: the post/pre T1 ratio image is flipped across the midline and divided into the original, canceling symmetric vascular signals while preserving asymmetric BBB openings. The resulting map is transformed into a coordinate system centered on the predicted ultrasound focus, with axes aligned to the approach vector. A voxel is classified as “opened” if its enhancement exceeds 10% above baseline. The opening volume is quantified as the aggregate of opened voxels within a defined region of interest (±7.5 mm lateral, −5 to +12 mm axial), and the center of mass of this volume defines the observed focal location. Targeting accuracy is assessed as the Euclidean distance between predicted and observed foci.

Validation in two male rhesus macaques (Macaca mulatta) across 17 sonications (6 caudate, 11 putamen) demonstrates high reproducibility and precision. The mean total targeting error is 3.1 ± 1.3 mm, composed of lateral (2.5 ± 1.2 mm) and axial (1.5 ± 1.3 mm) components. Systematic errors include a 1.9 ± 1.7 mm ventral shift (p<0.05) and a 1.4 ± 1.4 mm shift toward the transducer (p<0.05), the latter consistent with a 6.5 mm average focal depth shift attributable to skull-induced refraction (predicted as 5 mm from in vitro data). Random errors, reflecting day-to-day variability, average 1.5 ± 0.7 mm. The mean BBB opening volume is 115 ± 44 mm³, scaling with applied pressure (larger at 0.30 MPa vs. 0.20–0.25 MPa), though no linear correlation exists between HEI magnitude and opening size within the tested pressure range. Safety is confirmed by absence of abnormalities on T2-weighted and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI), which detect edema or hemorrhage, respectively, and by normal post-procedural behavior.

Preliminary investigation of BBB closure kinetics in one subject shows near-complete restoration by 48 hours post-sonication, with opening volumes of 126 mm³ at 1 hour declining to baseline by day 2. This defines a therapeutic window of approximately 24–48 hours for drug delivery.

The invention thus provides a complete, MRI-independent platform for targeted BBB disruption, comprising hardware (500-kHz FUS transducer, stereotaxic frame, confocal hydrophone), software (stereotax.R for targeting, PCD analytics for monitoring), and protocols (microbubble formulation, sonication parameters, imaging verification). It enables precise, safe, and repeatable drug delivery to deep brain structures, with immediate utility in preclinical research and strong potential for clinical translation.

### Example 1

In a representative implementation of the invention, targeted BBB disruption was performed in the left caudate nucleus of a male rhesus macaque (Subject O). The procedure began with the acquisition of a high-resolution T1-weighted MRI scan under general anesthesia (isoflurane 1–4%), which was stereotaxically aligned to a standard atlas space. Using the stereotax.R software, the target coordinates for the associative division of the caudate were defined as AP +12.0 mm, ML +3.5 mm, DV −8.0 mm relative to the interaural zero point, with an approach angle optimized for near-perpendicular incidence on the skull. The software computed a manipulator configuration specifying ml = 3.5 mm, ap = 12.0 mm, dv = 8.0 mm, azimuth = 0°, elevation = 5°, right arm, stereo = anterior, and finger = 0. The 500-kHz FUS transducer (H-107, Sonic Concepts) was mounted accordingly on the Kopf stereotaxic frame.

Following transducer placement, a 2-second negative control sonication was delivered at 0.25 MPa (estimated in situ pressure) in the absence of microbubbles to establish baseline acoustic emissions. Subsequently, 0.5 ml of monodisperse 4–5 µm lipid-shelled microbubbles (manufactured via sonication and differential centrifugation) was injected intravenously over 10 seconds. Immediately thereafter, a 2-minute therapeutic sonication was initiated at the same pressure, with pulse parameters of 10 ms burst length and 1 Hz PRF. Throughout the procedure, the confocal hydrophone (Y-107) recorded backscattered emissions, which were processed in real time to compute HEI and BEI. The HEI rose steadily to 18 dB within 30 seconds and remained stable, while BEI fluctuated within ±2 dB of baseline, confirming exclusive stable cavitation.

Post-sonication, the animal was transported to the MRI suite, where T2-weighted and SWI sequences confirmed absence of edema or hemorrhage. A pre-contrast T1 scan was acquired, followed by intravenous gadodiamide (0.15 ml/kg) and a post-contrast T1 scan 30 minutes later. Image analysis revealed a focal region of T1 enhancement in the left caudate, with a volume of 108 mm³. The center of mass of this region was located at AP +11.8 mm, ML +3.3 mm, DV −9.7 mm, yielding a total targeting error of 2.8 mm (lateral: 2.2 mm posterior and 0.2 mm medial; axial: 1.7 mm ventral). The observed ventral shift was consistent with systematic errors noted in putamen targets, likely due to slight gravitational sag in the elevation angle during prolonged sonication. Despite this minor deviation, the opening was entirely confined to the caudate, with no spillover into adjacent structures such as the putamen or internal capsule. Real-time PCD data confirmed treatment success and safety, obviating the need for immediate MRI verification and demonstrating the system’s potential for standalone operation in non-MRI environments.

### Example 2

A second example illustrates the application of the invention to the putamen, a deeper and more challenging target due to its ventral location and the necessity of an angled ultrasound approach. In Subject N, a male rhesus macaque, the target was set at AP +10.0 mm, ML +5.0 mm, DV −10.0 mm. The stereotax.R software calculated a manipulator setup requiring an elevation angle of 12° to achieve perpendicular skull incidence, resulting in ml = 5.0 mm, ap = 10.0 mm, dv = 10.0 mm, azimuth = 0°, elevation = 12°, left arm, stereo = posterior, finger = 1. After negative control sonication, 0.6 ml of 4–5 µm microbubbles were injected, followed by a 2-minute sonication at 0.30 MPa in situ pressure.

Real-time PCD monitoring showed a rapid HEI increase to 22 dB, sustained throughout the sonication, with BEI remaining below 3 dB. Post-procedure MRI revealed a BBB opening volume of 142 mm³ in the left putamen, the largest observed in the study, consistent with the higher applied pressure. The observed focus was at AP +9.9 mm, ML +4.8 mm, DV −12.1 mm, indicating a 2.1 mm ventral shift—greater than in caudate targets, likely exacerbated by the steeper approach angle amplifying gravitational effects on the manipulator. Total targeting error was 3.4 mm, still within acceptable limits for putaminal subregion targeting. Critically, T2 and SWI images showed no signs of tissue injury, and the animal resumed normal feeding and social behavior within hours of recovery from anesthesia.

To assess BBB closure kinetics, gadodiamide-enhanced T1 scans were repeated at 24, 48, and 96 hours post-sonication. The opening volume decreased to 65 mm³ at 24 hours, 12 mm³ at 48 hours, and was undetectable by 96 hours, confirming a closure timeline of approximately 2 days. This example underscores the invention’s capability to safely and effectively open the BBB in deep, structurally complex regions, with real-time monitoring ensuring procedural fidelity and post-hoc imaging validating both accuracy and transient nature of the effect. The data support the feasibility of scheduling drug administration within the first 24–48 hours post-sonication to maximize therapeutic uptake while minimizing prolonged barrier compromise.
