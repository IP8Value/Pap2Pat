# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Temperature is a fundamental physical parameter that governs numerous physiological and biochemical processes in biological systems. The ability to manipulate temperature with high spatial precision and temporal control at the cellular or subcellular level has become increasingly critical in modern biomedical research and therapeutic applications. Localized thermal stimulation enables non-invasive modulation of cellular activity, targeted destruction of pathological tissues, and precise control over drug release kinetics, among other functions. In neuroscience, for instance, thermal-sensitive ion channels such as TRPV1 can be selectively activated by mild heating (typically 40–45 °C), thereby triggering depolarization and action potential firing in neurons without the need for genetic modification or electrode insertion. Similarly, in oncology, hyperthermia therapy exploits the heightened sensitivity of cancer cells to temperatures above 42 °C, where elevated thermal stress impairs DNA repair mechanisms and synergizes with radiation or chemotherapy to enhance tumor cell death while sparing surrounding healthy tissue. Additional applications include accelerated wound healing through thermally induced angiogenesis, on-demand drug delivery from thermoresponsive nanocarriers, and microscale bioanalytical techniques such to polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and temperature gradient focusing (TGF), which rely on rapid and localized thermal cycling.

Despite the broad utility of controlled heating in biomedicine, existing thermal actuation technologies face significant limitations in achieving the necessary combination of spatial resolution, energy efficiency, biocompatibility, and closed-loop temperature regulation. Conventional resistive (ohmic) microheaters, fabricated using thin-film metal traces or doped semiconductor structures, offer excellent thermal response and integration with microelectronics but require direct physical contact with the biological sample. This constraint not only limits their applicability in minimally invasive or in vivo settings but also risks electrochemical damage, protein denaturation, or mechanical disruption at the heater-sample interface. Dielectric heating, which relies on the interaction of high-frequency electric fields with polar molecules (primarily water), suffers from poor spatial selectivity in aqueous biological environments due to the uniformly high permittivity of most tissues and cells. As a result, dielectric heating tends to produce diffuse thermal profiles that lack the sub-millimeter precision required for cellular-level interventions.

Magnetic heating, particularly through the use of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), has emerged as a promising alternative due to its inherent biocompatibility and potential for deep-tissue penetration without direct contact. MNPs—typically composed of iron oxide cores such as magnetite (Fe₃O₄) or maghemite (γ-Fe₂O₃)—can be functionalized with targeting ligands and introduced into biological systems via intravenous injection or local delivery. When exposed to an alternating magnetic field, these nanoparticles dissipate energy as heat through mechanisms including Néel relaxation (internal magnetic moment rotation), Brownian relaxation (physical particle rotation), or ferromagnetic resonance (FMR). Traditional MNP-based hyperthermia systems operate in the kilohertz to megahertz frequency range, leveraging Néel and Brownian relaxation losses. However, these approaches are hampered by two interrelated challenges: low heating efficiency and limited spatial resolution. The power dissipation in MNPs under low-frequency fields is proportional to the product of frequency and the square of the magnetic field amplitude, as dictated by the magnetic loss term in the generalized Joule heating equation. Consequently, achieving therapeutically relevant temperature rises (e.g., 43–45 °C) often necessitates high-amplitude magnetic fields (>10–30 kA/m), which can only be generated using large, power-intensive solenoid coils driven by kilowatt-scale amplifiers. These systems are bulky, expensive, and incompatible with portable or implantable platforms. Moreover, the spatial resolution of heating is constrained by the diffraction-limited nature of low-frequency magnetic fields and the physical dimensions of the excitation coils, which typically span several centimeters. Even with targeted MNP delivery—often relying on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in tumors—off-target accumulation in healthy vasculature remains a concern, and the inability to confine heating to sub-millimeter domains increases the risk of collateral thermal damage.

Recent theoretical and experimental work has suggested that shifting the operating frequency into the gigahertz (GHz) microwave regime could overcome these limitations by exploiting ferromagnetic resonance in MNPs. At GHz frequencies, the precessional motion of the magnetic moment synchronizes with the applied field, leading to resonant absorption and significantly enhanced heating efficiency per unit field strength. This allows for the use of much weaker magnetic fields—on the order of tens to hundreds of A/m—thereby enabling miniaturized, low-power field generators. Furthermore, the shorter wavelength of GHz fields facilitates tighter spatial confinement of the magnetic near-field when coupled with microscale inductors, potentially achieving heating zones on the order of tens to hundreds of micrometers. However, practical implementation of GHz magnetic heating in an integrated platform has been hindered by the lack of compact, efficient, and controllable RF sources capable of delivering the high voltage swings (>15 Vpp) required to generate sufficient magnetic field intensity over microscopic areas. Conventional cross-coupled LC oscillators in CMOS technology are limited to output swings of approximately twice the supply voltage (typically <5 Vpp in advanced nodes), which is insufficient for meaningful MNP heating. While external RF amplifiers could boost the signal, they introduce additional inductors, increase power consumption, and degrade spatial resolution due to larger footprints.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a fully integrated, scalable, and energy-efficient magnetic microheater array that operates at GHz frequencies, leverages ferromagnetic resonance in MNPs for high-efficiency heating, achieves sub-millimeter spatial resolution, and incorporates real-time closed-loop temperature regulation to ensure safety and precision. Such a system would represent a transformative advance in bioelectronic medicine, enabling new paradigms in neuromodulation, targeted cancer therapy, and lab-on-a-chip diagnostics.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a fully integrated magnetic microheater array based on ferromagnetic resonance (FMR) of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) at gigahertz (GHz) microwave frequencies, featuring closed-loop temperature regulation and sub-millimeter spatial resolution. The core innovation lies in a novel stacked oscillator topology implemented in a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) process, which generates high-voltage RF swings (>19 Vpp) using a single on-chip inductor footprint without requiring external amplification. Each pixel of the microheater array comprises a stacked oscillator coupled to a spiral inductor that produces a localized GHz magnetic field, and an electro-thermal feedback loop that dynamically adjusts the oscillator’s biasing current to maintain a user-specified temperature setpoint. The stacked oscillator architecture distributes the high RF voltage swing across multiple series-connected transistors, ensuring that no individual device exceeds its breakdown voltage while simultaneously achieving the large magnetic field intensities necessary to induce FMR-mediated heating in MNPs. The inductor geometry is optimized through multiphysics finite-element modeling to maximize temperature uniformity and heating efficiency within a confined area (~0.03 mm²), with a five-turn spiral having an inner radius of 51 μm selected as the optimal configuration for operation at 1.5 GHz.

The electro-thermal feedback loop integrates a Proportional-To-Absolute-Temperature (PTAT) sensor array positioned beneath the inductor to monitor local surface temperature, followed by programmable gain stages and digital-to-analog converters (DACs) that generate a control voltage for the tail transistor of the stacked oscillator. This closed-loop system enables precise temperature regulation within ±0.3 °C of the target (e.g., 43 °C for hyperthermia), automatically reducing RF power when the desired temperature is reached to prevent overheating. The array is scalable in two dimensions and supports multiple frequency tuning ranges (e.g., 1.2–1.6 GHz, 1.5–2.1 GHz, and 2.0–2.6 GHz) across different rows to accommodate MNPs with varying FMR frequencies due to differences in size, composition, or crystallinity. Experimental validation demonstrates that only regions containing MNPs are heated to therapeutic temperatures (up to 47 °C), while adjacent MNP-free areas remain below 37.8 °C, confirming high spatial specificity. Simultaneous activation of neighboring pixels shows minimal thermal crosstalk, verifying sub-millimeter resolution enabled by on-chip ground shielding and localized field confinement. The entire system operates at low DC power (tens of milliwatts per pixel) and is fabricated in a standard 45-nm CMOS SOI process, making it suitable for portable, implantable, or high-density bioelectronic applications. This invention thus overcomes the key limitations of prior art—namely, bulkiness, poor spatial resolution, high power consumption, and open-loop operation—by providing the first monolithically integrated GHz magnetic microheater array with autonomous temperature control.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a fully integrated magnetic microheater array that enables highly localized, efficient, and precisely regulated heating of biological samples containing magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) through ferromagnetic resonance (FMR) at gigahertz (GHz) frequencies. The system is implemented as a scalable two-dimensional array of independently controllable pixels, each comprising a high-efficiency stacked oscillator, an optimized on-chip inductor, and a closed-loop electro-thermal feedback circuit. This architecture eliminates the need for external RF amplifiers or bulky magnetic field generators, thereby achieving unprecedented spatial resolution (sub-millimeter scale), low power consumption, and robust temperature regulation in a compact CMOS-compatible platform.

Each pixel of the microheater array is designed around a novel stacked oscillator topology that overcomes the voltage swing limitations of conventional cross-coupled LC oscillators. In standard CMOS processes, the maximum differential output swing of a cross-coupled pair is approximately twice the supply voltage (2VDD), which is typically less than 5 Vpp in advanced nodes due to device reliability constraints. However, multiphysics simulations demonstrate that a minimum RF swing of approximately 16 Vpp at 1.5 GHz is required to generate sufficient magnetic field intensity to heat MNPs to therapeutic temperatures (e.g., 43 °C) within a localized volume. To achieve this without external amplification—which would increase pixel area and reduce spatial resolution—the invention employs a vertically stacked configuration of N transistors (where N is typically 4 or 5) connected in series between the supply rail and a tail current source. Unlike traditional cascode structures, the gates of the upper transistors are not AC-grounded; instead, they are capacitively coupled through a network of gate capacitors (C₂ to C_N) that form a capacitive voltage divider with the intrinsic gate-to-source capacitances (C_gs) of the transistors. This arrangement allows the RF voltage swing to build progressively from the bottom transistor (M₁) to the top transistor (M_N), distributing the total voltage stress evenly across all devices and ensuring that the drain-to-source (V_DS) and drain-to-gate (V_DG) voltages of each transistor remain below the breakdown limit (e.g., 3 V for 1.5 V thick-oxide devices in a 45-nm SOI process).

The oscillation is sustained through positive feedback: the differential output of the stacked pair is attenuated by a capacitor C₁ and fed back to the gate of M₁, maintaining the necessary phase condition for oscillation while keeping V_GS of M₁ within safe operating margins. A tail transistor, biased by a control voltage (V_tail) generated by the electro-thermal feedback loop, regulates the total current flowing through the stack and thereby controls the amplitude of the RF swing. The use of a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) CMOS process is particularly advantageous for this topology because it eliminates the body effect and reduces parasitic junction capacitances, enabling more predictable voltage distribution and higher frequency operation. In the disclosed embodiment, four-stacked oscillators operate from a 6 V supply, while five-stacked versions use a 7.5 V supply, both employing 1.5 V thick-gate-oxide transistors to ensure reliability under high RF stress.

The on-chip inductor is co-designed with the stacked oscillator to maximize magnetic field strength and heating uniformity over the target area. Through three-dimensional finite-element modeling in COMSOL Multiphysics, coupling electromagnetic and heat transfer physics, various inductor geometries were evaluated based on inner radius (R_in), number of turns, inductance, quality factor (Q), and resulting temperature distribution in an adjacent MNP-containing medium. Simulations revealed that smaller R_in improves field uniformity by minimizing the central cold spot observed in larger coils, but excessively small radii reduce inductance below the threshold needed for oscillator startup. Similarly, increasing the number of turns enhances inductance but degrades Q due to higher resistive losses and adds turns that contribute little to the effective heating zone. The optimal design was determined to be a five-turn square spiral with R_in = 51 μm, yielding an inductance of 4.0 nH and a Q-factor of 9.5 at 1.5 GHz. This geometry produces a peak temperature directly above the innermost turn, with a relatively uniform profile over an area of approximately 0.03 mm²—sufficient to cover single cells or small cell clusters.

Frequency tunability is incorporated to accommodate MNPs with different FMR frequencies, which vary with particle size, material composition (e.g., Fe₃O₄ vs. CoFe₂O₄), and anisotropy. Each row of the array is assigned a distinct tuning sub-range (e.g., 1.2–1.6 GHz, 1.5–2.1 GHz, 2.0–2.6 GHz) via a 4-bit binary-weighted capacitor bank connected in parallel with the inductor. To mitigate the trade-off between tuning range and Q-factor—where larger switch transistors improve Q but increase off-capacitance—the capacitor bank is segmented, and switch sizing is optimized to maintain nearly constant RF swing across the band. The switches themselves employ a four-stacked transistor architecture with gate resistors to prevent unintended turn-on and breakdown under large RF swings, ensuring reliable operation.

The electro-thermal feedback loop provides autonomous temperature regulation by sensing local temperature and adjusting V_tail to modulate oscillator power. Temperature sensing is performed by a PTAT sensor array consisting of four diode pairs placed at the corners of the inductor, beneath the ground plane, to avoid interference from the oscillator’s active core. The diodes are connected to small floating metal patches located in the symmetry plane of the differential inductor (a virtual ground), minimizing coupling from the strong RF fields. The PTAT output is multiplexed, amplified in two stages, and compared against a reference voltage (V_ref2) generated by a 7-bit coarse-fine resistor-string DAC. The second amplifier drives V_tail through a unity-gain buffer, with a 1 pF capacitor added to provide low impedance at high frequencies and filter RF noise. The loop is designed so that all electrical poles occur above 100 kHz, while the dominant thermal pole (typically <1 kHz) governs the overall dynamics, resulting in a stable first-order feedback system. The target temperature is programmed via the DAC, and the loop automatically reduces RF power once the setpoint is reached, preventing overshoot and ensuring long-term stability.

The chip, fabricated in GlobalFoundries 45-nm CMOS SOI technology, contains 12 pixels arranged in a 4×3 array, with pixel dimensions of 0.6 × 0.7 mm². Measurement results confirm >19.5 Vpp RF swing for four-stacked and >26.5 Vpp for five-stacked oscillators across 1.44–1.95 GHz, closely matching simulations. Continuous six-day monitoring shows no degradation in performance, validating the robustness of the stacked architecture. In localized heating demonstrations using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membranes—with and without embedded MNPs—only MNP-containing regions above active inductors reach temperatures of 41.9–47.1 °C, while control regions stay below 37.8 °C. Closed-loop operation achieves temperature regulation with a root-mean-square error of 0.29 °C over 37–49 °C. Adjacent pixel activation confirms sub-millimeter resolution with minimal thermal crosstalk, enabled by on-chip ground shielding. The system is particularly suited for in vitro and microfluidic applications, with a penetration depth of ~100 μm; however, the core oscillator design is readily adaptable to lower frequencies (hundreds of MHz) for deeper in vivo heating by using engineered MNPs with shifted FMR peaks.

### Examples

In a first example, the microheater array is employed for in vitro neuronal stimulation using thermosensitive TRPV1 channels expressed in cultured hippocampal neurons. A PDMS membrane containing 3.25% wt fatty-acid-coated 30-nm magnetite MNPs is placed over the chip surface, covering a region encompassing multiple pixels. A single pixel is activated in closed-loop mode with a target temperature of 42 °C. Within 50 milliseconds, the local temperature above the inductor rises to 42 °C and stabilizes, as confirmed by infrared thermography and on-chip PTAT sensors. Calcium imaging reveals transient intracellular Ca²⁺ influx in neurons directly above the heated zone, indicating channel activation and depolarization, while neurons outside the 0.03 mm² heated area show no response. The experiment is repeated across multiple pixels, demonstrating spatially addressable neuromodulation without chemical or optical agents.

In a second example, the array is used for targeted hyperthermia of cancer spheroids. Human breast adenocarcinoma (MCF-7) spheroids are incubated with MNPs and placed on the chip. Three adjacent pixels are simultaneously activated at 1.5 GHz with a closed-loop setpoint of 43 °C. After 10 minutes of exposure, live/dead staining shows extensive apoptosis in spheroids located above the active pixels, while control spheroids (without MNPs or outside the heated zones) remain viable. Temperature mapping confirms that heating is confined to the intended regions, with gradients exceeding 10 °C/mm at the boundaries, illustrating the system’s ability to spare healthy tissue.

In a third example, the platform is integrated into a microfluidic PCR chip. A microchannel is bonded over the array, and a droplet containing DNA template, primers, Taq polymerase, and MNPs is flowed over a pixel. The pixel is cycled between 95 °C (denaturation), 55 °C (annealing), and 72 °C (extension) using closed-loop control, completing 30 cycles in under 10 minutes. Gel electrophoresis confirms successful amplification of a 200-bp target sequence, demonstrating the utility of the invention in rapid, localized nucleic acid diagnostics.

These examples underscore the versatility, precision, and efficacy of the disclosed magnetic microheater array in diverse biomedical contexts, establishing it as a foundational technology for next-generation bioelectronic interfaces.
