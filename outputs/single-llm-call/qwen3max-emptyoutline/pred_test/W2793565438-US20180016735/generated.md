# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of biocomposite materials, and more specifically to lignocellulosic composites (LCs) fabricated from partially dissolved cellulose matrices reinforced with undissolved lignocellulosic biomass. These composites are entirely derived from natural, renewable, and biodegradable sources—namely cotton as a cellulose matrix precursor and lignocellulosic reinforcements such as Osage orange wood (OOW) particles and blue agave fibers (AF)—without the use of synthetic adhesives, resins, or chemical crosslinking agents. The invention further encompasses methods for producing such composites using cold aqueous alkaline solvent systems, particularly those containing lithium hydroxide monohydrate (LiOH·H₂O) and urea, under controlled sub-zero temperatures. The resulting LCs exhibit favorable mechanical properties relative to conventional bioplastics and packaging materials, while maintaining full compostability and environmental sustainability. This invention is situated at the intersection of green materials science, sustainable manufacturing, and circular bioeconomy principles, offering a novel pathway for valorizing agricultural and forestry by-products into high-performance, eco-friendly structural materials.

## BACKGROUND

The global demand for sustainable alternatives to petroleum-based plastics and composite materials has intensified due to mounting environmental concerns, including plastic pollution, non-biodegradability, and reliance on finite fossil resources. In response, researchers have turned to lignocellulosic biomass—a naturally abundant, renewable, and carbon-neutral feedstock—as a promising source for next-generation biocomposites. Lignocellulosic biomass, which constitutes the primary structural component of plant cell walls, is composed of three major constituents: cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. Cellulose, a linear polymer of β-(1→4)-linked glucose units, provides tensile strength and crystallinity; hemicellulose, a heterogeneous branched polysaccharide of pentoses and hexoses, acts as a matrix filler and interfacial binder; and lignin, a complex aromatic polymer derived from phenylpropanoid units, imparts rigidity and resistance to microbial degradation. Despite its abundance and favorable mechanical attributes, lignocellulosic biomass is inherently recalcitrant to processing due to the strong physical and chemical interactions among these three components, particularly the covalent and hydrogen bonding between cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, which collectively form a highly resistant supramolecular architecture.

To overcome this recalcitrance, various pretreatment strategies have been developed to disrupt the lignocellulosic matrix and enhance the accessibility of cellulose for downstream applications. Among these, alkaline pretreatments have long been recognized for their efficacy in solubilizing lignin and hemicellulose, thereby exposing cellulose microfibrils. Conventional alkaline methods typically employ elevated temperatures and high concentrations of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) or potassium hydroxide (KOH), but such conditions often lead to significant degradation of cellulose, energy-intensive processing, and the generation of inhibitory by-products. In recent years, a paradigm shift has emerged with the development of cold alkaline solvent systems, which operate at sub-zero temperatures (typically between −5 °C and −15 °C) and utilize aqueous mixtures of alkali hydroxides (e.g., NaOH, KOH, or LiOH) combined with urea or other chaotropic agents. These systems exploit the unique thermodynamic and kinetic properties of cellulose dissolution at low temperatures, where the formation of hydrated alkali-cellulose complexes is stabilized, enabling partial or complete dissolution of cellulose without extensive depolymerization.

Notably, Cai and Zhang demonstrated that a 7% NaOH/12% urea aqueous solution cooled to approximately −12 °C could effectively dissolve cellulose, leading to the fabrication of all-cellulose composites (ACCs)—materials composed entirely of cellulose in both matrix and reinforcement phases. Subsequent studies confirmed that LiOH·H₂O/urea systems, particularly at concentrations of 4.6% LiOH·H₂O and 15% urea cooled to −12 °C, exhibited superior cellulose-dissolving capacity compared to NaOH- or KOH-based formulations, attributed to the smaller ionic radius and higher hydration energy of Li⁺ ions, which facilitate deeper penetration into cellulose microfibrils and more efficient disruption of intermolecular hydrogen bonds. These ACCs have been used to produce films, gels, and fibers with tunable mechanical and thermal properties, and their fully biodegradable nature makes them attractive for packaging, biomedical, and textile applications.

However, despite their environmental benefits, ACCs face significant economic and practical limitations. The requirement for high-purity cellulose (e.g., cotton linters) as the sole raw material increases production costs, while the absence of lignin and hemicellulose—components that contribute to stiffness, dimensional stability, and UV resistance in natural fibers—can result in composites with suboptimal mechanical performance for structural applications. Moreover, the complete dissolution of cellulose necessitates precise control over solvent composition, temperature, and concentration, limiting scalability and process robustness. To address these challenges, researchers have explored hybrid systems that incorporate undissolved lignocellulosic fillers into dissolved cellulose matrices, but such approaches often rely on synthetic binders, coupling agents, or post-treatment modifications to achieve adequate interfacial adhesion, thereby compromising the “all-natural” premise and biodegradability of the final product.

In parallel, agricultural and forestry residues—such as blue agave bagasse (a by-product of tequila production) and Osage orange wood (an invasive species in the Midwestern United States)—have garnered attention as low-cost, underutilized lignocellulosic resources. Blue agave fibers are known for their high tensile strength, low density, and excellent aspect ratio, making them suitable as reinforcement in biocomposites. Osage orange wood, though often discarded as waste, possesses a dense, fine-grained structure with high cellulose content and mechanical robustness. Yet, these materials remain largely unexploited due to the lack of economically viable and environmentally benign processing technologies that can integrate them into high-performance composites without chemical modification or synthetic additives.

Prior art fails to disclose a method for fabricating lignocellulosic composites that simultaneously achieves (1) complete elimination of synthetic adhesives or resins, (2) use of raw, untreated lignocellulosic reinforcements, (3) partial dissolution of a cellulose matrix to form an in situ adhesive gel, and (4) scalable, low-energy processing using cold alkaline solvents. Existing biocomposite patents either rely on thermoplastic matrices (e.g., polylactic acid), require chemical functionalization of fibers, or employ hot-pressing with synthetic binders. Furthermore, no prior art teaches the critical balance between matrix concentration, solvent volume, reaction temperature, and alkaline formulation necessary to optimize mechanical properties while maintaining cost-effectiveness and biodegradability. There remains a clear and unmet need for a novel class of lignocellulosic composites that leverages the synergistic combination of a partially dissolved cellulose matrix and raw lignocellulosic reinforcements, processed under mild, cold alkaline conditions, to yield a fully biodegradable, high-strength, and economically viable material suitable for packaging, construction, or consumer goods.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel lignocellulosic composite (LC) and a method for its preparation, wherein the composite is formed from a partially dissolved cellulose matrix derived from natural cellulose sources (e.g., cotton) and undissolved lignocellulosic reinforcement materials (e.g., Osage orange wood particles and/or blue agave fibers), without the use of any synthetic adhesives, resins, or chemical crosslinking agents. The method involves the use of a cold aqueous alkaline solvent system, preferably comprising lithium hydroxide monohydrate (LiOH·H₂O) and urea, maintained at a temperature between −5 °C and −15 °C, to partially dissolve the cellulose source into a viscous gel-like matrix. This semidissolved matrix acts as a natural adhesive that intimately binds the lignocellulosic reinforcement particles during mixing, followed by solvent removal, water exchange, and thermal densification to yield a solid, cohesive composite panel.

The invention further discloses that the mechanical properties of the resulting LC are critically dependent on several key processing parameters: (1) the reaction temperature of the alkaline solvent, with optimal performance achieved at approximately −12.5 °C; (2) the concentration of the cellulose matrix, which is preferably between 15% and 35% by weight of the total dry solids, with 25% offering a favorable balance of cost and performance; (3) the volume of alkaline solvent used per unit of composite, with 60 mL per standard panel (approximately 10 g dry solids) yielding maximum mechanical strength; and (4) the specific alkaline formulation, wherein LiOH·H₂O/urea systems outperform NaOH/urea or KOH/urea counterparts due to enhanced cellulose gelation and interfacial binding.

The resultant LC exhibits tensile strength (σᵤ), elastic modulus (E), flexural strength (σ_fm), and flexural modulus (E_b) values that are comparable to or exceed those of several commercial bioplastics and packaging materials, while being fully biodegradable, compostable, and derived entirely from renewable resources. The composite demonstrates excellent interfacial adhesion between the matrix and reinforcement, as evidenced by optical microscopy and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, which also reveals partial removal of hemicellulose during processing—a phenomenon that may enhance cellulose accessibility and bonding. Thermogravimetric analysis confirms the thermal stability of the LC, with decomposition profiles reflecting the combined contributions of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin from its constituent materials.

Importantly, the invention enables the valorization of low-cost, underutilized biomass streams—such as cotton (a globally abundant cellulose source), blue agave bagasse (a tequila industry by-product), and Osage orange wood (an invasive species)—into high-value composite materials without requiring chemical pretreatment of the reinforcements. This not only reduces raw material costs but also aligns with circular economy principles by converting waste into functional products. Although the LC exhibits high water absorption and poor dimensional stability in humid environments—a limitation common to many hydrophilic biocomposites—the invention contemplates that surface coatings or post-treatments may be applied to mitigate this issue without compromising biodegradability.

In summary, the invention provides a scalable, environmentally benign, and economically viable method for producing high-performance lignocellulosic composites that bridge the gap between traditional all-cellulose composites and conventional fiber-reinforced plastics, offering a sustainable alternative for applications ranging from disposable packaging to semi-structural panels in construction and automotive interiors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Examples

The following examples illustrate the preparation and characterization of lignocellulosic composites (LCs) according to the present invention. These examples are provided for illustrative purposes only and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention.

**Example 1: Preparation of Lignocellulosic Composite Panels**

A lignocellulosic composite was prepared using cotton as the cellulose matrix source and a 1:1 mixture of Osage orange wood (OOW) particles and blue agave fibers (AF) as the reinforcement. Specifically, 2.5 g of cotton linters (Gossypium sp.), 3.75 g of OOW sawdust (particle size < 1 mm), and 3.75 g of AF (length 5–10 mm, diameter 100–300 μm) were used per panel, corresponding to a final dry composition of 25% cotton, 37.5% OOW, and 37.5% AF. The alkaline solvent consisted of an aqueous solution of 4.6% (w/w) lithium hydroxide monohydrate (LiOH·H₂O) and 15% (w/w) urea, pre-cooled to −12.5 °C in a refrigerated bath. A total of 60 mL of this solvent was placed in a double-walled glass vessel immersed in a cooling bath maintained at −12.5 °C. The cotton linters were added to the solvent and stirred at 350 rpm using a three-blade propeller mixer (Model 1750, Arrow Engineering, Hillside, NJ, USA) for 15 minutes, resulting in the partial dissolution of the cotton into a white, translucent, viscous gel. Subsequently, the OOW and AF were added to the vessel and mixed for an additional 10 minutes at the same speed to ensure uniform dispersion. The resulting slurry was poured into a polyethylene foam mold (outer dimensions: 105 mm width × 130 mm length × 5 mm depth; inner cavity: 80 mm × 100 mm × 5 mm) and sandwiched between two polyethylene meshes with 2 mm² openings to prevent fiber migration during solvent exchange.

The mold was then subjected to a series of solvent-water exchange steps to remove the alkaline solvent and regenerate the cellulose matrix. First, the composite was submerged in tap water under vacuum (25 inches Hg) for 15 minutes to facilitate rapid diffusion of solutes. This was followed by continuous immersion in deionized water for 24 hours, with the water replaced six times at regular intervals to ensure complete removal of LiOH, urea, and dissolved hemicellulose. After the first hour of soaking, the composite had sufficiently gelled to allow removal from the mold, enabling more efficient water exchange. Following the 24-hour soak, the panel was gently blotted dry with paper towels and placed between two stainless steel plates under a compressive pressure of 0.5 psi (0.0034 MPa). The assembly was then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C and 25 inches Hg for 24 hours. Finally, the dried panel was densified by hot pressing at 180 °C under 8 tons of pressure for 10 minutes, resulting in a smooth, homogeneous composite sheet approximately 1.2 mm thick with a final dry weight of 8.2 g (from an initial 10 g of dry solids).

**Example 2: Optimization of Reaction Temperature**

To evaluate the effect of reaction temperature on composite performance, panels were prepared as in Example 1 but using reaction temperatures of −5, −7.5, −10, −12.5, and −15 °C. Mechanical testing revealed that panels prepared at −12.5 °C and −15 °C exhibited significantly higher tensile strength (σᵤ), elastic modulus (E), flexural strength (σ_fm), and flexural modulus (E_b) compared to those prepared at higher temperatures. Specifically, the −12.5 °C panel showed σᵤ, E, σ_fm, and E_b values that were 108%, 151%, 183%, and 203% higher, respectively, than those of the −5 °C panel. This enhancement is attributed to the formation of a more viscous and cohesive cellulose gel at lower temperatures, which improves interfacial adhesion with the reinforcement. Based on these results, −12.5 °C was selected as the optimal reaction temperature for subsequent studies.

**Example 3: Variation of Matrix Concentration**

Panels were prepared with cotton matrix concentrations of 15%, 25%, 30%, and 35% (with the remainder being a 1:1 mixture of OOW and AF). Mechanical properties increased with higher cotton content, with the 35% cotton panel exhibiting σᵤ, E, σ_fm, and E_b values 135%, 343%, 153%, and 109% higher, respectively, than the 15% cotton panel. However, the marginal gain between 30% and 35% cotton was minimal, and given the higher cost of cotton relative to the reinforcements, a 25% cotton formulation was deemed optimal for balancing performance and economics.

**Example 4: Solvent Volume Optimization**

Using the 25% cotton: 75% OOW/AF formulation, panels were prepared with alkaline solvent volumes of 45, 60, 75, 90, and 105 mL per panel. The 60 mL volume yielded the highest mechanical properties; both lower and higher volumes resulted in diminished performance. Excess solvent (e.g., 105 mL) diluted the matrix, leading to a “runny” mixture with poor cohesion and reduced strength. The 60 mL/panel condition was thus established as the standard.

**Example 5: Comparison of Alkaline Formulations**

Panels were prepared using 60 mL of various cold alkaline solvents: 4.6% LiOH·H₂O/15% urea, 7% NaOH/12% urea, and 9.8% KOH/12% urea, all at −12.5 °C. The LiOH·H₂O/urea system produced composites with superior mechanical properties, confirming the advantage of lithium-based solvents in promoting effective cellulose gelation and interfacial binding.

**Example 6: Characterization**

Optical microscopy revealed a smooth surface with a continuous cotton matrix embedding dark AF and lighter OOW particles. Cross-sections showed a heterogeneous but well-bonded structure with minimal voids. FTIR spectroscopy confirmed the absence of a strong hemicellulose peak at 1742 cm⁻¹ in the composite, indicating hemicellulose removal during processing. TGA showed a multi-stage degradation profile consistent with the presence of cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, with 16.1% ash residue at 600 °C. Mechanical testing demonstrated that the LC outperformed several commercial bioplastics in key metrics, though it exhibited high water absorption (202% weight gain after 24-hour immersion), indicating a need for moisture-resistant coatings in certain applications.

### Exemplary Embodiments

The present invention may be embodied in various forms, as detailed below.

**Embodiment 1: Composition of the Lignocellulosic Composite**

In one embodiment, the lignocellulosic composite comprises a cellulose matrix derived from a partially dissolved natural cellulose source, wherein the cellulose matrix constitutes between 15% and 35% by weight of the total dry solids of the composite, and a lignocellulosic reinforcement phase comprising between 65% and 85% by weight of the total dry solids, wherein the reinforcement phase consists of one or more untreated lignocellulosic materials selected from the group consisting of wood particles, agricultural fibers, grasses, and bast fibers. Preferably, the cellulose source is cotton linters, and the reinforcement phase is a mixture of Osage orange wood particles and blue agave fibers in a 1:1 weight ratio. The composite is free of synthetic polymers, adhesives, resins, and crosslinking agents, and is fully biodegradable and compostable.

**Embodiment 2: Method of Fabrication**

In another embodiment, the method for producing the lignocellulosic composite comprises the following steps: (a) preparing a cold aqueous alkaline solvent by dissolving lithium hydroxide monohydrate and urea in water to form a solution containing 4.6% (w/w) LiOH·H₂O and 15% (w/w) urea, and cooling the solution to a temperature between −12 °C and −13 °C; (b) adding a cellulose source to the solvent at a concentration of 25% by weight of the total dry solids and stirring for 10 to 20 minutes to form a semidissolved cellulose gel; (c) adding lignocellulosic reinforcement materials to the gel at a concentration of 75% by weight of the total dry solids and mixing for 5 to 15 minutes to form a homogeneous slurry; (d) casting the slurry into a mold and subjecting it to a solvent-water exchange process involving vacuum-assisted water immersion followed by prolonged soaking with periodic water replacement; (e) drying the resulting gel under vacuum at 60 °C; and (f) densifying the dried composite by hot pressing at 180 °C under 8 tons of pressure for 10 minutes.

**Embodiment 3: Optimized Processing Parameters**

In a further embodiment, the method employs a solvent volume of 60 mL per 10 g of total dry solids, a reaction temperature of −12.5 °C, and a cellulose matrix concentration of 25% by weight. The lignocellulosic reinforcement is used in its raw, untreated form, without chemical or thermal pretreatment, thereby reducing processing costs and environmental impact.

**Embodiment 4: Applications**

The lignocellulosic composite may be used in applications requiring biodegradable, lightweight, and mechanically robust materials, including but not limited to disposable packaging, food containers, interior automotive panels, furniture components, and building insulation. Due to its high water absorption, the composite may be coated with a biodegradable hydrophobic layer (e.g., shellac, beeswax, or polylactic acid) for use in humid environments.

**Embodiment 5: Environmental and Economic Advantages**

The invention provides a sustainable route to valorize agricultural and forestry by-products—such as blue agave bagasse and Osage orange wood—into high-value composites, reducing waste and creating new revenue streams for rural economies. The use of cold alkaline processing minimizes energy consumption compared to conventional high-temperature methods, and the absence of synthetic additives ensures end-of-life compostability, aligning with zero-waste and circular economy objectives.
