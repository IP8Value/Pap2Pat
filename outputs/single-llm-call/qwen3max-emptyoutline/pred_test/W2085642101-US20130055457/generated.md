## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates generally to the field of plant biotechnology and agricultural science. More specifically, the disclosure provides compositions and methods for improving agronomic performance in maize (Zea mays) through the transgenic expression of nucleic acid molecules encoding ZAR1 polypeptides. The disclosure further encompasses recombinant expression cassettes comprising native or modified ZAR1 alleles, transgenic maize plants expressing these alleles either individually or in stacked heterozygous combinations, and methods for enhancing grain yield, drought tolerance, and other complex agronomic traits under varying environmental conditions. The invention particularly addresses the interaction between transgene efficacy and environmental factors, as well as the functional differences between naturally occurring allelic variants of the ZAR1 gene that have been selected during conventional breeding programs.

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Modern agriculture faces unprecedented challenges in meeting global food demands while adapting to increasingly variable climatic conditions. Traditional plant breeding has historically driven improvements in crop productivity, particularly in major cereal crops such as maize. Through decades of selective breeding, maize yields in the United States Corn Belt have steadily increased, largely due to the development of hybrid varieties that exploit heterosis, or hybrid vigor. These breeding efforts have focused on optimizing performance within specific target populations of environments (TPE), recognizing that genotype-by-environment interactions significantly influence complex agronomic traits like grain yield, drought tolerance, and nitrogen use efficiency.

While conventional breeding remains a cornerstone of crop improvement, biotechnology offers complementary tools for introducing novel traits into elite germplasm. Transgenic approaches have successfully delivered single-gene traits with relatively straightforward mechanisms of action, such as insect resistance through Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) toxins and herbicide tolerance through modified enzymes. However, extending biotechnology to complex quantitative traits—those controlled by multiple genes with small individual effects and strong environmental influences—has proven considerably more challenging. Unlike simple traits, complex agronomic characteristics often exhibit significant genotype-by-environment interactions, making consistent transgene performance across diverse growing conditions difficult to achieve.

Genes involved in the regulation of plant and organ size have emerged as promising candidates for improving intrinsic yield potential. These "intrinsic yield genes" modulate fundamental developmental processes that determine biomass accumulation and reproductive output. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the ARGOS (Auxin Regulated Gene involved in Organ Size) gene was identified as a key regulator of organ size through its influence on cell proliferation rather than cell expansion. Overexpression of ARGOS in Arabidopsis resulted in larger organs due to prolonged cell division activity, though this came at the cost of delayed flowering. The identification of maize orthologues of ARGOS, collectively designated as ZAR (Zea mays ARGOS) genes, presented an opportunity to explore whether similar mechanisms could be harnessed to improve maize productivity.

Maize breeding programs have systematically shaped the genetic architecture of elite germplasm through selection pressure applied over many generations. This process has led to the partitioning of genetic diversity into complementary heterotic groups, primarily the Stiff Stalk (SS) and Non-Stiff Stoll (NS) pools in North American maize. Within these groups, specific allelic combinations have been favored based on their contribution to hybrid performance under targeted environmental conditions. Recent genomic analyses have revealed that certain loci, including those potentially involved in stress tolerance and yield determination, show signatures of selection during breeding history. Understanding how naturally selected allelic variation interacts with transgenic approaches represents a critical frontier in integrated crop improvement strategies.

Despite advances in both conventional breeding and biotechnology, there remains a fundamental gap in understanding how transgenes interact with natural genetic variation and environmental conditions to influence complex agronomic traits. Most transgenic testing protocols evaluate candidate genes in isolation, without considering the context of elite germplasm structure or environmental variability. This limitation becomes particularly problematic when attempting to translate laboratory findings into field performance, where environmental stresses such as drought, heat, and high planting density can dramatically alter trait expression. Furthermore, the relationship between different allelic forms of a gene—whether naturally occurring or engineered—and their combined effects in heterozygous states has received limited attention in transgenic research.

The present disclosure addresses these challenges by providing a comprehensive framework for evaluating and deploying ZAR1-based transgenic technologies in maize. By systematically analyzing the effects of different ZAR1 alleles, both individually and in combination, across diverse environmental conditions, the invention establishes new paradigms for transgenic crop improvement that align with principles of conventional breeding. The disclosure demonstrates that careful consideration of allelic variation, environmental interactions, and heterotic effects can lead to more predictable and robust transgenic outcomes for complex agronomic traits.

Specifically, ZAR1 transgenes tend to confer yield advantages under warmer, drier environmental conditions (classified as Temperate Dry or Temperate environments) while potentially causing yield penalties under cooler, wetter conditions (Temperate Humid or High Latitude environments). This environmental dependency mirrors patterns observed in conventional breeding programs and underscores the importance of matching transgene deployment with appropriate target environments.

The disclosure further reveals that naturally occurring allelic variants of ZAR1, specifically the SS1 and NS1 alleles prevalent in elite maize germplasm, demonstrate functional differences that impact transgene efficacy. These differences arise from both cis-regulatory variations in promoter regions, leading to differential expression patterns, and amino acid substitutions in coding regions that may affect protein structure and function. When these alleles are expressed in their native heterozygous combination—either through conventional breeding or transgenic stacking—they produce synergistic effects that exceed the performance of either allele alone.

In yet another aspect, the disclosure provides methods for producing transgenic maize plants with enhanced agronomic performance by introducing ZAR1 nucleic acid sequences into maize cells and regenerating fertile transgenic plants therefrom. These methods include both Agrobacterium-mediated transformation and other suitable plant transformation techniques. The disclosure also encompasses methods for selecting optimal ZAR1 alleles or allele combinations based on target environmental conditions and desired agronomic outcomes.

Additionally, the disclosure provides methods for evaluating transgene-by-environment interactions through systematic field testing across diverse environmental classes. These evaluation methods enable the identification of environmental conditions that maximize transgene benefits while minimizing potential drawbacks, thereby facilitating informed deployment decisions. The disclosure further includes analytical methods for assessing allelic expression patterns, protein structural features, and their relationships to phenotypic outcomes.

Overall, the present disclosure establishes a comprehensive platform for integrating transgenic approaches with conventional breeding principles to improve complex agronomic traits in maize. By leveraging insights into natural allelic variation, environmental interactions, and heterotic effects, the invention provides practical solutions for developing maize varieties with enhanced yield stability and stress tolerance across diverse growing conditions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE DISCLOSURE

### Nucleic Acids

The present disclosure provides isolated nucleic acid molecules encoding ZAR1 polypeptides that confer enhanced agronomic performance when expressed in maize plants. These nucleic acid molecules encompass both naturally occurring allelic variants and engineered modifications designed to optimize specific functional properties. The ZAR1 gene family in maize comprises eight members (ZAR1-ZAR9, with ZAR2 being an allelic variant of ZAR1), each encoding relatively small proteins ranging from 64 to 152 amino acids in length. Among these family members, ZAR1 represents the closest orthologue to the Arabidopsis ARGOS gene and exhibits the most promising agronomic effects when overexpressed in transgenic maize.

The nucleic acid sequences provided herein include full-length coding sequences corresponding to specific ZAR1 alleles, particularly those derived from elite maize germplasm representing major heterotic groups. Two predominant allelic variants have been identified and characterized: the SS1 allele, commonly found in Stiff Stalk (SS) heterotic group lines, and the NS1 allele, prevalent in Non-Stiff Stalk (NS) heterotic group lines. These alleles differ both in their promoter regions and coding sequences, resulting in distinct functional properties when expressed in transgenic plants.

The SS1 allele coding sequence, as deposited in GenBank under accession number JN252296, encodes a polypeptide of [X] amino acids with specific sequence features that distinguish it from the NS1 variant. Similarly, the NS1 allele coding sequence corresponds to the same GenBank entry but contains nucleotide polymorphisms that result in amino acid substitutions at key positions. These substitutions include a leucine/methionine (L/M) variation at the most conserved motif region, which likely influences secondary protein structure, and an alanine/glycine (A/G) difference that may create a highly flexible glycine-glycine-glycine (GGG) loop adjacent to the conserved motif in the SS1 variant. These structural differences potentially affect protein-protein interactions, subcellular localization, or other functional aspects of the ZAR1 polypeptide.

In addition to full-length coding sequences, the disclosure encompasses functional fragments and variants of ZAR1 nucleic acids that retain the ability to enhance agronomic performance when expressed in maize. These variants may include conservative amino acid substitutions that maintain protein function while potentially improving stability, solubility, or other desirable properties. The disclosure also covers nucleic acid molecules with optimized codon usage for enhanced expression in maize cells, as well as synthetic variants designed to minimize sequence homology with endogenous maize genes while preserving functional domains.

The nucleic acid molecules provided herein may be used in various configurations depending on the intended application. For constitutive overexpression studies, the coding sequences may be placed under the control of strong constitutive promoters such as the maize ubiquitin promoter (ProZmUBI). Alternatively, for more targeted expression patterns, the native promoter regions of specific ZAR1 alleles may be utilized, taking advantage of their inherent regulatory properties. The SS1 and NS1 alleles possess distinct promoter architectures, with numerous insertions/deletions identified within the 1 kb region upstream of the presumed transcription start site. These promoter variations contribute to differential expression patterns observed between the two alleles in hybrid backgrounds.

The disclosure further encompasses nucleic acid constructs designed to test specific hypotheses about ZAR1 function. For instance, chimeric constructs combining the promoter of one allele with the coding sequence of another can help dissect the relative contributions of regulatory versus coding sequence variations to overall transgene efficacy. Additionally, constructs incorporating enhancer elements such as the CaMV 35S enhancer may be used to boost expression levels beyond those achieved with native promoters alone.

All nucleic acid molecules described herein are preferably substantially pure and isolated from their natural environment. They may be synthesized chemically, amplified through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using appropriate primers, or cloned from genomic DNA or cDNA libraries prepared from maize tissues. The sequences may be verified through standard molecular biology techniques including restriction enzyme analysis, sequencing, and functional assays in model systems prior to plant transformation.

### Recombinant Expression Cassettes

The present disclosure provides recombinant expression cassettes comprising ZAR1 nucleic acid sequences operably linked to regulatory elements suitable for driving expression in plant cells. These expression cassettes represent essential components for implementing the disclosed transgenic technologies in maize and other crop species. Each expression cassette typically includes, in operative combination, a promoter element, the ZAR1 coding sequence, and a transcription termination signal, along with additional regulatory elements as needed for optimal performance.

Promoter selection plays a crucial role in determining the spatial, temporal, and quantitative aspects of ZAR1 expression. The disclosure encompasses expression cassettes utilizing various types of promoters, each serving distinct purposes in transgenic applications. Constitutive promoters, such as the maize ubiquitin promoter (ProZmUBI), provide continuous expression throughout plant development and across multiple tissue types. This approach proved effective in initial proof-of-concept studies demonstrating ZAR1's ability to enhance plant and organ growth through increased cell proliferation.

For more refined control of ZAR1 expression, the disclosure provides expression cassettes incorporating native ZAR1 promoters from specific allelic variants. The SS1 and NS1 alleles possess distinct promoter architectures characterized by numerous insertions/deletions within the 1 kb region upstream of the transcription start site. These promoter variations contribute to differential expression patterns observed between the two alleles in hybrid backgrounds, suggesting that native promoters may provide more physiologically relevant expression profiles compared to heterologous constitutive promoters. Expression cassettes utilizing these native promoters enable researchers to study the effects of allelic variation in both regulatory and coding regions simultaneously.

The disclosure also encompasses expression cassettes incorporating tissue-specific promoters designed to restrict ZAR1 expression to particular organs or developmental stages. Given that ZAR1 affects multiple aspects of plant growth and development, targeted expression may help optimize beneficial effects while minimizing potential negative consequences. For example, promoters active specifically in reproductive tissues could enhance grain yield without affecting vegetative growth characteristics that might be undesirable in certain production systems.

Transcription termination signals included in the expression cassettes ensure proper processing of mRNA transcripts and contribute to overall expression efficiency. The disclosure specifically mentions the use of the PINII terminator in initial vector constructions, though other suitable termination signals known in the art may be employed as well. Additional regulatory elements, such as introns, untranslated regions (UTRs), and enhancer sequences, may be incorporated into the expression cassettes to further modulate expression levels and patterns.

A particularly innovative aspect of the disclosure involves expression cassettes designed to test the effects of heterozygous allele combinations. These constructs include separate expression units for both SS1 and NS1 alleles, either arranged in tandem within a single T-DNA region or distributed across different genomic locations following transformation. The molecular stacking of both alleles in a single transgenic event enables direct comparison with individual allele expressions and provides insights into potential synergistic interactions between different ZAR1 variants.

The expression cassettes described herein are typically assembled using modern molecular cloning techniques, including Gateway recombination technology as mentioned in the research paper. This system facilitates efficient transfer of ZAR1 coding sequences between different vector backbones while maintaining reading frame integrity and regulatory element functionality. The resulting expression cassettes are then incorporated into binary vectors suitable for plant transformation, such as those compatible with Agrobacterium-mediated delivery systems.

Each expression cassette is designed to include selectable markers and/or visual reporters to facilitate identification and selection of transgenic events. The disclosure specifically describes the use of a red-emitting fluorescent protein (Ds-Red) driven by an aleurone-specific promoter (PromHvLTP2) as a visible marker for transgene expression. This reporter system enables rapid screening of transgenic plants and their progeny without destructive sampling, streamlining the characterization process.

### Expression of Proteins in Host Cells

The present disclosure provides methods and compositions for expressing ZAR1 polypeptides in host cells, particularly maize cells, to achieve enhanced agronomic performance. Successful expression of ZAR1 proteins requires careful consideration of multiple factors, including transformation methodology, host cell selection, and post-transformation characterization procedures. The disclosure encompasses both transient and stable expression systems, though stable integration into the plant genome represents the primary approach for commercial applications.

Agrobacterium-mediated transformation serves as the preferred method for introducing ZAR1 expression cassettes into maize cells. This technique utilizes disarmed Agrobacterium strains, specifically LBA4404 as mentioned in the research paper, carrying binary vectors with the desired ZAR1 constructs. Immature embryos from elite maize inbred lines, such as Hi-II or PHWWZ, serve as suitable explants for transformation due to their high regeneration capacity and genetic uniformity. The transformation protocol involves co-cultivation of explants with Agrobacterium, followed by selection on media containing appropriate antibiotics or herbicides to eliminate non-transformed tissue.

Following successful transformation, primary transformants (T0 generation) undergo rigorous molecular characterization to confirm transgene integration and expression. Southern blot analysis or quantitative PCR determines transgene copy number, with single-copy events generally preferred for subsequent development due to their genetic stability and predictable inheritance patterns. Northern blot analysis or quantitative RT-PCR assesses transcript levels, ensuring that the transgene is actively expressed in transformed tissues. Western blot analysis may be employed to verify protein production, though the small size of ZAR1 polypeptides (64-152 amino acids) may present technical challenges for detection.

Transgenic plants derived from primary transformants are typically backcrossed to non-transgenic parental lines to introduce the transgene into desired genetic backgrounds while eliminating potential somaclonal variation introduced during tissue culture. The research paper describes backcrossing protocols involving either three generations (for Hi-II transformations) or one generation (for PHWWZ transformations) to obtain stable transgenic inbred lines. During this process, transgenic and non-transgenic null segregants are identified using the Ds-Red fluorescent marker, enabling precise tracking of transgene inheritance.

Expression of ZAR1 proteins in host cells leads to measurable phenotypic changes that reflect the gene's role in regulating plant and organ size. Microscopic analysis of leaf epidermal cells reveals that ZAR1 overexpression primarily increases cell number rather than cell size, consistent with its classification as an intrinsic yield gene. This mechanism differs from some other organ size regulators that act through cell expansion pathways. The increased cell proliferation translates into enhanced vegetative growth, as evidenced by greater stalk, ear, and total dry biomasses, as well as expanded leaf area in transgenic plants compared to non-transgenic controls.

Importantly, ZAR1 expression effects vary depending on environmental conditions and genetic background. Under controlled greenhouse conditions, transgenic inbred plants exhibit increased plant height primarily through elongation of upper internodes (sixth and above), though this effect may not manifest consistently under field conditions due to differences in light quality, temperature regimes, and photoperiod. Similarly, the magnitude of growth enhancement depends on planting density, with more pronounced effects observed at higher population densities that impose greater competitive stress on individual plants.

The disclosure emphasizes that ZAR1 expression does not extend the overall growth period but rather accelerates growth rate during active development phases. This contrasts with observations in Arabidopsis ARGOS overexpression lines, which exhibited delayed flowering alongside increased organ size. The absence of developmental timing alterations in maize ZAR1 transgenics represents a significant advantage for commercial applications, as it avoids potential mismatches between crop maturity and local growing season constraints.

Protein expression levels do not necessarily correlate directly with phenotypic outcomes, as demonstrated by comparisons between different ZAR1 allele configurations. While SS1 transgenic plants showed highest total ZAR1 expression (transgenic plus endogenous) in leaf tissue, they did not consistently outperform NS1 or stacked allele combinations in terms of grain yield enhancement. This suggests that factors beyond simple expression dosage—including spatial distribution, temporal dynamics, and protein functional differences—contribute significantly to overall transgene efficacy.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

This example describes the identification and characterization of the maize ZAR gene family, establishing the foundation for subsequent transgenic studies. Initial bioinformatic analyses identified nine putative ZAR homologues (ZAR1-ZAR9) in the maize genome through sequence similarity searches against public and proprietary databases using the Arabidopsis ARGOS gene as query. Subsequent detailed examination revealed that ZAR2 actually represented an allelic variant of ZAR1 rather than a distinct paralogue, reducing the confirmed family membership to eight genes in the B73 reference genome.

Sequence alignments of the ZAR family members demonstrated overall low sequence homology across full-length proteins, except for a highly conserved core domain region shared among all members. This conservation pattern suggested that despite divergent evolutionary trajectories, the ZAR proteins maintained common structural or functional motifs essential for their biological activity. All ZAR genes encoded relatively small polypeptides, with lengths ranging from 64 to 152 amino acids, consistent with their proposed roles as signaling molecules or regulatory factors rather than structural proteins.

Tissue-specific expression profiling using Solexa RNA sequencing data revealed diverse expression patterns among ZAR family members, indicating functional specialization within the gene family. ZAR1 exhibited broad expression across multiple tissues but showed particularly high transcript abundance in root and pericarp tissues. Other family members displayed more restricted expression patterns: ZAR3 was highly expressed in multiple tissues, ZAR4 and ZAR7 showed root-preferential expression, ZAR8 was predominantly expressed in kernel tissues, and ZAR9 demonstrated pollen-specific expression. These distinct expression profiles suggested that different ZAR family members might regulate organ size and development in tissue-specific contexts.

Based on its status as the closest orthologue to Arabidopsis ARGOS and its broad expression pattern, ZAR1 was selected as the primary candidate for transgenic overexpression studies aimed at improving maize agronomic performance. The identification and characterization of the complete ZAR gene family provided crucial context for interpreting transgenic results and highlighted the complexity of organ size regulation networks in maize compared to the simpler Arabidopsis model system.

### Example 2

This example details the construction of recombinant expression cassettes for ZAR1 overexpression studies and their introduction into maize through Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. Gateway recombination technology was employed to efficiently assemble expression cassettes containing various ZAR1 alleles under different promoter controls. The basic cassette architecture included a promoter element, the full-length ZAR1 cDNA sequence, and the PINII transcription terminator, all cloned into the pENTR/D-TOPO vector backbone.

For constitutive overexpression experiments, the maize ubiquitin promoter (ProZmUBI) was used to drive ZAR1 expression throughout plant development. Additional constructs incorporated native promoter regions from specific ZAR1 alleles to investigate the effects of natural regulatory variation. Due to haplotype differences in promoter regions between SS1 and NS1 alleles, final promoter fragments of 1,376 bp (SS1) and 1,724 bp (NS1) were obtained and used in respective constructs. Some vectors also included the CaMV 35S enhancer inserted upstream of the promoter in forward orientation to potentially boost expression levels.

Each expression cassette was co-integrated with a Ds-Red fluorescent protein reporter gene driven by the aleurone-specific PromHvLTP2 promoter. This visual marker enabled non-destructive identification of transgenic plants and their progeny through red fluorescence in seed aleurone layers. The complete T-DNA regions containing both ZAR1 expression cassettes and Ds-Red markers were transferred into Agrobacterium strain LBA4404 for plant transformation.

Immature embryos from either Hi-II maize or Pioneer inbred line PHWWZ served as explants for transformation. Standard protocols involving co-cultivation with Agrobacterium, followed by selection on appropriate media, yielded primary transformants (T0 generation). Typically, 20 independent transgenic events were generated per construct, from which 10 single-copy events with confirmed transgene expression were selected for further characterization. Molecular confirmation included Southern blot analysis for copy number determination and RT-PCR for transcript detection.

Selected transgenic events underwent backcrossing to non-transgenic parental lines to establish stable transgenic inbred lines. For Hi-II-derived transformants, three generations of backcrossing were performed, while PHWWZ-derived lines required only one generation due to differences in genetic background stability. Throughout this process, Ds-Red fluorescence facilitated accurate tracking of transgene inheritance and segregation, ensuring proper identification of transgenic and null segregant plants for comparative studies.

### Example 3

This example presents microscopic analyses of leaf epidermal cells from ZAR1-overexpressing maize plants to determine the cellular basis of observed growth enhancements. Leaf tissue samples were collected from greenhouse-grown plants at the V8 growth stage, with 2×2 cm sections excised from the midpoint of leaf number eight for analysis. Epidermal peels were prepared and examined under light microscopy to count cell numbers and stomatal densities per unit area.

Results demonstrated that ZAR1-overexpressing inbred plants exhibited either unchanged or slightly higher cell counts per unit area compared to non-transgenic controls. Similarly, stomatal densities remained comparable between transgenic and control plants. These findings indicated that the increased leaf area and overall plant size observed in ZAR1 transgenics resulted primarily from increased cell number rather than cell expansion. This mechanism aligned with previous observations in Arabidopsis ARGOS overexpression lines, where enhanced organ size was attributed to prolonged cell proliferation rather than enlarged cell dimensions.

The cellular analysis provided crucial insight into ZAR1's mode of action as an intrinsic yield gene. By promoting cell division without altering cell size, ZAR1 effectively increased the total number of photosynthetic units available for biomass production while maintaining normal cellular physiology. This approach contrasted with other organ size regulators that might affect cell expansion pathways, potentially leading to altered tissue mechanics or water relations.

Interestingly, the magnitude of cell number increases varied between different ZAR1 allele configurations and environmental conditions. While constitutive overexpression using the maize ubiquitin promoter produced consistent effects across multiple transgenic events, native promoter-driven expression showed more variable outcomes depending on specific allele combinations and growth conditions. These observations underscored the importance of both regulatory and coding sequence variations in determining ZAR1's functional impact at the cellular level.

### Example 4

This example describes field experiments conducted to evaluate the agronomic performance of ZAR1-overexpressing maize hybrids under varying planting densities and environmental conditions. Initial phenotypic measurements were carried out in 2006 at Johnston, Iowa, using a nested experimental design with transgenic and null control pairs, three replications, and 2-row plots (4 m row length) per event. Plants were thinned at the V3-V4 growth stage to achieve three different planting densities: 10,000, 25,000, and 40,000 plants per acre.

Measurements included early stand counts, leaf area determination using a LI-3100 leaf area meter, and biomass assessment through destructive sampling of four plants per plot at multiple time points. Yield component data were collected by harvesting five representative ears per plot. Statistical analyses using ANOVA with Duncan's t-test revealed significant differences between transgenic and null control plants for multiple traits, with effects becoming more pronounced at higher planting densities.

Specifically, ZAR1-overexpressing hybrids demonstrated enhanced vegetative and reproductive growth across all measured parameters, including increased stalk, ear, and total dry biomasses, as well as expanded leaf area. The positive effects on total kernel number became statistically significant only at the highest planting density (40,000 plants per acre), suggesting that ZAR1's benefits were particularly valuable under competitive stress conditions imposed by high population densities. This finding aligned with observations of reduced tip kernel abortion and barrenness rates in transgenic plants, indicating improved resource allocation and stress tolerance during reproductive development.

The experiment also revealed that ZAR1-enhanced growth resulted from accelerated growth rates rather than extended developmental periods. Transgenic plants reached full maturity faster than controls while achieving greater final sizes, demonstrating efficient conversion of resources into biomass without compromising developmental timing. This characteristic represented a significant advantage over some other growth-promoting genes that might delay flowering or maturity, potentially creating mismatches with local growing season constraints.

### Example 5

This example presents yield testing results from multi-location field trials conducted across the U.S. Central Corn Belt to evaluate ZAR1 transgene performance under diverse environmental conditions. Initial trials in 2007 at four locations revealed significant yield increases in three locations but a significant yield reduction in one location compared to non-transgenic controls. The second year (2008) of testing confirmed this pattern of variable performance, with significant yield increases or decreases depending on specific location characteristics.

To better understand these variable outcomes, test locations were classified using the EnClass® system based on environmental variables including rainfall, daily temperature, and cumulative solar radiation during the growing season. This classification identified four major environment classes: Temperate Dry (TD), Temperate (T), Temperate Humid (TH), and High Latitude (HL), representing a gradient from hotter, drier conditions with high solar radiation to cooler, wetter conditions with lower solar radiation.

Analysis of yield data across these environment classes revealed a clear pattern: ZAR1 transgenics tended to exhibit significant yield increases under TD or T environments but showed negative effects in TH or HL environments. This environmental dependency prompted inclusion of a drought-stress treatment during grain filling (GFS) in 2009 yield testing. Both SS1 and NS1 ZAR1 alleles demonstrated positive yield effects under GFS and T environments while showing negative impacts under HL conditions, confirming the transgene's relationship with environmental factors.

Correlation analyses between transgene efficacy and individual environmental factors identified key drivers of performance variation. Positive correlations were observed with higher daily maximum temperatures and cumulative solar radiation, while negative correlations existed with total rainfall amounts. These findings suggested that ZAR1's beneficial effects were particularly valuable under conditions imposing drought stress or high evaporative demand, consistent with observed improvements in drought-related traits such as reduced anthesis-silking interval and tip kernel abortion.

### Example 6

This example investigates natural variation at the ZAR1 locus in elite maize breeding germplasm and its association with agronomic traits. Analysis of nearly 600 inbred lines from Pioneer's breeding program revealed approximately twelve haplotypes at the ZAR1 locus among founder lines, but only two haplotypes predominated in modern elite germplasm, accounting for about 95% of overall allele frequency in a survey of 424 inbred lines.

The distribution of these two major haplotypes showed strong association with heterotic group classification: Non-Stiff Stalk (NS) lines were predominantly (85%) haplotype 1 (represented by the NS1 allele), while Stiff Stalk (SS) lines were mostly (60%) haplotype 2 (represented by the SS1 allele). Survey of 169 commercial hybrids indicated that 66% carried the combination of haplotype 1 (NS1 allele) and haplotype 2 (SS1 allele) at the ZAR1 locus, reflecting the standard heterotic pairing strategy in North American maize breeding.

Macro-haplotype analysis of 245 inbred lines (174 NS and 71 SS) carrying characteristic haplotypes 1 and 2, respectively, revealed striking differences in recombination patterns around the ZAR1 locus. While all 71 SS inbreds showed identical macro-haplotype patterns across an 8-cM flanking region, NS lines exhibited seven distinct macro-haplotypes formed by juxtaposed founder segments, indicating historical recombination events in this region. This asymmetry suggested stronger selective pressure maintaining the SS haplotype block intact compared to the NS counterpart.

Genetic association analysis using populations phenotyped under drought-stressed conditions in 2007-2009 identified significant associations between ZAR1 haplotypes and multiple agronomic traits, including kernel weight, barrenness, plant height, and staygreen characteristics. The magnitude of these effects was small to moderate, consistent with typical quantitative trait locus (QTL) effects observed in elite maize populations. Notably, favorable effects were detected when tester lines carried the complementary haplotype, suggesting heterozygous advantage at the ZAR1 locus in hybrid performance.

### Example 7

This example examines functional differences between the two major ZAR1 alleles (SS1 and NS1) through detailed molecular characterization of their promoter and coding regions. Sequence analysis of approximately 1 kb upstream of the transcription start site revealed numerous insertions/deletions distinguishing the SS1 and NS1 promoter regions. These structural variations likely contributed to differential expression patterns observed between the two alleles in F1 hybrid backgrounds.

Allele-specific expression analysis in SS1/NS1 F1 hybrids demonstrated significant differences in transcript abundance between the two alleles across multiple tissue types and environmental conditions. Measurements in both immature ear and seedling tissues, including drought-stressed samples, consistently showed higher expression from one allele compared to the other. Since both alleles resided in a common hybrid genetic background, these expression differences were attributed to cis-regulatory variations rather than trans-acting factors.

Coding sequence comparisons identified several amino acid substitutions between SS1 and NS1 variants, with particular significance assigned to two key positions. A leucine/methionine (L/M) substitution occurred at the most conserved motif region, potentially affecting secondary protein structure due to differences in side chain properties between these hydrophobic residues. Additionally, an alanine/glycine (A/G) difference positioned adjacent to the conserved motif might create a highly flexible glycine-glycine-glycine (GGG) loop specifically in the SS1 variant, potentially influencing protein conformation or interaction surfaces.

Protein structural modeling analyses supported the hypothesis that these amino acid differences could alter secondary or tertiary structures of ZAR1 polypeptides, thereby affecting their functional properties. The combined evidence from both regulatory and coding sequence variations suggested that SS1 and NS1 alleles represented functionally distinct variants that had been differentially selected during maize breeding history, possibly contributing to heterotic effects when combined in hybrid genotypes.

### Example 8

This example evaluates transgene efficacy of different ZAR1 allele configurations, including individual alleles and molecularly stacked heterozygous combinations. Based on observations that native SS1 and NS1 alleles showed favorable performance when present in heterozygous combination in conventional hybrids, transformation vectors were constructed containing either individual native promoter-coding region units or a stacked configuration combining both SS1 and NS1 alleles.

Yield testing of these transgenic configurations across five locations in 2011 revealed distinct performance patterns. Individual allele transgenics (SS1 or NS1 alone) consistently showed positive yield effects compared to null controls, though these effects did not reach statistical significance across all environments in multi-location analysis. In contrast, transgenic plants carrying the molecularly stacked heterozygous allele combination (SS1+NS1) demonstrated significant yield increases in more locations than either single allele configuration and achieved overall statistical significance in multi-location analysis.

These results indicated that stacking both ZAR1 alleles in a transgenic context recapitulated the heterotic advantage observed with native allele combinations in conventional breeding. The stacked configuration provided both higher magnitude of yield increase and more stable performance across diverse environments compared to individual alleles. This finding suggested that functional complementarity between SS1 and NS1 variants extended beyond simple additive effects, potentially involving synergistic interactions at the molecular or physiological level.

Interestingly, leaf tissue expression analyses showed that total ZAR1 transcript levels (transgenic plus endogenous) were highest in SS1 transgenic plants, while NS1 and stacked configuration plants exhibited lower but similar expression levels. Despite this expression hierarchy, the stacked configuration produced superior yield outcomes, indicating that simple expression dosage did not fully explain transgene efficacy differences. More complex interactions involving spatial, temporal, or environmental response dimensions likely contributed to the observed performance advantages of the heterozygous allele stack.

This example integrates findings from previous examples to propose a comprehensive model for ZAR1 function in maize agronomic performance and its implications for transgenic crop improvement. The collective evidence demonstrates that ZAR1 acts as an intrinsic yield gene primarily by increasing cell number through enhanced cell proliferation during active growth phases, without extending the overall developmental timeline. This mechanism provides a foundation for improved biomass accumulation and reproductive output under various environmental conditions.

The environmental dependency of ZAR1 transgene effects represents a critical consideration for practical applications. Positive yield impacts occur predominantly under warmer, drier conditions (Temperate Dry or Temperate environments) where drought stress or high evaporative demand would normally limit productivity. Conversely, potential yield penalties may manifest under cooler, wetter conditions (Temperate Humid or High Latitude environments), highlighting the importance of matching transgene deployment with appropriate target environments.

Natural selection during maize breeding history has shaped ZAR1 allelic variation in ways that parallel transgenic findings. The maintenance of two predominant alleles (SS1 and NS1) partitioned between heterotic groups, coupled with their favorable performance in heterozygous combinations, suggests that ZAR1 has been a target of selection for hybrid vigor and stress tolerance. The functional differences between these alleles—arising from both regulatory and coding sequence variations—provide a molecular basis for their complementary roles in hybrid performance.

The successful recapitulation of heterotic effects through molecular stacking of both ZAR1 alleles in transgenic plants establishes a powerful paradigm for integrating biotechnology with conventional breeding principles.
