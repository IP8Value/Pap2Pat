## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of optical upconversion materials and, more particularly, to lanthanide-doped nanocrystals functionalized with infrared-absorbing organic dye molecules that serve as optical antennas to enhance and spectrally broaden the upconversion response. The invention further pertains to methods for synthesizing such nanocomposite materials, techniques for covalently attaching dye molecules to the surface of the nanocrystals via a silica-based functionalization strategy, and processes for fabricating thin films or other solid-state forms of these materials for use in applications including but not limited to infrared detection, spectral discrimination, photovoltaic enhancement, bioimaging, and laser threat warning systems. The invention enables the conversion of invisible infrared radiation into visible light with high spectral specificity and enhanced efficiency through the synergistic coupling of molecular dye absorption and lanthanide-based photon upconversion.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Optical upconversion is a nonlinear optical process wherein two or more low-energy photons are sequentially absorbed and subsequently emitted as a single higher-energy photon. This phenomenon stands in contrast to harmonic generation processes, which rely on instantaneous, coherent interactions at extremely high optical intensities. Upconversion, by virtue of its sequential, probability-based nature, can occur at relatively modest power densities—typically in the range of 1 to 10³ W/cm²—making it suitable for applications driven by continuous-wave or low-power pulsed laser sources. The most efficient and widely studied upconversion materials are based on trivalent lanthanide ions (Ln³⁺) embedded within crystalline host matrices. These systems operate through well-defined energy transfer mechanisms between sensitizer and activator ions, leveraging the long-lived excited states of Ln³⁺ ions due to the Laporte-forbidden nature of their 4f-4f electronic transitions. However, this same forbidden character results in extremely low molar absorptivity coefficients (on the order of 0.5–5 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹), severely limiting the overall light-harvesting efficiency of such materials.

To address this fundamental limitation, researchers have explored the integration of organic chromophores—so-called “optical antennas”—onto the surface of upconverting nanoparticles. These dyes exhibit broad absorption bands and high extinction coefficients (typically 200–400 L g⁻¹ cm⁻¹, or ε ≈ 10⁵ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹), enabling efficient capture of incident radiation over a wide spectral range. When positioned within nanometers of the nanoparticle surface, the excited state energy of the dye can be nonradiatively transferred to the sensitizer ion via Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET), a dipole-dipole coupling mechanism whose efficiency scales inversely with the sixth power of the donor-acceptor distance. This approach has been shown to dramatically enhance upconversion efficiency; for instance, Zou et al. reported a 1,100-fold increase in peak upconversion intensity using a dye antenna coupled to NaYF₄:Yb³⁺,Er³⁺ nanoparticles.

Despite these advances, prior art suffers from several critical shortcomings. First, many existing strategies for dye attachment—such as physisorption or ligand exchange—lack chemical robustness, leading to dye desorption or degradation during subsequent processing or under operational conditions. Second, the synthesis of high-quality upconverting nanocrystals often requires high-temperature, organic-phase routes that yield hydrophobic particles capped with long-chain ligands like oleic acid, which are incompatible with aqueous environments or polymer matrix integration without further surface modification. Third, while dye-enhanced upconversion has been demonstrated, the ability to *discriminate* between different bands of infrared radiation based on the resulting visible emission has not been fully realized. This capability would be transformative for applications such as multispectral infrared sensing, where distinct threat signatures (e.g., from different laser designators) could be visually differentiated without complex detector arrays.

Moreover, the choice of host material is crucial. Among known hosts, sodium yttrium tetrafluoride (NaYF₄) stands out due to its exceptionally low phonon energy (~370 cm⁻¹), which minimizes nonradiative relaxation pathways and maximizes luminescence quantum yield. The hexagonal phase of NaYF₄ is particularly favorable, as the cubic phase exhibits negligible upconversion activity. Synthesis of phase-pure, monodisperse hexagonal NaYF₄ nanoparticles typically involves a thermal decomposition method in high-boiling-point solvents such as octadecene, using lanthanide acetates and ammonium fluoride precursors in the presence of oleic acid as a capping agent. Precise control over reaction temperature, time, and ligand-to-metal ratio governs particle size, morphology, and crystallinity—all of which influence upconversion performance.

Surface functionalization presents another challenge. To enable covalent dye attachment, the hydrophobic oleate-capped nanoparticles must be rendered hydrophilic and equipped with reactive functional groups. Silica coating via reverse microemulsion is a well-established technique for this purpose, as it provides a chemically inert, biocompatible shell while allowing incorporation of organofunctional silanes. However, standard silanization protocols employ strongly basic conditions (e.g., concentrated ammonia) that can degrade sensitive infrared dyes, especially those bearing electrophilic functional groups such as N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) esters or isothiocyanates. Prior attempts to pre-conjugate dye to silane before condensation have failed for many commercial NIR dyes due to their instability under hydrolytic and basic conditions.

Additionally, the selection of lanthanide dopants dictates both the excitation wavelength and the color of the upconverted emission. For example, Yb³⁺ is the most common sensitizer due to its large absorption cross-section at ~975 nm, and it is typically paired with activators such as Er³⁺ (green/red emission), Tm³⁺ (blue/NIR), or Ho³⁺ (green/red). Alternatively, certain ions like Er³⁺ can serve dual roles as both sensitizer and activator when excited at other wavelengths (e.g., 801 nm or 1550 nm), enabling upconversion without a separate sensitizer. This versatility allows for the design of multiple nanoparticle-dye pairs, each responsive to a specific infrared band and producing a unique visible output.

However, no prior art discloses a systematic approach to pairing distinct infrared-absorbing dyes with specifically doped lanthanide nanoparticles to achieve *wavelength-selective visible emission* from broadband or multi-band infrared illumination. Such a system would constitute a form of “optical encoding,” where the color of the emitted light directly reports the spectral composition of the incident infrared radiation. This functionality is absent in conventional upconverters, which respond only to narrow atomic transitions and cannot distinguish between different IR sources unless filtered externally.

Furthermore, the fabrication of practical devices—such as thin films for coatings, sensors, or display elements—requires stable, processable dispersions of the nanocomposites. Aggregation, poor film uniformity, and interfacial defects can all degrade optical performance. Existing methods for film formation from nanoparticle colloids often result in cracking, dewetting, or inhomogeneous dye distribution, limiting device reproducibility and scalability.

In summary, the background art reveals a clear need for: (1) a robust, covalent method for attaching chemically fragile infrared dyes to upconverting nanoparticles; (2) a synthetic protocol that yields high-quality, phase-pure NaYF₄ nanocrystals with controlled size and morphology; (3) a strategic pairing of dye absorption profiles with lanthanide excitation/emission characteristics to enable spectral discrimination; and (4) a reliable process for fabricating homogeneous, stable films of these nanocomposites. The present invention addresses all these needs in an integrated and novel manner.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of nanocomposite materials comprising lanthanide-doped sodium yttrium fluoride (NaYF₄) nanocrystals covalently functionalized with infrared-absorbing organic dye molecules that act as optical antennas to enhance and spectrally broaden the upconversion response. The invention further encompasses methods for synthesizing these nanocrystals, functionalizing their surfaces with amine groups via a controlled silica encapsulation process, attaching dye molecules through stable amide bonds, and fabricating thin films or other solid-state architectures for practical applications. A key innovation lies in the strategic pairing of specific dye-nanoparticle combinations to enable visible discrimination of different infrared radiation bands based on the color of the upconverted emission.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for synthesizing hexagonal-phase lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals via a modified thermal decomposition route, wherein stoichiometric mixtures of lanthanide acetates, sodium acetate, and yttrium acetate are reacted with ammonium fluoride in a high-boiling solvent (e.g., octadecene) in the presence of oleic acid as a capping ligand. The reaction is conducted at temperatures between 300°C and 324°C for durations of 10 to 100 minutes to ensure complete conversion to the hexagonal phase, which is essential for efficient upconversion. The resulting nanoparticles exhibit rod-like morphologies with dimensions tunable between 10 nm and 100 nm by adjusting reaction parameters.

In another aspect, the invention provides a surface functionalization method that renders the hydrophobic, oleate-capped nanoparticles water-dispersible and amenable to covalent dye attachment. This is achieved through a reverse microemulsion technique in which individual nanoparticles are isolated within nanoreactors formed by a nonionic surfactant (e.g., Igepal CO-520) in cyclohexane. An amine-functionalized siloxane monomer—preferably a secondary or tertiary amine derivative such as [3-(methylamino)propyl]trimethoxysilane or (3,3-dimethylaminopropyl)trimethoxysilane—is then hydrolyzed and condensed onto the nanoparticle surface under mild basic conditions to form a thin, amine-terminated silica shell. The use of secondary or tertiary amines, rather than primary amines, is critical to prevent nucleophilic degradation of NHS-ester- or isothiocyanate-functionalized dyes during subsequent conjugation.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a method for covalently attaching infrared-absorbing dye molecules to the functionalized nanoparticles. Dyes bearing carboxylic acid, isothiocyanate, or NHS ester functional groups are reacted with the surface amine groups to form stable amide linkages. The reaction is carried out in polar solvents such as ethanol or dimethyl sulfoxide at room temperature or slightly elevated temperatures (e.g., 35°C) to ensure efficient coupling without dye decomposition. The resulting dye-nanoparticle conjugates exhibit strong molecular absorption in the near-infrared (700–1000 nm) and efficient energy transfer to the lanthanide ions via FRET, leading to upconverted visible emission upon infrared excitation.

A central feature of the invention is the deliberate selection of dye-nanoparticle pairs to achieve wavelength-selective upconversion. For instance, a first nanocomposite may comprise NaYF₄:Yb³⁺,Ho³⁺ nanoparticles coupled with DY-831 dye, which absorbs broadly between 800 nm and 850 nm and emits near 975 nm, thereby exciting the Yb³⁺ sensitizer. This system produces green and red upconverted emission characteristic of Ho³⁺. A second nanocomposite may comprise NaYF₄:Er³⁺ (with Er³⁺ acting as both sensitizer and activator) coupled with DyLight 800 dye, which absorbs strongly around 785 nm and emits near 801 nm, matching the ⁴I₁₅/₂ → ⁴I₉/₂ transition of Er³⁺. This system produces green upconverted emission from Er³⁺. When exposed to different infrared wavelengths—e.g., 785 nm vs. 819 nm—each nanocomposite responds selectively, emitting distinct colors that allow visual discrimination of the incident radiation.

In a further aspect, the invention provides a method for fabricating thin films of the dye-nanoparticle conjugates. The conjugates are dispersed in a volatile solvent (e.g., ethanol) and cast onto substrates via spin-coating, drop-casting, or doctor-blading. The resulting films are homogeneous, crack-free, and retain the upconversion properties of the colloidal dispersions. These films can be integrated into devices such as infrared viewers, spectral sensors, or anti-counterfeiting tags.

The invention thus offers a comprehensive solution to the limitations of prior art by combining robust synthesis, gentle functionalization, selective dye-nanoparticle pairing, and scalable film fabrication to create a new class of smart optical materials capable of converting invisible infrared patterns into visible color codes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### X=—NH2, —CO2M′, —SH, —OH,

The present invention contemplates a variety of functional groups that may be employed to facilitate the covalent attachment of organic dye molecules to the surface of lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals. Specifically, the variable X represents a reactive moiety present on either the dye molecule or the nanoparticle surface, which enables the formation of a stable chemical linkage. In the preferred embodiment, X is selected from the group consisting of —NH₂ (primary amine), —CO₂M′ (carboxylate salt, where M′ is a monovalent cation), —SH (thiol), and —OH (hydroxyl). These functional groups are chosen for their compatibility with established bioconjugation chemistries and their ability to form robust bonds under mild reaction conditions that preserve the integrity of both the dye and the nanoparticle.

The primary amine group (—NH₂) is particularly advantageous when located on the nanoparticle surface, as it readily reacts with electrophilic functional groups on commercially available dyes, such as N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) esters, isothiocyanates, or carboxylic acids (in the presence of carbodiimide coupling agents). In the context of the present invention, the amine group is introduced via silanization using amine-functionalized alkoxysilanes, such as (3-aminopropyl)trimethoxysilane (APS). However, as disclosed herein, the use of primary amines can lead to undesired side reactions with certain NHS-ester-functionalized dyes, resulting in dye degradation. To circumvent this issue, the invention further contemplates the use of secondary or tertiary amine derivatives of APS, such as [3-(methylamino)propyl]trimethoxysilane or (3,3-dimethylaminopropyl)trimethoxysilane, which retain sufficient nucleophilicity for amide bond formation while exhibiting reduced reactivity toward the dye’s electrophilic centers.

The carboxylate group (—CO₂M′, where M′ is K, Li, Na, or H) is commonly found on dye molecules and serves as a handle for conjugation to amine-functionalized nanoparticles. Under standard carbodiimide chemistry (e.g., using EDC/NHS activation), the carboxylic acid is converted to an NHS ester in situ, which then reacts efficiently with surface amines to form an amide bond. This approach is highly versatile and applicable to a wide range of carboxylic acid-functionalized dyes, including those that are unstable under the basic conditions of direct silanization. The carboxylate group may also be present on the nanoparticle surface if alternative functionalization strategies are employed, such as the use of carboxylic acid-terminated silanes, though this is less common in the present invention.

The thiol group (—SH) offers an alternative conjugation pathway through maleimide-thiol or disulfide chemistry. While not the primary method disclosed herein, thiols may be useful for attaching dyes that are functionalized with maleimide groups, which are known for their rapid and specific reaction with thiols at neutral pH. Thiol-functionalized nanoparticles can be prepared by silanization with mercaptopropyltrimethoxysilane, followed by optional oxidation to form disulfide linkages if desired. However, the potential for oxidation and the lower stability of thiol-based linkages in air make this approach less favorable for long-term applications compared to amide bonds.

The hydroxyl group (—OH) is ubiquitous on silica surfaces and can be used for dye attachment via silane coupling or esterification. However, direct esterification of hydroxyls with carboxylic acid-functionalized dyes is inefficient and requires harsh conditions. More commonly, hydroxyl groups are first converted to more reactive intermediates, such as epoxides or isocyanates, which can then react with amine- or hydroxyl-containing dyes. In the present invention, the hydroxyl-rich silica shell formed during functionalization primarily serves as a scaffold for the incorporation of amine-functionalized silanes, rather than as a direct conjugation site.

In all cases, the choice of X is dictated by the chemical stability of the dye, the desired bond strength, and the compatibility with downstream processing steps. The invention emphasizes the use of amide bonds formed between amine-functionalized nanoparticles and carboxylic acid-, isothiocyanate-, or NHS ester-functionalized dyes as the optimal balance of reactivity, stability, and mildness.

### M=H2, Cu, Co, CoR3, Ni, SiR3R4, AlR3, Zn, Fe, FeR3, Mn

While the core of the present invention focuses on lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals, the variable M is introduced to acknowledge that the host matrix or dopant composition may be varied to tune the optical and physical properties of the upconverting material. In this context, M represents a metal or metal-containing species that may be incorporated into the crystal lattice either as a host cation, a co-dopant, or a surface modifier. The listed options include H₂ (indicating hydrogenation or protonation, though not typical in this context), transition metals such as Cu, Co, Ni, Zn, Fe, and Mn, and metalloids or main-group metals such as Si, Al.

It should be understood that the primary host material in the present invention is NaYF₄, where Y³⁺ is the dominant cation. However, partial substitution of Y³⁺ with other trivalent ions—including other lanthanides (e.g., Gd³⁺, Lu³⁺) or transition metals—can alter the crystal field symmetry, phonon energy, and energy transfer dynamics. For example, incorporation of Gd³⁺ can create intermediate energy levels that facilitate energy migration, while doping with Mn²⁺ has been shown to introduce additional emission bands in the orange-red region. Similarly, surface doping with transition metals like Cu or Ni may be used to introduce plasmonic effects or catalytic activity, though this is not the focus of the current invention.

The notation CoR₃, FeR₃, SiR₃R₄, and AlR₃ refers to organometallic or organosilicon species that may be used as precursors or surface modifiers. For instance, SiR₃R₄ represents organosilanes used in the functionalization step, where R and R₄ are organic substituents (e.g., methyl, ethyl, phenyl, methoxy, ethoxy). As detailed elsewhere, (3-aminopropyl)trimethoxysilane (where R = propylamine, R₄ = methoxy) is a key reagent for introducing amine groups. Similarly, AlR₃ (e.g., trimethylaluminum) could theoretically be used in atomic layer deposition to create alumina shells, but this is not practiced in the present invention.

The inclusion of M in this section is primarily to provide a comprehensive chemical framework and to acknowledge that the invention is not strictly limited to pure NaYF₄:Ln³⁺ systems. However, the optimal performance—particularly in terms of upconversion efficiency and spectral purity—is achieved with the undoped or lanthanide-only doped NaYF₄ host, as transition metal dopants often introduce nonradiative quenching pathways due to their d-d transitions.

### R4=—R3, —Cl, —CH3, —H, -Ph, —OCH3, —OCH2CH3

The variable R₄ appears in the context of organosilane reagents used for surface functionalization and refers to the substituents attached to the silicon atom. In the general formula for alkoxysilanes, R₃Si(OR₄)₃, R₃ is an organic functional group (e.g., aminopropyl), and R₄ is the alkoxy group that undergoes hydrolysis during silanization. The invention contemplates R₄ being selected from —R₃ (though this is chemically inconsistent and likely a typographical artifact in the outline), —Cl (chlorosilanes, which are highly reactive but moisture-sensitive), —CH₃ (methyl), —H (hydrosilanes, which are less common for this application), —Ph (phenyl), —OCH₃ (methoxy), and —OCH₂CH₃ (ethoxy).

In practice, the preferred R₄ groups are methoxy and ethoxy, as they offer a good balance of reactivity and stability. Methoxysilanes hydrolyze faster than ethoxysilanes, which can be advantageous for rapid shell formation but may lead to inhomogeneous coatings if not carefully controlled. Ethoxysilanes, being slightly less reactive, allow for more gradual condensation and are often preferred for forming uniform thin shells. Chlorosilanes, while highly reactive, are generally avoided due to the corrosive HCl byproduct and their sensitivity to trace moisture, which complicates handling.

Phenyl-substituted silanes (R₄ = Ph) are not typical alkoxy groups but may refer to arylsilanes used for specialized applications, such as enhancing refractive index or thermal stability. However, in the present invention, the focus is on standard alkoxysilanes with methoxy or ethoxy groups to ensure reproducible and biocompatible silica shells.

The choice of R₄ influences the kinetics of hydrolysis and condensation, which in turn affects the thickness and porosity of the silica shell. For the thin shells required in the present invention (≤2 nm), methoxysilanes are often preferred due to their faster reaction, allowing precise control over shell thickness by limiting the amount of silane added.

### M′=K, Li, Na, H

The variable M′ appears in the carboxylate group —CO₂M′ and denotes the counterion associated with the deprotonated carboxylic acid. In the context of dye molecules, M′ is typically H (i.e., the protonated carboxylic acid form, —CO₂H) when purchased from suppliers, as this form is more stable for storage. However, during conjugation reactions, the carboxylic acid may be deprotonated to form a carboxylate salt (—CO₂⁻ M′⁺) to enhance solubility or reactivity. The invention contemplates M′ being H, Li, Na, or K, with Na and K being the most common due to their prevalence in biological buffers and ease of handling.

When M′ = H, the carboxylic acid can be activated using carbodiimide reagents (e.g., EDC) to form an O-acylisourea intermediate, which is then stabilized by NHS to form the NHS ester. This activated ester readily reacts with primary amines to form amide bonds. If the dye is supplied as a sodium or potassium salt (M′ = Na or K), it may be necessary to acidify the solution to convert it to the protonated form before activation, or to use alternative coupling strategies that tolerate the ionic form.

The choice of M′ has minimal impact on the final conjugate, as the carboxylate is consumed during amide bond formation, leaving no residual counterion in the linkage. However, it can influence the solubility of the dye in the reaction solvent (e.g., ethanol, DMSO), which is critical for achieving homogeneous mixing and efficient conjugation.

### Synthesis of Lanthanide-Doped NaYF4 Nanocrystals

The synthesis of high-quality lanthanide-doped NaYF₄ nanocrystals is a foundational step in the present invention, as the upconversion efficiency is critically dependent on the crystalline phase, size, and morphology of the nanoparticles. The method employed is a modified thermal decomposition technique, adapted from the work of Zhang and others, which ensures the formation of the hexagonal phase—the only phase known to exhibit strong upconversion luminescence.

The process begins with the preparation of lanthanide oleate precursors. Stoichiometric amounts of lanthanide acetates (e.g., Yb(CH₃COO)₃, Er(CH₃COO)₃, Ho(CH₃COO)₃, Tm(CH₃COO)₃, and Y(CH₃COO)₃) and sodium acetate (NaCH₃COO) are combined in a three-neck flask equipped with a reflux condenser, thermometer, and nitrogen inlet. The total molar amount of lanthanides is typically set to 1 mmol, with sodium acetate added in a 1:1 molar ratio to the total lanthanides. To this mixture, 4 mL of oleic acid (a long-chain fatty acid that acts as both a surfactant and a capping ligand) and 11 mL of 1-octadecene (a high-boiling, nonpolar solvent) are added. The mixture is heated under vacuum at 120°C for 30–60 minutes to remove moisture and to convert the acetate salts into oleate complexes, resulting in a clear, homogeneous solution. The system is then cooled to room temperature under a nitrogen atmosphere to prevent oxidation.

Separately, ammonium fluoride (NH₄F), serving as the fluoride source, is dissolved in methanol (e.g., 4 mmol in 10 mL). This solution is rapidly injected into the lanthanide-oleate mixture, causing immediate precipitation of an amorphous sodium yttrium fluoride phase. The methanol is then removed by heating the mixture to 100°C under a gentle nitrogen flow for 10–15 minutes, ensuring complete evaporation of the volatile solvent. The reaction mixture is then heated to 324°C (the boiling point of 1-octadecene) and maintained at this temperature for 10 to 100 minutes. During this high-temperature annealing step, the amorphous phase undergoes a solid-state transformation into the thermodynamically stable hexagonal phase of NaYF₄. The duration of this step controls the final particle size: longer times promote Ostwald ripening, wherein smaller particles dissolve and reprecipitate onto larger ones, yielding particles with reduced surface defects and enhanced crystallinity.

After cooling to room temperature, the nanoparticles are precipitated by adding a two-fold excess of ethanol, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes. The supernatant is discarded, and the pellet is redispersed in a nonpolar solvent such as toluene or cyclohexane. This purification cycle is repeated twice to remove unreacted precursors and excess oleic acid. The resulting colloidal dispersion consists of monodisperse, hexagonal-phase NaYF₄ nanocrystals with rod-like morphologies. Typical dimensions for the optimized synthesis are 25 ± 5 nm in length and 15 ± 5 nm in width, as confirmed by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM). The aspect ratio and absolute size can be tuned by varying the oleic acid-to-lanthanide molar ratio (higher ratios favor shorter rods) and the reaction time (longer times increase size).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirms the exclusive presence of the hexagonal phase (JCPDS card no. 16-0334), with no detectable peaks corresponding to the cubic phase. The sharpness of the diffraction peaks and the clarity of the lattice fringes in HR-TEM images attest to the high crystallinity of the nanoparticles, which is essential for minimizing nonradiative losses and maximizing upconversion efficiency.

### Encapsulation of Attached Dye

In an alternative embodiment of the invention, the dye molecule is not only attached to the nanoparticle surface but also encapsulated within a protective silica shell to enhance its photostability and prevent leaching. This is achieved through a two-step silanization process. First, the amine-functionalized silane (e.g., APS or its secondary/tertiary amine analogues) is condensed onto the nanoparticle surface to form a thin, reactive inner shell. The dye is then conjugated to the amine groups on this inner shell under mild conditions. Subsequently, a second layer of nonfunctionalized silane (e.g., tetraethyl orthosilicate, TEOS) is added to grow an outer silica shell that completely encapsulates the dye-nanoparticle conjugate.

This encapsulation strategy offers several advantages. It shields the dye from environmental factors such as oxygen, moisture, and solvents that could cause photobleaching or chemical degradation. It also prevents direct contact between the dye and the external medium, which is particularly important for applications in biological or harsh chemical environments. Furthermore, the outer silica shell can be further functionalized with additional groups (e.g., polyethylene glycol for biocompatibility or vinyl groups for polymerization) to tailor the surface properties for specific applications.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of such encapsulated nanoparticles reveal a distinct core-shell structure, with the NaYF₄ core appearing as a dark hexagonal rod and the silica shell as a lighter, featureless halo surrounding it. Shell thicknesses can be precisely controlled by varying the amount of TEOS added, with typical values ranging from 5 nm to 20 nm. Thicker shells provide better protection but may increase the donor-acceptor distance, potentially reducing FRET efficiency. Therefore, an optimal balance must be struck—typically a 10–12 nm total shell thickness, with the inner functionalized layer being ≤2 nm and the outer encapsulating layer making up the remainder.

Encapsulation is particularly beneficial for dyes that are known to be photolabile, such as certain cyanine derivatives. By embedding the dye within a rigid silica matrix, rotational and vibrational motions that contribute to nonradiative decay are restricted, potentially enhancing the dye’s fluorescence quantum yield and, consequently, the FRET efficiency to the lanthanide ions.

### Attachment of Dye to Encapsulated Nanoparticle

The attachment of dye molecules to the amine-functionalized, silica-coated nanoparticles is a critical step that determines the efficiency of energy transfer and the overall performance of the nanocomposite. The procedure is designed to maximize dye loading while minimizing aggregation and maintaining colloidal stability.

Amine-functionalized nanoparticles are first dispersed in absolute ethanol at a concentration of approximately 10 mg/mL. Separately, the dye (e.g., DY-831-NHS, DyLight 800-NHS, NIR-797 isothiocyanate, or DLS-776A carboxylic acid) is dissolved in ethanol or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to a concentration of 5 mM. A volume of this dye solution (e.g., 300 μL) is added to 10 mL of the nanoparticle dispersion, resulting in an initial dye-to-nanoparticle molar ratio of approximately 200:1. This excess ensures that the surface amine groups are saturated, driving the reaction to completion.

For NHS-ester-functionalized dyes, the reaction proceeds rapidly at room temperature, typically requiring only 20 minutes of stirring. The NHS ester reacts with the primary or secondary amine groups on the nanoparticle surface to form a stable amide bond, releasing N-hydroxysuccinimide as a byproduct. For carboxylic acid-functionalized dyes, a carbodiimide coupling agent such as 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) is added along with NHS to activate the carboxylic acid in situ, and the reaction is carried out at 35°C for 1–2 hours to ensure complete conjugation.

After the reaction period, the dye-nanoparticle conjugates are purified by repeated centrifugation and washing with ethanol until the supernatant is clear, indicating removal of unbound dye. The final product is a stable colloidal dispersion of dye-functionalized nanoparticles, which can be characterized by UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy to quantify dye loading. Typically, saturation is reached at approximately 60 dye molecules per 25-nm nanoparticle, as determined by comparing the absorbance of the conjugate to that of a free dye solution of known concentration.

The covalent nature of the amide bond ensures that the dye remains firmly attached during subsequent processing steps, such as film fabrication or exposure to biological media. This robustness is a significant improvement over noncovalent attachment methods, which often suffer from dye leaching over time.

### Fabrication of Films

The final step in the invention is the fabrication of solid-state films or coatings comprising the dye-nanoparticle conjugates, which are essential for practical device integration. The method employed is solution-based casting, which is simple, scalable, and compatible with a variety of substrates including glass, silicon, polymers, and flexible plastics.

The purified dye-nanoparticle conjugates are first dispersed in a volatile solvent such as ethanol, isopropanol, or a mixture of ethanol and water. The concentration is adjusted to achieve an optical density of approximately 0.2 at the dye’s absorption maximum when measured in a 1-cm pathlength cuvette. This ensures that the resulting film will have sufficient nanoparticle density for strong upconversion while avoiding excessive scattering or aggregation.

The dispersion is then deposited onto a clean substrate using one of several techniques. Spin-coating is preferred for uniform, thin films: the substrate is spun at 1000–3000 rpm while the dispersion is dropped onto the center, resulting in a film thickness of 100–500 nm after solvent evaporation. Drop-casting is used for thicker films or when spin-coating is impractical; a fixed volume of dispersion is placed on the substrate and allowed to dry slowly under ambient conditions. Doctor-blading or bar-coating can be employed for large-area or roll-to-roll processing.

The resulting films are homogeneous, transparent in the visible region (due to the NIR-specific absorption of the dyes), and exhibit strong upconversion luminescence when irradiated with the appropriate infrared wavelength. The films are mechanically robust and adhere well to the substrate, with no signs of cracking or delamination even after repeated handling. They can be further encapsulated with a protective polymer layer (e.g., PMMA) if needed for environmental stability.

These films are directly applicable to devices such as infrared-to-visible converters, spectral sensors, and security inks. For example, a film containing both the NaYF₄:Yb,Ho-DY-831 and NaYF₄:Er-DyLight 800 conjugates would emit green light when illuminated with 785 nm radiation (activating only the Er system) and a different shade of green or red when illuminated with 819 nm radiation (activating the Ho system), thereby providing a visual readout of the incident IR wavelength.
