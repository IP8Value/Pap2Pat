# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to wireless communication systems and, more specifically, to methods and apparatus for improving the quality of experience (QoE) for streaming video users in cellular radio access networks through intelligent traffic classification and prioritized scheduling. The invention provides a system that enables real-time detection of Hypertext Adaptive Streaming (HAS) video flows and their associated playback states—including buffering status and video resolution—using only standard Internet Protocol (IP) and Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) header information from downlink packets. This detection capability is then leveraged by a modified scheduler within a base station (BS) to dynamically prioritize resource allocation for users experiencing poor video quality or prolonged initial playback delays, thereby enhancing overall user satisfaction without significantly degrading service levels for non-video traffic such as file downloads. The technical field encompasses machine learning-based network traffic classification, radio resource management, Quality of Service (QoS) provisioning, and adaptive bitrate streaming optimization in mobile broadband networks including Long-Term Evolution (LTE) and fifth-generation (5G) systems.

The invention operates at the intersection of several key domains: (1) machine learning for real-time network analytics, particularly using Random Forest Classifiers (RFCs) trained on minimal feature sets derived exclusively from unencrypted packet headers; (2) application-aware scheduling in cellular networks where traditional schedulers treat all best-effort traffic uniformly regardless of underlying service type or user experience; and (3) end-to-end QoE optimization for over-the-top (OTT) video services that dominate modern mobile data consumption. Unlike prior approaches that require deep packet inspection (DPI), bidirectional flow monitoring, or access to encrypted application-layer payloads, the disclosed system functions entirely within the constraints of existing cellular infrastructure by utilizing only readily available transport-layer metadata. This makes it particularly suitable for deployment in commercial networks where privacy regulations, computational limitations, and protocol obfuscation techniques render conventional classification methods ineffective. The invention thus addresses a critical gap in current radio access network (RAN) architectures, which lack mechanisms to differentiate between various types of best-effort traffic and consequently cannot implement differentiated treatment based on actual user-perceived quality metrics.

## BACKGROUND

The exponential growth of mobile video traffic has placed unprecedented pressure on cellular network operators to maintain acceptable levels of service quality while managing finite spectral resources. According to industry projections, streaming video and immersive multimedia content will account for over 80% of global mobile data traffic by 2022, with monthly volumes reaching hundreds of exabytes. This surge is primarily driven by the proliferation of over-the-top (OTT) video platforms such as Netflix, YouTube, and Amazon Prime Video, which deliver content directly to end-users over the public Internet rather than through operator-managed networks. These services typically employ Hypertext Adaptive Streaming (HAS) protocols like HTTP Adaptive Streaming (HAS) or Dynamic Adaptive Streaming over HTTP (DASH), which segment video content into small chunks encoded at multiple bitrates and allow clients to dynamically adjust playback quality based on prevailing network conditions.

Despite their widespread adoption, HAS-based delivery mechanisms introduce significant challenges for cellular network operators seeking to ensure consistent quality of experience (QoE) for subscribers. First, because OTT traffic originates outside the operator's domain and traverses the public Internet, it is generally classified as best-effort traffic under standard Quality of Service (QoS) frameworks such as those defined in 3GPP specifications for LTE and 5G. Specifically, such flows are assigned low-priority QoS Class Identifiers (QCIs)—typically QCI 8 or 9—which receive no preferential treatment during radio resource allocation compared to other background data traffic like file transfers or web browsing. Second, the adaptive nature of HAS means that client players continuously monitor available bandwidth and adjust requested video quality accordingly, often leading to frequent resolution changes, rebuffering events, and extended startup delays when network conditions deteriorate. Third, the encryption of application-layer payloads using Transport Layer Security (TLS) or similar protocols prevents traditional Deep Packet Inspection (DPI) techniques from identifying specific service types or extracting meaningful application semantics, rendering many legacy traffic classification schemes obsolete.

Prior attempts to address these issues have fallen into two broad categories: client-centric solutions and network-centric interventions. Client-side approaches focus on improving Adaptive Bitrate (ABR) algorithms that govern how video players select appropriate encoding rates based on observed throughput and buffer occupancy. While effective in certain scenarios, these methods are inherently reactive—they respond to congestion after it occurs rather than proactively mitigating its impact—and operate without knowledge of competing traffic demands or network-wide resource constraints. Network-centric strategies, on the other hand, aim to enhance scheduling decisions at the base station (BS) level by incorporating awareness of active video sessions and their QoE requirements. However, existing implementations suffer from several critical limitations. Many require access to both uplink and downlink packet streams to extract inter-arrival time statistics or other temporal features, introducing unpredictable latency due to asymmetric channel conditions and variable resource allocation policies across directions. Others depend on non-standard physical-layer signaling or modifications to control-plane procedures, making them incompatible with deployed infrastructure. Still others rely on high-latency buffer estimation techniques that take tens of seconds to converge, far exceeding the subframe-level decision intervals required for practical BS schedulers.

Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising alternative for traffic classification in encrypted environments, with various studies exploring supervised models such as Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), and decision trees for inferring application characteristics from packet header metadata. However, these approaches often demand complex feature engineering involving packet inter-arrival times, byte distribution patterns, or multi-directional flow analysis, resulting in high computational overhead and memory footprint unsuitable for real-time processing in resource-constrained BS hardware. Furthermore, most existing ML classifiers prioritize accuracy over inference speed, employing lengthy sliding windows or recursive feature extraction mechanisms that delay classification results beyond the timeframe needed for timely scheduling adjustments. Recent work has also investigated reinforcement learning for dynamic resource allocation among network slices, but these systems typically assume perfect knowledge of user application states or require extensive training data spanning diverse network topologies and traffic mixes—conditions rarely met in operational deployments.

Compounding these technical challenges is the economic reality faced by mobile network operators (MNOs), who must balance subscriber satisfaction against infrastructure costs and revenue models. Some operators have resorted to imposing artificial bitrate caps on video streams or implementing differential charging based on content provider partnerships, effectively limiting video quality even when sufficient network capacity exists. Such practices not only degrade user experience but also violate principles of net neutrality and may face regulatory scrutiny. A more principled approach would involve dynamically allocating resources based on actual user-perceived quality metrics, ensuring that those experiencing poor playback conditions receive priority treatment while maintaining fair service levels for other traffic classes. Yet achieving this goal requires a lightweight, deployable mechanism capable of accurately detecting unhappy video users—defined as those either awaiting initial playback or suffering from suboptimal resolution—and integrating this information into existing scheduling frameworks without disrupting normal operations.

The present invention addresses these shortcomings by providing a novel, end-to-end framework that combines efficient ML-based traffic classification with experience-aware scheduling. By leveraging only standard IP/TCP header fields from downlink packets, the system avoids dependencies on encrypted payloads, bidirectional monitoring, or specialized hardware accelerators. Its use of Random Forest Classifiers ensures high precision and recall with minimal computational complexity, enabling real-time detection of HAS flows and their playback states within milliseconds. Crucially, the invention demonstrates that prioritizing scheduling for unhappy users yields substantial QoE improvements—including reduced startup delays and higher average video quality—while preserving overall network throughput and imposing only tolerable degradation on non-video traffic. This represents a paradigm shift from traditional QoS models that treat all best-effort traffic equally toward an application-aware RAN architecture that actively optimizes user experience through intelligent, data-driven resource allocation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for improving streaming video quality of experience (QoE) in cellular radio access networks through real-time detection of unhappy Hypertext Adaptive Streaming (HAS) users and their subsequent prioritization in base station scheduling decisions. The core innovation lies in a lightweight machine learning framework that accurately identifies HAS video flows and determines their playback states—including buffering status and video resolution—using only standard Internet Protocol (IP) and Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) header information from downlink packets. This approach eliminates the need for deep packet inspection, bidirectional flow monitoring, or access to encrypted application-layer payloads, making it highly compatible with existing cellular infrastructure and privacy regulations.

The system comprises three primary components working in concert: a traffic service classifier, a HAS state classifier, and an experience-aware scheduler. The traffic service classifier employs a Random Forest Classifier (RFC) trained on a minimal feature set derived from the first five downlink packet headers of newly detected flows. These features include server and client port numbers, the count of packets with the TCP PUSH flag set, and the median IP packet size. This classifier rapidly categorizes incoming flows into predefined service types, with particular emphasis on distinguishing HAS video traffic from other best-effort applications such as file downloads.

For flows identified as HAS video, the system activates two specialized RFCs constituting the HAS state classifier. The first determines whether the client player is in Buffering state (awaiting sufficient data to commence playback) or Steady-State (maintaining a stable playback buffer). The second, invoked only during Steady-State, estimates the current video resolution quality from a discrete set of possible levels (e.g., 144p through 1080p). Both classifiers operate on simple temporal statistics extracted from downlink packet sequences—specifically, the number of packets and cumulative payload sizes observed over short, fixed-duration sampling intervals. This design ensures low detection latency (as low as 0.1 seconds for player state and 0.5 seconds for resolution) while maintaining high classification accuracy (>95% for player state and >90% for resolution).

The outputs of these classifiers feed into an enhanced proportional fair (PF) scheduler that dynamically adjusts user-specific prioritization weights based on detected playback conditions. Users are categorized as "unhappy" if either (1) their player is in Buffering state, indicating prolonged startup delay, or (2) their resolution quality falls below a predetermined threshold (e.g., 360p), signifying degraded viewing experience. Unhappy users receive elevated scheduling weights—either infinite priority in a Strict Priority (SP) scheme or fixed multiplicative boosts (e.g., weight=4) in a Weighted Proportional Fair (WPF) scheme—while "happy" users (those in Steady-State with acceptable resolution) retain baseline weights of one. This selective prioritization ensures that scarce radio resources are directed toward users most in need of QoE improvement without indiscriminately favoring all video traffic.

Extensive simulations demonstrate the efficacy of this approach across multiple dimensions. Compared to conventional PF scheduling, the proposed system reduces median initial playback delays by over 30% (WPF) to 50% (SP) and improves worst-case (5th percentile) video Mean Opinion Scores (MOS) by 0.44 points (12.1% relative gain). Importantly, these gains are achieved with minimal impact on overall network throughput: total transported traffic volume for both HAS and file download traffic remains nearly identical to baseline scenarios, indicating that the scheduler effectively balances QoE enhancement with resource efficiency. Analysis of misclassification scenarios further reveals that system performance is primarily sensitive to classifier recall (ability to detect true video flows) rather than precision (avoidance of false positives), guiding practical deployment toward maximizing video flow detection rates.

The invention's modular architecture facilitates seamless integration into existing base station implementations. Since all required features are extractable from standard downlink packet headers without requiring uplink monitoring or physical-layer modifications, the system can be deployed as a software upgrade atop current LTE or 5G NR schedulers. Computational requirements are modest—approximately 12.7 million instructions per second (MIPS) and 2.26 MB memory for handling 500 new flows per second and 1000 concurrent video sessions—representing less than 0.05% CPU utilization on modern server-grade processors. This combination of accuracy, efficiency, and deployability positions the invention as a practical solution for mobile network operators seeking to enhance subscriber satisfaction in an era of exploding video demand.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a detailed technical framework for implementing experience-aware scheduling in cellular radio access networks to improve streaming video quality of experience (QoE). The system architecture consists of three interconnected modules: a traffic service classifier, a HAS state classifier, and an experience-centric scheduler, each designed for real-time operation within the constraints of existing base station (BS) hardware. The following sections describe the implementation details, feature engineering, classification algorithms, scheduling logic, and performance characteristics of each component.

The traffic service classifier serves as the initial gatekeeper for identifying HAS video flows among heterogeneous best-effort traffic. Upon detection of a new traffic flow—defined by the standard IP five-tuple {protocol, client-IP-address, server-IP-address, client-Port, server-Port}—the BS packet processor extracts relevant features from the first five downlink packet headers belonging to that flow. This early classification window ensures minimal latency while providing sufficient statistical information for accurate service identification. The selected feature set comprises three elements: (1) server and client port numbers derived from TCP Source Port and Destination Port fields, which often reveal characteristic patterns for popular video services (e.g., YouTube frequently uses port 443 for HTTPS); (2) the count of packets with the TCP PUSH flag set within the initial five packets, as video streaming typically exhibits bursty transmission patterns with frequent PUSH indications to minimize receiver-side buffering; and (3) the median IP packet size calculated from the IP Total Length field, which tends to be larger and more consistent for video traffic compared to interactive applications like web browsing. These features are deliberately chosen to avoid dependencies on encrypted payloads, inter-arrival timing, or uplink observations, ensuring compatibility with TLS-encrypted OTT services and asymmetric channel conditions.

The classifier implements a Random Forest Classifier (RFC) comprising ten decision trees trained on balanced datasets covering seven major traffic categories including HAS video, file transfers, web browsing, email, chat, progressive video, and other miscellaneous services. Each tree is constructed using bootstrap aggregation (bagging) with Gini impurity as the split criterion, and feature selection is performed through iterative elimination based on importance scores. During inference, the RFC processes the extracted feature vector and outputs a probability distribution across service classes, with the highest-probability class designated as the flow's service type. Empirical evaluation demonstrates over 95% classification accuracy with balanced precision and recall exceeding 0.9 for all service categories, confirming the sufficiency of the minimal feature set for reliable service identification. The computational overhead per flow is modest—367 CPU instructions for feature extraction and classification, translating to 0.1835 MIPS for 500 new flows per second—with a memory footprint of 21,668 bytes including both per-flow feature storage and model parameters.

For flows classified as HAS video, the system activates the HAS state classifier, which consists of two specialized RFCs operating in sequence. The first RFC determines the client player's playback state, distinguishing between Buffering (initial buffer accumulation phase) and Steady-State (stable playback maintenance phase). The second RFC, invoked only during Steady-State, estimates the current video resolution quality from a discrete set of six levels: 144p, 240p, 360p, 480p, 720p, and 1080p. Both classifiers utilize a sliding window approach with configurable parameters: sampling interval duration Tw (in seconds) and number of samples n. At each time step t = m·Tw, the feature vector x is constructed from observations collected during the preceding n·Tw seconds, with each entry corresponding to either the total number of downlink packets or their cumulative payload size within a Tw-second subinterval. Missing entries (when fewer than n observations exist) are filled with the average of available data, ensuring consistent feature dimensionality.

The player status classifier achieves 95% target accuracy with Tw = 0.1 seconds and n = 5, resulting in a detection latency of 0.1 seconds—the minimum viable interval for real-time scheduling adjustments. The resolution classifier requires longer observation windows (Tw = 1 second, n = 15) to reach 90% accuracy, but benefits from feature reuse: since the 0.1-second samples from player status detection are already available, they can be aggregated to form the required 1-second resolution features without additional packet processing. This hierarchical design minimizes redundant computation while maintaining appropriate temporal resolution for each classification task. Both RFCs employ ten trees with maximum depths of 246 (player status) and 541 (resolution) nodes respectively, yielding inference complexities of 5.71 MIPS and 6.85 MIPS for 1000 concurrent video sessions. Memory requirements total 2.09 MB for resolution classification, including 660 bytes per session for feature extraction buffers and 1.43 MB for model storage.

The experience-aware scheduler integrates these classification outputs into a modified proportional fair (PF) framework that dynamically adjusts user-specific prioritization weights. The scheduler maintains a single bearer queue per user and computes a scheduling metric for each candidate user j at subframe n according to the formula:

Metric_j[n] = (d_j[n] / R_j[n]^α) · v_j[n] · w_j[n]

where d_j[n] represents the instantaneous achievable rate, R_j[n] denotes the exponentially averaged throughput updated as R_j[n] = β·R_j[n-1] + (1-β)·d_j[n], and α=1, β=0.98 are standard PF parameters controlling fairness and throughput smoothing. The weights v_j[n] and w_j[n] are determined based on the HAS state classifier outputs: for non-video users, both weights remain fixed at 1; for HAS users, v_j[n] reflects player status (Buffering vs. Steady-State) while w_j[n] indicates resolution quality relative to a predefined threshold (e.g., 360p).

Two prioritization schemes are implemented: Strict Priority (SP) and Weighted Proportional Fair (WPF). In SP mode, unhappy users—defined as those either in Buffering state or exhibiting sub-threshold resolution during Steady-State—receive infinite scheduling weights (v_j=∞ or w_j=∞), guaranteeing immediate resource allocation whenever they have pending data. If multiple unhappy users compete simultaneously, selection proceeds uniformly at random to maintain fairness among them. In WPF mode, unhappy users receive fixed multiplicative boosts (v_j=4 or w_j=4), providing preferential but not absolute priority over happy users. This graded approach better accommodates scenarios with numerous concurrent unhappy users while still delivering substantial QoE improvements.

System-level simulations validate the effectiveness of this framework across multiple performance dimensions. Using a 3GPP-compliant LTE simulator with 10 MHz bandwidth and TDD operation, tests compare Baseline (standard PF), WPF, and SP schedulers under realistic traffic mixes (5 HAS users, 15 file downloaders) and channel conditions. Key findings include: (1) SP scheduling reduces median initial playback delay by 52% and worst-case (95th percentile) delay by 58% compared to Baseline; (2) WPF achieves 34% and 37% respective reductions with more moderate resource allocation; (3) video MOS scores improve by 0.15 (3.5%) for median users and 0.44 (12.1%) for worst-case users under SP scheduling; (4) total transported traffic volume remains statistically unchanged across all schemes, indicating efficient resource utilization; and (5) file download User Perceived Throughput (UPT) degrades by only 10-30% despite video prioritization, with the impact partially offset by reduced HAS chunk request frequency during Steady-State.

Misclassification analysis reveals critical insights for practical deployment. When simulating 80% video recall (one HAS user misclassified as downloader), QoE gains diminish significantly—median MOS improvement drops from 0.15 to 0.08—highlighting the importance of maximizing true positive detection rates. Conversely, 80% precision (three downloaders misclassified as HAS) preserves most QoE benefits, suggesting that false positives impose relatively minor penalties. This asymmetry guides classifier tuning toward recall optimization, consistent with the RFC's demonstrated >95% recall in empirical tests.

The invention's computational efficiency enables straightforward integration into commercial BS implementations. Total processing requirements—12.7 MIPS and 2.26 MB memory for 500 new flows/sec and 1000 video sessions—represent negligible overhead (<0.05% CPU utilization on Intel i7-6700K) compared to typical BS workloads. Memory usage scales linearly with concurrent sessions, while CPU load depends primarily on flow arrival rates and classification frequency. The modular design permits independent upgrades: for instance, replacing the RFC with alternative ML models (e.g., gradient boosting machines) requires only retraining and model substitution without altering feature extraction or scheduling logic. Similarly, the scheduler interface accepts any binary happiness indicator, facilitating future extensions to additional service types like virtual reality or cloud gaming.

Implementation considerations include robustness to transient network conditions and classifier confidence thresholds. To prevent oscillatory behavior during state transitions, the system incorporates hysteresis mechanisms that require sustained evidence (e.g., three consecutive classifications) before updating user happiness status. Additionally, classification confidence scores can inform weight assignment—for example, applying partial boosts (weight=2) for borderline cases rather than full prioritization—though current evaluations assume binary decisions for simplicity. Future enhancements might integrate these probabilistic outputs directly into the scheduling metric as continuous weights, enabling finer-grained resource allocation.

In summary, the detailed implementation demonstrates a practical, high-performance solution for experience-aware scheduling that bridges the gap between theoretical QoE optimization and real-world network constraints. By combining minimal-feature ML classification with intelligent prioritization logic, the invention delivers measurable user benefits while maintaining compatibility with existing infrastructure and operational requirements.
