# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of semiconductor optoelectronics has long been dominated by crystalline silicon due to its abundance, well-understood processing techniques, and favorable electronic properties. However, the indirect bandgap of silicon—approximately 1.12 electron volts at room temperature—imposes a fundamental limitation on its ability to absorb or emit photons with energies below this threshold. This constraint significantly restricts the utility of conventional silicon in applications requiring sensitivity to infrared radiation or enhanced photovoltaic conversion efficiency through sub-bandgap photon harvesting. Over the past two decades, researchers have pursued various strategies to overcome this limitation, including the development of intermediate band materials, quantum dot structures, and hyperdoped semiconductors. Among these approaches, hyperdoped black silicon has emerged as a particularly promising candidate for next-generation infrared photodetectors and intermediate band solar cells.

Hyperdoped black silicon refers to a class of engineered silicon materials that simultaneously exhibit two distinct characteristics: supersaturation with low-solubility dopants beyond their thermodynamic equilibrium limits, and a nanostructured surface morphology that dramatically reduces optical reflectance across a broad spectral range. The term “hyperdoping” denotes the incorporation of dopant species—typically chalcogens such as sulfur, selenium, or tellurium—at concentrations several orders of magnitude greater than their equilibrium solubility in crystalline silicon, which is generally less than 0.0001 atomic percent (approximately 5×10¹⁶ cm⁻³). This extreme doping level introduces new electronic states within the silicon bandgap, effectively creating an intermediate band that enables absorption of sub-bandgap photons via two-step transitions. Concurrently, the “black” descriptor arises from the material’s exceptionally high broadband optical absorptance, often exceeding 90% across both visible and infrared wavelengths, which results from light-trapping surface structures formed during fabrication.

Historically, hyperdoped silicon and black silicon were developed as separate material systems. Black silicon was initially fabricated through reactive ion etching or femtosecond laser irradiation in reactive atmospheres, producing micro- and nano-scale surface textures that scatter incident light and increase the effective optical path length within the material. These textured surfaces drastically reduce specular reflection and enhance absorption, but without intentional doping, they do not introduce sub-bandgap optical activity. Conversely, hyperdoped silicon was first realized through ion implantation followed by pulsed-laser melting, which enabled the incorporation of high dopant concentrations into a recrystallized surface layer while avoiding precipitation or phase segregation. While such flat hyperdoped layers exhibited sub-bandgap absorption, their limited thickness and lack of light-trapping features constrained overall device performance.

The convergence of these two concepts—hyperdoping and surface texturing—into hyperdoped black silicon represented a significant advancement. Early methods involved irradiating silicon wafers with femtosecond laser pulses in the presence of gaseous dopant precursors such as sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆), resulting in simultaneous surface structuring and dopant incorporation. During this process, each laser pulse induces rapid melting of the silicon surface, allowing dopant atoms from the ambient gas to diffuse into the molten layer. Upon ultrafast resolidification—occurring on picosecond timescales—dopant atoms are kinetically trapped in non-equilibrium lattice sites, yielding a supersaturated solid solution. Simultaneously, the interplay between laser-induced plasma formation, capillary instabilities, and redeposition of ablated material leads to the self-organization of conical microstructures typically ranging from one to ten micrometers in height. These structures provide geometric light trapping, while the hyperdoped layer enables sub-bandgap absorption, collectively producing a material with exceptional broadband absorptance.

Despite these advantages, early hyperdoped black silicon suffered from critical limitations that hindered its integration into practical optoelectronic devices. Microstructural analysis revealed that the surface layer produced by femtosecond laser processing is often polyphasic, containing amorphous silicon, metastable high-pressure crystalline phases (such as Si-III and Si-XII), and regions of defective crystallinity. These non-equilibrium phases degrade carrier mobility and lifetime, impeding efficient charge extraction. Moreover, the electrical junction formed between the hyperdoped surface layer and the underlying substrate frequently exhibited poor rectification characteristics, limiting diode performance. Attempts to improve crystallinity through conventional thermal annealing—typically performed at temperatures around 700°C in forming gas atmospheres—proved counterproductive: while such annealing enhanced structural order and improved homojunction rectification, it concurrently deactivated the sub-bandgap optical absorptance. This deactivation occurs because thermal energy enables dopant atoms to diffuse from their optically active, non-equilibrium lattice positions to energetically favorable but optically inactive sites, such as substitutional lattice positions or precipitates, thereby eliminating the intermediate band responsible for sub-bandgap transitions.

Subsequent research demonstrated that partial reactivation of sub-bandgap absorption could be achieved through rapid thermal cycling or secondary femtosecond laser irradiation. However, these methods either failed to fully restore optical performance or reintroduced amorphous phases, negating the benefits of prior crystallization. This fundamental trade-off—between high crystallinity (necessary for good electrical transport) and preserved sub-bandgap absorption (dependent on metastable dopant configurations)—represented a major bottleneck in the development of functional hyperdoped black silicon devices. Alternative strategies, such as plasmonic enhancement or hybrid architectures combining hyperdoped layers with conventional photodiodes, offered incremental improvements but did not resolve the core materials challenge.

The need for a processing technique capable of decoupling structural ordering from dopant deactivation thus became apparent. Such a method would need to operate on timescales fast enough to prevent dopant diffusion to inactive sites, yet sufficiently energetic to melt and recrystallize disordered surface regions. Pulsed laser annealing, particularly with nanosecond-duration pulses, presented a compelling candidate due to its ability to deliver controlled thermal energy with precise spatial and temporal localization. Unlike continuous-wave or furnace-based annealing, nanosecond laser pulses can induce surface melting depths on the order of tens to hundreds of nanometers—comparable to the hyperdoped layer thickness—while maintaining bulk substrate integrity. Crucially, the rapid cooling rates inherent to laser resolidification (often exceeding 10⁹ K/s) can kinetically trap dopants in active configurations, analogous to the initial hyperdoping process. Prior work had established the efficacy of nanosecond laser melting for recrystallizing ion-implanted hyperdoped silicon, but its application to the more complex topography of black silicon remained unexplored.

This background sets the stage for the present invention, which introduces a novel processing sequence leveraging nanosecond laser annealing to overcome the longstanding dichotomy between crystallinity and sub-bandgap optical activity in hyperdoped black silicon. By strategically integrating non-equilibrium laser processing with conventional thermal treatments, the invention enables the fabrication of hyperdoped black silicon that simultaneously exhibits high structural order, robust electrical rectification, and strong broadband absorption—including in the technologically critical infrared regime. This approach not only resolves a key materials science challenge but also opens new pathways for the design of high-performance silicon-based optoelectronic devices operating beyond the traditional bandgap limit.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for fabricating hyperdoped black silicon that achieves concurrent high crystallinity, preserved sub-bandgap optical absorptance, and electrical rectification through a novel sequence of thermal and nanosecond laser annealing steps. Hyperdoped black silicon, as defined herein, comprises a silicon substrate bearing a surface layer that is both supersaturated with chalcogen dopants—such as sulfur, selenium, or tellurium—at concentrations exceeding the equilibrium solubility limit by at least two orders of magnitude, and textured with microscale conical structures that impart light-trapping properties and reduce optical reflectance across a broad spectral range. The invention specifically addresses the long-standing challenge wherein conventional thermal annealing, while improving crystallinity and junction quality, deactivates the sub-bandgap optical absorptance by enabling dopant diffusion to optically inactive lattice sites. This deactivation has previously precluded the realization of high-performance optoelectronic devices based on hyperdoped black silicon.

The method of the invention begins with the fabrication of hyperdoped black silicon using established techniques, such as irradiation of a monocrystalline silicon wafer with femtosecond laser pulses in the presence of a dopant-containing atmosphere, for example sulfur hexafluoride gas. This initial step produces a material with strong above- and below-bandgap optical absorptance due to the combined effects of dopant-induced intermediate band formation and geometric light trapping from surface microstructures. However, the as-fabricated material typically contains amorphous silicon phases, pressure-induced crystalline phases (e.g., Si-III, Si-XII), and a heterogeneous microstructure that limits carrier mobility and electrical performance.

In accordance with the invention, the hyperdoped black silicon is then subjected to a controlled thermal annealing step, preferably conducted in a forming gas atmosphere (e.g., 95% N₂, 5% H₂) at a temperature between 600°C and 800°C for a duration of 10 to 60 minutes. This thermal treatment serves to increase the overall crystallinity of the material, remove residual stress, passivate defects, and—critically—improve the electrical characteristics of the homojunction formed between the hyperdoped surface layer and the underlying silicon substrate. As a consequence of this thermal processing, however, the sub-bandgap optical absorptance is substantially deactivated, typically reduced from initial values exceeding 80% to levels below 30% in the infrared region (wavelengths from 1.2 to 4.0 micrometers).

The key innovation of the present invention lies in the subsequent application of nanosecond laser annealing to the thermally treated hyperdoped black silicon. Nanosecond laser pulses—having durations between 1 and 100 nanoseconds, wavelengths in the ultraviolet to near-ultraviolet range (e.g., 193 nm, 308 nm, or 355 nm), and fluences between 0.6 and 2.2 J/cm²—are scanned across the surface such that each location receives between one and forty pulses. This laser treatment induces localized melting of the surface layer to a depth of approximately 50 to 200 nanometers, followed by rapid resolidification at cooling rates sufficient to kinetically trap dopant atoms in optically active, non-equilibrium lattice configurations. Remarkably, this process reactivates the sub-bandgap optical absorptance to near-original levels—typically restoring absorptance to 70–80% in the infrared—while preserving the high crystallinity and improved electrical properties conferred by the prior thermal annealing. Furthermore, the nanosecond laser annealing smooths nanoscale surface roughness without destroying the micron-scale conical structures responsible for light trapping, thereby maintaining high above-bandgap absorptance.

In a preferred embodiment, the method further includes the deposition of metal electrodes onto the hyperdoped black silicon surface following the initial thermal and nanosecond laser annealing steps. To ensure ohmic contact formation, a rapid thermal annealing step—conducted at temperatures between 200°C and 500°C for 10 to 60 seconds in an inert atmosphere—is applied after electrode deposition. Although this rapid annealing may partially deactivate the sub-bandgap absorptance again, a final nanosecond laser annealing step can be employed to once more restore optical performance without damaging the electrodes, provided appropriate measures such as electrode shielding or the use of robust or transparent conductive materials are implemented.

The invention thus provides a flexible and robust processing sequence that decouples the optimization of structural, optical, and electrical properties in hyperdoped black silicon. By combining equilibrium thermal processing—which enhances crystallinity and junction quality—with non-equilibrium nanosecond laser annealing—which preserves or restores sub-bandgap optical activity—the method enables the fabrication of hyperdoped black silicon suitable for high-performance infrared photodetectors, intermediate band solar cells, and other sub-bandgap optoelectronic devices. The insensitivity of the nanosecond laser annealing effect to specific laser parameters (wavelength, pulse duration, number of pulses) within the disclosed ranges further enhances the manufacturability and reproducibility of the process. Additionally, the principles underlying this invention are extensible to other non-equilibrium semiconductor systems where the stabilization of metastable dopant configurations is required alongside structural ordering.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for producing hyperdoped black silicon possessing simultaneously high crystallinity, strong broadband optical absorptance—including in the sub-bandgap infrared regime—and functional electrical rectification. This is achieved through a synergistic combination of thermal annealing and nanosecond laser annealing, which together overcome the historical incompatibility between structural ordering and preservation of optically active dopant configurations in hyperdoped silicon systems. The detailed description that follows outlines the materials, processing steps, physical mechanisms, and resultant properties that define the invention, providing sufficient detail for one skilled in the art to practice the claimed method without undue experimentation.

The starting material for the invention is a monocrystalline silicon wafer, typically of p-type conductivity doped with boron to a resistivity in the range of 1 to 100 ohm-cm, though n-type substrates may also be employed depending on the desired device architecture. The wafer is cleaned using standard semiconductor cleaning protocols, such as RCA cleaning or sequential solvent rinses in acetone, isopropanol, and deionized water, to remove organic and particulate contaminants. The wafer is then placed in a vacuum-compatible chamber equipped with optical access for laser irradiation. The chamber is evacuated to a base pressure below 0.1 Torr to minimize interference from atmospheric gases during the initial fabrication step.

Hyperdoped black silicon is first fabricated by irradiating the silicon wafer surface with femtosecond laser pulses in the presence of a dopant precursor gas. In a preferred embodiment, the laser source is a Ti:sapphire regenerative amplifier producing pulses at a wavelength of 800 nm, with a pulse duration of approximately 100 femtoseconds and a repetition rate of 1 kHz. The laser beam is focused onto the silicon surface using a lens or mirror system to achieve a Gaussian spot with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) diameter between 0.5 and 1 millimeter. The pulse fluence is maintained between 2.5 and 8 kJ/m², with higher fluences yielding taller and broader conical surface structures. The dopant precursor is introduced into the chamber as sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆) gas at a pressure of 500 Torr, although other chalcogen-containing gases such as hydrogen selenide (H₂Se) or dimethyl telluride may be substituted to incorporate selenium or tellurium, respectively.

During irradiation, the femtosecond pulses induce rapid melting of the silicon surface, accompanied by plasma formation and ablation. Dopant atoms from the dissociated SF₆ gas diffuse into the molten silicon layer, and upon ultrafast resolidification—occurring within picoseconds—these atoms are trapped in non-equilibrium lattice sites at concentrations far exceeding the equilibrium solubility limit. Concurrently, hydrodynamic instabilities and redeposition of ablated material lead to the self-organization of conical microstructures on the surface, typically 1 to 10 micrometers in height and 1 to 5 micrometers in base width, depending on the laser fluence. These structures create a graded refractive index profile that minimizes reflection and enhances light trapping through multiple scattering events. The resulting material exhibits near-unity optical absorptance across wavelengths from the ultraviolet to the mid-infrared (0.4 to 10 micrometers), with sub-bandgap absorptance (for photons with energy less than 1.12 eV) exceeding 80% in many cases.

However, the as-fabricated hyperdoped black silicon possesses a complex microstructure that limits its electrical utility. Cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals a thin skin of amorphous silicon, approximately 50 to 100 nanometers thick, covering the conical structures. Within the crystalline cores of these cones, pockets of amorphous silicon and regions of high-pressure crystalline phases (notably Si-III and Si-XII) are frequently observed. Raman spectroscopy confirms the presence of these phases through characteristic peaks at wavenumbers distinct from the 520 cm⁻¹ peak of conventional crystalline silicon (Si-I). Additionally, the electrical junction between the hyperdoped layer and the substrate often exhibits poor rectification, with turn-on voltages as low as 0.2 volts—indicative of Schottky-like behavior rather than a well-formed p-n homojunction.

To address these limitations, the invention employs a thermal annealing step. The hyperdoped black silicon sample is transferred to a tube furnace and annealed in a forming gas atmosphere (95% nitrogen, 5% hydrogen) at a temperature of 700°C for 30 minutes. This treatment serves multiple purposes: it promotes the recrystallization of amorphous regions, eliminates metastable high-pressure phases by allowing the silicon lattice to relax to its equilibrium Si-I structure, passivates dangling bonds through hydrogen incorporation, and—most importantly—enhances the electrical quality of the homojunction. After thermal annealing, current-voltage measurements reveal a significant increase in diode turn-on voltage to between 0.5 and 1.5 volts, consistent with the formation of a rectifying p-n junction rather than a Schottky barrier. Rectification ratios at ±10 V typically exceed 50, demonstrating functional diode behavior.

Unfortunately, this thermal processing comes at the cost of optical performance. The sub-bandgap optical absorptance is dramatically reduced, often falling to 20% or lower in the 1.2–4.0 micrometer wavelength range. This deactivation is attributed to the thermally activated diffusion of dopant atoms from their initial non-equilibrium, optically active sites—believed to be interstitial or distorted substitutional positions—to energetically favorable but optically inactive configurations, such as substitutional lattice sites or dopant clusters. Once in these stable positions, the dopants no longer contribute to the formation of an intermediate band, thereby eliminating the mechanism for sub-bandgap photon absorption.

The core innovation of the present invention is the application of nanosecond laser annealing to reverse this deactivation while preserving the structural and electrical improvements conferred by thermal annealing. A nanosecond pulsed laser—such as an ArF excimer laser (193 nm, 25 ns pulse duration), XeCl excimer laser (308 nm, 25 ns), or frequency-tripled Nd:YAG laser (355 nm, 4 ns)—is used to irradiate the thermally annealed hyperdoped black silicon. The laser beam is passed through a square aperture mask (e.g., 2.5×2.5 mm²) to homogenize the spatial intensity profile and then focused or defocused to achieve a desired fluence on the sample surface, typically between 0.6 and 2.2 J/cm². The sample is raster-scanned under the beam such that each surface location receives a predetermined number of pulses, usually between 1 and 40.

Upon absorption of the nanosecond laser pulse, the surface layer of the hyperdoped black silicon undergoes rapid heating and melting. Due to the high optical absorptance of the material, energy deposition is efficient even at moderate fluences. The melting depth is estimated to be between 50 and 200 nanometers, commensurate with the original hyperdoped layer thickness. Crucially, the subsequent resolidification occurs at extremely high cooling rates—on the order of 10⁹ K/s or greater—due to heat conduction into the underlying cold substrate. These conditions are sufficient to kinetically trap dopant atoms in non-equilibrium lattice sites, effectively “resetting” their configuration to an optically active state. As a result, the sub-bandgap optical absorptance is restored to 70–80% of its original value, with a broad absorption peak centered near 2.0 micrometers.

Simultaneously, the nanosecond laser annealing improves the microstructural quality of the surface. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) shows that nanoscale granular roughness—likely composed of redeposited ablation debris—is smoothed, yielding a cleaner conical morphology. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected area diffraction (SAD) confirm that the surface becomes predominantly single-crystalline, with the elimination of amorphous pockets and pressure-induced phases. Raman spectroscopy reveals a significant reduction in the amorphous silicon signal (peak near 480 cm⁻¹) and a narrowing of the crystalline silicon peak (at 520 cm⁻¹) by approximately 1 cm⁻¹, indicating reduced lattice strain and improved crystalline order. Importantly, the micron-scale conical structures remain intact, preserving the geometric light-trapping properties and maintaining above-bandgap absorptance near 95%.

Electrical characterization demonstrates that nanosecond laser annealing does not degrade the rectifying behavior of the homojunction. Current-voltage curves measured before and after laser treatment show nearly identical turn-on voltages and rectification ratios, confirming that the laser processing does not introduce deleterious defects at the junction interface or alter the doping profile in a manner that compromises diode function. This compatibility between optical reactivation and electrical integrity is a critical feature of the invention.

In a complete device fabrication sequence, metal electrodes are deposited after the initial thermal and nanosecond laser annealing steps. Standard photolithography is used to define contact areas, followed by plasma cleaning and a brief hydrofluoric acid dip to remove native oxide. Electron-beam evaporation is then employed to deposit a multilayer stack—such as 20 nm titanium, 20 nm nickel, and 200 nm silver—on the hyperdoped surface, while aluminum is evaporated on the backside for substrate contact. A rapid thermal anneal at 500°C for 30 seconds in nitrogen is performed to alloy the contacts and ensure ohmic behavior. Although this step may cause partial deactivation of sub-bandgap absorptance due to localized heating, a final nanosecond laser annealing step can be applied to restore optical performance. To prevent electrode damage during this final laser exposure, the metal contacts may be shielded with a physical mask, fabricated from thicker or more refractory metals, or replaced with transparent conductive oxides such as indium tin oxide (ITO).

The invention is not limited to the specific parameters disclosed herein. Nanosecond laser wavelengths between 193 nm and 355 nm are effective due to the strong absorption of silicon in this range, with optical penetration depths of 5–10 nm ensuring surface-localized energy deposition. Pulse durations from 1 to 100 nanoseconds are suitable, as they provide sufficient time for melting while maintaining rapid cooling kinetics. Fluences below 0.6 J/cm² may be insufficient to fully melt the surface layer, while fluences above 2.2 J/cm² risk excessive melting that can collapse delicate surface structures, particularly in samples with smaller cones (e.g., 1 micrometer tall). The number of pulses has minimal effect beyond the first few, as subsequent pulses encounter a higher-melting-point crystalline surface rather than the initial amorphous layer.

The physical mechanism underlying the invention involves a delicate balance between melting depth, cooling rate, and dopant kinetics. The nanosecond laser pulse melts a layer just deep enough to encompass the entire hyperdoped region but shallow enough to avoid disturbing the underlying substrate junction. The rapid resolidification prevents dopant diffusion over distances greater than a few atomic spacings, thereby preserving the supersaturated, non-equilibrium configuration necessary for intermediate band formation. This process is analogous to the initial femtosecond laser hyperdoping but operates on a slightly longer timescale, allowing for more controlled melting and epitaxial regrowth from the crystalline substrate.

### EXEMPLIFICATION

The following examples illustrate the practice and efficacy of the invention, demonstrating the restoration of sub-bandgap optical absorptance in hyperdoped black silicon following thermal deactivation through the application of nanosecond laser annealing. These examples are provided for clarity and should not be construed as limiting the scope of the invention.

**Example 1: Fabrication and Initial Characterization of Hyperdoped Black Silicon**

A p-type monocrystalline silicon wafer (boron-doped, 7–14 ohm-cm resistivity) was cleaned and placed in a vacuum chamber. The chamber was evacuated to 0.05 Torr and backfilled with sulfur hexafluoride (SF₆) to a pressure of 500 Torr. The wafer was irradiated with femtosecond laser pulses (800 nm wavelength, 100 fs duration, 1 kHz repetition rate) at a fluence of 8 kJ/m². The laser beam, focused to a 0.8 mm FWHM spot, was scanned across a 1×1 cm² area such that each point received approximately 200 pulses. The resulting hyperdoped black silicon exhibited conical surface structures approximately 10 µm tall and 5±2 µm wide at the base, with nanoscale granular features superimposed on the cone surfaces. Optical measurements showed above-bandgap absorptance of 95% and sub-bandgap absorptance greater than 90% across wavelengths from 1.2 to 4.0 µm. Raman spectroscopy confirmed the presence of amorphous silicon (broad peak at 480 cm⁻¹) and pressure-induced crystalline phases (peaks at 460 and 510 cm⁻¹), in addition to the standard crystalline silicon peak at 520 cm⁻¹.

**Example 2: Thermal Annealing and Deactivation**

The hyperdoped black silicon sample from Example 1 was annealed in a tube furnace at 700°C for 30 minutes in forming gas (95% N₂, 5% H₂). Post-annealing characterization revealed complete disappearance of amorphous and pressure-induced Raman peaks, indicating full conversion to conventional crystalline silicon (Si-I). The FWHM of the 520 cm⁻¹ peak narrowed from 8.4 cm⁻¹ to 7.0 cm⁻¹, suggesting reduced lattice strain. Electrically, the diode turn-on voltage increased from 0.2 V to 1.0 V, and the rectification ratio at ±10 V reached 60. However, optical absorptance measurements showed that sub-bandgap absorption had dropped to approximately 20%, while above-bandgap absorptance remained near 95%.

**Example 3: Nanosecond Laser Annealing and Reactivation**

The thermally annealed sample from Example 2 was subjected to nanosecond laser annealing using a frequency-tripled Nd:YAG laser (355 nm, 4 ns pulse duration). The laser fluence was set to 1.1 J/cm² using a calibrated attenuator, and the beam was passed through a 2.5×2.5 mm² square aperture before being scanned across the sample surface, delivering one pulse per location. After treatment, Raman spectroscopy showed no detectable amorphous or pressure-induced phases, and the 520 cm⁻¹ peak width remained at 7.0 cm⁻¹, confirming retention of high crystallinity. SEM imaging revealed smoothing of nanoscale surface roughness while preserving the 10 µm conical morphology. Most significantly, optical measurements demonstrated restoration of sub-bandgap absorptance to 75%, with a broad peak centered at 2.0 µm. Electrical testing confirmed that the diode turn-on voltage and rectification ratio were unchanged from the post-thermal-anneal values.

**Example 4: Full Device Integration with Electrode Deposition**

An identical hyperdoped black silicon sample underwent the sequence described in Examples 1–3. Metal electrodes were then deposited: four 2×2 mm² contact pads were defined by photolithography on the front surface, and the sample was cleaned via oxygen plasma (75 W, 40 s) and HF dip (5%, 1 min). A Ti/Ni/Ag stack (20/20/200 nm) was evaporated on the front, and 200 nm Al on the back. Rapid thermal annealing at 500°C for 30 s in N₂ yielded ohmic contacts, but sub-bandgap absorptance decreased to 60%. A final nanosecond laser annealing step (355 nm, 1.1 J/cm², single pulse) was applied with the electrode areas masked by a stainless steel stencil. Post-processing, sub-bandgap absorptance returned to 75%, and current-voltage characteristics showed stable rectification with a turn-on voltage of 1.1 V and rectification ratio of 55 at ±10 V.

**Example 5: Parameter Sensitivity and Structure Preservation**

Hyperdoped black silicon was fabricated using a lower femtosecond laser fluence of 2.5 kJ/m², yielding smaller conical structures (~1 µm tall). Thermal annealing at 700°C deactivated sub-bandgap absorptance from 80% to 20%. Nanosecond laser annealing at 0.6 J/cm² restored absorptance to 85% while preserving the 1 µm cones and achieving full crystallinity. However, when the fluence was increased to 2.2 J/cm², the cones were partially melted and collapsed, reducing above-bandgap absorptance to 85% and sub-bandgap absorptance to 60%, despite full crystallization. This demonstrates the importance of matching laser fluence to surface structure scale to avoid morphological damage.

These examples collectively validate the invention’s ability to produce hyperdoped black silicon that is simultaneously highly crystalline, strongly absorbing across a broad spectrum, and electrically functional—resolving a critical materials challenge that has impeded the development of silicon-based sub-bandgap optoelectronic devices.

## CONCLUSION

The present invention establishes a transformative methodology for the fabrication of hyperdoped black silicon that reconciles previously incompatible material properties—namely, high crystallinity, robust electrical rectification, and strong sub-bandgap optical absorptance—through the strategic integration of thermal and nanosecond laser annealing processes. By first employing conventional thermal annealing to enhance structural order and junction quality, and subsequently utilizing nanosecond laser pulses to reactivate optically active dopant configurations without compromising crystallinity, the invention overcomes a fundamental limitation that has hindered the practical implementation of hyperdoped silicon in optoelectronic devices. The nanosecond laser annealing step operates through a mechanism of localized melting and ultrafast resolidification, which kinetically traps dopant atoms in non-equilibrium lattice sites essential for intermediate band formation, thereby restoring sub-bandgap absorption to near-original levels. Critically, this process preserves the micron-scale surface morphology responsible for geometric light trapping, maintains the integrity of the electrical homojunction, and exhibits remarkable insensitivity to variations in laser wavelength, pulse duration, and number of pulses within the disclosed operational ranges.

The flexibility of the disclosed processing sequence enables its adaptation to diverse device architectures and manufacturing constraints. For instance, the method accommodates post-electrode-deposition reactivation through simple modifications such as electrode masking or material selection, ensuring compatibility with standard semiconductor fabrication workflows. Furthermore, the principles underlying this invention extend beyond hyperdoped black silicon to other non-equilibrium semiconductor systems where the stabilization of metastable dopant states is required alongside structural refinement. While challenges remain in precisely controlling dopant concentration profiles and hyperdoped layer thicknesses to optimize carrier extraction efficiency, the present invention provides a foundational platform upon which high-performance silicon-based infrared photodetectors, intermediate band solar cells, and related sub-bandgap optoelectronic technologies can be reliably constructed. By decoupling the optimization of optical, structural, and electrical properties through a judicious combination of equilibrium and non-equilibrium processing techniques, the invention represents a significant advance in the engineering of functional hyperdoped semiconductor materials.
