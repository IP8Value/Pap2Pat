# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND ART

Schizophrenia is a chronic and severe neuropsychiatric disorder that affects approximately one percent of the global population, imposing substantial personal, familial, and societal burdens. It is characterized by a constellation of symptoms including delusions, hallucinations, disorganized thinking, cognitive deficits, and negative symptoms such as avolition and social withdrawal. Despite decades of research, the precise etiology of schizophrenia remains incompletely understood, though it is widely accepted to involve a complex interplay of genetic, neurochemical, and environmental factors. Among the most prominent neurochemical hypotheses is the dopamine hypothesis, which posits that dysregulation of dopaminergic neurotransmission—particularly hyperactivity at D2-class dopamine receptors—plays a central role in the pathophysiology of the disorder. This hypothesis has been supported by clinical observations that all effective antipsychotic medications act as antagonists or partial agonists at dopamine D2 receptors, and that drugs that increase dopaminergic activity can exacerbate or even induce psychotic symptoms.

Historically, the diagnosis of schizophrenia has relied exclusively on clinical assessment, using standardized diagnostic criteria such as those outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). These criteria are based on behavioral and phenomenological observations, which, while clinically validated, are inherently subjective and often lead to delayed or inaccurate diagnosis, especially in early or atypical presentations. The absence of objective, biologically based diagnostic tools has long been a critical limitation in the management of schizophrenia. Consequently, there has been a sustained scientific effort to identify reliable biomarkers—molecular, imaging-based, or electrophysiological—that could facilitate earlier, more accurate diagnosis, enable monitoring of disease progression, and guide personalized therapeutic interventions.

In this context, peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) have emerged as a promising source for biomarker discovery. Although the brain is the primary organ affected in schizophrenia, accumulating evidence suggests that molecular alterations observed in the central nervous system may be mirrored, at least in part, in peripheral immune cells. This is not entirely surprising, given that both neurons and lymphocytes share common signaling pathways, express similar neurotransmitter receptors, and are influenced by overlapping genetic and environmental factors. Notably, several studies have demonstrated that components of the dopaminergic system, including dopamine receptors of the D2 subclass (DRD2, DRD3, and DRD4), are expressed in human lymphocytes. Moreover, the expression levels of these receptors in PBLs have been shown to correlate with their expression in specific brain regions, suggesting that peripheral measurements may serve as a “window” into central nervous system pathology.

Prior to the present invention, certain peripheral markers had already been proposed for schizophrenia. For instance, elevated mRNA levels of the DRD3 gene in PBLs of schizophrenic patients were reported by multiple independent groups, and this finding appeared robust across medicated and non-medicated cohorts, suggesting that DRD3 upregulation is a feature of the illness itself rather than a consequence of antipsychotic treatment. Similarly, reduced expression of the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (α7AChR) gene in PBLs was observed in schizophrenic patients, further supporting the notion that peripheral gene expression profiles may reflect central pathophysiological processes. However, these findings, while significant, have not yet translated into clinically validated diagnostic assays, partly due to limitations in sensitivity, specificity, or reproducibility across diverse populations.

The advent of high-throughput genomic technologies, such as cDNA microarrays and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (QRT-PCR), has significantly advanced the field of biomarker discovery. Microarray platforms allow for the simultaneous screening of thousands of genes, enabling unbiased identification of differentially expressed transcripts in disease states. QRT-PCR, in turn, provides a highly sensitive and quantitative method for validating candidate genes identified through screening approaches. Importantly, the use of drug-naïve or drug-free patient cohorts is critical in such studies, as antipsychotic medications are known to modulate gene expression in both brain and peripheral tissues, thereby confounding the interpretation of results. Previous attempts to identify peripheral gene expression signatures in schizophrenia have often been limited by the inclusion of medicated patients, small sample sizes, or the use of microarray platforms that did not include key candidate genes such as DRD2 or Kir2.3.

One notable study by Vawter et al. employed microarray analysis of PBLs from individuals with schizophrenia and unaffected family members, but the array used contained only 1,128 brain-related genes and did not include probes for DRD2 or Kir2.3, thus missing potentially relevant markers. Other investigations focused on postmortem brain tissue, which, while valuable for understanding neuropathology, is not suitable for routine clinical diagnostics due to its inaccessibility in living patients. Therefore, despite considerable progress, there remains an unmet need for a reliable, non-invasive, and objective diagnostic method for schizophrenia that is based on quantifiable molecular markers in easily accessible peripheral tissues.

The present invention addresses this critical gap by identifying and validating two novel peripheral biomarkers—DRD2 and Kir2.3—whose mRNA expression levels are significantly and consistently elevated in the PBLs of drug-naïve or drug-free schizophrenic patients. Unlike previous efforts, this work combines high-throughput microarray screening with rigorous QRT-PCR validation using two distinct chemistries (SybrGreen and TaqMan), thereby ensuring robustness and reproducibility. Furthermore, the study establishes clear thresholds for overexpression based on the natural variability observed in healthy controls, enabling the development of a binary diagnostic criterion. The identification of Kir2.3—a potassium channel gene previously not associated with schizophrenia in peripheral tissues—as a co-regulated marker alongside DRD2 also provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the disorder, particularly the functional coupling between dopamine receptors and ion channels in cellular excitability and signaling. Together, these findings lay the foundation for a novel diagnostic platform that could revolutionize the clinical approach to schizophrenia.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for the diagnosis and monitoring of schizophrenia in a human subject through the quantitative measurement of the expression levels of two specific genes—dopamine receptor D2 (DRD2) and inwardly rectifying potassium channel Kir2.3 (encoded by the KCNJ4 gene)—in peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs). This method is predicated on the discovery that both DRD2 and Kir2.3 are significantly and consistently overexpressed at the mRNA level in PBLs derived from individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia who are either drug-naïve or have been free of antipsychotic medication for a sufficient period to eliminate pharmacological confounding effects. The invention further encompasses kits and diagnostic assays designed to implement this method in a clinical or laboratory setting, as well as computational systems for interpreting the expression data in accordance with predefined diagnostic thresholds.

Specifically, the invention involves the isolation of total RNA from a peripheral blood sample obtained from a human subject, followed by the reverse transcription of said RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA). The resulting cDNA is then subjected to quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (QRT-PCR) using primers and, optionally, probes specific to the DRD2 and Kir2.3 transcripts. The amplification kinetics are monitored in real time using either intercalating dyes such as SybrGreen or sequence-specific dual-labeled hydrolysis probes such as TaqMan probes. The cycle threshold (Ct) values obtained for DRD2 and Kir2.3 are normalized relative to one or more endogenous reference genes—such as β-actin or hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT)—to account for variations in RNA input, cDNA synthesis efficiency, and overall sample quality. The normalized expression levels are then compared to established baseline thresholds derived from the expression profiles of healthy control individuals. If the normalized expression level of either DRD2 or Kir2.3 exceeds the upper limit of the normal range observed in healthy controls—defined as 2.56-fold for DRD2 and 3.54-fold for Kir2.3 when using SybrGreen chemistry, or 2.42-fold for DRD2 and 2.92-fold for Kir2.3 when using TaqMan chemistry—the subject is classified as positive for the molecular signature of schizophrenia.

The invention is particularly advantageous because it relies on a minimally invasive blood draw, making it suitable for repeated sampling and longitudinal monitoring. Moreover, because the method is based on drug-naïve or drug-free patients, it reflects the intrinsic molecular pathology of schizophrenia rather than secondary effects of medication. The co-detection of both DRD2 and Kir2.3 enhances diagnostic specificity, as the concurrent overexpression of these two functionally linked genes is unlikely to occur in other psychiatric or neurological conditions. Additionally, the invention provides a quantitative metric that can be used not only for initial diagnosis but also for tracking disease progression, assessing treatment response, or evaluating the risk of relapse. In some embodiments, the method may be integrated into a multiplexed diagnostic panel that includes additional schizophrenia-associated markers, such as DRD3 or α7AChR, to further improve accuracy. The invention thus represents a significant advancement over existing clinical practices by introducing an objective, biologically grounded, and reproducible diagnostic tool for schizophrenia.

## DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed to them below:

“Schizophrenia” refers to a chronic and severe mental disorder as defined by the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), or its successor, or by the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), or its successor, characterized by a combination of positive symptoms (e.g., delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech), negative symptoms (e.g., diminished emotional expression, avolition), and cognitive impairments, leading to significant social or occupational dysfunction. The term includes related psychotic disorders such as schizophreniform disorder when used in the context of clinical diagnosis within the scope of the invention.

“Drug-naïve” or “drug-free” patient means a human subject diagnosed with schizophrenia who has never received antipsychotic medication (“drug-naïve”) or who has not taken any antipsychotic or psychotropic medication for a period of at least four weeks prior to blood collection (“drug-free”), as confirmed by clinical history, urine toxicology screening, and medical records. This definition ensures that observed gene expression changes are attributable to the disease state and not to pharmacological intervention.

“Peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs)” refers to the subset of white blood cells isolated from whole blood that includes T cells, B cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, typically obtained by density gradient centrifugation or erythrocyte lysis methods. In the context of this invention, PBLs serve as the biological source material for RNA extraction and subsequent gene expression analysis.

“DRD2” denotes the human dopamine receptor D2 gene, located on chromosome 11q23, and includes all naturally occurring splice variants, isoforms, and allelic forms thereof. The term encompasses the mRNA transcript(s) encoded by this gene, which are detectable by nucleic acid amplification techniques using primers and probes specific to conserved regions of the DRD2 sequence.

“Kir2.3” refers to the inwardly rectifying potassium channel 2.3, encoded by the human KCNJ4 gene located on chromosome 11q24. This term includes the mRNA transcript of KCNJ4 and any functionally equivalent variants or isoforms that retain the core structural and regulatory features of the Kir2.3 channel protein.

“Expression level” or “mRNA expression level” means the relative abundance of a specific mRNA transcript in a biological sample, as quantified by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (QRT-PCR) or an equivalent nucleic acid quantification method. Expression levels are typically reported as normalized ratios relative to one or more endogenous reference genes.

“Endogenous reference gene” or “internal control gene” refers to a gene whose expression is constitutively stable across the experimental conditions and biological samples being compared. Examples include β-actin (ACTB) and hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT), which are used in the present invention to normalize target gene expression data and correct for technical variability.

“Quantitative real-time PCR (QRT-PCR)” is a laboratory technique that amplifies and simultaneously quantifies a targeted DNA molecule in real time using fluorescent reporters. The method includes both dye-based detection systems (e.g., SybrGreen) and probe-based systems (e.g., TaqMan hydrolysis probes), and relies on the measurement of the cycle threshold (Ct) value, which is the PCR cycle number at which the fluorescence signal exceeds a predefined threshold.

“Overexpression” or “upregulation” means an increase in the mRNA expression level of a target gene in a test sample relative to a control sample, where the magnitude of increase exceeds a statistically and clinically defined threshold. In the context of this invention, overexpression is determined by comparing the normalized expression ratio of a test sample to the upper limit of the normal range observed in a cohort of healthy control individuals.

“Diagnostic threshold” refers to a pre-established cutoff value for the normalized expression ratio of a target gene, above which a sample is considered positive for the molecular signature of schizophrenia. These thresholds are empirically derived from the distribution of expression levels in healthy controls and are specific to the detection chemistry used (e.g., SybrGreen vs. TaqMan).

“Kit” means a packaged set of reagents, instructions, and optionally instrumentation, designed to perform the method of the invention. A kit may include primers, probes, reverse transcription enzymes, PCR master mix, RNA isolation reagents, and software for data analysis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a novel and non-obvious method for diagnosing schizophrenia based on the detection of elevated mRNA expression levels of two specific genes—DRD2 and Kir2.3—in peripheral blood lymphocytes (PBLs) of human subjects. This method represents a paradigm shift from purely symptom-based diagnosis to an objective, molecularly informed approach that leverages the accessibility of peripheral blood as a surrogate tissue for central nervous system pathology. The invention is grounded in the unexpected discovery that both DRD2 and Kir2.3 are coordinately upregulated in the PBLs of individuals with schizophrenia who have not been exposed to antipsychotic medications, thereby reflecting the intrinsic biological state of the disorder.

The method begins with the collection of a peripheral blood sample from a human subject suspected of having schizophrenia or undergoing psychiatric evaluation. The blood is collected in standard anticoagulant tubes, such as those containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), to prevent coagulation and preserve RNA integrity. From this sample, PBLs are isolated using well-established protocols, such as red blood cell lysis with a hypotonic buffer containing magnesium chloride, sodium chloride, and Tris-HCl at pH 7.0. The resulting lymphocyte pellet is washed multiple times to remove residual erythrocytes and plasma contaminants, ensuring a pure population of nucleated immune cells suitable for downstream molecular analysis.

Total RNA is then extracted from the isolated PBLs using commercially available RNA isolation kits, such as those provided by Macherey-Nagel, which employ silica-membrane-based purification to yield high-quality RNA free of genomic DNA and protein contamination. The integrity and concentration of the extracted RNA are verified using spectrophotometric methods (e.g., NanoDrop) and electrophoretic analysis to ensure suitability for reverse transcription. To prevent RNA degradation during storage and processing, the samples are maintained at –80°C in the presence of RNase inhibitors, such as Prime RNAse inhibitor.

The next step involves the conversion of total RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA) through reverse transcription. This is carried out using a reverse transcriptase enzyme, such as SuperScript II RNase H– Reverse Transcriptase, in the presence of random hexamer primers, deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs), dithiothreitol (DTT), and RNase inhibitor. The reaction is incubated at 42°C for two hours, followed by enzyme inactivation at 70°C for 15 minutes. The resulting cDNA is diluted to an appropriate concentration and serves as the template for quantitative real-time PCR (QRT-PCR).

QRT-PCR is performed using two alternative detection chemistries to ensure robustness and cross-validation. In the first approach, the SybrGreen method is employed, wherein the fluorescent dye SybrGreen I intercalates into double-stranded DNA during amplification, allowing real-time monitoring of product accumulation. Gene-specific primers for DRD2 and Kir2.3 are designed using bioinformatics software such as Primer Express (Applied Biosystems) and synthesized with high purity, as verified by mass spectrometry. Amplification is carried out in a real-time thermocycler under stringent conditions: an initial denaturation at 95°C for 15 minutes, followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds, 60°C for 25 seconds, and 72°C for 25 seconds. A melting curve analysis is performed after each run to confirm the specificity of amplification and rule out primer-dimer artifacts.

In the second approach, the TaqMan probe-based method is used, which offers higher specificity through the use of dual-labeled oligonucleotide probes. These probes contain a 5′ reporter dye (e.g., FAM) and a 3′ quencher dye (e.g., Dabsyl), and are cleaved during amplification by the 5′→3′ exonuclease activity of the DNA polymerase, resulting in increased fluorescence proportional to the amount of amplified product. Reactions are performed with 300 nM primers and 250 nM TaqMan probes under thermal cycling conditions consisting of 95°C for 15 minutes followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 1 minute. Both methods include non-template controls to detect genomic DNA contamination or reagent impurities.

The raw Ct values obtained from QRT-PCR are normalized to one or more endogenous reference genes, such as β-actin or HPRT, whose expression is stable across schizophrenia and control samples. Normalization is performed using the Pfaffl method, which accounts for differences in amplification efficiency between the target and reference genes, thereby providing a more accurate relative quantification. The normalized expression ratio for each sample is then calculated relative to the average of sex-matched healthy controls, establishing a fold-change value.

Critically, the invention defines diagnostic thresholds based on the natural variability of DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression in healthy individuals. Through empirical analysis of control cohorts, the upper limits of normal expression were determined to be 2.56-fold for DRD2 and 3.54-fold for Kir2.3 using SybrGreen, and 2.42-fold for DRD2 and 2.92-fold for Kir2.3 using TaqMan. Any sample exceeding these thresholds is classified as positive for the schizophrenia-associated molecular signature. In validation studies, the majority of drug-naïve or drug-free schizophrenic patients exhibited overexpression of both genes, with average fold-changes of 21.4 for DRD2 and 22.0 for Kir2.3 (SybrGreen), and 7.8 for DRD2 and 8.2 for Kir2.3 (TaqMan), all with statistically significant p-values.

The invention also contemplates the use of hierarchical clustering algorithms to classify samples based on their combined DRD2 and Kir2.3 expression profiles. Such computational analysis correctly segregates schizophrenic and control samples into distinct clusters, with high concordance between the two QRT-PCR methods, thereby reinforcing the diagnostic utility of the dual-marker approach.

Furthermore, the invention extends to kits comprising all necessary reagents for performing the method, including RNA isolation buffers, reverse transcription mixes, gene-specific primers and probes, PCR master mix, and instructional materials. Software modules for automated data analysis and threshold-based classification may also be included. The method is compatible with high-throughput platforms, enabling its integration into clinical laboratories for routine screening.

In summary, the invention provides a reliable, objective, and minimally invasive diagnostic tool for schizophrenia that is rooted in the molecular pathology of the disease. By focusing on drug-naïve or drug-free patients and validating findings with two independent QRT-PCR methodologies, the invention overcomes key limitations of prior art and offers a clinically actionable solution for early diagnosis and monitoring.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Patient Cohort and Sample Collection**

Thirteen drug-naïve or drug-free schizophrenic patients (7 female, 6 male; mean age 34 ± 12 years) and ten healthy control individuals (5 female, 5 male; mean age 38 ± 10 years) were recruited from the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Szeged. All patients met DSM-IV criteria for schizophrenia or schizophreniform psychosis, as confirmed by the Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview Plus (MINI Plus). None had received antipsychotic or other psychotropic medication for at least four weeks prior to enrollment, as verified by clinical interview and urine drug screening. Ten milliliters of peripheral blood were collected in EDTA tubes from each participant. PBLs were isolated by lysing erythrocytes with red blood cell lysis buffer (5 mM MgCl₂, 10 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.0), followed by three washes. RNA was extracted using the Macherey-Nagel RNA isolation kit and stored at –80°C with RNase inhibitor.

**Example 2: Microarray Screening and Candidate Gene Identification**

Pooled RNA from all 13 schizophrenic patients and all 10 controls was reverse transcribed and labeled using the Genisphere Expression Array 350 Detection Kit. The labeled cDNA was hybridized to a custom human cDNA microarray containing 3,200 clones, including 1,200 from a lymphocyte cDNA library and 2,000 from a mixed human library. Hybridization was performed in a Ventana station using a two-step protocol. Scanning and data analysis revealed that DRD2 and Kir2.3 were among the most significantly upregulated genes in the schizophrenic pool, with 2.56-fold and 6.75-fold increases, respectively, compared to controls. These candidates were selected for QRT-PCR validation.

**Example 3: QRT-PCR Validation Using SybrGreen and TaqMan Chemistries**

Individual RNA samples from all 23 participants were reverse transcribed as described. QRT-PCR was performed using both SybrGreen and TaqMan methods. For DRD2, 12 of 13 schizophrenic patients showed overexpression by SybrGreen (5.7- to 64.7-fold; average 21.4 ± 18.5; p = 0.0019), and 11 of 13 by TaqMan (3.7- to 24.1-fold; average 7.8 ± 6.3; p = 0.0023). For Kir2.3, 12 of 13 patients were positive by SybrGreen (4- to 133.6-fold; average 22 ± 34.7; p = 0.0535), and 11 of 13 by TaqMan (2.8- to 15.8-fold; average 8.2 ± 4.8; p = 0.0001). Only one patient (F5) showed expression below threshold for both genes, possibly indicating a subtype or technical outlier. Healthy controls exhibited expression within defined normal ranges.

**Example 4: Diagnostic Threshold Determination and Clinical Classification**

The upper limits of normal expression in controls were established as 2.56 (DRD2) and 3.54 (Kir2.3) for SybrGreen, and 2.42 (DRD2) and 2.92 (Kir2.3) for TaqMan. These thresholds were used to classify all samples. Hierarchical clustering of the combined expression data correctly grouped all but one schizophrenic sample (F5) into a distinct cluster separate from controls, demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy. The method achieved a sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 100% in this cohort.

**Example 5: Development of a Diagnostic Kit**

A prototype diagnostic kit was assembled containing: (1) RNA isolation reagents, (2) reverse transcription mix, (3) lyophilized primers and TaqMan probes for DRD2, Kir2.3, and HPRT, (4) QRT-PCR master mix, and (5) software for automated Ct analysis and threshold-based classification. The kit was validated using the cohort from Example 1, yielding results consistent with manual analysis. The kit is designed for use in clinical laboratories with standard real-time PCR instrumentation.
