# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of antimalarial prophylaxis, and more specifically to long-acting injectable pharmaceutical compositions comprising atovaquone formulated as solid drug nanoparticles for the prevention of malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum. The invention provides a novel method of causal chemoprophylaxis through sustained release of atovaquone from an intramuscular depot, thereby achieving prolonged systemic exposure sufficient to inhibit the development of liver-stage parasites following sporozoite inoculation. This approach overcomes the limitations of conventional oral dosing regimens, which are frequently associated with poor patient adherence and suboptimal pharmacokinetic profiles that fail to maintain protective drug concentrations throughout the duration of exposure risk. The invention is particularly directed to high-drug-content solid drug nanoparticle formulations of atovaquone, prepared using emulsion-templated freeze-drying techniques, which enable administration of a single intramuscular injection capable of providing effective malaria prophylaxis for at least four weeks in a preclinical model, with projected human efficacy extending beyond one month. The disclosed formulations leverage the unique pharmacological properties of atovaquone—including its activity against both liver and blood stages of the parasite, its favorable safety profile, and its resistance profile that prevents transmission of resistant strains through mosquitoes—while addressing the critical need for improved adherence and extended duration of protection in travelers and populations at risk of malaria infection.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Malaria remains one of the most significant global health challenges, with Plasmodium falciparum responsible for hundreds of millions of infections and hundreds of thousands of deaths annually, predominantly among children in endemic regions. Despite substantial progress in malaria control efforts, the incidence of malaria among travelers to endemic areas continues to rise, with unprotected visitors to West Africa facing a monthly infection risk of 3.4%. While vaccine development has shown promise, no currently available malaria vaccine provides reliable, high-level, long-lasting protection, leaving chemoprophylaxis as an essential component of malaria prevention strategies for non-immune individuals traveling to or residing in malarious regions.

Current antimalarial prophylaxis relies heavily on daily or weekly oral drug regimens, including medications such as mefloquine, doxycycline, and atovaquone-proguanil combinations. However, these dosing schedules are notoriously associated with poor compliance, with adherence rates often falling below 50% in real-world settings. This non-adherence significantly compromises the effectiveness of prophylactic interventions and contributes to breakthrough infections. The fundamental challenge lies in maintaining therapeutic drug concentrations throughout the entire period of potential exposure to infectious mosquito bites, which requires consistent medication intake over extended periods that may span weeks or months.

The life cycle of Plasmodium falciparum presents distinct opportunities for therapeutic intervention. Human infection begins when sporozoites are injected into the skin by an infected female Anopheles mosquito. These sporozoites rapidly migrate to the liver, where they invade hepatocytes and undergo extensive replication, amplifying approximately 20,000-fold over a period of 6.5 days before releasing merozoites into the bloodstream to initiate the erythrocytic cycle responsible for clinical disease manifestations. Most conventional antimalarial drugs target the erythrocytic stages, but several compounds, including atovaquone, demonstrate activity against both liver and blood stages. Causal prophylaxis, which targets the liver stage before the massive amplification occurs, offers several advantages over suppressive prophylaxis that acts only on blood-stage parasites. These advantages include targeting a much smaller number of parasites (before four-log amplification in the liver), requiring shorter post-exposure dosing periods (one week versus four weeks), and potentially preventing establishment of infection entirely rather than merely suppressing symptoms.

Atovaquone, a hydroxynaphthoquinone compound that functions as a structural mimic of ubiquinone, selectively inhibits the cytochrome b complex in the mitochondrial electron transport chain of malaria parasites. This inhibition collapses the trans-mitochondrial membrane potential, effectively blocking cellular respiration and leading to parasite death. Atovaquone has been used safely for over 25 years in immunosuppressed patients for the prophylaxis and treatment of Pneumocystis pneumonia, with established dosing regimens that maintain plasma concentrations above 20 µg/mL (54 µM). The compound exhibits several favorable pharmacological properties for prophylactic use: it is not metabolized systemically, demonstrates minimal drug-drug interactions, possesses a plasma half-life measurable in days, and shows potent sub-nanomolar activity against erythrocytic P. falciparum in vitro. Importantly, atovaquone is not cross-resistant with other antimalarial classes and maintains activity against both liver and blood stages of the parasite.

A critical consideration in antimalarial therapy is the development of drug resistance. While atovaquone resistance can emerge through mutations in the cytochrome b gene during treatment of established erythrocytic infections, recent studies have revealed a remarkable phenomenon: atovaquone-resistant P. falciparum parasites cannot survive in or be transmitted by mosquitoes. This biological constraint effectively prevents the spread of resistant strains through natural transmission cycles, making atovaquone monotherapy particularly suitable for prophylactic applications where the primary concern is preventing initial infection rather than treating established disease.

Despite these favorable properties, the clinical utility of atovaquone for malaria prophylaxis has been limited by formulation challenges and pharmacokinetic constraints associated with oral administration. The compound's extreme hydrophobicity (log P = 4.7) results in variable absorption and bioavailability when administered orally, necessitating combination with proguanil to enhance solubility and reduce resistance development in treatment settings. However, for prophylaxis in healthy individuals, where drug concentrations can be established before parasite exposure and the numerical advantage favors prevention over treatment, monoprophylaxis with atovaquone represents a viable strategy if adequate and sustained systemic exposure can be achieved.

Traditional approaches to developing long-acting injectable formulations have focused on either drug-associated nanocarriers (through conjugation, complex formation, or encapsulation) or excipient-stabilized solid drug nanoparticles (SDNs) produced via attrition methods such as nanomilling or high-pressure homogenization. Both approaches typically achieve low drug-to-excipient ratios, commonly not exceeding 25% by weight, which necessitates large injection volumes that may be poorly tolerated by patients. Emulsion-templated freeze-drying (ETFD) represents an alternative methodology that enables the creation of high-drug-content SDN formulations, often achieving drug loadings of 70% or greater by weight. This approach utilizes inactive commercial excipients commonly found in FDA-approved medicines, minimizing safety concerns while maximizing the amount of active pharmaceutical ingredient delivered per unit volume.

Recent advances in long-acting injectable antiretroviral formulations have demonstrated the clinical feasibility and therapeutic benefits of sustained-release nanoparticle systems, with some formulations providing therapeutic plasma drug exposure for periods ranging from one to three months. These developments have validated the long-acting injectable approach for both treatment and pre-exposure prophylaxis applications. Given atovaquone's favorable pharmacokinetic profile—including low systemic clearance and high hydrophobicity—the compound is particularly well-suited for incorporation into long-acting injectable formulations that could provide sustained protection against malaria infection.

The need for improved malaria prophylaxis options is particularly acute for travelers whose trips typically last four weeks or less, representing a substantial portion of the at-risk population. A single-administration, long-acting injectable formulation that provides reliable protection throughout the duration of travel would address the critical adherence issues associated with current oral regimens while offering the convenience and reliability more characteristic of vaccination. Such an intervention would represent a significant advance in malaria prevention strategies and could substantially reduce the burden of disease among travelers and potentially contribute to broader malaria control efforts in endemic regions.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides long-acting injectable pharmaceutical compositions comprising atovaquone formulated as solid drug nanoparticles (SDNs) for the causal prophylaxis of malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum. These compositions are prepared using emulsion-templated freeze-drying (ETFD) techniques to produce high-drug-content formulations containing at least 70% by weight atovaquone, stabilized by a combination of polymer and surfactant excipients selected from FDA-approved inactive ingredients. The resulting SDN formulations, when administered as a single intramuscular injection, provide sustained release of atovaquone over an extended period, maintaining plasma concentrations sufficient to prevent the development of liver-stage parasites following sporozoite challenge.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising atovaquone solid drug nanoparticles, wherein the composition contains from 70% to 90% by weight atovaquone, from 10% to 20% by weight polymer stabilizer, and from 5% to 10% by weight surfactant stabilizer. The polymer stabilizer may be selected from the group consisting of poly(ethylene oxide)20 sorbitan monolaurate (Tween 20), poly(ethylene oxide)20 sorbitan monooleate (Tween 80), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), hydroxymethyl propyl cellulose (HPMC), poly(ethylene oxide)101-block-poly(propylene oxide)56-block-poly(ethylene oxide)101 (F127), poly(ethylene oxide)80-block-poly(propylene oxide)27-block-poly(ethylene oxide)80 (F68), and polyvinyl alcohol-graft-poly(ethylene glycol) copolymer (Kollicoat). The surfactant stabilizer may be selected from the group consisting of α-tocopherol poly(ethylene glycol) succinate (TPGS), sodium deoxycholate (NDC), polyethylene glycol15-hydroxystearate (Solutol), sodium 1,4-bis(2-ethylhexoxy)-1,4-dioxobutane-2-sulphonate (AOT), and poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP K30).

In another embodiment, the invention provides a method of preventing malaria in a subject comprising administering to the subject a single intramuscular injection of a pharmaceutical composition as described herein, wherein the composition provides plasma atovaquone concentrations of at least 200 ng/mL for a period of at least 28 days following administration. The method is particularly effective for preventing infection caused by Plasmodium falciparum sporozoite challenge and operates primarily through causal prophylaxis by inhibiting the development of liver-stage parasites.

In a specific embodiment, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition designated ATQSDN7, comprising atovaquone solid drug nanoparticles formulated with poly(ethylene oxide)101-block-poly(propylene oxide)56-block-poly(ethylene oxide)101 (F127) as the polymer stabilizer and α-tocopherol poly(ethylene glycol) succinate (TPGS) as the surfactant stabilizer, in a weight ratio of approximately 80:13:7 (atovaquone:polymer:surfactant). This composition, when administered intramuscularly at doses ranging from 36 mg/kg to 200 mg/kg, provides complete protection against Plasmodium berghei sporozoite challenge for up to 28 days in a preclinical mouse model.

The invention further provides a method for determining the efficacy of atovaquone SDN formulations through in vitro rapid equilibrium dialysis assays using simulated interstitial fluid (SIF), which correlate strongly with in vivo performance. This screening methodology enables the identification of formulations with optimal release kinetics for sustained prophylactic activity. Additionally, the invention encompasses methods for characterizing SDN formulations based on particle size distribution, with preferred formulations exhibiting Z-average diameters less than 1000 nm, polydispersity indices less than 0.4, and stability during analysis as indicated by less than 5% standard deviation across multiple measurements.

The pharmaceutical compositions of the present invention offer several advantages over existing malaria prophylaxis options. They provide extended duration of protection from a single administration, eliminating the adherence issues associated with daily or weekly oral regimens. The high-drug-content formulations minimize injection volume requirements, improving patient tolerability. The causal mechanism of action targets parasites at the liver stage, preventing establishment of infection rather than merely suppressing symptoms. Furthermore, the use of atovaquone monotherapy in this prophylactic context is supported by the inability of resistant parasites to survive mosquito transmission, mitigating concerns about resistance development in the community.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Examples

The present invention is exemplified through the development and characterization of atovaquone solid drug nanoparticle (ATQSDN) formulations prepared using emulsion-templated freeze-drying (ETFD) methodology. A comprehensive library of 42 candidate formulations was created by combining atovaquone with various binary combinations of polymer and surfactant excipients, all selected from the FDA Center for Drug Evaluation and Research (CDER) list of inactive ingredients. Each formulation contained 80% by weight atovaquone, 13% by weight polymer, and 7% by weight surfactant, representing a high-drug-loading approach that maximizes the amount of active pharmaceutical ingredient delivered per unit volume while minimizing the total injection volume required for therapeutic efficacy.

The preparation of these formulations involved creating an oil-in-water emulsion using chloroform as the organic phase containing atovaquone at 80 mg/mL, combined with aqueous stock solutions of polymer and surfactant excipients at 22.5 mg/mL each. The emulsion was formed in a 1:4 oil-to-water ratio and subjected to controlled sonication using a Covaris S2x instrument with specific parameters (duty cycle of 20, intensity of 10, 500 cycles/burst in frequency sweeping mode) for 30 seconds to ensure uniform droplet formation. The resulting emulsions were immediately cryogenically frozen and subsequently lyophilized for 42 hours to produce dry, porous monoliths that could be stored in sealed vials until needed for reconstitution.

Initial physical characterization of the 42 candidate formulations was performed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) to assess particle size distribution and stability. Formulations were dispersed in water at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and analyzed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument with automatic measurement optimization. Eleven formulations met stringent selection criteria, including complete aqueous dispersion, Z-average diameter less than 1000 nm, stability during analysis (less than 5% standard deviation across three scans), and polydispersity index less than 0.4 to ensure uniform drug release characteristics. These eleven "hit" formulations, designated ATQSDN1 through ATQSDN11, demonstrated reproducible synthesis across multiple batches and scale-independent production, as confirmed by comparing full-scale and half-scale preparations.

The in vitro release kinetics of the eleven candidate formulations were evaluated using a rapid equilibrium dialysis model designed to simulate an intramuscular depot environment. Each SDN formulation was dispersed in simulated interstitial fluid (SIF) containing 35 mg/mL bovine serum albumin and 2 mg/mL bovine γ-globulin to mimic physiological conditions. The formulations were placed in the donor compartment of 8 kDa molecular weight cutoff dialysis inserts, with fresh SIF in the acceptor compartment, and samples were collected at regular intervals over six hours while maintaining constant temperature (37°C) and agitation (100 rpm). Scintillation counting of 3H-labeled atovaquone enabled precise quantification of drug release rates, revealing a 50-fold range of release kinetics among the candidates. Five formulations—ATQSDN2, ATQSDN4, ATQSDN6, ATQSDN7, and ATQSDN8—were selected for in vivo testing based on their diverse release profiles, representing the highest, lowest, and intermediate release rates observed in the screening.

In vivo efficacy testing was conducted using the stringent Plasmodium berghei ANKA-C57BL/6 mouse model, which closely approximates human P. falciparum malaria in terms of disease progression and therapeutic response. This model is particularly valuable because infection is uniformly lethal, with parasitemia reliably resulting from inoculation of just 50 sporozoites, and the liver stage duration (48 hours) and erythrocytic cycle (24 hours) are well-characterized. In preliminary surveys, all five candidate nanoformulations provided some protection when administered one day before sporozoite challenge, but only ATQSDN7 and ATQSDN8 demonstrated complete protection against challenge administered seven days after dosing. These two formulations were therefore advanced to comprehensive dose-ranging and duration-of-protection studies.

Mice received single intramuscular injections of ATQSDN7 at doses of 36, 100, or 200 mg/kg and were challenged with 5000 P. berghei sporozoites at intervals ranging from 1 to 42 days after dosing. Prophylaxis was deemed successful only if all animals in a dosing cohort remained parasite-free for 42 days following challenge, with confirmation required in at least two independent experiments. The results demonstrated dose-dependent protection, with the 200 mg/kg dose providing complete protection for up to 28 days after administration. Control groups receiving equivalent doses of oral atovaquone failed to protect against challenge even when administered just one day before sporozoite inoculation, highlighting the superior pharmacokinetic profile achieved through the long-acting injectable formulation.

To determine the mechanism of prophylactic action, paired challenge experiments were conducted using both sporozoite and erythrocytic forms of P. berghei. Mice treated with 36 mg/kg ATQSDN7 were challenged with either 5000 sporozoites or 150,000 infected erythrocytes at various time points after dosing. The sporozoite-challenged mice remained completely protected, while those challenged with erythrocytic parasites developed patent parasitemia, demonstrating that the primary mechanism of protection is causal prophylaxis targeting the liver stage rather than suppressive activity against blood-stage parasites. This finding is consistent with clinical observations in human sporozoite challenge studies, where atovaquone prophylaxis prevents establishment of detectable erythrocytic infection as measured by sensitive PCR methods.

Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed that intramuscular administration of ATQSDN7 resulted in sustained plasma atovaquone concentrations with a terminal half-life of 105 hours, substantially longer than the 9-hour half-life observed after oral administration in mice. Plasma concentrations demonstrated dose proportionality, with linear relationships between administered dose and plasma drug levels measured 14 days after injection. Critically, plasma concentrations exceeding 200 ng/mL at the time of sporozoite challenge correlated strongly with prophylactic success, establishing a clear therapeutic threshold for efficacy. This benchmark aligns with human data from P. falciparum sporozoite challenge studies, where plasma concentrations of at least 200 ng/mL provided 96% protection against infection.

Resistance testing was conducted to evaluate whether therapeutic failures resulted from the emergence of atovaquone-resistant parasites. Mice that developed parasitemia following suboptimal dosing (36 mg/kg ATQSDN7 with challenge 14 days after administration) had parasites harvested and subinoculated into naive recipients. These parasites, along with controls from untreated animals, were successfully cured with standard oral atovaquone therapy, confirming that they remained drug-sensitive. In contrast, known atovaquone-resistant P. berghei parasites failed to respond to the same treatment regimen. This evidence indicates that prophylactic failures result from subtherapeutic drug concentrations rather than resistance development, supporting the safety of atovaquone monotherapy in this prophylactic context.

The specific formulation ATQSDN7, comprising atovaquone with F127 polymer and TPGS surfactant in an 80:13:7 weight ratio, emerged as the lead candidate due to its optimal balance of sustained release kinetics, high drug loading, and robust in vivo efficacy. The formulation's Z-average particle diameter of approximately 300 nm, polydispersity index of 0.25, and zeta potential of -12 mV contribute to its stability and predictable release profile. The high-drug-content approach minimizes the required injection volume to 40 µL total (20 µL per limb) for the 200 mg/kg dose in mice, which translates to clinically acceptable volumes for human administration.

Additional studies investigated the effect of drug loading on release kinetics by systematically varying the atovaquone content from 20% to 80% by weight in three representative formulations (ATQSDN4, ATQSDN6, and ATQSDN8). The results demonstrated that release rates generally decreased as drug content increased to 60% by weight, though ATQSDN4 exhibited higher release rates at 80% loading. This finding underscores the importance of optimizing drug loading for each specific excipient combination to achieve desired release profiles, as the relationship between drug content and release kinetics is not universally predictable across different formulation systems.

### Materials and Methods

The materials and methods employed in the development and characterization of the atovaquone solid drug nanoparticle formulations of the present invention are described in detail below. All materials were obtained from commercial suppliers and used without further purification unless otherwise specified. Atovaquone was sourced from WIS Pharmtech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China), while excipients were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Dorset, UK), BASF (Royal Tunbridge Wells, UK), or Fluka Chemicals (Dorset, UK). Specifically, the polymer excipients included α-tocopherol poly(ethylene glycol) succinate (TPGS), poly(ethylene oxide)20 sorbitan monolaurate (Tween 20), poly(ethylene oxide)20 sorbitan monooleate (Tween 80), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), hydroxymethyl propyl cellulose (HPMC), poly(ethylene oxide)101-block-poly(propylene oxide)56-block-poly(ethylene oxide)101 (F127), poly(ethylene oxide)80-block-poly(propylene oxide)27-block-poly(ethylene oxide)80 (F68), and polyvinyl alcohol-graft-poly(ethylene glycol) copolymer (Kollicoat). Surfactant excipients included sodium deoxycholate (NDC), polyethylene glycol15-hydroxystearate (Solutol), sodium 1,4-bis(2-ethylhexoxy)-1,4-dioxobutane-2-sulphonate (AOT), and poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP K30). Statistically labeled 3H-atovaquone was obtained from RC Tritec Ltd (Switzerland) for radiometric studies.

The ETFD process for preparing solid drug nanoparticles began with the preparation of stock solutions: atovaquone was dissolved in chloroform at 80 mg/mL, while polymer and surfactant excipients were each prepared as aqueous solutions at 22.5 mg/mL. For full-scale preparations, these stock solutions were combined in precise proportions—100 µL atovaquone solution, 63.7 µL polymer solution, 31.5 µL surfactant solution, and 304.8 µL water—to achieve the target solid mass ratio of 80% atovaquone:13% polymer:7% surfactant in a 1:4 oil-to-water emulsion. Half-scale preparations used proportionally reduced volumes (50 µL atovaquone, 31.85 µL polymer, 15.75 µL surfactant, and 152.4 µL water) to enable efficient screening while maintaining identical formulation ratios.

The emulsification step was performed using a Covaris S2x focused ultrasonicator with specific parameters optimized for nanoparticle formation: duty cycle of 20, intensity of 10, and 500 cycles per burst in frequency sweeping mode, applied for 30 seconds. This controlled sonication ensured uniform droplet size distribution in the emulsion, which is critical for achieving consistent nanoparticle characteristics after freeze-drying. Immediately following emulsification, samples were cryogenically frozen using liquid nitrogen or dry ice/isopropanol baths to preserve the emulsion structure, then transferred to a Virtis benchtop K lyophilizer for 42 hours under vacuum to produce dry, porous monoliths. The resulting products were stored in sealed vials under desiccated conditions until analysis or administration.

For physical characterization, samples were reconstituted by adding 16 mL of deionized water to each vial and vortexing to ensure complete dispersion. Particle size analysis was performed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument, employing automatic measurement optimization and Malvern Zetasizer software version 7.11 for data analysis. Measurements were conducted in triplicate at 25°C, with Z-average diameter, polydispersity index, and zeta potential recorded for each formulation. Stability criteria required less than 5% standard deviation across the three replicate measurements, ensuring that the formulations maintained consistent particle characteristics during analysis.

In vitro release kinetics were evaluated using a rapid equilibrium dialysis model that simulates the intramuscular depot environment. Formulations were dispersed in simulated interstitial fluid (SIF) consisting of 35 mg/mL bovine serum albumin and 2 mg/mL bovine γ-globulin in distilled water, achieving a final atovaquone concentration of 1 mg/mL. For comparison, unformulated atovaquone was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted into SIF such that the final DMSO concentration remained below 1% by volume. Dialysis was performed using 8 kDa molecular weight cutoff inserts (ThermoFisher Scientific) with 0.5 mL of formulation in the donor compartment and 1 mL of SIF in the acceptor compartment. The plates were placed on an orbital shaker (Heidolph Rotomax 120) at 100 rpm and 37°C for six hours, with 0.5 mL samples removed from the acceptor compartment at 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 hours and replaced with fresh pre-warmed SIF to maintain sink conditions. Samples were mixed with 4 mL of Ultima Gold scintillation cocktail and analyzed using a Perkin Elmer 3100TS scintillation counter to determine disintegrations per minute (DPM), which were converted to drug release percentages based on the initial donor amount.

For in vivo studies, all animal protocols were approved by the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with established guidelines. Male C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old, Jackson Laboratory) weighing approximately 20 g were used for efficacy and pharmacokinetic studies. Intramuscular injections were administered into the biceps femoris muscle using 27-gauge needles and 100 µL Hamilton syringes (part number 7656-01), with a total volume of 40 µL (20 µL per limb) to ensure proper depot formation without excessive tissue damage. Oral administration used Mepron (GSK) diluted with 10% poloxamer 188, with animals fasted overnight prior to and four hours after dosing to standardize absorption conditions.

Parasite challenge materials were prepared using standardized protocols. For sporozoite challenges, female Anopheles stephensi mosquitoes (Liston strain) were fed on Swiss Webster mice infected with P. berghei ANKA 2.34 strain (ATCC), then maintained for 18 days under controlled conditions (19°C, 80% relative humidity) before salivary gland dissection. Sporozoites were released by mechanical disruption through a 28-gauge needle and counted using a hemocytometer, then diluted to 25,000 sporozoites/mL in RPMI medium. Blood-stage challenges used donor C57BL/6 mice infected with 5000 sporozoites, with blood harvested by cardiac puncture when parasitemia reached at least 0.5%, then diluted to achieve 1.5 × 10^5 infected erythrocytes per 200 µL challenge dose.

Efficacy assessment involved challenging mice with either 5000 sporozoites or 1.5 × 10^5 infected erythrocytes via tail vein infusion at predetermined intervals after dosing. Blood samples were collected by tail snip starting four days after challenge and weekly thereafter for 42 days, with thin smears prepared and stained with Giemsa for microscopic examination. A sample was considered negative if no parasites were observed in 2000 erythrocytes (detection limit of 0.05% parasitemia). Animals were monitored daily for morbidity and mortality, with protection defined as complete absence of patent parasitemia throughout the 42-day observation period. Each experiment included vehicle controls to validate challenge infectivity, and successful prophylaxis required confirmation in at least two independent experiments with cohorts of 3-5 mice each.

Pharmacokinetic sampling involved collecting blood in microtainer tubes (BD Biosciences), centrifuging at 1300×g for 10 minutes at 4°C, and storing plasma at -80°C until analysis. Atovaquone concentrations were determined using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) with modifications to a previously validated method. Briefly, 25 µL of thawed plasma was mixed with acetonitrile and deuterated atovaquone internal standard (Toronto Research Chemicals), vortexed, and centrifuged to obtain supernatant for analysis. Separation was achieved using a linear gradient on a 50 × 2 mm, 2.5 µm Polar-RP 100 Å column (Synergi), with detection by triple-quadrupole API 4000 mass analyzer (SCIEX) using electrospray ionization. The assay was validated for the range of 5-5000 ng/mL using 1/x²-weighted least-squares-fitted quadratic regression, with all reported values determined by this method.

Statistical analyses were performed using appropriate software packages. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Plasma half-life was estimated by linear regression of log-transformed concentration versus time using sparse sampling non-compartmental analysis in Phoenix WinNonlin 8.0 (Certara). Fisher's exact test was used to compare proportions between treatment groups using Stata 14.0 (StataCorp), with significance defined as p ≤ 0.05. All experimental data are available upon request from the authors.
