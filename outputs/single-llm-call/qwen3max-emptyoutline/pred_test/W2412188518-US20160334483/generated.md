# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a powerful non-invasive diagnostic modality that provides detailed anatomical and functional information without the use of ionizing radiation. Among the various MRI techniques developed over the past several decades, ultrashort echo time (UTE) sequences have emerged as particularly valuable for imaging tissues with extremely short transverse relaxation times (T₂*), such as cortical bone, tendons, ligaments, menisci, lung parenchyma, and dental structures. These tissues typically decay too rapidly to be captured effectively by conventional MRI sequences, which employ echo times (TEs) on the order of milliseconds or longer. In contrast, UTE sequences are capable of achieving TEs well below 100 microseconds, thereby enabling visualization of otherwise "invisible" short-T₂* components.

One prominent class of UTE techniques includes zero echo time (ZTE) and sweep imaging with Fourier transformation (SWIFT). ZTE, in particular, has gained significant attention due to its ability to acquire data immediately after radiofrequency (RF) excitation, effectively achieving a nominal TE of zero. In ZTE, magnetic field gradients are applied before the RF excitation pulse—typically a constant-amplitude “hard” pulse—and remain active throughout the subsequent signal acquisition. This approach allows radial sampling of k-space starting from its center, which is essential for capturing rapidly decaying signals. However, this design imposes a critical constraint: the excitation bandwidth and the readout bandwidth must be identical because the same gradient amplitude governs both the spatial encoding during excitation and the frequency encoding during signal reception. Consequently, any attempt to increase the readout bandwidth to mitigate image blurring—caused by off-resonance effects and accelerated T₂* decay at higher static magnetic field strengths—necessarily entails a proportional increase in the excitation bandwidth.

This coupling between excitation and readout bandwidths presents several practical limitations in clinical applications. First, a higher excitation bandwidth requires a shorter RF pulse duration, which directly increases the peak RF power and the specific absorption rate (SAR)—a measure of the rate at which RF energy is absorbed by biological tissue. Regulatory safety limits on SAR and RF amplifier hardware constraints often restrict the maximum allowable flip angle or necessitate longer repetition times (TR), thereby compromising image contrast or prolonging scan duration. Second, most standard clinical MRI scanners exhibit relatively slow transmit-to-receive (T/R) switching times—typically exceeding 20 microseconds—which results in the loss of critical k-space data points near the origin during the transition period between RF transmission and signal reception. This missing central k-space region manifests as image blurring and reduced spatial resolution, particularly problematic in high-field systems (e.g., 7 Tesla) where chemical shift artifacts and susceptibility-induced distortions are exacerbated.

To address the T/R switching limitation, the pointwise-encoding time reduction with radial acquisition (PETRA) technique was introduced. PETRA combines the radial k-space sampling of ZTE with single-point imaging (SPI), wherein the missing central k-space points are acquired separately by stepping through discrete gradient amplitudes to sample individual k-space locations one at a time. While PETRA successfully circumvents the need for ultrafast T/R switching, it introduces a new challenge: when higher readout bandwidths are employed to reduce blurring, the number of missing k-space center points increases proportionally, thereby extending the SPI acquisition time significantly. This trade-off between image fidelity and scan efficiency becomes especially acute in high-resolution or volumetric imaging protocols, where thousands of radial spokes may be required.

Moreover, the fundamental constraint in conventional PETRA—that excitation and readout bandwidths remain equal—means that efforts to improve image sharpness via increased bandwidth inevitably intensify SAR and RF power demands, limiting applicability in human subjects, particularly at ultra-high field strengths such as 7 T. Alternative strategies, such as using frequency-modulated RF pulses or post-processing corrections for sinc-shaped excitation profiles, have been explored but often require complex reconstruction algorithms or specialized hardware not readily available on clinical platforms.

Thus, there exists a long-felt but unresolved need in the field of MRI for a method that decouples excitation and readout bandwidths in radial UTE sequences, thereby enabling high readout bandwidths to suppress image blurring while maintaining low excitation bandwidths to comply with SAR and RF power constraints. Such a method should be compatible with existing clinical MRI hardware, avoid excessive prolongation of scan time, and preserve sensitivity to short-T₂* tissues without introducing new artifacts or requiring extensive modifications to standard imaging workflows. The present invention addresses these unmet needs through a novel gradient modulation scheme integrated into the PETRA framework, offering a practical and effective solution to a persistent technical challenge in ultrashort TE MRI.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) method and system that significantly reduces image blurring caused by off-resonance effects and rapid T₂* signal decay in ultrashort echo time (UTE) imaging, particularly in high-field MRI applications, while simultaneously mitigating the specific absorption rate (SAR) and radiofrequency (RF) peak power limitations inherent in conventional zero echo time (ZTE) and pointwise-encoding time reduction with radial acquisition (PETRA) techniques. This is achieved through the introduction of gradient modulation (GM) into the PETRA sequence, thereby enabling independent control of excitation and readout bandwidths—a capability previously unattainable in standard radial UTE methods.

In accordance with the invention, an MRI pulse sequence is executed in which a hard RF excitation pulse is applied under a constant initial gradient amplitude (G_ex), defining a low excitation bandwidth that minimizes SAR and RF peak power. Following a transmit-to-receive (T/R) switching delay, during which no signal is acquired, the gradient amplitude is dynamically ramped upward from G_ex to a higher maximum value (G_max) over a controlled time interval. This gradient modulation phase occurs after the receiver gate is enabled and precedes a brief flat-gradient delay period used to acquire missing central k-space points via single-point imaging (SPI). By increasing the gradient amplitude during signal readout, the effective readout bandwidth is substantially enhanced without altering the excitation bandwidth, thereby accelerating k-space traversal and reducing vulnerability to off-resonance shifts and T₂* decay.

The decoupling of excitation and readout bandwidths constitutes a fundamental advancement over prior art. In conventional PETRA or ZTE, any increase in readout bandwidth necessitates a commensurate increase in excitation bandwidth, leading to prohibitively high SAR and RF power at ultra-high fields. The present invention circumvents this limitation by maintaining a narrow excitation bandwidth—sufficient for adequate slice or volume excitation with manageable SAR—while employing a broad readout bandwidth during data acquisition to minimize blurring from chemical shift, magnetic susceptibility variations, and fast signal decay. This dual-bandwidth strategy preserves the high sensitivity to short-T₂* tissues characteristic of UTE methods while dramatically improving spatial fidelity.

Furthermore, because the number of missing k-space center points in each radial acquisition is proportional to the product of the T/R switching delay and the excitation bandwidth, the use of a lower excitation bandwidth in the present invention inherently reduces the size of the central k-space gap that must be filled by SPI. This leads to a shorter SPI acquisition time compared to conventional high-bandwidth PETRA, thereby improving overall scan efficiency without sacrificing image quality. The gradient ramp is implemented with a controlled slew rate—preferably at or below 50% of the scanner’s maximum—to minimize eddy current artifacts and acoustic noise, ensuring compatibility with standard clinical MRI systems.

The invention is particularly advantageous for imaging anatomical regions with strong magnetic susceptibility gradients, such as the inner ear, sinuses, lungs, and bone-tissue interfaces, where conventional MRI suffers from severe distortion and signal loss. Experimental validation at 7 Tesla demonstrates that the GM-PETRA technique yields markedly sharper images of fine structures in the inner ear and apple mesocarp—tissues with extremely short T₂* values—compared to both standard PETRA and conventional 3D gradient echo (GRE) sequences. Simulations confirm that increasing the readout bandwidth via gradient modulation effectively suppresses off-resonance blurring (e.g., fat-water chemical shift artifacts) and enhances edge definition in short-T₂* compartments.

Importantly, the method requires no specialized hardware beyond what is standard on modern clinical MRI scanners. It operates within existing RF and gradient safety envelopes and can be implemented through software modifications to the pulse sequence controller. Image reconstruction employs standard non-uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT) techniques with iterative density correction to account for variable k-space sampling density during the gradient ramp phase, ensuring accurate and artifact-free image formation.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, clinically feasible MRI technique that overcomes longstanding limitations in UTE imaging by enabling high readout bandwidths for improved image sharpness while maintaining low excitation bandwidths to satisfy SAR and RF power constraints. This innovation expands the diagnostic utility of UTE MRI in challenging anatomical contexts and at ultra-high field strengths, facilitating clearer visualization of microstructures with short T₂* relaxation times.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) method that integrates gradient modulation into a pointwise-encoding time reduction with radial acquisition (PETRA) sequence to achieve independent optimization of excitation and readout bandwidths. This approach resolves critical limitations in conventional ultrashort echo time (UTE) MRI, particularly at high and ultra-high static magnetic field strengths, where image blurring from off-resonance effects and rapid T₂* signal decay severely degrades image quality, and where regulatory and hardware constraints on specific absorption rate (SAR) and RF peak power restrict the use of high-bandwidth excitation pulses. The following detailed description sets forth the structure, operation, and advantages of the invention with sufficient particularity to enable those skilled in the art to make and use it.

The method begins with the application of a hard radiofrequency (RF) excitation pulse under a constant initial gradient amplitude, denoted G_ex. This gradient is applied prior to and during the RF pulse, consistent with zero echo time (ZTE) methodology, such that the center of k-space corresponds temporally to the center of the RF pulse. The excitation bandwidth (BW_ex) is determined by the product of the gyromagnetic ratio (γ), the initial gradient amplitude (G_ex), and the field of view (FOV), i.e., BW_ex = γ·G_ex·FOV. By selecting a relatively low G_ex, the excitation bandwidth is kept narrow, which directly reduces both the peak RF power (B₁,max) and the SAR, as these quantities scale with the square of the excitation bandwidth for a given flip angle. This is crucial for human imaging at 7 Tesla and above, where SAR limits are stringent and RF amplifier capabilities are often insufficient for very short, high-power pulses.

Immediately following the RF pulse, the MRI system undergoes a transmit-to-receive (T/R) switching delay, denoted t_s, during which the RF transmitter is disabled and the receiver is gated off to protect sensitive electronics from residual transmit energy. This delay, typically ranging from 10 to 30 microseconds on clinical scanners, results in the absence of signal acquisition during the early portion of k-space traversal. Consequently, a central region of k-space—defined by |k| < γ·G_ex·(pw/2 + t_s), where pw is the pulse width of the hard RF pulse—is not sampled during the radial acquisition phase. This missing region, referred to as the k-space gap, is responsible for image blurring and loss of low-spatial-frequency information.

In conventional PETRA, this gap is filled by performing additional single-point imaging (SPI) acquisitions, wherein the gradient amplitude is stepped through a series of discrete values to sample individual k-space points at the origin. The number of required SPI points scales linearly with the size of the k-space gap, which in turn depends on both t_s and BW_ex. Thus, any attempt to increase BW_ex to reduce blurring from off-resonance or T₂* decay paradoxically enlarges the k-space gap, necessitating longer SPI acquisition times and reducing overall scan efficiency.

The present invention overcomes this dilemma through a gradient modulation (GM) scheme. After the T/R switching delay and once the receiver gate is opened, the gradient amplitude is not held constant but instead is ramped upward from the initial value G_ex to a higher maximum value G_max over a controlled time interval. This ramp is executed with a predetermined slew rate—preferably limited to no more than 50% of the scanner’s maximum slew rate (e.g., 100 mT/m/ms on a system with 200 mT/m/ms capability)—to suppress eddy currents and associated image distortions. Following the ramp, a short flat-gradient delay period, denoted t_d, is inserted; during this interval, the gradient remains at G_max, and the system acquires the central k-space points via SPI, just as in conventional PETRA. However, because the excitation bandwidth remains low (dictated by G_ex), the size of the k-space gap is minimized, thereby reducing the number of SPI acquisitions needed.

During the gradient ramp and subsequent flat-readout period, k-space is traversed radially but at an accelerating rate due to the increasing gradient amplitude. This results in a non-uniform sampling density in k-space, with higher density near the center (during the slower initial phase) and lower density in the periphery (during the faster high-gradient phase). To compensate for this variable density and avoid streaking or blurring artifacts in the reconstructed image, an iterative density correction algorithm is applied during reconstruction. This correction weights each k-space sample according to the local sampling density, effectively normalizing the contribution of each data point. The corrected k-space data are then reconstructed into a three-dimensional image using a non-uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT), which accurately handles the non-Cartesian, time-varying k-space trajectory induced by the gradient modulation.

The key innovation lies in the decoupling of excitation and readout bandwidths. While BW_ex is determined solely by G_ex and remains low to satisfy SAR and RF power constraints, the effective readout bandwidth (BW_read) is governed by G_max and can be set independently to a much higher value—e.g., 125 kHz or 200 kHz compared to an excitation bandwidth of 60 kHz. A higher BW_read reduces the time required to traverse a given segment of k-space, thereby minimizing the impact of off-resonance frequency offsets (Δf) on spatial encoding. Specifically, the spatial displacement error due to off-resonance is proportional to Δf / BW_read; thus, doubling the readout bandwidth halves the blurring artifact. Similarly, for tissues with very short T₂* (e.g., 200 microseconds), a faster k-space traversal ensures that more signal is captured before it decays, preserving contrast and structural detail.

Experimental validation was performed on a 7 Tesla MRI scanner equipped with a multi-channel head coil. Imaging of a breast phantom containing fat (off-resonance at +1050 Hz) and water compartments demonstrated that GM-PETRA with a 60-kHz excitation bandwidth and 125-kHz readout bandwidth produced off-resonance artifacts comparable to conventional PETRA with a 120-kHz excitation/readout bandwidth, but without exceeding SAR limits. Moreover, fine structures between fat compartments, previously obscured by blurring, became clearly visible. In apple imaging, which models short-T₂* tissues due to air-filled intercellular spaces, GM-PETRA revealed intricate mesocarp fibers invisible to both conventional PETRA and 3D GRE, the latter of which suffered complete signal loss at its minimum TE of 2.8 ms. Inner ear imaging in a healthy volunteer further confirmed the clinical utility: GM-PETRA resolved delicate structures such as the cochlea and semicircular canals with unprecedented clarity, whereas PETRA exhibited severe blurring from air-tissue susceptibility interfaces, and 3D GRE failed to depict short-T₂* nerves.

The method is fully compatible with standard clinical MRI hardware. No modifications to the RF amplifier, gradient coils, or T/R switch are required. The only implementation changes involve pulse sequence programming to incorporate the gradient ramp and associated timing adjustments. Acoustic noise, though slightly elevated compared to silent PETRA due to gradient switching, remains within acceptable levels when slew rates are moderated. Additionally, the technique can be combined with advanced reconstruction methods such as compressed sensing to further enhance image quality or accelerate acquisition by exploiting sparsity in transform domains (e.g., wavelet or total variation).

In conclusion, the gradient-modulated PETRA technique of the present invention provides a practical, efficient, and high-fidelity solution to the long-standing problem of image blurring in UTE MRI. By enabling high readout bandwidths without increasing excitation bandwidth, it simultaneously improves spatial resolution, preserves short-T₂* signal, and complies with safety and hardware constraints—thereby expanding the diagnostic potential of MRI in challenging anatomical and pathological contexts.
