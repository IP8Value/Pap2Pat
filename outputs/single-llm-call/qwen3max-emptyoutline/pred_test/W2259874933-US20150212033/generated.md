# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of capacitive sensing technology, and more particularly to multi-channel capacitive sensor arrays that utilize frequency-domain multiplexing through resonant structures to enable simultaneous, independent detection of proximity or touch events at multiple spatial locations. The invention further pertains to sensor architectures in which each capacitive sensing element is coupled with a distinct inductive component—such as surface-mounted inductors, on-chip meander inductors, or metamaterial-based split-ring resonators—to form individual LC resonators operating at unique, non-overlapping resonant frequencies within a shared transmission line. This configuration allows for the decoupling of sensing channels in the frequency domain, thereby enabling high-resolution, multi-point detection without the need for time-division multiplexing or complex switching circuitry. The invention finds application in advanced human-machine interfaces, industrial automation, medical diagnostics, security systems, robotics, and any domain requiring spatially resolved, contactless sensing with enhanced signal isolation and sensitivity.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Capacitive sensing has long been employed as a reliable and cost-effective method for detecting the presence, position, or motion of objects based on changes in capacitance induced by the dielectric properties or conductive characteristics of a target in proximity to a sensor electrode. Traditional capacitive sensors operate by applying a fixed-frequency excitation signal—typically an alternating current at a single radio frequency (RF) or intermediate frequency—and monitoring the amplitude, phase, or impedance of the resulting response. When an object approaches the sensor, it alters the local electric field distribution, thereby changing the effective capacitance between the sensor electrode and a reference ground plane. This change is then converted into a measurable electrical signal, which can be processed to infer the occurrence of a touch or proximity event. While such single-frequency systems are simple to implement and widely used in consumer electronics (e.g., smartphone touchscreens), industrial proximity switches, and automotive occupancy detection, they suffer from significant limitations in terms of functionality and information content. Specifically, conventional capacitive sensors provide only binary or scalar output—indicating the mere presence or absence of an object—without the ability to distinguish between different types of interactions, multiple simultaneous events, or spatially distributed stimuli across an array of sensing elements.

To address these shortcomings, researchers have explored the use of capacitive sensor arrays, wherein multiple discrete sensing electrodes are arranged in a planar or three-dimensional configuration. In most existing implementations, these arrays rely on time-division multiplexing (TDM) to sequentially interrogate each sensor element, thereby avoiding cross-talk between channels. However, TDM inherently limits the temporal resolution of the system, as the total sampling period increases linearly with the number of sensors. Moreover, the switching circuitry required for TDM introduces additional parasitic capacitances and resistances, which can degrade signal integrity and reduce overall sensitivity. Alternative approaches based on frequency-division multiplexing (FDM) have been proposed to overcome these limitations by assigning a unique operating frequency to each sensor channel, allowing all elements to be interrogated simultaneously. Early FDM-based capacitive sensors often employed fixed-frequency oscillators or narrowband filters tuned to predetermined frequencies, but these designs lacked the dynamic range and adaptability needed for robust real-world operation, particularly in environments with electromagnetic interference or varying environmental conditions.

A more sophisticated strategy involves embedding each capacitive sensing element within a resonant circuit, typically an inductor-capacitor (LC) tank, whose natural resonant frequency is determined by the values of inductance (L) and capacitance (C) according to the well-known relation \( f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \). In such resonant capacitive sensors, the presence of a proximate object alters the effective capacitance C, thereby shifting the resonant frequency \( f_c \). This frequency shift can be detected either by sweeping the excitation frequency across a band and identifying the new resonance peak, or by monitoring the transmission or reflection coefficient (e.g., S-parameters) at a fixed frequency near the nominal resonance. The key advantage of this approach lies in its inherent signal amplification: small changes in capacitance produce relatively large shifts in resonant frequency or pronounced changes in impedance magnitude near resonance, thereby enhancing sensitivity. Furthermore, when multiple LC resonators are integrated into a common feed structure—such as a microstrip transmission line—each tuned to a distinct resonant frequency, the resulting system functions as a multi-channel sensor array wherein each channel is spectrally isolated from the others.

Despite these conceptual advantages, practical implementation of resonant capacitive sensor arrays has been hindered by several technical challenges. Chief among these is the issue of resonance coupling and spectral overlap, which occurs when the quality factor (Q-factor) of the individual resonators is too low. The Q-factor, defined as \( Q = f_c / \delta f \), where \( \delta f \) is the half-power bandwidth of the resonance, quantifies the sharpness and selectivity of the resonant peak. Low-Q resonators exhibit broad frequency responses, causing their stopbands or passbands to overlap significantly in the frequency domain. This overlap leads to cross-talk between channels, making it difficult to attribute a measured signal change to a specific sensor element and introducing uncertainty in data interpretation. Consequently, much of the recent research in resonant sensing has focused on developing high-Q resonant structures, particularly through the use of advanced materials, optimized geometries, and novel electromagnetic designs.

Metamaterials—artificially engineered structures that exhibit electromagnetic properties not found in nature—have emerged as a promising platform for achieving high-Q resonances in compact form factors. Among the most widely studied metamaterial elements are split-ring resonators (SRRs), which consist of conductive rings with one or more gaps that support strong localized electric fields and magnetic dipole moments at microwave frequencies. SRRs have been successfully employed in various sensing applications due to their ability to concentrate electromagnetic energy in sub-wavelength volumes, thereby enhancing sensitivity to minute changes in the surrounding dielectric environment. However, conventional SRR-based sensors typically require the target analyte to be placed directly within the high-field gap region, limiting their utility for non-contact or large-area proximity sensing. Moreover, while SRRs can achieve high Q-factors (often exceeding 50 in optimized designs), integrating them into scalable, multi-element arrays with independent frequency addressing remains a non-trivial engineering challenge.

Another limitation of prior art resonant capacitive arrays is the reliance on discrete, surface-mounted inductive components, which introduce parasitic effects during assembly (e.g., solder joints, lead inductance) and complicate miniaturization and mass production. Although on-chip inductors—fabricated directly on printed circuit boards (PCBs) using meander or spiral traces—offer a path toward monolithic integration, they typically suffer from lower Q-factors due to ohmic losses in the conductive traces and dielectric losses in the substrate material. As a result, there exists a critical need for a capacitive sensor array architecture that combines the spatial resolution of multi-element layouts with the spectral isolation of high-Q resonances, while also enabling flexible, scalable, and manufacturable implementation across a range of form factors—from linear strips to three-dimensional configurations.

The present invention addresses these unmet needs by introducing a novel class of multi-channel capacitive sensor arrays in which each sensing element is uniquely paired with a tailored inductive structure—selected from surface-mounted inductors, on-chip meander inductors, or metamaterial-based split-ring resonators—to establish spectrally isolated LC resonances within a shared microstrip feed line. By carefully designing the inductance values and resonance quality factors, the invention ensures that perturbations in one sensor channel (e.g., due to an approaching object) produce detectable changes only in the corresponding frequency band, without affecting other channels. This enables true parallel interrogation of all sensor elements, supports simultaneous multi-object detection, and provides a pathway to high-density sensor integration within limited spectral bandwidths. Furthermore, the invention demonstrates that the choice of inductive element directly governs the achievable Q-factor and, consequently, the number of resolvable channels per unit bandwidth, thereby offering a design trade-off between performance, complexity, and cost.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a multi-channel capacitive sensor array comprising a plurality of capacitive sensing elements, each operatively coupled to a distinct inductive component to form a set of LC resonators, wherein each resonator is configured to exhibit a unique resonant frequency within a predetermined operational bandwidth. These resonators are integrated into a common transmission line structure—preferably a microstrip line—such that the entire array is excited by a single broadband RF signal, and the response is analyzed in the frequency domain to independently monitor changes in each sensing channel. The core innovation lies in the deliberate spectral separation of the resonant frequencies through precise tuning of the inductive components, combined with the enhancement of resonance quality factors to minimize inter-channel interference. This architecture eliminates the need for time-division multiplexing or complex switching networks, thereby enabling real-time, parallel detection of multiple proximity or touch events at distinct spatial locations.

In one embodiment, the inductive components are implemented as discrete surface-mounted inductors, each selected to provide a specific inductance value that, in combination with the intrinsic capacitance of its associated sensing patch, yields a target resonant frequency. The sensing patches may be fabricated as copper films on a dielectric substrate, arranged in various geometric configurations—including linear arrays, planar grids, or three-dimensional polyhedral structures—to suit different application requirements. Experimental validation demonstrates that when an object approaches one sensing patch, only the resonant frequency corresponding to that patch shifts, while all other resonances remain unaffected, confirming effective channel decoupling.

In another embodiment, the inductive components are realized as on-chip structures—such as meander-shaped or spiral inductors—patterned directly onto the same printed circuit board as the capacitive patches and transmission line. This monolithic approach reduces parasitic effects associated with discrete components and simplifies manufacturing, albeit at the cost of moderately reduced Q-factors due to conductor and substrate losses. Nevertheless, simulations and measurements confirm that sufficient spectral isolation is maintained for practical multi-channel operation.

In a third and preferred embodiment, the inductive components are implemented using metamaterial-inspired split-ring resonators (SRRs) that are electromagnetically coupled—but not directly connected—to the microstrip feed line. Each SRR is paired with a capacitive sensing patch, forming a hybrid resonant structure that leverages the strong field localization and high Q-factor inherent to metamaterial designs. Fabricated prototypes using this approach achieve Q-factors on the order of 90—significantly higher than the 5–12 range observed with surface-mounted inductors—thereby enabling denser packing of resonant channels within a given frequency band. For instance, while a four-element array using discrete inductors occupies a fractional bandwidth exceeding 100% (spanning 250 MHz to 850 MHz), an equivalent SRR-coupled array operates within a narrow 12% fractional bandwidth (2.6 GHz to 3.3 GHz), illustrating the dramatic improvement in spectral efficiency.

The invention further encompasses methods for detecting proximity events by either tracking shifts in resonant frequency or monitoring changes in transmission coefficient magnitude at fixed frequencies near resonance. Both approaches are shown to provide sub-millimeter spatial resolution and sensing ranges on the order of 10 mm for typical dielectric targets (e.g., wood), with performance metrics—quantified via a figure-of-merit defined as the ratio of corrected sensing range to effective sensor dimension—that exceed those of commercially available proximity sensors. Additionally, the system is capable of simultaneous multi-object detection, as demonstrated by experiments in which two adjacent sensors respond independently when loaded concurrently.

Overall, the invention establishes a versatile and scalable framework for capacitive sensing that transcends the limitations of conventional single-frequency or time-multiplexed arrays. By harnessing the principles of resonant frequency multiplexing and high-Q metamaterial design, it enables new capabilities in spatially resolved, contactless interaction detection with applications spanning consumer electronics, industrial automation, healthcare monitoring, and beyond.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention will now be described in detail with reference to specific embodiments, which are provided for illustrative purposes and should not be construed as limiting the scope of the claims. It will be understood by those skilled in the art that various modifications, substitutions, and combinations of features may be made without departing from the spirit and scope of the invention.

In accordance with the invention, a multi-channel capacitive sensor array is constructed by integrating multiple capacitive sensing elements into a common microstrip transmission line, wherein each sensing element is associated with a unique inductive component to form an LC resonator tuned to a distinct resonant frequency. The fundamental operating principle relies on the fact that the resonant frequency \( f_c \) of an LC circuit is inversely proportional to the square root of the product of inductance \( L \) and capacitance \( C \), as given by \( f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} \). In the absence of external perturbations, each resonator exhibits a characteristic stopband or notch in the transmission spectrum of the microstrip line at its designated \( f_c \). When a dielectric or conductive object approaches a particular sensing patch, the effective capacitance \( C \) of that patch increases due to electrostatic coupling, thereby causing a downward shift in its resonant frequency. This shift manifests as a displacement of the corresponding notch in the frequency domain, which can be detected using standard RF measurement techniques.

The physical layout of the sensor array may vary depending on the intended application. In a first exemplary embodiment, a three-dimensional multi-directional array is fabricated by assembling four identical capacitive sensing patches onto the faces of a cubic structure. Each patch consists of a circular copper film (diameter approximately 18 mm) deposited on the top layer of a three-layer printed circuit board (PCB) with FR-4 as the dielectric substrate. A ground plane is situated in the middle layer, separated from the top sensing layer by a 1.2-mm thick dielectric, and from the bottom layer—containing the 50-Ω microstrip feed line—by a 0.3-mm dielectric. Electrical connection between each patch and the feed line is established via a plated through-hole via. Surface-mounted inductors with nominal values of 100 nH, 68 nH, 33 nH, and 10 nH are soldered between each patch and its respective via, thereby defining four LC resonators with calculated resonant frequencies of approximately 174 MHz, 211 MHz, 303 MHz, and 550 MHz, respectively. Although actual measured resonances deviate slightly from these ideal values due to parasitic effects and transmission line loading, the spectral separation remains sufficient to prevent overlap.

Experimental testing of this cubic array is performed using a vector network analyzer (VNA) to measure the transmission coefficient \( S_{21} \) across a frequency sweep from 100 MHz to 600 MHz. A wooden block (relative permittivity ≈ 2) is positioned at varying distances (2 mm to 20 mm in 2-mm increments) in front of each of the four sensor faces. Results show that when the block approaches one face, only the resonance associated with that face shifts to a lower frequency, while the other three resonances remain stable. For example, approaching Sensor 1 causes the 174-MHz notch to shift, but leaves the 211-MHz, 303-MHz, and 550-MHz notches unchanged. This confirms that the channels are effectively decoupled in the frequency domain. Furthermore, by selecting a fixed monitoring frequency near each resonance (e.g., 170 MHz for Sensor 1), the magnitude of \( S_{21} \) at that frequency varies monotonically with object distance, enabling distance estimation with sub-millimeter resolution within a 10-mm sensing range.

In a second embodiment, a linear sensor array is implemented on a two-layer FR-4 PCB (1.6 mm thick). Four square capacitive patches (10 mm × 10 mm) are aligned along a straight microstrip line, each connected in series with a surface-mounted inductor of values 100 nH, 68 nH, 33 nH, and 10 nH, respectively. Full-wave electromagnetic simulation using Advanced Design System (ADS) software predicts four sharp, non-overlapping resonances in the transmission spectrum. Fabrication and measurement confirm these predictions: when a wooden target is placed 5 mm above one patch, only the corresponding resonance is perturbed. Difference plots (loaded minus baseline) reveal attenuation changes exceeding 3.5 dB at the affected resonance, with negligible variation elsewhere. Critically, the array successfully distinguishes simultaneous loading of two adjacent sensors—for instance, Sensors 1 and 2—by showing independent shifts in their respective frequency bands, demonstrating true multi-object detection capability.

A third and preferred embodiment replaces the discrete inductors with metamaterial-based split-ring resonators (SRRs). Each SRR consists of a single-turn copper ring with a narrow gap, positioned adjacent to a capacitive patch and electromagnetically coupled to the microstrip line without direct electrical contact. The SRR-patch pair forms a hybrid resonant mode whose frequency is determined by both the patch capacitance and the SRR’s effective inductance. Due to the strong field confinement in the SRR gap and reduced ohmic losses compared to lumped inductors, this configuration achieves a Q-factor of approximately 90—nearly an order of magnitude higher than the discrete-inductor version. Consequently, four resonances are packed into a narrow 700-MHz band (2.6–3.3 GHz), corresponding to a fractional bandwidth of only 12%. Measurements again confirm channel independence: object proximity affects only the targeted resonance.

The choice of inductive implementation offers a design trade-off. Surface-mounted inductors provide simplicity and ease of prototyping but suffer from moderate Q-factors (5–12) and assembly-induced parasitics. On-chip meander inductors enable full PCB integration and eliminate soldering but exhibit even lower Q due to trace resistance. SRR-coupled designs deliver superior spectral efficiency and sensitivity but require more complex layout and fabrication. All three approaches, however, adhere to the core inventive concept of frequency-domain channel isolation through resonant multiplexing.

Signal processing may employ either frequency-sweeping or fixed-frequency monitoring. In the former, a processor analyzes the full \( S_{21} \) spectrum to detect resonance shifts, suitable for applications requiring high accuracy. In the latter, dedicated detectors at pre-selected frequencies provide lower-cost, real-time output, ideal for embedded systems. The sensing range and resolution are influenced by the Q-factor, patch size, and target dielectric properties, but consistently outperform commercial sensors when normalized by a figure-of-merit (FOM = corrected sensing range / effective sensor dimension).

Thus, the invention provides a robust, scalable platform for multi-point capacitive sensing that overcomes the limitations of prior art through innovative use of resonant frequency multiplexing and high-Q metamaterial design.
