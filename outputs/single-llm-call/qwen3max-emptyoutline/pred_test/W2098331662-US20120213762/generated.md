# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Pompe disease, also known as glycogen storage disease type II, is a rare and often fatal autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the gene encoding acid α-glucosidase (GAA), an enzyme essential for the degradation of glycogen within lysosomes. In the absence or deficiency of functional GAA, glycogen accumulates progressively in the lysosomes of various tissues, particularly in cardiac and skeletal muscle, leading to severe myopathy, cardiomyopathy, respiratory insufficiency, and, in the most severe infantile form, death within the first year of life if left untreated. The disease manifests along a clinical spectrum, ranging from the rapidly progressive infantile-onset form to the more slowly progressive late-onset forms that can present in childhood or adulthood. Despite advances in supportive care, the underlying pathophysiology remains unaddressed without targeted therapeutic intervention.

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) has emerged as the primary therapeutic strategy for Pompe disease. The current standard of care involves intravenous administration of recombinant human GAA (rhGAA), marketed as alglucosidase alfa. This therapy relies on the natural cellular uptake mechanism mediated by the cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (CI-MPR), which recognizes mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) residues appended to N-linked glycans on the recombinant enzyme during its biosynthesis in mammalian expression systems, typically Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells. Following binding to CI-MPR on the cell surface, the enzyme-receptor complex is internalized via clathrin-coated pits and trafficked through the endosomal system to the lysosome, where the enzyme is released to degrade accumulated glycogen.

However, this M6P-dependent targeting strategy suffers from several critical limitations that compromise its therapeutic efficacy. First, the efficiency of M6P modification is inherently variable and highly dependent on the specific cell line, culture conditions, and purification processes used during manufacturing. Glycosylation is a complex, non-templated post-translational modification, and subtle changes in bioreactor parameters can lead to significant batch-to-batch heterogeneity in the number, position, and phosphorylation status of glycans on the final protein product. This variability directly impacts the affinity of the enzyme for the CI-MPR and, consequently, its cellular uptake and biodistribution. Indeed, regulatory agencies have noted differences in the pharmacokinetic and tissue distribution profiles of rhGAA produced at different manufacturing scales, attributing these differences to alterations in glycosylation patterns, which necessitated the submission of a new biological license application.

Second, the M6P content on current rhGAA therapeutics is suboptimal. The native GAA protein is poorly phosphorylated even in its wild-type form, and recombinant versions often display insufficient M6P residues to achieve high-affinity binding to the CI-MPR. This results in a large fraction of the administered dose being cleared from circulation by non-specific mechanisms, such as uptake by the liver via the mannose receptor, rather than being delivered to the critical target tissues—skeletal and cardiac muscle. Consequently, very high doses of the enzyme are required to achieve a modest therapeutic effect, which increases the cost of therapy and the risk of immunogenic reactions.

Third, the CI-MPR itself is a multifunctional receptor with at least two distinct ligand-binding sites: one for M6P-bearing glycoproteins and another for insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II). While the M6P-binding site is used for lysosomal enzyme trafficking, the IGF-II binding site plays a role in the regulation of IGF-II bioavailability and signaling. The existence of this high-affinity, non-glycan-dependent binding site presents an alternative and potentially superior route for targeting lysosomal enzymes to the CI-MPR. IGF-II binds to its site on the CI-MPR with a much higher affinity (in the low nanomolar range) than M6P-bearing enzymes, which typically bind in the micromolar range. Exploiting this high-affinity interaction could dramatically improve the efficiency of cellular uptake and lysosomal delivery.

In response to these challenges, a novel glycosylation-independent lysosomal targeting (GILT) technology was developed. This approach involves the genetic fusion of a peptide tag derived from IGF-II to the lysosomal enzyme of interest. The GILT tag contains the minimal structural elements of IGF-II necessary for high-affinity binding to the CI-MPR’s IGF-II binding site, thereby bypassing the need for M6P modification altogether. This strategy decouples the targeting function from the unpredictable and variable process of glycosylation, offering a more robust, consistent, and efficient method for delivering therapeutic enzymes to the lysosome. The invention described herein represents the successful application of this GILT technology to GAA, resulting in a novel chimeric enzyme, designated BMN 701, with significantly enhanced therapeutic potential for the treatment of Pompe disease.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel chimeric polypeptide for the treatment of Pompe disease, comprising a human acid α-glucosidase (GAA) enzyme fused to a glycosylation-independent lysosomal targeting (GILT) tag. The GILT tag is a peptide sequence derived from insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II) that confers high-affinity binding to the cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (CI-MPR) independent of any glycan-based modifications. This engineered chimeric enzyme, referred to herein as BMN 701, exhibits dramatically improved cellular uptake and lysosomal delivery compared to conventional recombinant human GAA (rhGAA) used in current enzyme replacement therapies.

In one embodiment, the invention is a chimeric polypeptide comprising an amino acid sequence of human GAA, wherein the GAA sequence is fused at its N-terminus to a GILT tag. The GILT tag comprises at least the amino acid residues 8 to 67 of mature human IGF-II, and may further include the native IGF-II signal peptide at its N-terminus to facilitate proper secretion from the host cell. A short spacer peptide, typically three amino acids in length, may be interposed between the GILT tag and the GAA sequence to ensure optimal folding and function of both domains. The resulting chimeric polypeptide retains the full enzymatic activity of wild-type GAA while gaining the ability to bind to the CI-MPR with high affinity via the IGF-II-derived GILT tag.

The invention further provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising the chimeric polypeptide of the invention in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier. This composition is formulated for parenteral administration, such as intravenous infusion, for use in enzyme replacement therapy. The invention also encompasses a method of treating Pompe disease in a subject in need thereof, which comprises administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of the chimeric polypeptide or the pharmaceutical composition of the invention. The enhanced binding affinity of the chimeric enzyme for the CI-MPR translates to a significantly greater efficiency in clearing pathological glycogen accumulations from affected tissues, including skeletal and cardiac muscle, as demonstrated in a preclinical mouse model of Pompe disease. The data show that the chimeric enzyme is at least five-fold more potent than conventional rhGAA in reducing tissue glycogen levels, allowing for lower dosing regimens or substantially improved clinical outcomes at equivalent doses.

## DEFINITIONS

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms are defined as follows:

“Acid α-glucosidase” or “GAA” refers to a lysosomal enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of terminal α-1,4 and α-1,6 linkages in glycogen and maltose, releasing glucose. The term encompasses the full-length human GAA polypeptide, as well as any biologically active fragment or variant thereof that retains the core enzymatic function. The canonical human GAA protein consists of 952 amino acids, including a signal peptide that is cleaved during maturation. In the context of the present invention, the GAA sequence used in the chimeric construct typically begins at or around amino acid 70 of the full-length precursor, corresponding to the mature, processed form of the enzyme.

“Cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor” or “CI-MPR” refers to a large, multifunctional transmembrane receptor protein, also known as the IGF-II/M6P receptor. It is responsible for the trafficking of newly synthesized lysosomal enzymes from the trans-Golgi network to the endosomal-lysosomal system. The CI-MPR possesses at least 15 extracellular domains, with domains 1-3 and 7-9 containing binding sites for mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) and domains 10-11 and 13-15 containing binding sites for insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II). The receptor is expressed on the cell surface, where it can also mediate the endocytosis of extracellular lysosomal enzymes bearing M6P or ligands like IGF-II.

“Glycosylation-independent lysosomal targeting tag” or “GILT tag” refers to a peptide sequence derived from IGF-II that is capable of binding to the IGF-II binding site(s) on the CI-MPR with high affinity, thereby directing a fused protein to the lysosome without requiring any glycan-based modifications such as M6P. The GILT tag of the present invention specifically comprises a contiguous sequence of amino acids from the mature human IGF-II protein, preferably from residue 8 to residue 67. It may optionally include the native IGF-II signal peptide at its N-terminus to direct the nascent chimeric polypeptide into the secretory pathway of the host cell.

“Chimeric polypeptide” refers to a single polypeptide chain created by the genetic fusion of two or more distinct protein or peptide domains that are not naturally contiguous in a single protein. In the present invention, the chimeric polypeptide is formed by fusing the GILT tag to the N-terminus of the GAA enzyme.

“Pompe disease” refers to a genetic disorder caused by a deficiency in the activity of the acid α-glucosidase enzyme, leading to the pathological accumulation of glycogen in the lysosomes of various cell types, primarily muscle cells. It is also known as glycogen storage disease type II (GSD II).

“Enzyme replacement therapy” or “ERT” refers to a medical treatment in which a missing or deficient enzyme is administered to a patient to compensate for the genetic defect causing the enzyme’s absence or dysfunction.

“Pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” refers to a diluent, excipient, or vehicle that is compatible with the other ingredients of a pharmaceutical formulation and is not deleterious to the recipient. Such carriers are well known in the art and include, for example, sterile water, saline, phosphate-buffered saline, and various buffers at physiological pH.

“Therapeutically effective amount” refers to an amount of the chimeric polypeptide that is sufficient to produce a desired therapeutic effect in a subject, such as a reduction in lysosomal glycogen storage in muscle tissues, improvement in muscle strength, or stabilization of cardiac function, without causing unacceptable side effects.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Lysosomal Enzymes

Lysosomal enzymes are a class of hydrolytic enzymes that reside within the lysosome, an organelle responsible for the degradation and recycling of macromolecules. These enzymes function optimally in the acidic environment of the lysosome (pH ~4.5-5.0) and are essential for breaking down complex carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids into their basic building blocks. The correct targeting of these enzymes from their site of synthesis in the endoplasmic reticulum to the lysosome is a critical cellular process. In humans, the primary mechanism for this targeting is the addition of a mannose 6-phosphate (M6P) recognition marker to N-linked oligosaccharides on the nascent enzyme in the cis-Golgi network. This M6P tag is then recognized by the cation-independent mannose 6-phosphate receptor (CI-MPR) in the trans-Golgi network, which packages the enzyme into clathrin-coated vesicles for transport to late endosomes. The acidic pH of the endosome causes the enzyme to dissociate from the receptor, which recycles back to the Golgi or the cell surface, while the enzyme is delivered to the lysosome. Defects in either the enzyme itself or in the M6P tagging machinery can lead to lysosomal storage diseases, a group of over 50 inherited metabolic disorders characterized by the accumulation of undigested substrates within the lysosome, causing progressive cellular damage and organ dysfunction.

### Pompe Disease

Pompe disease is a prototypical lysosomal storage disease caused by mutations in the GAA gene located on chromosome 17q25.3. These mutations result in a quantitative or qualitative deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme acid α-glucosidase (GAA; EC 3.2.1.20). GAA is the sole enzyme responsible for the degradation of glycogen within the lysosome. In its absence, glycogen cannot be broken down and accumulates to toxic levels, primarily within the lysosomes of cardiac and skeletal muscle fibers. This accumulation leads to massive vacuolization of the muscle cells, disruption of the contractile apparatus, and progressive muscle weakness. The disease is classified into two main forms based on age of onset and rate of progression: the classic infantile-onset form, which presents within the first few months of life with profound hypotonia (“floppy baby” syndrome), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and respiratory failure, and is universally fatal by one year of age if untreated; and the late-onset form, which can present from childhood to late adulthood, is characterized by progressive proximal limb-girdle weakness and respiratory insufficiency, but without significant cardiac involvement. The current standard of care, enzyme replacement therapy with alglucosidase alfa, has significantly improved survival and cardiac outcomes in infantile patients and has stabilized or slowed disease progression in some late-onset patients. However, the therapy is not curative, and many patients, especially those with advanced skeletal muscle disease, show a suboptimal response, highlighting the need for a more effective therapeutic agent.

### Enzyme Replacement Therapy

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) is a well-established treatment paradigm for several lysosomal storage diseases, including Gaucher disease, Fabry disease, and Pompe disease. The principle of ERT is to administer a functional, recombinant version of the deficient enzyme to the patient, typically via intravenous infusion. The recombinant enzyme is taken up by cells throughout the body, trafficked to the lysosome, and restores the missing enzymatic activity, thereby clearing the accumulated substrate. The success of ERT is critically dependent on the efficiency of the enzyme's uptake into the target cells. For most lysosomal enzymes, including GAA, this uptake is mediated by the CI-MPR, which recognizes the M6P residues on the enzyme's glycan chains. The recombinant enzyme is produced in mammalian cell lines, such as CHO cells, which possess the necessary machinery to perform human-like glycosylation and, to a limited extent, M6P modification. However, the process of generating a recombinant enzyme with a high density of M6P residues is technically challenging and inefficient. The resulting product often has a heterogeneous glycan profile with a low proportion of high-affinity, multi-phosphorylated glycans. This inefficiency in targeting is a major reason why ERT for Pompe disease requires very high doses (20 mg/kg every other week) and is associated with high costs and variable patient responses. The invention described herein addresses this fundamental limitation by providing a recombinant GAA enzyme that utilizes a completely different, high-affinity, and glycosylation-independent mechanism for CI-MPR binding and cellular uptake.

### Glycosylation Independent Lysosomal Targeting

Glycosylation Independent Lysosomal Targeting (GILT) is a proprietary protein engineering technology designed to overcome the limitations of M6P-dependent targeting. The GILT strategy is based on the discovery that the CI-MPR has a second, high-affinity binding site for the protein hormone insulin-like growth factor II (IGF-II). IGF-II binds to this site with a dissociation constant (Kd) in the low nanomolar range, which is orders of magnitude tighter than the micromolar affinity of the receptor for M6P. The GILT technology involves the identification and isolation of the minimal peptide sequence from IGF-II that is necessary and sufficient for high-affinity binding to the CI-MPR. This peptide sequence, the GILT tag, is then genetically fused to the N-terminus of a lysosomal enzyme of interest. The resulting chimeric protein is secreted by the host cell and, upon administration, binds to the CI-MPR on the surface of target cells via the GILT tag. This binding event triggers the same endocytic and trafficking machinery as the natural M6P-dependent pathway, leading to the efficient delivery of the enzyme to the lysosome. Because the GILT tag is a defined amino acid sequence, its presence and function are not subject to the variability inherent in glycosylation processes. This results in a recombinant enzyme product with a consistent, high-affinity targeting capability, leading to dramatically improved cellular uptake and therapeutic efficacy.

### Furin-Resistant GILT Tag

A critical consideration in the design of a GILT-tagged enzyme is the stability of the tag during biosynthesis and secretion. The GILT tag is derived from IGF-II, a protein that is itself processed by proteases, including the proprotein convertase furin, during its maturation. If the junction between the GILT tag and the therapeutic enzyme is a substrate for furin or other proteases in the secretory pathway, the tag could be cleaved off before the protein is secreted, rendering the enzyme unable to bind to the CI-MPR via the IGF-II site. To prevent this, the GILT tag used in the present invention is engineered to be resistant to furin cleavage. This is achieved by carefully selecting the precise C-terminal boundary of the IGF-II-derived sequence and, if necessary, by introducing point mutations at the fusion junction to eliminate any consensus furin cleavage sites (typically, the motif R-X-R/K-R). The furin-resistant GILT tag ensures that the chimeric enzyme is secreted as a single, intact polypeptide chain, with the GILT tag fully available to mediate high-affinity binding to the CI-MPR on the target cell surface. This engineering step is crucial for the consistent production and predictable in vivo performance of the therapeutic agent.

### Binding Affinity for the Insulin Receptor

While the GILT tag is designed to bind specifically to the IGF-II binding site on the CI-MPR, it is essential to ensure that it does not cross-react with other members of the insulin/IGF signaling family, particularly the insulin receptor (IR) and the type 1 IGF receptor (IGF-1R). Activation of these tyrosine kinase receptors by their cognate ligands (insulin, IGF-I, IGF-II) triggers potent mitogenic and metabolic signaling cascades, including the PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways. Unintended activation of these pathways by a therapeutic enzyme could lead to serious off-target effects, such as hypoglycemia or stimulation of cell proliferation. Therefore, the GILT tag of the present invention is specifically engineered to have high selectivity for the CI-MPR over the IR and IGF-1R. The selected IGF-II-derived sequence (residues 8-67) contains the key structural elements for CI-MPR binding but lacks the full complement of residues required for high-affinity interaction with the signaling receptors. Surface plasmon resonance and cell-based signaling assays have confirmed that the chimeric enzyme BMN 701 binds to the CI-MPR with high affinity (Kd in the low nanomolar range) but shows no significant binding or activation of the insulin receptor or IGF-1R at therapeutically relevant concentrations. This high degree of specificity is a critical safety feature of the invention.

### Mutagenesis

The creation of the chimeric polypeptide of the present invention was accomplished through the techniques of molecular biology and site-directed mutagenesis. The DNA sequence encoding the human GAA enzyme (amino acids 1-952) was obtained from a cDNA clone. A second DNA cassette was constructed that encodes the GILT tag, which includes the IGF-II signal peptide, followed by IGF-II residues 8-67, a short three-amino acid spacer (e.g., Gly-Gly-Gly), and then the GAA sequence starting from Ala-70. This design places the GILT tag at the extreme N-terminus of the mature enzyme, a location that was found to be permissive for both the proper folding and secretion of the chimeric protein and the retention of full GAA enzymatic activity. Initial attempts to fuse the GILT tag to the C-terminus of GAA resulted in a misfolded, enzymatically inactive protein, demonstrating the importance of the N-terminal fusion strategy. The DNA cassettes were cloned into mammalian expression vectors, such as pCEP4, under the control of a strong promoter (e.g., CMV). The resulting plasmids, p635 (for untagged GAA) and p701 (for GILT-tagged GAA), were used to transiently transfect human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells for initial characterization. For large-scale production, the coding sequence from p701 was transferred into a GPEx retrovector expression system for stable integration into the genome of Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells, creating a high-producing master cell bank for the manufacture of the clinical candidate, BMN 701.

### Spacer

The inclusion of a short peptide spacer between the GILT tag and the GAA enzyme domain is a key element of the invention’s design. The spacer serves to provide physical separation and conformational flexibility between the two functional domains of the chimeric protein. Without a spacer, the close proximity of the bulky GILT tag to the N-terminus of GAA could sterically hinder the proper folding of the enzyme’s active site or interfere with its catalytic function. The spacer also helps to ensure that the GILT tag is fully exposed and accessible for binding to the CI-MPR. In the preferred embodiment of the invention, the spacer is a tripeptide sequence, such as Gly-Gly-Gly or Ala-Ala-Ala. These sequences are chosen because they are small, flexible, and unlikely to introduce any unwanted secondary structure or immunogenic epitopes. Experimental data confirmed that the chimeric enzyme with the spacer (BMN 701) retained enzymatic specific activity and kinetic parameters (Km, Vmax) that were indistinguishable from those of the untagged, wild-type GAA, demonstrating that the spacer successfully decoupled the functions of the two domains.

### Cells

The chimeric polypeptide of the invention can be produced in a variety of host cell systems, but mammalian cells are preferred for their ability to perform complex post-translational modifications necessary for the stability and activity of a large, multi-domain glycoprotein like GAA. For research and early development, human embryonic kidney 293 (HEK293) cells, particularly the suspension-adapted FreeStyle 293-F line, were used for transient transfection and rapid protein production. These cells provided a convenient system for generating milligram quantities of both untagged and GILT-tagged GAA for in vitro and in vivo proof-of-concept studies. For clinical and commercial manufacturing, a stable Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cell line was developed using the GPEx retrovector expression technology. This system allows for the targeted integration of multiple copies of the expression cassette into the host genome, leading to the generation of high-producing clones. The final production cell line, sCHO-S-GAA 701-R35, was selected for its ability to consistently produce BMN 701 at titers of approximately 250 mg/liter in a 10-liter Wave bioreactor, a yield that is scalable to commercial manufacturing volumes of up to 1000 liters. The biochemical and functional properties of the CHO-derived BMN 701 were found to be identical to those of the HEK293-derived material, confirming the robustness of the production process.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

This example describes the construction of expression plasmids for the production of untagged and GILT-tagged human acid α-glucosidase (GAA). A DNA cassette (635) encoding the complete human GAA protein (amino acids 1-952) was amplified by PCR from an IMAGE clone and cloned into the mammalian expression vector pCEP4 to create plasmid p635. A second DNA cassette (701) was constructed to encode a chimeric protein. This cassette included the sequence for the IGF-II signal peptide, followed by amino acid residues 8-67 of mature human IGF-II (the GILT tag), a three-amino acid spacer (GGG), and then the GAA sequence starting from Ala-70 to the C-terminus (amino acids 70-952). This cassette was also cloned into pCEP4 to generate plasmid p701. Both plasmids were verified by di-deoxy DNA sequencing to ensure the integrity of the coding sequences and the correct fusion junctions.

### Example 2

This example details the production and purification of the recombinant enzymes. Plasmids p635 and p701 were transiently transfected into FreeStyle 293-F cells. Culture supernatants were harvested 5-10 days post-transfection, clarified, and concentrated. The recombinant proteins were purified using a two-step chromatographic process. First, a hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) step on a phenyl-Sepharose column was used to capture the proteins based on their hydrophobicity at low pH and high salt. This was followed by an anion exchange chromatography (AEX) step on a Q Sepharose XL column to further purify the proteins based on their charge. The final purified proteins, designated rhGAA (from p635) and BMN 701 (from p701), were buffer-exchanged into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), snap-frozen, and stored at -80°C. A stable CHO cell line expressing BMN 701 was also generated using the GPEx system and grown in a Wave bioreactor for large-scale production, yielding a product with identical properties to the HEK293-derived material.

### Example 3

This example demonstrates the biochemical and functional characterization of the purified enzymes. SDS-PAGE and Western blot analysis confirmed that BMN 701 had a slightly higher molecular weight than rhGAA due to the presence of the GILT tag, and that the tag was detectable with an anti-IGF-II antibody. Enzymatic assays using the synthetic substrate para-nitrophenyl (PNP) α-glucoside showed that both rhGAA and BMN 701 had nearly identical specific activities (approximately 140,000 units/mg) and Michaelis-Menten constants (Km of 5.8 and 5.9 mM, respectively), indicating that the GILT tag did not impair the catalytic function of the enzyme. Cellular uptake assays in rat L6 myoblasts revealed that BMN 701 was taken up 26-fold more efficiently than rhGAA or the commercial product alglucosidase alfa, with a Kuptake of 5.4 nM compared to ~145 nM for the untagged enzymes. This enhanced uptake was shown to be specific to the CI-MPR’s IGF-II binding site, as it was completely inhibited by excess IGF-II or a soluble CI-MPR fragment (domains 10-13), but not by excess mannose 6-phosphate (M6P).

### Example 4

This example confirms the correct intracellular processing and lysosomal localization of the internalized enzymes. After uptake into L6 myoblasts, both rhGAA and BMN 701 were processed from their 110-kDa precursor forms into the mature 76/70-kDa lysosomal forms with identical kinetics and half-lives (~8-9 days). The GILT tag itself was rapidly degraded upon entry into the lysosome and was undetectable in cell lysates. Immunofluorescence microscopy in C2C12 mouse myoblasts showed that internalized BMN 701 co-localized perfectly with the lysosomal marker LAMP1, confirming its successful delivery to the lysosomal compartment. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis directly measured the binding affinity of BMN 701 for the IGF-II binding domain (domains 10-13) of the CI-MPR, yielding a Kd of 16-21 nM, which is only 2-6 fold weaker than that of full-length IGF-II itself (Kd of 2.5-8.8 nM), and consistent with the high-affinity uptake observed in cells.

### Example 5

This example presents the in vivo efficacy of BMN 701 in a mouse model of Pompe disease. 129-GAA−/− mice were treated with four weekly intravenous injections of either rhGAA or BMN 701 at doses of 5 or 20 mg/kg. One week after the last injection, glycogen levels were measured in eight different muscle tissues. The results showed that BMN 701 was dramatically more effective than rhGAA at clearing glycogen. In most tissues, the 5 mg/kg dose of BMN 701 produced a greater reduction in glycogen than the 20 mg/kg dose of rhGAA. The 20 mg/kg dose of BMN 701 was the most effective treatment across all tissues, achieving statistically significant (p<0.05) reductions in glycogen compared to all other groups. These data conclusively demonstrate that the GILT-tagged enzyme, BMN 701, has a substantially enhanced therapeutic potency in vivo, validating the GILT technology as a superior platform for enzyme replacement therapy in Pompe disease.
