## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of genetic engineering, and more specifically to methods and systems for achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of specific allelic variants through CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homology-directed repair mechanisms. The invention provides novel gene drive architectures that enable the preferential transmission of desired point mutations or small sequence variants across generations in sexually reproducing organisms. These systems are particularly useful for disseminating beneficial genetic traits throughout populations, constructing complex genotypes for research purposes, and potentially reversing undesirable evolutionary adaptations such as pesticide resistance in pest species. The invention encompasses both copy-cutting and copy-grafting strategies that operate through distinct but complementary molecular mechanisms to achieve highly efficient allelic conversion while simultaneously suppressing the emergence of resistant alleles through lethal mosaicism phenomena.

## BACKGROUND

Traditional Mendelian inheritance dictates that each allele at a given locus has an equal probability of being transmitted to offspring, resulting in predictable segregation ratios in subsequent generations. However, numerous applications in agriculture, biomedicine, and ecological management would benefit from systems capable of biasing inheritance patterns to favor specific genetic variants. Gene drive systems represent one approach to achieving non-Mendelian inheritance by promoting the copying of large DNA sequences containing both the drive element and associated cargo genes. While effective for propagating substantial genetic payloads, conventional gene drives are poorly suited for disseminating subtle genetic variations such as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or small indels that often underlie important phenotypic traits like drought tolerance, pest resistance, or altered metabolic properties.

CRISPR-Cas9 technology has revolutionized genome editing by enabling precise cleavage of target DNA sequences guided by programmable RNA molecules. In standard gene editing protocols, double-strand breaks induced by Cas9 are repaired through either non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which frequently introduces disruptive mutations, or homology-directed repair (HDR), which can incorporate exogenous template sequences. When applied to germline cells, these mechanisms have been harnessed to create gene drive systems where the Cas9 and guide RNA components are encoded within the drive construct itself, ensuring their propagation alongside any cargo sequences. However, existing gene drive architectures face significant limitations when attempting to spread point mutations rather than large insertions, primarily because they require the mutation to be physically linked to the drive element within the same genomic interval.

Several challenges have hindered the development of efficient allelic drive systems. First, designing guide RNAs that selectively target only the undesired allele while sparing the preferred variant requires specific sequence differences between alleles, typically involving PAM site disruptions or critical mismatches within the seed region of the guide RNA binding site. Second, even when selective targeting is possible, the efficiency of HDR-mediated allele conversion must compete with error-prone NHEJ pathways that generate resistant alleles incapable of further cleavage. Third, the physical distance between the cleavage site and the desired allelic variant affects conversion efficiency, with closer proximity generally yielding better results due to the limited extent of DNA resection during HDR. Finally, potential fitness costs associated with either the drive element or the preferred allele may reduce overall transmission rates in natural populations.

Previous attempts to address these challenges have included the development of split-drive systems where Cas9 is provided separately from the guide RNA component, reducing the mutational load associated with drive elements. Additionally, researchers have explored various strategies to enhance HDR efficiency relative to NHEJ, including temporal control of Cas9 expression, manipulation of DNA repair pathway components, and optimization of guide RNA design parameters. Despite these advances, no general solution has emerged for efficiently driving arbitrary point mutations through populations without requiring extensive customization for each specific allelic pair.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges by introducing two complementary allelic drive strategies—copy-cutting and copy-grafting—that overcome the limitations of conventional approaches. These systems leverage the natural homology between allelic variants while exploiting CRISPR-Cas9's precision targeting capabilities to achieve unprecedented levels of allelic conversion efficiency. Furthermore, the invention incorporates built-in mechanisms to suppress the emergence of resistant alleles through lethal mosaicism phenomena, ensuring sustained drive performance across multiple generations.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel methods and compositions for achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of specific allelic variants through CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homology-directed repair mechanisms. In particular, the invention discloses two distinct but complementary allelic drive strategies: copy-cutting and copy-grafting. The copy-cutting strategy involves the use of a dual-guide RNA system wherein one guide RNA directs the propagation of a gene drive element at a first genomic locus while a second guide RNA selectively targets a non-preferred allele at a second genomic locus for cleavage, enabling its replacement with a cleavage-resistant preferred allele present on the homologous chromosome. The copy-grafting strategy represents a more general approach that does not require the preferred allele itself to confer cleavage resistance; instead, it relies on the association of the preferred allele with nearby sequences that are resistant to cleavage, allowing the entire genomic interval encompassing the preferred allele to be copied onto the targeted chromosome during homology-directed repair.

In accordance with the invention, a gene drive construct is engineered to contain at least two guide RNA expression cassettes: a primary guide RNA that targets the insertion site of the drive element itself, and a secondary guide RNA that targets a specific allele of interest at a separate genomic location. The drive construct may be implemented as either a full gene drive system incorporating both Cas9 and guide RNA components, or as a split-drive system where Cas9 is provided from an unlinked genomic locus. When introduced into heterozygous individuals carrying both the drive element and the preferred allele on one chromosome and the non-preferred allele on the homologous chromosome, the system achieves highly efficient conversion of the non-preferred allele to the preferred state through homology-directed repair using the preferred allele as a repair template.

A key feature of the invention is the phenomenon of lethal mosaicism, which acts as a powerful selection mechanism against the emergence of resistant alleles generated through non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). In cases where the targeted gene is essential for viability or reproduction, NHEJ-induced loss-of-function mutations result in dominant lethality when combined with maternally deposited Cas9-guide RNA complexes, effectively eliminating all progeny carrying such resistant alleles. This mechanism ensures that only individuals maintaining the association between the drive element and the functional preferred allele survive to reproduce, thereby sustaining high drive efficiency across generations.

The invention also encompasses the discovery of shadow-drive phenomena, wherein maternally inherited Cas9-guide RNA complexes persist in the germline of offspring that do not inherit the Cas9 transgene, enabling continued allelic conversion for at least one additional generation. This property extends the effective range of the drive system beyond immediate carriers of the Cas9 component, providing additional flexibility in breeding schemes and population modification strategies.

Furthermore, the invention demonstrates strong co-drive effects between the gene drive element and the preferred allele, resulting in their correlated transmission at frequencies significantly exceeding those expected from independent assortment. This co-drive phenomenon depends on proper chromosome pairing during meiosis and is abolished when chromosomal inversions disrupt alignment between homologous regions. The tight linkage between drive element propagation and allelic conversion enhances overall system efficiency and reduces the likelihood of uncoupling events that could compromise drive performance.

The disclosed allelic drive systems have broad applicability across diverse biological contexts, including but not limited to agricultural improvement through the aggregation of multiple favorable traits in crop plants, biomedical research through the rapid construction of complex animal models, and ecological management through the reversal of pesticide resistance in pest populations. The invention provides a general framework for driving virtually any allelic variant through populations, regardless of whether the variant itself confers cleavage resistance, by strategically positioning cleavage-resistant sequences in proximity to the desired allele.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides comprehensive methods and compositions for achieving super-Mendelian inheritance of specific allelic variants through CRISPR-Cas9-mediated homology-directed repair mechanisms. Central to the invention are two distinct allelic drive strategies—copy-cutting and copy-grafting—that operate through different molecular mechanisms but share common principles of selective allele targeting and efficient homology-directed repair. Both strategies rely on the fundamental observation that when a double-strand break is introduced into one allele of a heterozygous individual, the intact homologous allele can serve as a repair template during homology-directed repair, leading to the conversion of the cleaved allele to match the sequence of the repair template.

In the copy-cutting strategy, the preferred allele inherently possesses sequence features that render it resistant to cleavage by the guide RNA-Cas9 complex, typically through disruption of the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) site or introduction of critical mismatches within the guide RNA binding region. The gene drive construct contains two guide RNA expression cassettes: a primary guide RNA that targets the insertion site of the drive element itself, ensuring its propagation through standard gene drive mechanisms, and a secondary guide RNA that specifically targets the non-preferred allele at a separate genomic locus. When expressed in heterozygous individuals carrying both the drive element and the preferred allele on one chromosome and the non-preferred allele on the homologous chromosome, the secondary guide RNA directs Cas9-mediated cleavage exclusively of the non-preferred allele. During homology-directed repair, the intact preferred allele serves as the repair template, resulting in its copying onto the previously cleaved chromosome and thereby converting the non-preferred allele to the preferred state.

The copy-grafting strategy represents a more general approach that does not require the preferred allele itself to confer cleavage resistance. Instead, this method relies on the strategic placement of cleavage-resistant sequences in close proximity to the desired allelic variant. The gene drive construct contains a single guide RNA that targets a site near the allelic variant of interest, with the cleavage-resistant sequences positioned such that they encompass the preferred allele within the genomic interval subject to homology-directed repair. When the guide RNA directs cleavage of the non-preferred chromosome, the repair process copies the entire genomic interval from the donor chromosome, including both the cleavage-resistant sequences and the preferred allele, effectively grafting the desired variant onto the recipient chromosome. This approach dramatically expands the range of allelic variants that can be driven through populations, as it eliminates the requirement for the preferred allele itself to possess intrinsic cleavage resistance properties.

Both allelic drive strategies benefit from several key mechanistic features that enhance their efficiency and reliability. First, the invention exploits the natural homology between allelic variants, which provides optimal conditions for homology-directed repair compared to exogenous repair templates. Second, the systems incorporate built-in selection mechanisms against resistant alleles through the phenomenon of lethal mosaicism. When the targeted gene is essential for viability or reproduction, NHEJ-induced loss-of-function mutations generated during imperfect repair result in dominant lethality in the presence of maternally deposited Cas9-guide RNA complexes. This mechanism effectively eliminates all progeny carrying resistant alleles, ensuring that only individuals maintaining the functional preferred allele survive to reproduce.

The invention also leverages the discovery of shadow-drive phenomena, wherein maternally inherited Cas9-guide RNA complexes persist in the germline of offspring that do not inherit the Cas9 transgene, enabling continued allelic conversion for at least one additional generation. This property extends the effective range of the drive system beyond immediate carriers of the Cas9 component, providing additional flexibility in breeding schemes and population modification strategies. Experimental evidence demonstrates that shadow-drive can achieve conversion efficiencies approaching half of those observed in the primary drive generation, representing a significant enhancement over traditional Mendelian inheritance patterns.

Another critical aspect of the invention is the strong co-drive effect observed between the gene drive element and the preferred allele. Statistical analysis reveals a highly significant positive correlation between the transmission of the drive element and the preferred allele, with co-conversion frequencies far exceeding those expected from independent assortment. This co-drive phenomenon depends on proper chromosome pairing during meiosis and is abolished when chromosomal inversions disrupt alignment between homologous regions. The tight linkage between drive element propagation and allelic conversion enhances overall system efficiency and reduces the likelihood of uncoupling events that could compromise drive performance.

The gene drive constructs of the invention can be implemented in either full gene drive or split-drive configurations. In the full gene drive configuration, both Cas9 and guide RNA components are encoded within the same genomic construct, ensuring their co-inheritance and sustained activity across generations. In the split-drive configuration, Cas9 is provided from an unlinked genomic locus, typically as a Mendelian transgene, while the guide RNA components are contained within the drive element. This arrangement offers greater flexibility in experimental design and may reduce potential fitness costs associated with constitutive Cas9 expression. Both configurations have been demonstrated to achieve high levels of allelic conversion efficiency, with the choice between them depending on specific application requirements and regulatory considerations.

The invention provides detailed guidance for optimizing allelic drive performance through careful selection of target sites and guide RNA design parameters. For copy-cutting applications, preferred alleles should ideally contain PAM site disruptions or critical mismatches within the seed region of the guide RNA binding site to ensure complete cleavage resistance. For copy-grafting applications, cleavage-resistant sequences should be positioned within approximately 100-200 base pairs of the desired allelic variant to maximize conversion efficiency, taking advantage of the typical extent of DNA resection during homology-directed repair in the target organism. Guide RNAs should be designed to minimize off-target effects while maximizing on-target cleavage efficiency, with particular attention paid to GC content, secondary structure formation, and potential cross-reactivity with related genomic sequences.

The disclosed allelic drive systems have been validated in Drosophila melanogaster using the X-linked Notch locus as a model system, demonstrating conversion efficiencies exceeding 75% for both copy-cutting and copy-grafting strategies. However, the underlying principles are broadly applicable across diverse eukaryotic organisms, including plants, mammals, and other insects. The invention provides specific examples of potential applications in agricultural improvement, where multiple favorable traits such as drought tolerance, pest resistance, and increased yield could be aggregated into single crop varieties through sequential allelic drive crosses. Similarly, in biomedical research, complex animal models containing multiple disease-associated alleles could be constructed with unprecedented speed and efficiency. In ecological management, allelic drive systems could be employed to reverse pesticide resistance in pest populations by driving wild-type sensitive alleles back into resistant populations, particularly when combined with fitness cost advantages of the sensitive alleles in the absence of pesticide pressure.

Safety considerations have been carefully addressed in the development of these allelic drive systems. The requirement for proper chromosome pairing during co-drive provides a natural limitation on drive spread in populations with high levels of structural variation. Additionally, the dependence on maternal deposition of Cas9-guide RNA complexes for lethal mosaicism effects creates a built-in temporal limitation on drive activity, as these complexes are diluted over successive generations in the absence of continued Cas9 expression. These features, combined with the ability to implement split-drive configurations where Cas9 and guide RNA components can be segregated through standard genetic crosses, provide multiple layers of containment and control for responsible deployment of allelic drive technologies.

### EXAMPLES

Example 1 describes the development and validation of a copy-cutting allelic drive system targeting the Drosophila Notch locus. A dual-guide RNA CopyCat element was constructed containing gRNA-y1 for propagation at the yellow locus and gRNA-N+ for selective cleavage of the wild-type Notch allele (N+). The preferred allele, NAx16, contains a G→A substitution that disrupts the PAM site required for gRNA-N+ recognition, rendering it cleavage-resistant. The CopyCat element was marked with a DsRed fluorescent reporter and inserted into the yellow locus, creating the y<ccN> allele. When combined with an unlinked source of Cas9 provided in trans, this system achieved super-Mendelian inheritance of both the CopyCat element (85.3% transmission in males) and the NAx16 allele (93.6% transmission in males). Analysis of individual F2 female lines derived from single founders confirmed these results, showing 75% transmission of the CopyCat element and 87.5% transmission of the NAx16 allele. The higher conversion efficiency for the NAx16 allele compared to the CopyCat element was attributed to the lethal mosaicism phenomenon, where NHEJ-induced loss-of-function Notch alleles resulted in dominant lethality in males and severe fitness costs in females.

Example 2 demonstrates the lethal mosaicism mechanism that suppresses the emergence of resistant alleles in the copy-cutting system. Embryo collections from F1 master females crossed to wild-type males revealed approximately 20% of embryos displaying classic neurogenic phenotypes characteristic of Notch loss-of-function mutations, confirming the generation of NHEJ-induced N− alleles. However, no such alleles were recovered among viable F2 female lines, indicating their elimination through lethal mosaicism. To test this hypothesis directly, F1 master females were crossed to males carrying a cleavage-insensitive wild-type Notch allele (N+IS), which protected heterozygous female progeny from mosaic lethality. Under these conditions, 22% of F2 females displayed the characteristic N−/+ wing phenotype, confirming the generation and survival of NHEJ-induced alleles when protected from lethal mosaicism. Additional experiments crossing y<ccN> N−/Balancer females to vasaCas9 males demonstrated complete failure to recover viable N− F1 females in the presence of zygotic Cas9 expression, further validating the lethal mosaicism mechanism.

Example 3 characterizes the shadow-drive phenomenon observed in the copy-cutting system. F2 females inheriting the y<ccN> NAx16 alleles but lacking the Cas9 transgene were crossed to N+IS males, and their F3 progeny were analyzed for residual drive activity. Results showed substantial levels of allelic conversion in the absence of zygotic Cas9 expression, including 29.3% conversion of the receiver chromosome to the y<ccN> state in males, 27% conversion to the NAx16 allele in males, and 12% recovery of N−/+ females. These shadow-drive effects amounted to approximately half the efficiency observed in the primary F2 generation, demonstrating the persistence of maternally deposited Cas9-guide RNA complexes in the germline for at least one additional generation.

Example 4 investigates the co-drive phenomenon between the CopyCat element and the NAx16 allele. Analysis of 104 individual F2 female lines revealed a striking correlation between the two conversion events, with 96% of receiver chromosomes carrying the DsRed+ y<ccN> element also having copied the NAx16 allele, compared to only 38% conversion frequency in the absence of y<ccN> copying. This co-drive effect was shown to depend on proper chromosome pairing, as it was abolished when the receiver chromosome carried a multiply inverted balancer (Basc) that disrupted alignment between the Notch and yellow loci. Additional experiments using a "single cut" configuration, where the receiver chromosome carried an NHEJ-induced point mutation at the y-gRNA cleavage site, demonstrated reduced transmission of the NAx16 allele compared to wild-type receiver chromosomes, indicating that active copying at the yellow locus enhances allelic drive at the Notch locus.

Example 5 validates the copy-grafting strategy using a reverse-drive scenario at the Notch locus. A wild-type cleavage-insensitive Notch allele (N+IS) containing a C→A substitution at position −4 relative to the PAM site was recombined with the y<ccN> CopyCat element. This donor chromosome was placed in trans to a receiver chromosome carrying the cleavage-sensitive NAxE2 allele, which contains a C→T substitution located 21 bp upstream of the NAx16 alteration. The gRNA-N+ carried on the CopyCat element directed cleavage of the NAxE2 allele while sparing the N+IS allele. Results demonstrated highly efficient inverse allelic drive via copy-grafting, with 78.2% conversion of receiver chromosomes in males, comparable to the 79.5% conversion observed in the copy-cutting system. The copy-grafting system also exhibited the characteristic features of lethal mosaicism (16% N− allele generation when protected by N+IS fathers) and strong co-drive between the y<ccN> and N+IS alleles.

Example 6 explores the versatility of allelic drive systems by testing de novo Abruptex alleles generated through NHEJ-induced in-frame indels at the gRNA-N+ cut site. One such allele, Ax103, was recombined with the y<ccN> element and tested for its ability to sustain allelic drive. Results showed equivalent performance to the NAx16 allele in driving conversion of wild-type N+ alleles to an Abruptex phenotype, demonstrating that allelic variants with mutations at the Cas9 cleavage site are equally well suited for sustaining efficient allelic drive as those disrupting PAM sites. This finding significantly expands the range of allelic variants that can be driven through populations using the copy-cutting strategy.

Example 7 demonstrates the compatibility of allelic drive systems with different Cas9 expression sources. Experiments comparing vasaCas9 and nanosCas9 transgenes showed equivalent allelic drive efficiency with both germline-specific promoters, indicating that the systems function effectively with various Cas9 expression strategies. This flexibility allows researchers to optimize Cas9 expression timing and tissue specificity based on specific experimental requirements and target organisms.

Example 8 outlines potential applications of allelic drive systems in agricultural improvement. A hypothetical breeding scheme is described for aggregating multiple favorable traits in polyploid crops such as wheat or rye. Individual plants bearing different preferred alleles (e.g., for drought resistance, higher yields, optimal architecture, or rapid growth) would be sequentially crossed to strains carrying Mendelian cassettes with Cas9 and guide RNAs targeting the corresponding non-preferred alleles. After accumulating all desired alleles in a single strain, a final cross would segregate out the Cas9-guide RNA cassettes, resulting in elite varieties containing multiple beneficial traits that would be difficult or impossible to assemble through conventional breeding methods.

Example 9 illustrates the potential for allelic drive systems to reverse pesticide resistance in pest populations. Many insecticide-resistant alleles in disease vectors and crop pests target essential nervous system components such as sodium channels or glutamate receptors. Allelic drive systems could be designed to drive wild-type sensitive alleles back into resistant populations, particularly when combined with the reduced fitness of resistant alleles in the absence of pesticide pressure. Even modest reductions in resistant allele prevalence (e.g., below 50%) could have major positive impacts on disease control strategies, making this approach highly relevant for public health and agricultural pest management.

Example 10 addresses safety considerations and containment strategies for allelic drive systems. The requirement for proper chromosome pairing during co-drive provides a natural limitation on drive spread in genetically diverse populations. The dependence on maternal deposition of Cas9-guide RNA complexes for lethal mosaicism effects creates a built-in temporal limitation on drive activity. Split-drive configurations allow for segregation of Cas9 and guide RNA components through standard genetic crosses, providing additional layers of control. These features, combined with strict laboratory containment protocols following Institutional Biosafety Committee guidelines, ensure responsible development and potential deployment of allelic drive technologies.
