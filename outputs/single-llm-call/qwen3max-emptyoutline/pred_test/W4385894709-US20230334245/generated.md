## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language processing (NLP), and more specifically to systems and methods for improving the computational efficiency of zero-shot text classification models. The invention further pertains to the integration of conformal prediction frameworks with large pre-trained language models (PLMs) to dynamically filter candidate label sets prior to final classification inference, thereby reducing both computational overhead and carbon footprint without compromising predictive coverage or accuracy. The disclosed techniques are particularly applicable in real-world deployment scenarios involving large-scale intent recognition, topic classification, and other semantic labeling tasks where the number of potential target labels is substantial and inference latency must be minimized.

## BACKGROUND

Zero-shot text classification has emerged as a critical capability in modern natural language understanding systems, enabling models to assign semantic labels to input texts even when those labels were not observed during training. This paradigm is especially valuable in dynamic environments—such as customer service chatbots, content moderation platforms, or enterprise knowledge management systems—where new categories may be introduced continuously without the feasibility of retraining or fine-tuning underlying models. Early approaches to zero-shot classification relied on mapping both input texts and class labels into a shared embedding space, typically using lexical or semantic similarity metrics derived from word embeddings or knowledge bases. These methods computed embeddings for the input text and each candidate label independently, then selected the label with the highest similarity score. While computationally efficient—requiring only a single forward pass over the input text—these techniques often suffered from limited expressiveness due to their reliance on shallow representations and static embeddings.

Subsequent advancements introduced architectures that explicitly incorporate label information during text encoding. For instance, generative models conditioned on label embeddings were proposed to reconstruct input texts given a hypothesized class, while attention-based mechanisms allowed the model to focus on text segments most relevant to each candidate label. Although these approaches improved classification accuracy by enabling richer interactions between text and label representations, they incurred significant computational costs: each label required a separate processing pass over the input, leading to inference times that scaled linearly with the number of candidate classes. This limitation became increasingly problematic as the scope of zero-shot applications expanded to domains with hundreds or even thousands of potential labels.

More recently, zero-shot classification has been reformulated using natural language inference (NLI) and next sentence prediction (NSP) paradigms, leveraging the powerful contextual representations of transformer-based pre-trained language models (PLMs) such as BERT and BART. In these formulations, each candidate label is converted into a natural language hypothesis sentence (e.g., “This text is about sports”), and the PLM is used as a cross-encoder to compute a compatibility score between the input text (as premise) and each hypothesis. Because cross-encoders apply full self-attention across the concatenated premise-hypothesis pair, they capture deep semantic relationships but require recomputing the entire encoding for every label-text pair. Consequently, inference time grows linearly with the number of labels, and the computational burden becomes prohibitive when deploying large PLMs like BART-large in production settings with extensive label vocabularies.

This inefficiency is compounded by environmental and economic concerns. Large-scale inference using massive PLMs consumes substantial energy, contributing to a non-negligible carbon footprint—a growing issue in sustainable AI development. Moreover, in latency-sensitive applications such as real-time conversational agents or high-throughput document routing systems, the linear scaling of inference time with label count can render NLI- or NSP-based zero-shot classifiers impractical despite their superior accuracy.

To address this challenge, researchers have explored various optimization strategies, including distillation to smaller models, caching of intermediate representations, and approximate nearest neighbor search in embedding spaces. However, these methods often involve trade-offs in accuracy, require additional training data, or fail to provide formal guarantees on prediction reliability. Crucially, none offer a principled mechanism to dynamically reduce the candidate label set while maintaining statistical control over error rates.

Conformal prediction (CP), a framework rooted in algorithmic learning theory, offers a promising solution. CP provides a model-agnostic method to generate prediction sets—rather than point predictions—with guaranteed coverage under the assumption of data exchangeability. Specifically, for any desired error rate α, a conformal predictor outputs a set of labels that includes the true label with probability at least 1−α. This property makes CP ideally suited for pre-filtering unlikely labels before applying a computationally expensive zero-shot classifier: by constructing a conformal predictor using a fast, lightweight base model, one can eliminate a subset of candidate labels while ensuring that the final prediction (made by the full zero-shot model over the reduced set) retains the original model’s correctness with high probability.

Prior work has applied conformal prediction to classification tasks, but its integration with zero-shot NLP systems—particularly in label filtering for cross-encoder architectures—remains unexplored. Existing CP implementations typically assume access to labeled calibration data, which contradicts the core premise of zero-shot learning where no task-specific labels are available during deployment. Furthermore, the choice of non-conformity measure (which quantifies how “unusual” a prediction is relative to calibration examples) significantly impacts both the size of the prediction set and computational overhead, yet no systematic study exists on designing efficient, scalable non-conformity functions for text classification.

Thus, there remains a critical need for a zero-shot classification system that combines the high accuracy of NLI/NSP-based PLMs with the efficiency gains of dynamic label pruning, all while providing statistically rigorous error control. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel conformal prediction framework specifically tailored for zero-shot text classification, wherein the calibration data is generated automatically using the zero-shot model itself, and multiple base classifiers of varying complexity serve as non-conformity measures to balance speed and prediction set tightness.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Computer Environment

The present invention may be implemented in a variety of computing environments, ranging from cloud-based distributed systems to edge devices with constrained resources. At a minimum, the system requires a processor capable of executing machine learning inference operations, memory sufficient to load pre-trained language models and associated data structures, and storage for model weights, calibration datasets, and application-specific configurations. In a typical deployment scenario, the system operates within a server-client architecture: client devices (e.g., mobile phones, web browsers, IoT sensors) transmit raw text inputs to a backend inference server, which hosts the zero-shot classification pipeline comprising the conformal predictor and the primary NLI/NSP-based classifier.

The backend server is equipped with one or more hardware accelerators—such as GPUs or TPUs—to expedite tensor computations inherent in transformer-based models. The software stack includes a deep learning framework (e.g., PyTorch or TensorFlow), a model serving engine (e.g., TorchServe or TensorFlow Serving), and libraries for natural language processing (e.g., Hugging Face Transformers). The conformal predictor component is implemented as a modular service that interfaces with the main classifier via well-defined APIs, allowing for independent updates, scaling, or replacement of either module without disrupting the overall workflow.

Crucially, the system does not require persistent storage of human-annotated labels for the target task, aligning with the zero-shot paradigm. Instead, it leverages unlabeled text samples—either drawn from the same domain as the input data or sourced from general corpora—to construct calibration sets through self-labeling using the zero-shot model. This design ensures applicability in scenarios where labeled data is unavailable, prohibitively expensive to obtain, or subject to privacy constraints.

The computer environment also supports dynamic configuration of key hyperparameters, including the conformal error rate α, the choice of base classifier for the conformal predictor, and the batch size for parallelized inference. These parameters can be tuned based on operational requirements: for instance, a lower α (e.g., 0.01) yields tighter error control at the cost of larger prediction sets, while a higher α reduces computational load but increases the risk of excluding the correct label. Similarly, the selection of base classifier (e.g., token overlap vs. distilled BERT) allows operators to trade off between preprocessing speed and prediction set compactness depending on the relative sizes of the zero-shot model and label space.

Security and privacy considerations are integrated into the architecture. Input texts are processed in encrypted form during transmission, and intermediate representations (e.g., logits from the base classifier) are not persisted beyond the duration of a single inference request. Additionally, the system includes monitoring modules that track empirical coverage rates in real time, alerting administrators if deviations from nominal error rates suggest distributional shifts or adversarial inputs.

### Work Flows

The operation of the present invention follows a multi-stage workflow designed to maximize computational efficiency while preserving statistical guarantees on prediction correctness. The workflow begins when a raw text input is received by the system. This input may originate from any textual source, such as user queries, social media posts, support tickets, or news articles, and is assumed to belong to a domain for which a zero-shot classifier has been configured with a predefined set of candidate labels.

In the first stage, the system invokes a conformal predictor (CP) to generate a reduced candidate label set. The CP consists of two primary components: a fast base classifier and a calibration dataset. The base classifier, selected from a repertoire of options with varying computational profiles, processes the input text to produce preliminary scores for all candidate labels. These scores are then transformed into non-conformity measures—quantitative indicators of how poorly each label aligns with the input according to the base model.

The calibration dataset, constructed offline prior to deployment, comprises unlabeled text samples that have been automatically annotated using the same zero-shot classifier that will be used in the final prediction stage. For each calibration sample, the system records the non-conformity score corresponding to the label assigned by the zero-shot model. From these scores, an empirical quantile threshold q is computed such that a fraction (1−α) of the calibration scores fall below q, where α is the user-specified error tolerance (e.g., 0.01 for 99% coverage).

During inference, the non-conformity scores for the current input are compared against q. Any label whose score exceeds q is deemed “non-conforming” and excluded from further consideration. The remaining labels constitute the prediction set Γ_α, which is guaranteed—under the assumption of data exchangeability—to contain the true label with probability at least 1−α.

In the second stage, the reduced label set Γ_α is passed to the primary zero-shot classifier, which employs either an NLI or NSP formulation based on a large pre-trained language model (e.g., BART-large or BERT-base). The classifier constructs a hypothesis sentence for each label in Γ_α, concatenates it with the input text, and computes a compatibility score using the cross-encoder architecture. Because |Γ_α| is typically much smaller than the full label set, the number of forward passes—and thus the total inference time—is substantially reduced.

Finally, the label with the highest compatibility score is returned as the system’s prediction. Notably, because the conformal filtering step ensures that the correct label (as determined by the full zero-shot model) is retained with high probability, the final accuracy remains statistically indistinguishable from that of the unfiltered baseline, while computational costs are lowered.

This workflow is adaptive: if the base classifier produces an empty prediction set (a rare event at low α), the system defaults to using the full label set to ensure robustness. Additionally, the calibration dataset can be periodically refreshed using newly observed unlabeled inputs to maintain coverage guarantees in the face of concept drift.

### Example Data Experiment and Performance

The efficacy of the present invention was evaluated across five benchmark datasets spanning intent recognition and topic classification tasks. These include SNIPS (7 labels), ATIS (17 labels), HWU64 (64 labels), AG’s News (4 labels), and Yahoo! Answers (10 labels). Two zero-shot baselines were employed: a BART-large model fine-tuned on NLI (facebook/bart-large-mnli) and a BERT-base model adapted for NSP (bert-base-uncased). Four distinct conformal predictors were tested, each using a different base classifier to define the non-conformity measure:

1. **CP-Token**: Computes token overlap between the input text and representative tokens collected per label from the calibration set. The non-conformity score is defined as the negative percentage of overlapping unique tokens.
2. **CP-Glove**: Uses cosine distance between bag-of-words representations of the input and label descriptions, with word embeddings derived from GloVe.
3. **CP-Distil**: Leverages a distilled RoBERTa-based cross-encoder (cross-encoder/nli-distilroberta-base) trained on NLI data, using negative logits as non-conformity scores.
4. **CP-CLS**: Fine-tunes a DistilBERT model on pseudo-labels generated by the zero-shot classifier, then uses its negative logits as non-conformity scores.

All conformal predictors were calibrated using unlabeled data from the respective datasets, with labels assigned by the zero-shot model itself. An error rate of α = 0.01 was used throughout, ensuring 99% nominal coverage.

Results demonstrate that the conformal filtering framework consistently reduces computational load without sacrificing accuracy. On average across all datasets and models, the number of labels processed by the zero-shot classifier was reduced by 41.09% for the NLI-based system and 43.38% for the NSP-based system. Correspondingly, average inference time decreased by 25.6% and 22.2%, respectively. Notably, accuracy was preserved or even slightly improved in most cases: CP-Token incurred a minor drop of −0.46% absolute accuracy, while CP-Distil yielded a +0.31% gain, suggesting that removing implausible labels may reduce noise and enhance decision confidence.

The choice of base classifier involves a trade-off between preprocessing speed and prediction set size. CP-Token, despite producing larger sets, achieved the best end-to-end speedup on smaller-label datasets (e.g., ATIS, SNIPS, AG’s News) due to its negligible computational overhead. In contrast, CP-CLS and CP-Distil excelled on HWU64 (64 labels), where their ability to generate tighter sets outweighed their higher base inference cost. This confirms that the optimal base classifier depends on the relative scale of the zero-shot model and label space.

Empirical coverage closely matched the nominal 99% rate across all configurations, validating the theoretical guarantees of conformal prediction even when calibration data is self-labeled. Experiments with reduced calibration set sizes (as few as 200 samples) showed that coverage remains stable at low α, enabling deployment in data-scarce scenarios. Cross-dataset calibration was also feasible at α = 0.01, though with larger prediction sets, indicating potential for transferable conformal predictors in multi-task settings.

In summary, the present invention delivers a practical, theoretically grounded solution to the efficiency bottleneck of modern zero-shot classifiers, enabling their scalable deployment in real-world applications with large label vocabularies.
