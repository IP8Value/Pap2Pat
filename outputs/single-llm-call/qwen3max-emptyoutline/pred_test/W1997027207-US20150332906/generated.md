# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry has emerged as a cornerstone analytical technique in the field of biomolecular analysis, offering rapid, sensitive, and high-throughput capabilities for the characterization of peptides, proteins, nucleic acids, and other macromolecules. Since its inception in the late 1980s, MALDI-TOF technology has undergone significant advancements, particularly through the integration of delayed extraction ion optics and reflectron (ion mirror) systems, which collectively enhance mass resolution and mass accuracy. Modern high-performance MALDI-TOF instruments routinely achieve mass resolutions exceeding 15,000 (full width at half maximum, FWHM) and mass accuracies better than 5 parts per million (ppm) when internal calibration standards are employed. These performance metrics have rendered MALDI-TOF indispensable in applications ranging from microbial identification and clinical diagnostics to imaging mass spectrometry and proteomics.

Despite these impressive specifications, empirical observations across multiple instrument platforms—including those from different manufacturers—have revealed a persistent and often unexplained variability in replicate mass measurements of the same analyte under identical experimental conditions. Specifically, even when internal calibration is applied using well-characterized peptide standards, individual replicate spectra of the same sample frequently exhibit mass deviations that exceed the instrument’s stated accuracy specifications, with errors commonly ranging from under 1 ppm to over 20 ppm. This inconsistency is particularly problematic in applications such as peptide mass fingerprinting (PMF), where protein identification relies critically on the precise matching of experimentally observed peptide masses to theoretical masses derived from protein sequence databases. In such contexts, even small systematic or random errors can lead to false negatives, ambiguous identifications, or reduced confidence scores.

A key limitation in current MALDI-TOF practice is the absence of any a priori method to assess or correct for the accuracy of an individual mass measurement in real time. While internal calibration improves overall accuracy by correcting for global instrumental drift, it does not account for stochastic fluctuations inherent in the data acquisition process. These fluctuations appear to be uncorrelated across different peptides within the same spectrum and are not mitigated by increasing the number of laser shots per spectrum, suggesting that the source of variability lies not in signal-to-noise limitations but in fundamental aspects of the instrument’s analog-to-digital (AD) conversion and data binning architecture.

Contemporary MALDI-TOF instruments utilize high-speed digitizers to convert the continuous analog ion current arriving at the detector into discrete digital intensity values sampled at regular time intervals. These time intervals correspond to fixed “bins” along the time-of-flight axis, which are subsequently converted to mass units via a calibration function. Critically, the absolute positioning of these bins relative to the true ion arrival time is subject to minor electronic jitter during each acquisition cycle—particularly upon initiation of a new spectral acquisition. Although this positional shift is typically less than the width of a single bin (often corresponding to 13–20 ppm in mass units depending on m/z), it introduces a small but non-negligible uncertainty in the measured flight time. Because peak detection and centroiding algorithms must interpolate between discrete bins to estimate the true peak apex, any shift in bin alignment alters the interpolated mass value, even if the underlying physical ion arrival time remains constant.

This bin repositioning effect is reset independently for each spectrum acquisition, meaning that replicate measurements of the same sample yield mass values that are perturbed by independent, random offsets. Empirical data demonstrate that these offsets follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution, implying that they arise from the superposition of multiple small, independent error sources within the instrument’s timing electronics, clock synchronization, or trigger circuitry. Consequently, while any single measurement may deviate significantly from the true mass, the ensemble average of multiple independently acquired spectra converges toward the true value, with the standard deviation of the population serving as a quantitative indicator of measurement reliability.

Existing MALDI-TOF data acquisition workflows typically involve accumulating hundreds or thousands of laser shots into a single composite spectrum before applying calibration. While this approach improves signal-to-noise ratio, it does not address the underlying bin-positioning variability because all shots within a single acquisition share the same bin alignment. Thus, the resulting calibrated mass retains the systematic offset introduced at the start of that acquisition. In contrast, acquiring multiple independent spectra—each with its own bin alignment and independent calibration—captures the full distribution of possible measurement outcomes, enabling statistical mitigation of random errors through averaging.

Although the theoretical basis for time-of-flight mass spectrometry is well established, and numerous refinements in ion optics, detector design, and vacuum engineering have pushed instrumental performance closer to theoretical limits, the impact of AD converter discretization and binning on mass accuracy has been largely overlooked in both commercial implementations and academic discourse. Current instrument control software generally treats the digitized spectrum as a faithful representation of the analog signal, without accounting for the statistical implications of bin misalignment across acquisitions. As a result, the full potential of MALDI-TOF instrumentation—particularly in high-accuracy applications—remains unrealized.

There exists, therefore, a critical need for a method that systematically addresses the intrinsic variability arising from the discontinuous nature of AD-converted MALDI-TOF data. Such a method should be compatible with existing hardware, require minimal modification to standard operating procedures, and provide measurable improvements in mass accuracy, reproducibility, and confidence in downstream analyses such as protein identification. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel data acquisition and processing paradigm based on the statistical aggregation of multiple independently calibrated spectra, coupled with descriptive statistical evaluation to identify and exclude unreliable measurements.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for improving the mass accuracy, precision, and reliability of matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry through the acquisition and statistical analysis of multiple independently calibrated mass spectra. Unlike conventional approaches that accumulate numerous laser shots into a single composite spectrum prior to calibration, the invention entails acquiring a plurality of discrete spectra, each generated from a defined number of laser shots and subjected to an independent internal calibration procedure. The resulting set of mass measurements for each analyte ion is then treated as a statistical population, from which a mean mass value and associated descriptive statistics—such as standard deviation, variance, and outlier indicators—are computed. This ensemble-based approach leverages the random nature of bin-positioning errors inherent in the analog-to-digital conversion process to cancel out stochastic deviations, thereby yielding mass estimates that more closely approximate the true analyte mass.

In accordance with the invention, a sample containing one or more analytes is prepared using standard MALDI protocols, including co-crystallization with a suitable matrix compound on a target plate. The sample is then analyzed on a MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer configured to acquire a series of individual spectra, wherein each spectrum is composed of a predetermined number of laser shots (e.g., 100 to 1000 shots) and is processed independently through the instrument’s calibration algorithm using internal calibrant ions spiked into the sample or deposited adjacently on the target. The number of replicate spectra is selected to ensure sufficient statistical power—typically between 10 and 30 replicates—while balancing practical considerations of analysis time and sample consumption.

For each analyte ion detected across the replicate spectra, the measured mass values are compiled into a dataset. Descriptive statistical parameters are then calculated, including the arithmetic mean, standard deviation, and measures of distribution normality (e.g., Shapiro-Wilk test). Additionally, outlier detection methods such as Grubbs’ test may be applied to identify and optionally exclude anomalous measurements that fall outside expected statistical bounds. The final reported mass for the analyte is the mean of the accepted measurements, accompanied by its standard deviation as a measure of uncertainty.

This method exploits the observation that the primary source of unexplained mass variability in MALDI-TOF instruments arises from minute, random shifts in the alignment of data acquisition bins relative to the true ion arrival time, caused by electronic jitter during the initialization of each spectral acquisition. Because these shifts are uncorrelated between acquisitions and normally distributed, their effects are averaged out when multiple independent spectra are combined. Crucially, each spectrum must undergo its own calibration, as the bin shift affects both the raw flight time measurement and the calibration function derived from internal standards. Averaging pre-calibrated spectra—rather than raw time-domain data—ensures that both the analyte and calibrant peaks are subject to the same binning perturbation within each acquisition, preserving the integrity of the calibration while allowing inter-acquisition variability to be statistically resolved.

The invention further provides that the standard deviation of the replicate mass measurements serves as a diagnostic metric for data quality. Measurements exhibiting unusually high standard deviations are flagged as potentially unreliable, enabling selective exclusion of low-confidence data points prior to final averaging. This capability enhances the robustness of downstream applications such as peptide mass fingerprinting, where the inclusion of inaccurate masses can compromise protein identification. By filtering out high-variance measurements, the method increases the specificity and confidence of database search results.

Moreover, the invention is broadly applicable across the operational mass range of MALDI-TOF instruments, including both linear and reflectron modes, and is compatible with any instrument employing analog-to-digital conversion for signal detection—a feature common to virtually all modern time-of-flight mass spectrometers. The method requires no hardware modifications and can be implemented through software updates to existing instrument control and data processing platforms. It is equally effective for synthetic peptide standards, complex proteolytic digests, and biological samples such as immunoprecipitated proteins.

In addition to improving mass accuracy, the invention enables more rigorous error estimation in MALDI-TOF experiments. Rather than relying on manufacturer-specified accuracy tolerances, users obtain an empirical, sample-specific uncertainty metric for each measured mass. This facilitates more informed decision-making in analytical workflows, particularly in regulatory, clinical, or forensic contexts where measurement traceability and uncertainty quantification are essential.

The invention also extends to other time-of-flight mass spectrometry platforms that utilize similar AD conversion architectures, including electrospray ionization quadrupole-time-of-flight (ESI-Q-TOF) and liquid chromatography-time-of-flight (LC-TOF) systems. Although these instruments often exhibit superior mass stability due to continuous ion sources and more stable timing references, they remain susceptible to analogous discretization effects, and thus may benefit from the same statistical averaging strategy.

In summary, the present invention transforms MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry from a technique that reports single-point mass estimates of uncertain reliability into a statistically grounded analytical method capable of delivering high-accuracy, high-confidence mass measurements with quantifiable uncertainty. By harnessing the randomness of instrumental noise rather than merely attempting to suppress it, the invention unlocks performance levels consistent with the theoretical capabilities of modern MALDI-TOF hardware, thereby expanding the utility of this widely deployed technology in research, clinical, and industrial settings.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for enhancing the accuracy, precision, and reliability of mass measurements obtained from matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometers through the acquisition and statistical analysis of multiple independently acquired and calibrated mass spectra. The method addresses a previously unrecognized but pervasive source of mass measurement variability inherent in the analog-to-digital (AD) signal detection architecture of MALDI-TOF instruments, wherein the discrete sampling of ion arrival times into fixed temporal bins introduces small, random offsets that affect both raw flight time measurements and subsequent calibration functions. By treating replicate mass measurements as a statistical population and applying descriptive statistical techniques, the invention mitigates these random errors and provides a robust framework for high-confidence mass determination.

In practicing the invention, a sample containing one or more analytes of interest is first prepared according to standard MALDI sample preparation protocols. Typically, the analyte—such as a peptide, protein digest, or small molecule—is mixed with a UV-absorbing organic matrix compound, such as α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) or 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB), in a solvent system comprising water, acetonitrile, and a volatile acid like trifluoroacetic acid or formic acid. The mixture is then deposited onto a conductive target plate, commonly a stainless steel MALDI plate with multiple sample wells, and allowed to dry, forming co-crystalline deposits of analyte and matrix. For internal calibration, a solution containing a plurality of known calibrant compounds—typically synthetic peptides with precisely characterized monoisotopic masses—is either mixed directly with the sample prior to spotting or deposited in adjacent wells on the target plate to enable close external calibration.

The prepared target plate is loaded into the vacuum chamber of a MALDI-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with a pulsed UV laser (e.g., nitrogen laser at 337 nm), ion optics including delayed extraction and a reflectron for improved resolution, and a microchannel plate (MCP) detector coupled to a high-speed analog-to-digital converter (digitizer). The digitizer samples the analog ion current at regular time intervals determined by an internal clock, typically operating at frequencies between 500 MHz and 2 GHz, thereby converting the continuous ion signal into a series of discrete intensity values assigned to fixed time bins. The width of each time bin corresponds to a specific mass interval that varies with m/z due to the nonlinear relationship between flight time and mass in TOF instruments; for example, at m/z 1000, a 1-nanosecond bin may correspond to approximately 0.02 amu, whereas at m/z 2000, the same bin width corresponds to a larger mass interval.

According to the invention, instead of acquiring a single composite spectrum by accumulating thousands of laser shots, the instrument is configured to acquire a series of N independent spectra, where N is an integer typically between 10 and 30, though it may be adjusted based on required statistical confidence and sample availability. Each individual spectrum is generated by firing the laser a predetermined number of times—commonly between 100 and 1000 shots—onto the same sample spot or across multiple homogeneous spots of the same sample. Critically, each spectrum is initiated as a separate acquisition event, meaning that the digitizer resets its time-base reference and redefines the absolute positions of the time bins at the start of each acquisition. This reset introduces a small, random shift in bin alignment relative to the true ion arrival time, with the magnitude of the shift being less than one bin width but sufficient to perturb interpolated mass values.

Following acquisition, each spectrum is processed independently through the instrument’s internal calibration routine. Internal calibration is performed using the known masses of the calibrant ions present in the spectrum, which may be co-spotted with the sample or acquired from a neighboring spot via close external calibration. A nonlinear calibration function—typically a polynomial of second or third order—is fitted to map the observed flight times of the calibrants to their theoretical masses. This calibration function is then applied to convert the flight times of all detected analyte ions in that spectrum to mass units. Because both the calibrant and analyte peaks in a given spectrum are subject to the same bin alignment shift, the calibration corrects for global instrumental drift but preserves the relative offset induced by the bin repositioning. Thus, each calibrated spectrum yields a mass measurement for each analyte that includes a unique, random error component.

After all N spectra have been acquired and individually calibrated, the mass measurements for each analyte ion are compiled into a dataset comprising N values (or fewer, if the ion was not detected in all spectra due to shot-to-shot variability in ion yield). Descriptive statistical analysis is then performed on this dataset. The arithmetic mean of the mass values is calculated as the best estimate of the true analyte mass. The sample standard deviation (σ) is computed to quantify the dispersion of the measurements and serves as an empirical measure of uncertainty. Additional statistical tests may be applied to assess the validity of the dataset: the Shapiro-Wilk test evaluates whether the mass measurements follow a normal (Gaussian) distribution, supporting the assumption that errors are random and independent; Grubbs’ test identifies potential outliers that deviate significantly from the rest of the population and may be excluded from the final average if deemed spurious.

The final reported mass for the analyte is the mean of the accepted measurements, accompanied by its standard deviation. In applications requiring high-confidence identifications—such as peptide mass fingerprinting for protein identification—only analyte masses with standard deviations below a predefined threshold (e.g., 0.015 amu or 5 ppm) are retained for database searching. This filtering step effectively removes measurements compromised by poor signal-to-noise, chemical interference, or other sources of elevated variance, thereby improving the specificity of the identification.

The invention has been validated using both standard protein digests (e.g., bovine serum albumin, myoglobin, β-casein) and complex biological samples (e.g., β-tubulin immunoprecipitated from HEK293 cell lysates). In all cases, the mean mass derived from replicate spectra exhibited significantly improved accuracy compared to individual measurements, with errors consistently reduced to within 2–3 ppm of reference values obtained from high-resolution LC-Q-TOF MS/MS. Moreover, peptides exhibiting high standard deviations were found to correlate with incorrect database matches or post-translational modifications not accounted for in the search parameters, demonstrating the diagnostic utility of the statistical metrics.

The method is fully compatible with existing MALDI-TOF hardware and requires only software-level modifications to implement automated replicate acquisition, independent calibration, and statistical post-processing. It is applicable across the entire mass range of the instrument, including high-mass analyses in linear mode, and can be extended to other TOF-based platforms such as ESI-Q-TOF and MALDI-TOF/TOF systems. While the invention primarily focuses on mass accuracy, the same statistical framework can be adapted for intensity-based quantitation, though greater variability in ion yield may necessitate larger replicate numbers.

By transforming MALDI-TOF from a single-measurement technique into a statistically robust analytical method, the invention enables researchers to achieve mass accuracy and reliability commensurate with the instrument’s theoretical performance, thereby enhancing the utility of MALDI-TOF in proteomics, metabolomics, clinical diagnostics, and other fields reliant on precise mass determination.
