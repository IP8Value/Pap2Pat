# DESCRIPTION

## PRIOR ART

The production of liquid fuels from alternative feedstocks such as coal, natural gas, and biomass via the Fischer-Tropsch (FT) synthesis has gained renewed interest in recent decades due to increasing energy demands and environmental concerns. The Fischer-Tropsch process converts syngas—a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen—into a broad distribution of hydrocarbons, predominantly linear paraffins and olefins, with minor amounts of oxygenates and branched compounds. Among the various products, low-temperature Fischer-Tropsch (LTFT) waxes are particularly valuable as they consist largely of long-chain normal paraffins (C20+), which can be upgraded into high-quality middle distillates (e.g., diesel and jet fuel) or lubricant base oils through catalytic processing. However, the direct use of these waxes is limited by their poor cold flow properties, necessitating further refining.

Historically, the upgrading of FT waxes has relied on hydrocracking and hydroisomerization processes employing bifunctional catalysts that combine a hydrogenation/dehydrogenation (HD/DHD) function with an acidic function. Conventional hydrocracking catalysts used in petroleum refining typically consist of sulfided transition metals such as Ni-Mo or Co-Mo supported on amorphous silica-alumina or zeolitic materials like Y zeolite. These catalysts are designed to crack heavy hydrocarbons into lighter fractions while simultaneously saturating olefins and removing heteroatoms such as sulfur and nitrogen. However, FT-derived feedstocks are inherently free of sulfur and nitrogen contaminants, which alters the requirements for catalyst design. In particular, the absence of catalyst poisons allows the use of noble metals such as platinum and palladium, which offer superior hydrogenation activity and stability under low-sulfur conditions.

Early efforts in FT wax hydrocracking focused on maximizing the yield of middle distillates (C10–C20) while minimizing the formation of light gases (C1–C4) and naphtha (C5–C9). It was recognized that ideal hydrocracking—characterized by primary cracking without significant secondary reactions—could theoretically yield up to 79% middle distillates by weight from a model compound such as tricosane. Achieving this selectivity requires precise control over the balance between the HD/DHD and acidic functions, as well as optimal catalyst intimacy to ensure rapid shuttling of intermediates between active sites. Catalysts based on ultrastable Y (USY) zeolite impregnated with platinum have demonstrated near-ideal behavior under mild conditions, producing symmetrical carbon number distributions centered around half the original chain length, indicative of single scission events.

Despite these advances, conventional hydrocracking catalysts often suffer from overcracking, especially when processing real FT feeds containing oxygenates such as alcohols, carboxylic acids, and esters. These compounds can adsorb on acid sites or interact with the metal function, disrupting the delicate balance required for ideal hydrocracking. For instance, alcohols may decompose to water and alkanes, with water competitively inhibiting acid sites, thereby reducing activity but sometimes improving selectivity by suppressing excessive cracking. In contrast, carboxylic acids can poison metal sites or generate CO/CO2, which may deactivate the HD/DHD function and lead to non-ideal behavior characterized by increased light gas production.

In parallel, the production of lubricant base oils from FT waxes has driven the development of selective hydroisomerization catalysts. Unlike hydrocracking, which aims for controlled chain scission, hydroisomerization seeks to introduce skeletal branching into long-chain paraffins to lower their pour point without significant loss of molecular weight. This requires catalysts that favor isomerization over cracking, even at high conversion levels. Medium-pore zeolites with one-dimensional channel systems—such as ZSM-22 (TON framework), ZSM-23, and SAPO-11—have emerged as preferred supports due to their shape-selective properties. These materials restrict the formation of highly branched carbocations that are prone to β-scission, thereby promoting monobranched and selectively dibranched isomers with excellent cold flow properties.

A key concept in understanding the selectivity of these zeolites is "pore mouth catalysis," wherein reactions occur not deep within the pores but at or near the pore entrances. This mechanism explains the preferential formation of 2-methyl and 2,7-dimethyl isomers during n-decane conversion over ZSM-22, as the bulky transition states required for internal branching cannot fit within the narrow 10-membered ring channels. Instead, the terminal methyl groups interact with acid sites located at the pore mouths, leading to selective end-branching. Similarly, "key-lock catalysis" has been proposed to describe configurations where both ends of a long paraffin chain enter adjacent pores, allowing central branching while maintaining steric constraints that disfavor cracking.

However, the application of these concepts has been largely confined to well-defined zeolites like ZSM-22. Less attention has been paid to structurally complex materials such as ZSM-48 and its structural analogs (ZBM-30, EU-11, EU-2), which exhibit X-ray diffraction patterns indicative of intergrown polytypes with disordered frameworks. Although these materials share similar local pore topologies, their catalytic performances vary significantly depending on synthesis conditions, suggesting that subtle differences in crystal morphology, aluminum distribution, or pore mouth geometry critically influence activity and selectivity. Prior art lacks a systematic understanding of how these structural features govern hydroisomerization behavior, particularly for long-chain paraffins like octadecane.

Moreover, existing patents and literature disclose catalysts that either optimize for hydrocracking (e.g., Pt/USY, Pt/silica-alumina) or hydroisomerization (e.g., Pt/ZSM-22), but fail to address the dual challenge of tailoring catalyst architecture to the specific demands of FT feedstocks—namely, the need for high isomerization selectivity in dewaxing applications and high middle distillate yield in fuel production, all while accommodating the presence of oxygenates. There remains a critical gap in the art regarding catalysts that leverage the unique topology of intergrown medium-pore zeolites to achieve unprecedented isomerization yields with minimal cracking, particularly for C18+ paraffins. The present invention addresses this gap by identifying and optimizing a class of ZSM-48-type zeolites—specifically ZBM-30 synthesized with triethylenetetramine—that exhibit superior hydroisomerization performance, thereby enabling the efficient production of high-quality lubricant base oils from FT waxes.

## Subject-Matter of the Invention

The present invention relates to a novel hydrocracking and hydroisomerization catalyst system specifically engineered for the upgrading of Fischer-Tropsch-derived waxes, with a focus on achieving exceptional selectivity toward either high-quality middle distillates or premium lubricant base oils. The invention encompasses a bifunctional catalyst comprising a hydrogenation/dehydrogenation (HD/DHD) component and a tailored acidic support, wherein the acidic support is selected from a specific class of medium-pore zeolites exhibiting structural intergrowths and optimized pore mouth geometries. More particularly, the invention discloses that among the ZSM-48 family of zeolites—including ZSM-48, ZBM-30, EU-11, and EU-2—only certain variants, notably ZBM-30 synthesized using triethylenetetramine as a structure-directing agent, demonstrate unexpectedly high isomerization yields and low cracking rates during the hydroconversion of long-chain n-paraffins such as n-octadecane. This performance surpasses that of conventional hydroisomerization catalysts like Pt/ZSM-22 and represents a significant advancement in the field of catalytic dewaxing.

The subject-matter of the invention further includes the method of preparing said catalyst, which involves the synthesis of the zeolitic support under controlled conditions that promote the formation of specific crystal terminations and aluminum distributions conducive to pore mouth and key-lock catalysis. The HD/DHD function is preferably provided by a noble metal such as platinum, loaded at concentrations sufficient to maintain ideal bifunctional behavior—i.e., ensuring that dehydrogenation/hydrogenation steps are in quasi-equilibrium and that the rate-determining step occurs on the Brønsted acid sites. The invention also covers the use of this catalyst in integrated processes for converting Fischer-Tropsch waxes into either transportation fuels or lubricants, with the ability to tune product selectivity by adjusting reaction parameters such as temperature, pressure, and hydrogen-to-hydrocarbon ratio.

Crucially, the invention recognizes that the catalytic performance is not solely dictated by bulk properties such as Si/Al ratio or X-ray diffraction pattern, but is exquisitely sensitive to nanoscale features of the zeolite crystal surface, including the spatial arrangement of pore openings and the location of aluminum atoms near the external surface. This insight enables the rational design of catalysts that maximize the formation of desirable monobranched and centrally dibranched isomers while suppressing β-scission pathways that lead to light gas production. The invention thus provides a solution to the long-standing problem of balancing high conversion with high isomerization selectivity in the hydroprocessing of linear paraffins, offering a commercially viable route to value-added products from renewable and synthetic feedstocks.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### The Hydrocracking and Hydroisomerisation Catalyst

The catalyst according to the present invention is a bifunctional material comprising two essential components: a hydrogenation/dehydrogenation (HD/DHD) function and a tailored acidic support. The HD/DHD function is responsible for the reversible conversion of paraffins to olefins and vice versa, while the acidic support provides Brønsted acid sites that facilitate carbocation-mediated isomerization and cracking reactions. The synergy between these two functions, governed by the Weisz intimacy criterion, ensures that reaction intermediates shuttle efficiently between sites, enabling high selectivity and activity.

The HD/DHD component is preferably a noble metal, most advantageously platinum, although palladium, iridium, or rhodium may also be employed. Platinum is particularly favored due to its high hydrogenation activity, resistance to sintering, and compatibility with low-sulfur environments typical of Fischer-Tropsch feedstocks. The metal is incorporated into the catalyst via incipient wetness impregnation or ion exchange, typically at loadings ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 weight percent, with 0.3 wt% being optimal for balancing cost and performance. The metal must be in a reduced state to exhibit catalytic activity, which is achieved by calcination in air followed by reduction in hydrogen at temperatures between 400 and 600°C.

The acidic support is the defining feature of the invention and consists of a medium-pore zeolite belonging to the ZSM-48 structural family. This family includes materials designated as ZSM-48, ZBM-30, EU-11, and EU-2, all of which exhibit X-ray diffraction patterns characteristic of intergrown polytypes with orthorhombic or pseudo-orthorhombic symmetry. Despite their structural similarities, these materials display markedly different catalytic behaviors, which the present invention attributes to variations in crystal morphology, aluminum siting, and pore mouth architecture rather than bulk composition. The zeolite framework is composed of TO4 tetrahedra (where T is silicon or aluminum), with Si/Al ratios typically between 20 and 200, although the invention demonstrates that catalytic performance is not directly correlated with this parameter.

The synthesis of the zeolitic support is carried out hydrothermally using organic structure-directing agents (SDAs). Critical to the invention is the selection of the SDA, as it dictates the resulting crystal habit and surface topology. For instance, ZBM-30 synthesized with triethylenetetramine (C6H18N4) yields a catalyst with superior isomerization selectivity compared to the same material prepared with hexamethylenediamine (C6H16N2), despite nearly identical Si/Al ratios and XRD patterns. This observation underscores that the catalytic efficacy arises from nanoscale features—such as the distance between adjacent pore mouths or the density of acid sites near the crystal surface—that are influenced by the SDA but not captured by conventional characterization techniques.

After synthesis, the zeolite is subjected to calcination to remove the organic template, followed by ammonium ion exchange to convert sodium or other cations to the protonic form, thereby generating Brønsted acid sites. The final catalyst is formed by compressing the zeolite powder into pellets or extrudates of suitable size (0.25–0.50 mm diameter) for fixed-bed reactor applications. The intimate contact between metal and acid sites is ensured by uniform metal dispersion and appropriate pellet porosity, satisfying the Weisz criterion for ideal bifunctional catalysis.

### The Embodiments According to the Invention

The invention is embodied in several specific catalyst formulations and process configurations designed to maximize either middle distillate yield or lubricant base oil quality from Fischer-Tropsch waxes. In a first embodiment, the catalyst comprises platinum (0.3 wt%) supported on ZBM-30 zeolite synthesized using triethylenetetramine as the structure-directing agent. This catalyst exhibits a maximum isomerization yield of over 85% for n-octadecane at 99% conversion, with cracked product yields below 5%, significantly outperforming conventional Pt/ZSM-22 catalysts under identical conditions. The isomer distribution is characterized by a high proportion of monobranched isomers (peaking at ~65% yield) and dibranched isomers with methyl groups positioned at carbon atoms 4–6, indicative of central branching facilitated by key-lock catalysis.

In a second embodiment, the catalyst is tailored for hydrocracking applications by incorporating a mild solid acid such as silica-alumina or silicated alumina in combination with a small amount (<5 wt%) of USY zeolite to enhance activity without sacrificing selectivity. The HD/DHD function remains platinum-based, and the catalyst is operated under mild conditions (T = 280–320°C, P = 3–5 MPa, H2/HC = 500–1000 Nm3/m3) to promote ideal hydrocracking. This configuration achieves middle distillate selectivities exceeding 75% by weight from C20+ FT wax, with minimal C1–C2 formation.

A third embodiment addresses the presence of oxygenates in raw FT effluents. Here, the catalyst system includes a pre-treatment step wherein the feed is contacted with a dehydration catalyst (e.g., γ-alumina) to remove water-forming oxygenates, followed by separation of the aqueous phase. The dried organic phase is then processed over the primary hydroisomerization or hydrocracking catalyst. Alternatively, the invention contemplates operating the main catalyst in the presence of controlled amounts of alcohols (e.g., 1–2 wt% decanol), which act as moderators to fine-tune acid site availability and suppress overcracking.

All embodiments share the common feature of leveraging the unique pore mouth and key-lock catalysis mechanisms inherent to the ZSM-48 family zeolites. The invention teaches that optimal performance is achieved not by maximizing acid site density or metal loading, but by engineering the spatial arrangement of active sites at the crystal surface to favor desired reaction pathways. This is accomplished through precise control of synthesis parameters, including SDA selection, crystallization time, and post-synthesis treatments, thereby enabling reproducible manufacture of high-performance catalysts.

## The Products Obtained

### Example 1

In Example 1, the hydroisomerization performance of Pt/ZBM-30 catalysts synthesized with different structure-directing agents was evaluated using n-octadecane as a model compound. Two ZBM-30 samples were prepared: Sample A using hexamethylenediamine and Sample B using triethylenetetramine. Both samples had similar Si/Al ratios (~45) and XRD patterns indicative of the ZSM-48 intergrowth structure. The zeolites were ion-exchanged to the ammonium form, calcined, and impregnated with 0.3 wt% platinum via incipient wetness using tetrammine platinum(II) chloride. The catalysts were tested in a fixed-bed reactor at 233°C, 0.45 MPa total pressure, and H2/HC molar ratio of 13.1, with a feed consisting of 2 mol% n-octadecane in heptane.

Sample B (triethylenetetramine-derived) exhibited superior isomerization selectivity, achieving a maximum total isomer yield of 86.2% at 99.1% conversion, with only 3.8% cracked products. In contrast, Sample A reached a maximum isomer yield of 81.5% at 98.7% conversion, with 8.5% cracking. Monobranched isomer yields peaked at 65.3% for Sample B versus 60.1% for Sample A, while multibranched isomer yields were 55.8% and 48.2%, respectively. Gas chromatography analysis revealed that Sample B produced a higher proportion of isomers with methyl branches at positions 4–6, consistent with enhanced key-lock catalysis. These results demonstrate that the choice of SDA profoundly influences catalytic performance, independent of bulk composition.

### Example 2

Example 2 compares the hydroisomerization activity of various ZSM-48 family zeolites. Catalysts were prepared by supporting 0.3 wt% platinum on ZSM-48 (two samples: octylamine- and ethylenediamine-derived), ZBM-30 (as in Example 1), EU-2, and EU-11. All materials showed similar XRD patterns and Si/Al ratios between 40 and 50. Testing under identical conditions as Example 1 revealed stark differences in activity: ZBM-30 (Sample B) was the most active, requiring a contact time (W/F0) of 12.5 g·s/mol to reach 99% conversion, whereas ZSM-48 (octylamine) required 25.3 g·s/mol, and EU-2 and EU-11 showed negligible conversion even at high contact times. Isomer yields followed the same trend, with ZBM-30 (Sample B) giving 86.2% isomers, ZSM-48 (octylamine) 80.1%, and the others below 20%. This confirms that structural disorder alone does not guarantee performance; specific synthesis protocols are essential to unlock the catalytic potential of these materials.

### Example 3

Example 3 demonstrates the application of the invention in hydrocracking mode. A catalyst comprising 0.3 wt% Pt on a physical mixture of 95 wt% silica-alumina (SiO2/Al2O3 = 85/15) and 5 wt% USY zeolite (Si/Al = 30) was tested with a commercial LTFT wax (C20+ content >90%). The reaction was conducted at 300°C, 4 MPa, and H2/feed ratio of 800 Nm3/m3. The product slate contained 78.5 wt% middle distillates (C10–C20), 12.3 wt% naphtha (C5–C9), and 9.2 wt% LPG (C3–C4), with negligible C1–C2. When 1.5 wt% 1-decanol was added to the feed, conversion decreased slightly (requiring a 5°C temperature increase to compensate), but middle distillate selectivity improved to 80.1 wt%, illustrating the beneficial moderating effect of oxygenates on acid site activity. This example validates the invention’s flexibility in handling real FT feeds and tuning selectivity through feed composition.
