# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of carbon nanomaterials and, more specifically, to methods for producing high-quality graphene from petroleum-derived feedstocks. In particular, the invention provides a novel and scalable process for converting needle coke—a by-product of petroleum refining—into electrochemically exfoliated graphene (EEG) through a controlled electrochemical exfoliation technique performed in a compressed expandable chamber. This process enables the transformation of a low-value, high-emission industrial residue into a high-value, functional nanomaterial suitable for use in advanced applications such as energy storage, electronics, composites, and flexible devices. The invention further encompasses the resulting graphene product, which exhibits structural, morphological, and electrical properties consistent with few-layer graphene, as well as post-processing methods—including thermal annealing—that enhance its conductivity and performance. By repurposing needle coke, a material traditionally used in high-emission applications such as steelmaking anodes or fuel combustion, the invention offers a sustainable pathway to valorize refinery waste streams while reducing the environmental footprint associated with both petroleum refining and nanomaterial production. The disclosed method is particularly advantageous due to its compatibility with existing industrial infrastructure, its ability to operate under ambient conditions with minimal chemical inputs, and its capacity to yield graphene with low oxygen content and high crystallinity, thereby bridging the gap between petrochemical processing and next-generation materials manufacturing.

## BACKGROUND

The global demand for advanced carbon nanomaterials, particularly graphene, has grown exponentially over the past two decades due to their exceptional mechanical, electrical, thermal, and optical properties. Graphene—a single atomic layer of sp²-hybridized carbon arranged in a hexagonal lattice—has been integrated into a wide range of technologies, including lithium-ion batteries, supercapacitors, conductive inks, transparent electrodes, polymer composites, and wearable electronics. Despite its promise, the widespread commercial adoption of graphene has been hindered by challenges related to scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally sustainable production methods. Traditional approaches to graphene synthesis include mechanical exfoliation (e.g., the “Scotch tape” method), chemical vapor deposition (CVD), and chemical oxidation-reduction of graphite (e.g., Hummers’ method for graphene oxide). While CVD yields high-quality monolayer graphene suitable for electronics, it is energy-intensive, requires high-purity substrates, and is not easily scalable for bulk applications. Conversely, chemical routes often produce graphene with high defect densities, residual oxygen functionalities, and compromised electrical properties, limiting their utility in high-performance applications.

Natural graphite remains the dominant precursor for most graphene production methods, yet it is a finite resource with significant geopolitical and environmental constraints. Global reserves of natural graphite are estimated at approximately 800 million tons, but only 10–15% of this material is highly crystalline and suitable for graphene exfoliation; the remainder consists of amorphous carbon, silicate minerals, and metallic impurities that complicate processing and degrade product quality. Moreover, mining and purification of natural graphite are associated with substantial ecological disruption, water consumption, and energy use. These limitations have spurred interest in alternative carbon sources, including renewable feedstocks such as biomass and waste plastics, as well as industrial by-products from established chemical processes.

Petroleum coke (petcoke), a solid carbonaceous residue generated during the coking of heavy oil fractions in refineries, represents a compelling candidate for such repurposing. Produced in quantities exceeding one million tons annually worldwide, petcoke is categorized into three main grades: fuel coke, anode coke, and needle coke. Among these, needle coke—derived from highly aromatic feedstocks such as fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) decant oil—is distinguished by its high crystallinity, low impurity content, and aligned, needle-like microstructure. These characteristics make it ideal for manufacturing graphite electrodes used in electric arc furnaces for steel production. However, despite its value, needle coke remains a by-product of fossil fuel processing, and its conventional end-uses—whether as an anode or as a fuel—contribute significantly to greenhouse gas emissions. For instance, when combusted, petcoke emits more CO₂ per unit mass than coal, and its use in aluminum smelting releases perfluorocarbons, potent greenhouse gases.

Efforts to convert petcoke into graphene have been limited and largely unsuccessful. Prior studies have attempted to produce graphene oxide from petcoke via strong acid oxidation, but the resulting materials often lack the structural integrity and electronic properties of true graphene. Other approaches, such as ball-milling petcoke with surfactants, have yielded ambiguous products whose graphene nature is difficult to confirm due to inadequate separation protocols and inconsistent characterization. Electrochemical exfoliation (ECE)—a method that intercalates ions into layered carbon materials under an applied voltage to induce layer separation—has emerged as a promising route for graphene production due to its mild conditions, scalability, and potential for high yield. However, conventional ECE methods using graphite monoliths suffer from loss of electrical contact during exfoliation, which halts the reaction prematurely. Recent advances have introduced compressed expandable chambers that maintain electrical continuity throughout the exfoliation process, enabling high-yield production of electrochemically exfoliated graphene (EEG) from graphite. Yet, the application of this technology to petcoke, particularly needle coke, has not been systematically explored.

A critical challenge in adapting ECE to petcoke lies in the material’s inherent heterogeneity. Not all needle cokes are equally suited for exfoliation; differences in particle size, morphology, surface chemistry, and crystallinity can dramatically affect the efficiency of ion intercalation and layer separation. Furthermore, the presence of unreacted precursor, amorphous carbon fragments, and non-graphitic nanoparticles in the post-exfoliation mixture necessitates robust separation techniques to isolate pure graphene. Without such purification, the final product may be contaminated, leading to misleading characterizations and poor performance in applications. Additionally, while initial EEG products may exhibit acceptable structural features, their electrical conductivity is often suboptimal due to residual functional groups or structural defects, requiring post-synthesis treatments such as thermal annealing to achieve target performance metrics.

In light of these challenges, there exists a clear need for a reliable, scalable, and economically viable method to convert needle coke into high-quality graphene. Such a method must address feedstock selection, pre-treatment optimization, electrochemical process parameters, and post-reaction purification to ensure consistent product quality. Moreover, it should demonstrate tangible improvements in material properties—such as electrical conductivity, crystallinity, and layer count—relative to both the starting petcoke and conventional graphene precursors. The present invention fulfills this need by providing a comprehensive process that transforms needle coke fines into few-layer graphene with low oxygen content, high structural order, and tunable electrical properties, thereby establishing a new paradigm for sustainable nanomaterial production from petroleum waste streams.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and industrially scalable method for producing high-quality graphene from petroleum-derived needle coke, thereby converting a low-value, high-emission refinery by-product into a premium nanomaterial with broad technological applications. The invention is based on the discovery that certain grades of needle coke—specifically fine particulate forms with high crystallinity and angular, layered morphology—can serve as effective precursors for electrochemically exfoliated graphene (EEG) when processed under optimized electrochemical conditions within a compressed expandable chamber. This method overcomes the limitations of prior art by integrating a multi-step protocol that includes selective feedstock pre-treatment, controlled electrochemical exfoliation at elevated voltage, and a two-stage centrifugation-based purification process to isolate graphene nanosheets from unreacted coke and non-graphitic impurities.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a method for producing graphene comprising the steps of: (a) providing a needle coke feedstock characterized by a fine particle size, jagged edges, and a layered microstructure; (b) pre-treating the feedstock with nitric acid to enhance hydrophilicity and electrolyte accessibility; (c) subjecting the pre-treated feedstock to a pre-reaction centrifugation step to remove undersized particles and enrich the reactive fraction; (d) compacting the enriched feedstock within a porous membrane to form an electrically conductive monolith; (e) immersing the monolith in an aqueous electrolyte solution containing ammonium sulfate and applying a direct current voltage of approximately 12 volts for a duration of about 2 hours to induce electrochemical exfoliation; and (f) isolating the resulting graphene product through a two-step centrifugation process wherein a first low-speed spin removes large unexfoliated particles and a second high-speed spin separates graphene nanosheets from smaller non-graphitic debris.

In another aspect, the invention provides a graphene material produced by the aforementioned method, referred to herein as Coke-EEG, which exhibits structural and compositional characteristics consistent with few-layer graphene. Specifically, the Coke-EEG comprises nanosheets with lateral dimensions predominantly in the range of 1 to 3 micrometers, a hexagonally ordered crystalline lattice as confirmed by high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED), a 2D Raman peak indicative of graphitic stacking, and an oxygen-to-carbon atomic ratio of less than 0.3 as determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The material demonstrates significantly enhanced electrical conductivity relative to the parent coke, with values exceeding 50 S/m in as-produced form and up to 474 S/m following thermal annealing at 1100°C under inert atmosphere.

In a further aspect, the invention includes a post-processing annealing step that improves the electrical and structural properties of the Coke-EEG. Thermal treatment at temperatures ranging from 500°C to 1100°C for durations of 12 to 24 hours under argon flow reduces residual impurities, enhances inter-sheet connectivity, and increases electrical conductivity without introducing significant structural degradation. This annealed graphene is particularly suitable for use in lithium-ion battery anodes, where conductivities between 10 and 1000 S/m are required for optimal performance.

The invention also encompasses the use of specific needle coke grades—designated herein as CK-1—as the preferred feedstock due to their superior exfoliability compared to coarser or more rounded needle coke variants (e.g., CK-2 and CK-3). The efficacy of the process is critically dependent on the physical morphology of the starting material; only feedstocks with fine particle size, angular edges, and visible layering yield graphene with the desired structural and electronic properties. This selectivity enables refineries to identify and divert appropriate coke streams toward nanomaterial production rather than combustion or anode manufacturing, thereby aligning with sustainability goals and creating new revenue channels.

By leveraging existing petrochemical infrastructure and transforming waste into value, the present invention offers a commercially viable and environmentally responsible pathway for graphene production. It addresses key shortcomings in the prior art—including low yield, poor product purity, and reliance on finite natural graphite—while delivering a graphene product with performance metrics suitable for real-world applications in energy storage, electronics, and advanced composites.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for producing high-quality graphene from petroleum-derived needle coke, specifically through an optimized electrochemical exfoliation (ECE) process conducted in a compressed expandable chamber, followed by a rigorous purification protocol and optional thermal annealing. The invention capitalizes on the unique structural properties of certain needle coke grades to enable the scalable, cost-effective, and sustainable production of electrochemically exfoliated graphene (EEG) with low defect density, minimal oxygen content, and tunable electrical conductivity. The following detailed description outlines the essential components, process parameters, material characteristics, and operational principles that define the invention.

The method begins with the selection of an appropriate needle coke feedstock. Needle coke is a premium grade of petroleum coke produced from highly aromatic feedstocks such as fluid catalytic cracking (FCC) decant oil under controlled coking conditions. Unlike fuel coke or sponge coke, which exhibit amorphous or honeycomb-like morphologies, needle coke possesses a highly ordered, graphitic structure composed of elongated, aligned crystallites. However, not all needle cokes are equally suitable for graphene production. The invention identifies that only fine particulate needle coke—characterized by particle sizes typically below 40 micrometers, jagged and angular edges, and visible layering under scanning electron microscopy—is capable of yielding high-quality graphene through the disclosed ECE process. Coarser or more rounded needle coke variants, even when derived from similar feedstocks, fail to produce well-defined graphene nanosheets due to insufficient layer alignment and poor electrolyte intercalation kinetics. This feedstock specificity is critical and forms a foundational element of the invention.

Once the appropriate needle coke is selected, it undergoes a pre-treatment step to enhance its reactivity during electrochemical exfoliation. The raw coke is first washed with dichloromethane and deionized water to remove surface hydrocarbons and soluble impurities. It is then treated with 6 M nitric acid at 120°C for 4 hours in a sealed autoclave. This oxidative pre-treatment serves to introduce polar functional groups onto the coke surface, rendering it more hydrophilic and thus improving wettability by the aqueous electrolyte used in the subsequent ECE step. Enhanced wettability ensures uniform electrolyte penetration between the graphitic layers, facilitating efficient ion intercalation and exfoliation. After acid treatment, the material is thoroughly rinsed to neutrality and dried at 100°C, yielding a pre-treated feedstock designated as “CK-1a” in the experimental nomenclature.

Following pre-treatment, the material undergoes a pre-reaction separation step to refine the particle size distribution. The pre-treated coke is dispersed in water at a concentration of 5 mg/mL and subjected to centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes. This step removes fine, non-reactive particles that would otherwise interfere with the formation of a cohesive, electrically conductive monolith during ECE. The sediment, enriched in larger, layered particles, is collected as the working electrode material (“CK-1b”) and is essential for maintaining electrical continuity throughout the exfoliation process.

The core of the invention lies in the electrochemical exfoliation step, which is performed in a specially designed compressed expandable chamber. The CK-1b material is compacted into a hollow cellulose dialysis membrane (approximately 16 cm long and 2.5 cm wide) to form a cylindrical monolith. A platinum wire is inserted into the center of the monolith to serve as the working electrode, while a copper mesh wrapped around the exterior of the membrane acts as the counter electrode. To maintain compression and prevent disintegration of the monolith during exfoliation, an external weight of approximately 0.75 kg is placed on top of the assembly. The entire setup is submerged in 2 liters of 0.1 M ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄) electrolyte solution. A direct current voltage of 12 volts is applied across the electrodes for 2 hours. This voltage is notably higher than those typically used for graphite exfoliation (which often range from 3 to 10 V) but is necessary to overcome the higher interlayer binding energy and lower conductivity of needle coke. Voltages below 12 V result in negligible exfoliation yield, while voltages above 12 V risk damaging the dialysis membrane. During the reaction, sulfate and ammonium ions intercalate into the coke structure, generating gas evolution and internal pressure that drive layer separation, producing a mixture of exfoliated graphene, unreacted coke, and fragmented carbon debris (“CK-1c”).

After exfoliation, the reaction mixture is washed to remove residual electrolyte and then subjected to a two-step centrifugation purification process to isolate the graphene product. In the first step, the suspension is centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 minutes. The sediment, consisting of large, unexfoliated coke particles, is discarded. The supernatant, containing smaller exfoliated sheets and fine debris, is retained. In the second step, this supernatant is centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 20 minutes. Here, the graphene nanosheets sediment due to their higher density and sheet-like morphology, while smaller, non-graphitic particles remain in the supernatant and are removed. The final sediment—designated “CK-1d”—is the purified Coke-EEG product. This two-stage separation is crucial; without it, the product would be contaminated with amorphous carbon and unreacted precursor, obscuring its true graphene nature and degrading performance.

The resulting Coke-EEG exhibits structural and compositional features consistent with few-layer graphene. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) reveals sheet-like morphologies with lateral dimensions predominantly between 1 and 3 micrometers. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) confirms the presence of continuous, hexagonally packed lattices with no observable grain boundaries within the imaged fields, indicating high crystallinity. Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns display sharp, six-fold symmetric spots characteristic of graphitic stacking, in contrast to the diffuse rings observed in the parent coke, which indicate amorphous character. Raman spectroscopy provides further validation: the CK-1d sample exhibits a prominent 2D peak—a signature of few-layer graphene—absent in the parent coke, along with a reduced D/G intensity ratio, indicating lower defect density relative to the intermediate exfoliated product (CK-1c). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) reveals an oxygen-to-carbon atomic ratio of 0.29, with minimal C–O bonding, confirming that the product is closer to pristine graphene than to graphene oxide. Electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) corroborates this, showing no significant π* shoulder peak associated with high oxygen content.

The electrical properties of the Coke-EEG are markedly superior to those of the parent coke. Vacuum-filtered buckypapers of CK-1d exhibit a conductivity of approximately 56.9 S/m, compared to 0.07 S/m for the original CK-1. This enhancement is directly attributable to the isolation of sp²-conjugated graphene sheets from insulating amorphous domains. To further improve performance, the invention includes a thermal annealing step. Annealing CK-1d under argon at temperatures from 500°C to 1100°C for 12–24 hours progressively increases conductivity, with the highest value (474 S/m) achieved at 1100°C. This improvement is not solely due to deoxygenation—since oxygen content is already low—but likely results from removal of adsorbed species, healing of point defects, and improved inter-sheet contact. The annealed material meets the conductivity requirements for lithium-ion battery anodes, opening avenues for energy storage applications.

Importantly, the method is selective: when applied to coarser needle coke grades (CK-2 and CK-3), the same process fails to produce graphene-like structures, as evidenced by the absence of 2D Raman peaks, sheet-like morphology in SEM/TEM, and low electrical conductivity. This underscores the necessity of feedstock control and validates the invention’s focus on fine, layered needle coke.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, reproducible, and scalable process for converting a specific class of petroleum coke into high-value graphene, with defined pre-treatment, exfoliation, purification, and annealing steps that collectively ensure product quality and performance. The method is compatible with industrial-scale operations and offers a sustainable alternative to conventional graphene production routes.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the production of electrochemically exfoliated graphene (Coke-EEG) from fine needle coke (CK-1) is demonstrated according to the method of the present invention. The starting material, CK-1, was obtained as a fine powder with particle sizes predominantly below 40 μm, exhibiting jagged edges and visible layering under optical microscopy. The material was first washed with dichloromethane (100 mL per gram of coke) via vacuum filtration to remove hydrocarbon residues, followed by rinsing with deionized (DI) water until the filtrate was neutral. The washed coke was then pre-treated by mixing with 6 M nitric acid at a ratio of 1 mL acid per gram of coke. The mixture was sealed in a stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 120°C for 4 hours. After cooling, the solid was filtered, washed repeatedly with DI water to pH 7, and dried overnight at 100°C on a hotplate, yielding the pre-treated material designated CK-1a.

The CK-1a material was then subjected to pre-reaction separation. A 500 mg portion was dispersed in 100 mL of DI water (5 mg/mL) and sonicated for 10 minutes to ensure homogeneity. The suspension was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 minutes using a benchtop centrifuge. The supernatant, containing fine, non-reactive particles, was discarded. The sediment, enriched in larger, layered particles, was collected and dried at 60°C, yielding CK-1b.

For electrochemical exfoliation, 300 mg of CK-1b was compacted into a cellulose dialysis bag (Spectra/Por, MWCO 12–14 kDa, dimensions 16 cm × 2.5 cm), which was sealed at both ends with plastic clips. A 0.5 mm diameter platinum wire was inserted axially into the center of the coke column to serve as the working electrode. A copper mesh (100 mesh, 99.9% purity) was wrapped around the exterior of the dialysis bag to function as the counter electrode. The assembly was placed in a glass beaker, and a 750 g stainless-steel weight was positioned on top to maintain compression. The beaker was filled with 2 L of 0.1 M ammonium sulfate ((NH₄)₂SO₄) electrolyte solution. A DC power supply was connected, with the positive terminal to the platinum wire and the negative to the copper mesh. A constant voltage of 12 V was applied for 2 hours at room temperature. During the reaction, gas evolution was observed, and the coke column expanded slightly but remained intact due to the compressive force.

After the reaction, the dialysis bag was removed, and the contents were transferred to a beaker. The material was washed three times with 500 mL of DI water via vacuum filtration to remove residual electrolyte. The washed product, designated CK-1c, was then suspended in 200 mL of a 1:1 ethanol-water mixture and sonicated for 15 minutes to disperse the exfoliated material.

The CK-1c suspension was subjected to a two-step centrifugation purification. First, it was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 minutes. The sediment, consisting of large, unexfoliated particles, was discarded. The supernatant was retained and centrifuged again at 5000 rpm for 20 minutes. The resulting sediment, designated CK-1d, was collected and dried under vacuum at 60°C for 24 hours.

Characterization of CK-1d confirmed its identity as few-layer graphene. FE-SEM imaging revealed numerous sheet-like structures with lateral dimensions of 1–3 μm. HR-TEM showed continuous hexagonal lattices with interlayer spacing of ~0.34 nm, consistent with graphite. SAED displayed sharp, six-fold symmetric diffraction patterns. Raman spectroscopy exhibited a distinct 2D peak at ~2700 cm⁻¹, with an I_D/I_G ratio of 0.85, indicating low defect density. XPS analysis gave an O:C atomic ratio of 0.29, with C 1s peak deconvolution showing minimal C–O contribution. The electrical conductivity of a vacuum-filtered buckypaper was measured at 56.9 S/m using a four-point probe. The yield of CK-1d, calculated via UV-vis absorbance at 666 nm using an extinction coefficient of 1293 L/g/m, was approximately 1.6% relative to the initial CK-1b mass. This example demonstrates the successful conversion of fine needle coke into high-quality graphene using the method of the present invention.

### Example 2

This example illustrates the failure of the inventive method when applied to a coarser needle coke grade (CK-2), thereby highlighting the feedstock specificity of the invention. The CK-2 material was received as large granules (>100 μm) with rounded edges and no visible layering under optical microscopy. It was ground to a powder using a mortar and pestle to achieve a particle size comparable to CK-1, though the resulting particles retained smooth, rounded morphologies.

The ground CK-2 underwent the same pre-treatment and pre-reaction separation as described in Example 1, yielding CK-2a and CK-2b, respectively. The CK-2b material was then subjected to identical electrochemical exfoliation conditions: 300 mg compacted in a dialysis bag, 12 V applied for 2 hours in 0.1 M (NH₄)₂SO₄, with 750 g compression. The resulting product, CK-2c, was washed and purified via the two-step centrifugation protocol to yield CK-2d.

However, characterization of CK-2d revealed no evidence of graphene formation. FE-SEM images showed only fragmented, needle-like structures without sheet-like morphology. HR-TEM failed to detect any continuous graphitic lattices; instead, the material appeared amorphous or turbostratic. SAED patterns consisted of diffuse rings, indicative of short-range order only. Raman spectroscopy showed no 2D peak, and the I_D/I_G ratio remained high (~2.1), similar to the parent material. The electrical conductivity of CK-2d buckypaper was only 0.42 S/m, barely above that of the original CK-2 (0.23 S/m). XPS indicated an O:C ratio of 0.31, but with no significant change in bonding environment. These results confirm that the physical morphology of the starting coke—specifically, the presence of fine, angular, layered particles—is essential for successful graphene production. The failure of CK-2 underscores the importance of feedstock selection in the inventive method and validates the use of fine needle coke fines (CK-1) as the optimal precursor.

### Example 3

This example demonstrates the enhancement of electrical conductivity in Coke-EEG (CK-1d) through thermal annealing, as provided by the present invention. The CK-1d material produced in Example 1 was divided into four aliquots and annealed under flowing argon (100 mL/min) in a tube furnace (Thermo Scientific Lindberg/Blue M). The samples were heated at a rate of 10°C/min to the target temperatures and held for specified durations: (1) 500°C for 12 h (CK-1d A1), (2) 500°C for 24 h (CK-1d A2), (3) 900°C for 12 h (CK-1d A3), and (4) 1100°C for 12 h (CK-1d A4). After annealing, the samples were cooled to room temperature under argon and stored in a desiccator.

Each annealed sample was dispersed in water via bath sonication and vacuum-filtered to form buckypapers for conductivity testing. The electrical conductivities were measured using a four-point probe system and found to be: 250 S/m (A1), 258 S/m (A2), 345 S/m (A3), and 474 S/m (A4). The modest increase from 250 to 258 S/m upon doubling the annealing time at 500°C indicates that time has a limited effect on conductivity improvement. In contrast, raising the temperature to 1100°C nearly doubled the conductivity relative to the 500°C sample, demonstrating that temperature is the dominant factor in enhancing electrical performance.

Additional characterization was performed on CK-1d A4. Raman spectroscopy showed a slight decrease in the I_D/I_G ratio (from 0.85 to 0.78), suggesting partial defect healing. XPS revealed a marginal reduction in O:C ratio (to 0.26), but EELS showed no significant changes in the carbon K-edge, indicating that the primary mechanism for conductivity improvement is not deoxygenation but likely involves the removal of adsorbed species and improved inter-sheet contact through thermal restructuring. The final conductivity of 474 S/m falls within the 10–1000 S/m range required for lithium-ion battery anodes, making the annealed Coke-EEG suitable for energy storage applications. This example confirms that the post-annealing step of the invention effectively tailors the electrical properties of the graphene product to meet specific application requirements.
