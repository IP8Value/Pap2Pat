# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of virology has long been challenged by the rapid genetic variability and high mutation rates characteristic of single-stranded RNA (ssRNA) viruses, which include some of the most prevalent and persistent human pathogens. Among these, the caliciviruses and picornaviruses represent two of the most genetically diverse and clinically significant families. Caliciviruses, encompassing genera such as noroviruses, sapoviruses, lagoviruses, and vesiviruses, are responsible for a wide range of gastrointestinal and systemic diseases in humans and animals. Picornaviruses, which include enteroviruses, rhinoviruses, hepatoviruses, and others, are associated with conditions ranging from the common cold to poliomyelitis and hepatitis. The extraordinary diversity within these virus families arises not only from their error-prone RNA-dependent RNA polymerases but also from strong selective pressures exerted by host immune responses, particularly targeting the viral capsid proteins.

Traditional approaches to strain classification and identification have relied heavily on homology-based sequence comparisons, wherein query sequences are aligned against known reference sequences to compute similarity scores. These methods often employ arbitrary or empirically derived cut-off values to delineate taxonomic boundaries—such as genogroups, species, serotypes, or strains—based on percent identity thresholds. While such approaches have yielded useful insights, they suffer from several critical limitations. First, the cut-off values are not universally applicable across different virus genera or even within the same family, leading to inconsistent classifications. Second, when only partial genomic sequences are available—common in clinical or environmental surveillance settings—these methods lose accuracy due to insufficient phylogenetic signal. Third, the computational complexity of full-sequence alignment scales poorly with increasing dataset size, making large-scale strain screening impractical for real-time outbreak response. Moreover, many human caliciviruses, notably noroviruses, cannot be cultured in vitro, precluding traditional antigenic characterization and forcing reliance on computational inference for strain assignment.

The capsid protein, as the primary interface between the virus and the host immune system, exhibits the highest degree of sequence variability among viral genomic regions. In picornaviruses, the VP1 subunit is immunodominant and contains the majority of neutralizing epitopes, making it the gold standard for molecular typing and evolutionary studies. In caliciviruses, which possess a single capsid protein (VP1), this protein similarly serves as the principal determinant of antigenic diversity and strain specificity. Despite its variability, the capsid retains sufficient structural and functional constraints to preserve phylogenetic signal, rendering it an ideal target for computational strain prediction. However, existing bioinformatics tools fail to fully exploit the hierarchical evolutionary relationships embedded within capsid sequences. Conventional alignment-based methods treat all residues equally and do not account for the nested, tree-like structure of viral evolution, where mutations accumulate along specific lineages over time.

This limitation becomes particularly acute when attempting to classify novel or recombinant strains. Recombination—a common mechanism in ssRNA viruses—involves the exchange of genomic segments between co-infecting strains, potentially generating chimeric viruses with altered pathogenicity or immune evasion properties. Detecting recombination typically requires constructing multiple phylogenetic trees from different genomic regions and comparing their topologies, a process that is both computationally intensive and subjective. Similarly, spontaneous point mutations can obscure strain identity if they occur in key diagnostic residues, yet current methods lack robust mechanisms to distinguish between meaningful phylogenetic divergence and random noise.

In light of these challenges, there exists a pressing need for a novel, scalable, and biologically grounded approach to viral strain identification—one that leverages the inherent phylogenetic architecture of capsid sequences while remaining computationally efficient and adaptable to both complete and partial input data. Such a method would not only improve the accuracy and speed of strain classification but also provide deeper insights into the evolutionary dynamics and functional constraints governing viral capsid diversity. The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a residue-wise, partition-based comparison framework that transforms static sequence databases into dynamic, tree-structured knowledge repositories capable of resolving complex taxonomic ambiguities and detecting subtle evolutionary events such as recombination and spontaneous mutation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel computational method and associated software system for the rapid, accurate, and automated identification of viral strains within the highly divergent calicivirus and picornavirus families, based on amino acid or nucleotide sequences of viral capsid proteins. Unlike conventional homology-based approaches that rely on global sequence similarity scores and arbitrary identity thresholds, the invention implements a phylogenetically informed, residue-wise comparison strategy that exploits the hierarchical evolutionary structure of viral lineages. This method enables robust strain classification even when only partial or fragmented capsid sequences are available, and it operates with significantly reduced computational overhead compared to traditional alignment-intensive techniques.

The core of the invention lies in the construction of partitioned phylogenetic databases derived from complete capsid sequences of reference strains. These databases are generated by first aligning representative capsid sequences from each virus genus, constructing phylogenetic trees using distance-based neighbor-joining methods, and then dividing each tree into ten equally spaced evolutionary partitions from root to tips. Within each partition, the sequences are grouped according to shared ancestry, and for each group, a set of “characteristic residues” is identified—amino acids that are conserved within the group but differ systematically from those in other groups at the same alignment positions. These characteristic residues serve as phylogenetic markers that uniquely define each lineage at successive levels of taxonomic resolution, from broad genogroups down to fine-grained strain clusters.

When a target (query) capsid sequence is submitted for analysis, the system aligns it to a reference sequence and performs a sequential, partition-wise comparison against the stored characteristic residues. Starting from the second partition (which typically separates major genogroups), the system identifies the group within each partition that exhibits the highest number of matching characteristic residues. Critically, the search is constrained to only those groups that are directly descended from the best-matching group in the previous partition, thereby following a path through the phylogenetic tree and drastically reducing the number of comparisons required. This tree-guided search ensures computational efficiency while maintaining biological fidelity. To handle ambiguities—such as those arising from partial sequences, sequencing errors, or genuine mutations—the system employs a dynamic flagging mechanism: residues are only marked as “used” once a clear match is established; otherwise, they are retained for evaluation in subsequent partitions, allowing later evolutionary signals to resolve earlier uncertainties.

The invention further extends its utility to cases where the genus of the input sequence is unknown. In such scenarios, the system first performs a rapid preliminary alignment of the query against a small set of representative sequences from each genus database, selects the genus with the highest alignment score, and then proceeds with detailed strain identification within that genus. This two-stage approach ensures both speed and accuracy across diverse viral inputs.

Additionally, the partitioned residue framework enables the detection of recombination events and spontaneous mutations. By comparing strain assignments derived from different genomic regions (e.g., VP1 vs. VP2 in picornaviruses), incongruences in the inferred phylogenetic paths can pinpoint potential recombination breakpoints. Similarly, isolated mismatches in otherwise consistent residue patterns may indicate spontaneous mutations, especially when the overall tree topology remains intact. While the current implementation requires manual interpretation of such events, the underlying data structure is inherently suited for future automation.

Validated on over 300 complete and partial capsid sequences from caliciviruses and picornaviruses—including noroviruses, enteroviruses, and other clinically relevant strains—the system achieves high accuracy in strain identification, often outperforming public database annotations. It processes sequences in approximately five seconds per query on standard hardware, making it suitable for real-time epidemiological surveillance. The software is implemented in Perl with a Java-based graphical user interface, supporting both Windows and Linux operating systems, and accepts input in FASTA format.

In summary, the invention represents a paradigm shift in viral strain classification by replacing static sequence similarity with dynamic, evolution-aware residue matching. It provides a scalable, interpretable, and biologically grounded solution to the challenges posed by the extreme diversity of ssRNA viruses, with direct applications in diagnostics, outbreak tracking, vaccine design, and evolutionary virology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Capsid Embodiment of the Invention

The present invention is embodied in a computational system and method that utilizes the structural and evolutionary properties of viral capsid proteins to enable precise and efficient strain identification across the calicivirus and picornavirus families. The capsid, as the outer protein shell of the virus, is not only essential for virion assembly and host cell entry but also serves as the primary target for neutralizing antibodies, driving intense selective pressure and resulting in high sequence variability. This variability, while posing challenges for conventional classification methods, is harnessed by the present invention as a rich source of phylogenetic information. The method specifically focuses on amino acid sequences of the capsid protein(s)—VP1 in picornaviruses (which have four capsid subunits, VP1–VP4) and the single VP1 protein in caliciviruses—as these regions exhibit the strongest correlation between genetic divergence and antigenic differences.

The foundation of the invention is the creation of genus-specific phylogenetic databases derived from complete capsid sequences of reference strains. For each of the four calicivirus genera (norovirus, sapovirus, lagovirus, vesivirus) and nine picornavirus genera (including enterovirus, rhinovirus, hepatovirus, etc.), a multiple sequence alignment is performed using the ClustalW algorithm with the Gonnet-250 substitution matrix and specific gap penalties (gap opening: 10, gap extension: -1, gap separation: 0.2, end gap separation: 4). This alignment captures homologous positions across diverse strains, enabling meaningful residue comparisons. From this alignment, a phylogenetic tree is constructed using the Fitch-Margoliash distance matrix and the neighbor-joining Kitsch algorithm from the PHYLIP package, assuming a constant molecular clock. The choice of this method is validated by its robustness: the topology of capsid-based trees remains largely invariant under alternative tree-building approaches (e.g., UPGMA, minimum evolution with Poisson correction), whereas trees built from non-capsid regions (e.g., RNA polymerase) show high method-dependence, confirming the superior phylogenetic signal in capsid sequences.

Each resulting phylogenetic tree is then partitioned into ten equally spaced segments (P01–P10) along the evolutionary distance axis from the root (most ancestral) to the tips (most recent). This partitioning reflects the hierarchical nature of viral evolution: early partitions correspond to deep divergences (e.g., between genogroups), while later partitions resolve finer distinctions (e.g., between serotypes or outbreak strains). For example, in the norovirus genus tree, partition P2 cleanly separates the two major genogroups GI and GII, while partition P6 further subdivides GII into four subgroups (GIIa1–GIIa4), and partition P7 splits GIIa3 into GIIa3_1 and GIIa3_2. Each partition thus acts as a “similarity filter,” grouping sequences that share a more recent common ancestor than those in other groups of the same partition.

For each group within every partition, a set of characteristic residues is defined. These are alignment positions where the amino acid is conserved across all members of the group but differs from the consensus residue in at least one other group of the same partition. For instance, in norovirus partition P2, position 70 is Isoleucine (I) in GI and Valine (V) in GII, making it a diagnostic residue for genogroup assignment. Similarly, in enterovirus partition P2, a set of 18 residues (e.g., 40P, 42L, 44A, etc.) distinguishes the main enterovirus cluster from porcine enterovirus serotype 9. These characteristic residues are stored in a structured, two-dimensional array database, indexed by genus, partition, group, and alignment position, forming the core knowledge base of the system.

When a target capsid sequence is analyzed, the system first aligns it to a reference sequence from the appropriate genus (or determines the genus automatically if unknown). The aligned query is then compared residue-by-residue against the characteristic residues of each partition, starting from P2. In each partition, the system counts matches between the query and each group’s characteristic residues and selects the group with the highest match count. The search is then restricted to descendant groups in the next partition, following the phylogenetic tree structure. This progressive narrowing ensures that the system efficiently converges on the most likely strain without exhaustive comparisons. Ambiguities—such as ties in match counts or no matches—are handled by deferring residue evaluation to subsequent partitions, preserving information for later resolution. For example, a partial norovirus sequence (4JP3) showing conflicting signals at P2 (70V suggesting GII, but 20Q suggesting GI) was correctly assigned to GI in P3 based on residue 44A, demonstrating the system’s ability to resolve mixed signals.

The method is equally effective for partial sequences, as demonstrated by successful strain identification of fragments as short as 20% of the full capsid length. Because the characteristic residues are distributed across the entire protein, even small fragments often contain sufficient diagnostic positions to enable classification. Moreover, the system’s reliance on multiple residues per partition provides redundancy against sequencing errors or localized mutations. In validation tests, the system correctly identified strains for over 200 calicivirus and 100 picornavirus sequences, including challenging cases like the poliovirus-2 strain, which was distinguished from closely related enteroviruses only in partition P9.

The capsid embodiment of the invention thus provides a powerful, evolutionarily grounded framework for viral strain identification that overcomes the limitations of traditional methods. By encoding phylogenetic relationships directly into residue-level databases and leveraging tree-guided search algorithms, it achieves high accuracy, speed, and robustness, making it an indispensable tool for modern virology.

## CONCLUSIONS

The present invention establishes a transformative approach to the classification and identification of viral strains within the calicivirus and picornavirus families, addressing longstanding limitations of conventional homology-based methods through a novel, phylogenetically informed computational framework. By focusing on the capsid protein—the epicenter of viral antigenic diversity and evolutionary innovation—the invention capitalizes on the hierarchical structure of viral evolution to deliver unprecedented accuracy, speed, and robustness in strain prediction. The core innovation lies in the conversion of static sequence databases into dynamic, partitioned phylogenetic repositories, where characteristic residues serve as lineage-specific markers that guide a tree-constrained search process. This methodology not only resolves ambiguities inherent in partial or mutated sequences but also provides a scalable solution for real-time epidemiological surveillance in an era of rapidly emerging viral threats.

Extensive validation across more than 300 complete and partial capsid sequences demonstrates the system’s reliability in correctly assigning strains even when public database annotations are inconsistent or incomplete. For instance, the invention reclassified a norovirus sequence (5TP1) from the GII.4 cluster to GII.3 based on residue-level evidence, highlighting its potential to refine existing taxonomic frameworks. Similarly, it successfully distinguished poliovirus-2 from a dense cluster of related enteroviruses, a feat accomplished only in the ninth evolutionary partition, underscoring the method’s sensitivity to fine-grained phylogenetic signals. The system’s average processing time of approximately five seconds per sequence renders it practical for high-throughput applications, such as outbreak investigation or environmental monitoring, where rapid turnaround is critical.

Beyond strain identification, the invention offers valuable insights into viral evolution and function. The characteristic residues that define phylogenetic groups are often located in structurally or functionally important regions of the capsid, suggesting that the method may indirectly capture biologically relevant variation. Furthermore, the partitioned architecture provides a natural framework for detecting recombination and spontaneous mutations, offering a pathway toward more comprehensive genomic surveillance. While the current implementation requires manual interpretation of such events, the underlying data structure is inherently extensible to automated recombination detection in future versions.

In conclusion, the invention represents a significant advancement in computational virology, bridging the gap between raw sequence data and actionable biological insight. By embedding evolutionary principles directly into the strain identification process, it provides a robust, interpretable, and future-proof solution to the challenges posed by the extraordinary diversity of ssRNA viruses. Its applications span diagnostics, vaccine development, antiviral research, and public health response, making it a vital tool for managing the ongoing threat of viral epidemics.

## Availability and Requirements

The software implementing the present invention, designated RECOVIR, is made available to the scientific and public health communities under the following specifications. The project home pages for downloading the software, accessing documentation, and obtaining updates are hosted on institutional servers and are publicly accessible via standard web browsers. The software is compatible with two major operating system platforms: Microsoft Windows XP and various distributions of the Linux operating system, ensuring broad accessibility across academic, clinical, and governmental computing environments. The core functional modules of RECOVIR are implemented in the Perl programming language, chosen for its flexibility in text processing and bioinformatics applications, while the user interface is developed in Java to provide a consistent, cross-platform graphical experience. 

To run the software on a Linux system, particularly in remote or server-based configurations, X-Windows support is required; this can be facilitated through compatibility layers such as Cygwin when accessing the program from a Windows client. The software accepts input sequences exclusively in the FASTA format, a widely adopted standard in bioinformatics, and automatically filters out any non-alphabet characters or whitespace to ensure parsing integrity. There is no upper limit on the number of input sequences or files that can be processed in a single session, enabling batch analysis of large datasets. The graphical user interface provides intuitive controls for specifying the input sequence genus (if known), selecting reference sequences for alignment, and configuring output detail levels, ranging from concise summaries to comprehensive partition-wise match reports. All software components, including source code, executable binaries, and sample databases, are distributed without licensing restrictions for non-commercial research use, in accordance with standard academic practice. Regular updates to the reference databases will be provided to incorporate newly characterized viral strains, ensuring the system remains current with the evolving landscape of viral diversity.
