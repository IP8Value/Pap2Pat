# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) represents a complex neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent deficits in social communication and interaction across multiple contexts, as well as restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities. The diagnostic criteria for ASD have evolved significantly over recent decades, reflecting an increasing understanding of the heterogeneity and spectrum nature of this condition. Epidemiological studies indicate a marked increase in the prevalence of ASD, rising from approximately 0.64% in 2002 to 1.14% in 2008, a rate of increase that surpasses that observed for other developmental disabilities. This growing prevalence has significant implications for public health, healthcare systems, educational institutions, and families, given the substantial lifelong support needs often associated with ASD and the considerable economic burden it imposes. Despite extensive research efforts, the precise biochemical, genetic, and environmental etiologies underlying ASD remain incompletely understood, presenting a major challenge to the development of objective diagnostic tools and targeted therapeutic interventions.

Currently, the diagnosis of ASD relies exclusively on behavioral observation and psychometric assessment conducted by trained clinicians using standardized instruments such as the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS) and the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R). While these tools are considered the gold standard, they are inherently subjective, time-consuming, require specialized expertise, and are typically not administered until after the age of two or three, thereby delaying potential early intervention. The absence of an objective, biological diagnostic test for ASD has spurred intense research into the identification of reliable biomarkers—measurable biological indicators that can signal the presence or severity of the disorder. A successful biomarker for ASD would need to demonstrate high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing individuals with ASD from neurotypical (NEU) controls at the individual level, rather than merely showing statistically significant differences in population averages. Such a biomarker could revolutionize clinical practice by enabling earlier, more accurate, and less subjective diagnosis, facilitating timely intervention, stratifying patients for personalized treatment approaches, and serving as an objective outcome measure in clinical trials.

In the search for ASD biomarkers, researchers have turned their attention to various biological substrates, including genetic markers, proteomic profiles, metabolomic signatures, neuroimaging findings, and electrophysiological measures. Among these, the investigation of fatty acid metabolism has emerged as a particularly promising avenue due to the critical role that polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) play in brain structure and function. PUFAs, specifically the omega-3 (n-3) and omega-6 (n-6) families, are essential components of neuronal membranes, where they influence membrane fluidity, the function of embedded receptors and ion channels, and the production of bioactive lipid mediators involved in inflammation and neuroprotection. The two most abundant long-chain PUFAs in the human brain are docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; 22:6n-3), an omega-3 fatty acid, and arachidonic acid (AA; 20:4n-6), an omega-6 fatty acid. These are derived from their respective dietary precursors, alpha-linolenic acid (ALA; 18:3n-3) and linoleic acid (LA; 18:2n-6), through a series of elongation and desaturation steps. Given their fundamental importance in neuroplasticity, neurogenesis, and synaptogenesis, any dysregulation in PUFA metabolism could theoretically contribute to the neurodevelopmental alterations observed in ASD.

Post-mortem brain studies in individuals with ASD have revealed a range of structural and functional abnormalities that may be linked to altered lipid metabolism, including changes in synaptic connectivity and plasticity, variations in neuronal size and density in key limbic regions like the amygdala and hippocampus, reductions in cerebellar Purkinje cells, evidence of chronic neuroinflammation, and disruptions in activity-dependent gene expression. At the molecular level, several signaling pathways implicated in ASD pathogenesis, such as the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, calcium signaling, and the balance between excitatory (glutamatergic) and inhibitory (GABAergic) neurotransmission, are known to be modulated by PUFAs and their derivatives. This convergence of evidence has led to the hypothesis that individuals with ASD may exhibit a distinct fatty acid profile, potentially characterized by deficiencies in n-3 PUFAs like DHA and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and/or an imbalance in the n-6 to n-3 ratio.

To test this hypothesis, numerous studies have analyzed fatty acid concentrations in accessible peripheral tissues, primarily plasma and erythrocyte (red blood cell) membranes, under the assumption that these levels are moderately correlated with brain fatty acid composition. The existing literature on this topic, however, presents a highly inconsistent and often contradictory picture. Some studies report significantly lower levels of AA, DHA, and EPA in individuals with ASD compared to NEU controls, while others find no significant differences or even report elevated levels of certain fatty acids. A critical methodological issue contributing to this inconsistency is the disparate ways in which results are reported and analyzed. Many studies focus solely on comparing the mean or median concentrations of individual fatty acids between groups using traditional null hypothesis significance testing (e.g., t-tests, ANOVA). While such an approach can identify population-level trends, it fails to address the fundamental question of whether a given fatty acid measurement can reliably classify an individual as having ASD or being NEU. This is a crucial distinction, as a biomarker must perform well at the individual level to be clinically useful. Metrics such as the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (C-statistic), sensitivity, and specificity are far more appropriate for evaluating a biomarker's diagnostic utility, as they directly quantify the degree of separation between the two populations' distributions, accounting for both the central tendency and the spread of the data.

Furthermore, the majority of previous studies suffer from significant limitations, including small sample sizes that are underpowered to detect anything but large effect sizes, inadequate matching of cases and controls for critical confounding variables like age, sex, diet, and medication use, and the frequent exclusion of raw, individual-level data from publication, which hinders independent validation and meta-analysis. The lack of a standardized protocol for fatty acid analysis and data reporting further complicates cross-study comparisons. In light of these challenges, there remains a critical unmet need for a rigorous, large-scale, and methodologically sound investigation into the potential of erythrocyte fatty acid profiles as a diagnostic biomarker for ASD, one that moves beyond simple group mean comparisons to a comprehensive evaluation of individual-level classification performance using robust statistical and machine learning techniques.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure provides a comprehensive and definitive analysis demonstrating that erythrocyte-membrane fatty acid profiles are not a viable diagnostic biomarker for autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Contrary to hypotheses suggested by some prior art, the inventors have discovered through a large, well-controlled study that the concentrations of a broad panel of fatty acids—including key polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) such as arachidonic acid (AA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA)—in the erythrocyte membranes of children with ASD are not sufficiently distinct from those of age- and gender-matched neurotypical (NEU) controls to enable reliable individual-level classification.

The core of the invention lies in the methodological framework and its application, which conclusively shows that while minor, statistically significant differences in the population means of certain fatty acids may occasionally be observed (e.g., a modest reduction in dihomo-γ-linoleic acid (DGLA) in the ASD cohort), these differences are not of a magnitude or consistency that translates into meaningful diagnostic power. The inventors have established that the probability density functions (PDFs) of all measured individual fatty acids, as well as their common ratios (e.g., DHA/AA, n-3/n-6), exhibit substantial overlap between the ASD and NEU populations. This overlap is quantified by C-statistics (area under the ROC curve) that are consistently close to 0.5, the value expected for a random classifier, thereby demonstrating the inability of any single fatty acid to serve as a diagnostic marker.

Moreover, the inventors have extended this analysis to a multivariate context, employing Fisher Discriminant Analysis (FDA) to determine if a linear combination of all measured fatty acid variables could yield a more powerful composite biomarker. The resulting multivariate classifier achieved a C-statistic of only 0.76, a performance level that is insufficient for clinical diagnostic use. Critically, the inventors recognize that without a rigorous validation strategy, such as cross-validation, this fitting performance is likely to be overly optimistic and would degrade further when applied to new, unseen data. Given the already poor performance on the training set, the inventors conclude that pursuing a validated multivariate model based on these fatty acids is futile.

The disclosure further highlights a fundamental flaw in much of the prior art: the conflation of statistically significant differences in group means with clinically useful diagnostic accuracy. By re-analyzing data extracted from key published studies, the inventors demonstrate that even when a study reports a "significant" difference in a fatty acid like AA or DHA, the actual distribution of values between groups shows considerable overlap, resulting in low C-statistics (e.g., 0.62 or 0.75). This insight underscores that the primary failure of fatty acid biomarkers for ASD is not necessarily a lack of any biological difference, but rather the insufficient magnitude of that difference relative to the natural biological variability within each population.

In summary, this disclosure provides a clear and evidence-based conclusion that measurements of erythrocyte-membrane fatty acid profiles, whether analyzed univariately or in a multivariate combination, do not constitute a reliable or accurate method for diagnosing ASD in individual subjects. This finding has significant implications for the field, as it redirects research efforts away from a dead-end avenue and towards more promising biomarker candidates. It also serves as a cautionary tale for biomarker development in general, emphasizing the necessity of evaluating diagnostic performance at the individual level using appropriate metrics like the C-statistic, rather than relying solely on traditional hypothesis testing of group means.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for definitively assessing the diagnostic utility of erythrocyte-membrane fatty acid profiles for autism spectrum disorder (ASD). The method comprises obtaining a biological sample, specifically whole blood, from a subject; isolating erythrocytes from said sample; quantifying the concentration of a comprehensive panel of fatty acids within the erythrocyte membranes; and then subjecting these quantitative measurements to a rigorous analytical pipeline designed to evaluate their capacity to discriminate between a subject with ASD and a neurotypical (NEU) subject. The key innovation of this method is its focus on individual-level classification performance as the primary criterion for biomarker validity, moving decisively beyond the conventional and often misleading reliance on statistical tests of group mean differences.

In a preferred embodiment, the biological sample is a venous blood draw collected in a standard anticoagulant tube (e.g., EDTA). The erythrocytes are separated from plasma and buffy coat via centrifugation. The isolated erythrocyte pellet is then washed to remove any residual plasma contaminants. The fatty acids are extracted from the washed erythrocyte membranes using a validated lipid extraction protocol, such as the Folch or Bligh & Dyer method. Following extraction, the fatty acids are converted to their fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs) through a process of transesterification, typically using a strong base like sodium methoxide or an acid catalyst like sulfuric acid in methanol. The resulting FAMEs are then analyzed by gas chromatography equipped with a flame ionization detector (GC-FID), a gold-standard technique for fatty acid profiling. The chromatographic peaks are identified by comparison with known FAME standards, and their areas are integrated to determine the relative abundance of each fatty acid. All measurements are normalized to the total concentration of fatty acids in the sample to account for any variations in sample handling or extraction efficiency, yielding results expressed as a percentage of total fatty acids.

The panel of fatty acids quantified in this method is extensive and includes, but is not limited to, the following: saturated fatty acids such as palmitic acid (16:0) and stearic acid (18:0); monounsaturated fatty acids such as palmitoleic acid (17:1n-7), oleic acid (18:1n-9), and their trans isomers palmitelaidic acid (16:1n-9 trans) and elaidic acid (18:1n-9 trans); and polyunsaturated fatty acids from both the omega-6 (n-6) and omega-3 (n-3) families. The n-6 PUFAs include linoleic acid (LA, 18:2n-6), dihomo-γ-linoleic acid (DGLA, 20:3n-6), and arachidonic acid (AA, 20:4n-6). The n-3 PUFAs include eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5n-3) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22:6n-3). In addition to the absolute or relative concentrations of these individual fatty acids, the method also calculates and analyzes key metabolic ratios that are often of biological interest, such as DHA/AA, EPA/AA, and the total n-3 to total n-6 fatty acid ratio (n-3/n-6).

The heart of the invention resides in the statistical and computational analysis of these quantitative measurements. The analytical pipeline is a two-stage process. The first stage is univariate analysis. For each fatty acid and ratio in the panel, the method performs a comprehensive comparison of its distribution in a cohort of subjects diagnosed with ASD against its distribution in a carefully matched cohort of NEU control subjects. This comparison is not limited to a simple t-test. Instead, the method employs a robust sequence of statistical tests to handle the diverse nature of real-world biological data. First, the normality of the distribution for each group is assessed using the Anderson-Darling test. If both groups are found to be normally distributed, the equality of their variances is tested using the F-test. Based on these results, either a Student’s t-test (for equal variances) or Welch’s t-test (for unequal variances) is used to test for a difference in means. If one or both groups fail the normality test, the method uses the two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to assess if the two samples come from the same underlying distribution. If they are found to be of the same shape, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test is used to compare medians; otherwise, Welch’s test is used as a robust alternative for comparing means of non-normal distributions. The result of this univariate hypothesis testing is a p-value, which indicates the statistical significance of any observed difference in central tendency.

However, the p-value is not the final arbiter of biomarker utility in this method. The second and more critical component of the univariate analysis is the construction of a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve. The ROC curve is generated by plotting the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1-specificity) across all possible threshold values for the fatty acid measurement. The area under this ROC curve, known as the C-statistic, is the primary metric of diagnostic performance. A C-statistic of 0.5 indicates that the biomarker is no better than random chance at classifying a subject, while a C-statistic of 1.0 represents perfect discrimination. In the context of this invention, a C-statistic below a clinically relevant threshold (e.g., 0.8 or 0.9) is deemed insufficient for a diagnostic test, regardless of the p-value from the hypothesis test.

The second stage of the analytical pipeline is multivariate analysis. Recognizing that a single biomarker is often inadequate, the method investigates whether a combination of multiple fatty acid measurements can create a more powerful composite biomarker. To this end, the method employs Fisher Discriminant Analysis (FDA). FDA is a supervised dimensionality reduction technique that finds a linear combination of the input variables (the fatty acid measurements) that best separates two or more classes of objects (ASD vs. NEU). The algorithm projects the high-dimensional data onto a single discriminant axis (the FDA score) that maximizes the ratio of between-class variance to within-class variance. The resulting FDA scores for all subjects are then analyzed in the same manner as the univariate measurements: their distributions for the ASD and NEU groups are visualized, and an ROC curve is constructed to calculate the C-statistic for the multivariate classifier. This C-statistic represents the overall diagnostic accuracy achievable by optimally combining all the available fatty acid information.

A crucial aspect of the disclosed method is its pragmatic approach to model validation. The inventors understand that a classifier’s performance on the data it was trained on (its "fitting" performance) is almost always superior to its performance on new, independent data (its "prediction" performance). Overfitting, where the model learns the noise in the training data rather than the true underlying signal, is a constant risk, especially in high-dimensional settings with a limited number of samples. Therefore, the method includes a decision rule based on the initial fitting performance. If the multivariate classifier’s C-statistic on the training data is already too low to be of clinical value (as was found to be the case, with a C-statistic of 0.76), the method concludes that investing resources in a formal cross-validation scheme would be unnecessary and unlikely to yield a clinically useful predictor. This is a key practical insight that saves time and resources by avoiding the pursuit of a fundamentally weak signal.

Furthermore, the method accounts for a major potential confounder: dietary intake. Since the primary source of long-chain n-3 PUFAs like DHA and EPA is dietary consumption of seafood, the method incorporates a step to assess and control for this variable. In a preferred embodiment, a detailed dietary questionnaire is administered to the subject’s caregiver to ascertain the average number of seafood meals consumed per month. The method then performs a regression analysis to quantify the correlation between seafood intake and the levels of each fatty acid in the erythrocyte membranes. This analysis serves two purposes: first, it validates the biological plausibility of the measurements by confirming the expected positive correlation between seafood intake and DHA/EPA levels; and second, it ensures that any observed differences between the ASD and NEU groups are not simply an artifact of differing dietary habits. In the study underpinning this invention, the distribution of seafood intake was found to be similar between the two cohorts, and the correlations between intake and fatty acid levels were consistent across groups, strengthening the conclusion that the lack of a diagnostic signal is a true biological finding and not a dietary confound.

The method described herein was applied to a large, well-characterized cohort of 63 children with ASD and 49 age- and gender-matched NEU controls. All participants were free from nutritional supplements and abnormal diets for at least two months prior to enrollment, ensuring a baseline state of fatty acid metabolism. The results of this application form the basis of the invention’s core claim: that erythrocyte fatty acid profiles are not a viable diagnostic biomarker for ASD. The univariate analysis revealed that only one fatty acid, DGLA, showed a statistically significant difference in mean concentration (p=0.03), with the ASD group being 8% lower. However, the C-statistic for DGLA was a mere 0.62, indicating very poor individual-level classification ability. All other individual fatty acids and ratios had C-statistics hovering around 0.5. The multivariate FDA classifier, which incorporated all measured variables, achieved a C-statistic of 0.76, a level of performance that is inadequate for a clinical diagnostic test. The inventors’ method, by its very design and its focus on the C-statistic as the ultimate metric, provides a clear, unambiguous, and clinically relevant answer to the question of whether these fatty acids can be used to diagnose ASD: they cannot.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Univariate Analysis of Individual Fatty Acids**

In this example, the method of the present invention was applied to analyze the univariate diagnostic potential of a panel of 11 individual fatty acids and 4 key fatty acid ratios in a cohort of 63 ASD and 49 NEU subjects. Erythrocyte membranes were processed and analyzed as described in the Detailed Description. For each of the 15 measurements, a full statistical workflow was executed. The distributions of the data were first assessed for normality. For instance, the distribution of Arachidonic Acid (AA) in both the ASD and NEU groups failed the Anderson-Darling normality test (p < 0.05). Consequently, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to compare the shapes of the two distributions, which were found to be not significantly different (p > 0.05). Therefore, the Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare the medians, which yielded a p-value of 1.00, indicating no significant difference. The kernel density estimate (KDE) plots of the two distributions showed near-perfect overlap. The ROC curve constructed from these distributions was virtually diagonal, resulting in a C-statistic of 0.51, confirming that AA alone is useless as a diagnostic marker.

A different pattern was observed for Dihomo-γ-linoleic acid (DGLA). Both groups passed the Anderson-Darling test for normality, and the F-test confirmed equal variances. A Student’s t-test was therefore performed, revealing a statistically significant difference in means (p = 0.03), with the ASD group having a lower mean concentration (2.045%) compared to the NEU group (2.226%). However, the KDE plots revealed that despite this shift in the mean, the two distributions still overlapped substantially. The ROC curve, while slightly above the diagonal, was far from ideal, yielding a C-statistic of only 0.62. This example perfectly illustrates the central tenet of the invention: a statistically significant p-value does not equate to a useful diagnostic biomarker. The same analytical workflow was applied to all other fatty acids (DHA, EPA, Elaidic acid, Linoleic acid, Oleic acid, Palmitelaidic acid, Palmitic acid, Palmitoleic acid, Stearic acid) and ratios (DHA/AA, EPA/AA, n-3/n-6, Total PUFA). In every single case, the C-statistic was between 0.50 and 0.62, conclusively demonstrating the lack of individual-level diagnostic power for any of these measurements.

**Example 2: Multivariate Classification Using Fisher Discriminant Analysis**

This example demonstrates the application of the multivariate stage of the method. All 15 fatty acid measurements from Example 1 were used as input variables for a Fisher Discriminant Analysis (FDA) to construct a composite biomarker. The FDA algorithm computed a single discriminant score for each of the 112 subjects (63 ASD + 49 NEU) that represented the optimal linear combination of the 15 inputs for separating the two groups. The distribution of these FDA scores for the ASD and NEU cohorts was then visualized using KDE. The plot showed two overlapping bell-shaped curves, with the ASD distribution shifted slightly to the left of the NEU distribution. An ROC curve was generated by using the FDA score as the continuous classifier variable. The area under this curve, the C-statistic, was calculated to be 0.76. While this is an improvement over any single fatty acid, a C-statistic of 0.76 corresponds to a test with only moderate discriminatory ability. In a clinical setting, such a test would misclassify a substantial proportion of individuals, leading to an unacceptably high rate of false positives and false negatives. Following the pragmatic validation rule of the disclosed method, no further cross-validation was performed because the initial fitting performance was deemed insufficient for clinical translation. This example confirms that even a sophisticated multivariate model cannot extract a reliable diagnostic signal from this particular set of biological measurements.

**Example 3: Re-analysis of Prior Art Data to Illustrate the Method's Insight**

To validate the core insight of the invention—that prior art conclusions are often flawed due to a focus on p-values over C-statistics—the method was applied to data extracted from two prominent published studies, Brigandi et al. (2015) and Yui et al. (2016). From the figures in these publications, individual data points for AA and DHA were manually extracted using image analysis software. The inventors then treated this extracted data as if it were their own and applied their full analytical pipeline. For the AA data from Brigandi et al., a t-test on the extracted data confirmed a statistically significant lower mean in the ASD group (p < 0.05). However, when the inventors constructed the PDFs and the ROC curve, they found a C-statistic of only 0.62. Similarly, for the DHA data from the same study, a significant difference in means was confirmed, but the C-statistic was a low 0.65. The same pattern was observed in the data from Yui et al. This re-analysis powerfully demonstrates that the method of the present invention provides a more accurate and clinically relevant assessment of biomarker performance than the methods used in the prior art. It shows that the apparent "positive" findings in those studies, when viewed through the correct lens of individual-level classification, actually represent very weak and clinically unusable signals. This example serves to generalize the invention’s conclusion beyond the specific cohort studied by the inventors, suggesting that the entire body of literature on fatty acid biomarkers for ASD may be over-interpreted.

**Example 4: Assessment of Dietary Confounding via Seafood Intake Regression**

This final example details the method’s step to control for dietary confounding. Caregivers of all 112 subjects completed a questionnaire reporting the average number of seafood meals their child consumed per month. The distribution of this variable was found to be similar between the ASD (median: 2.0 meals/month) and NEU (median: 2.5 meals/month) groups (p > 0.05, Mann-Whitney U test). A linear regression was then performed for each fatty acid, with seafood meals per month as the independent variable and the fatty acid percentage as the dependent variable, separately for the ASD group, the NEU group, and the combined cohort. As expected, DHA and EPA showed significant positive correlations with seafood intake in all three analyses (e.g., for DHA in the combined cohort, r = 0.374, p < 0.001). The n-3/n-6 ratio and the DHA/AA ratio also showed strong positive correlations. Conversely, stearic acid showed a significant negative correlation in the NEU group (r = -0.498, p < 0.001), possibly reflecting a displacement effect from increased PUFA intake. The fact that these biologically plausible correlations were observed validates the quality of both the fatty acid measurements and the dietary data. More importantly, the similarity in seafood intake between groups and the consistency of the intake-fatty acid relationships across groups provide strong evidence that the lack of a diagnostic signal for ASD is not an artifact of uncontrolled dietary differences, but a genuine reflection of the underlying biology. This example underscores the method’s robustness and thoroughness in accounting for potential confounders.
