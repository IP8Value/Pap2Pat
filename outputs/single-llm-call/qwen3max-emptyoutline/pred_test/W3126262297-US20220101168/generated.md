# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of quantum computing, and more specifically to photonic architectures for scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computation. The invention provides a novel system and method for implementing measurement-based quantum computing using hybrid resource states composed of both Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) encoded qubits and Gaussian squeezed states of light. This architecture enables room-temperature operation while maintaining compatibility with probabilistic sources of non-Gaussian resources, thereby overcoming significant barriers to scalability that have plagued previous photonic quantum computing approaches. The invention further encompasses specialized decoding procedures tailored to handle the unique error structure introduced by the hybrid nature of the resource state, as well as a planar chip configuration that facilitates fabrication using existing silicon photonics technology.

## BACKGROUND

Quantum computing represents a paradigm shift in computational capabilities, promising exponential speedups for certain classes of problems that are intractable for classical computers. Among the various physical platforms being explored for quantum computation, photonic systems offer several compelling advantages that make them particularly attractive for scalable implementations. Photonic quantum computers can operate at room temperature, eliminating the need for complex and expensive cryogenic infrastructure required by superconducting and trapped ion systems. This room-temperature operation enables full miniaturization and mass manufacturing using established silicon electronics and photonics fabrication techniques, potentially leading to rapid scaling to large numbers of qubits. Furthermore, photonic systems are intrinsically compatible with existing optical communication infrastructure, facilitating high-fidelity connections between multiple quantum processing modules without the noisy transduction steps required in other platforms. The temporal degrees of freedom available in photonic systems also provide flexibility in implementing various quantum error-correcting codes, including high-dimensional encodings that can enhance fault tolerance.

Despite these advantages, photonic quantum computing faces significant challenges, particularly in the generation and manipulation of the quantum states required for universal computation. Current architectures for scalable photonic quantum computing can be broadly categorized into two approaches, each with its own limitations. The first approach leverages continuous-variable (CV) entangled resource states to implement computation on discrete-variable (DV) information encoded in bosonic modes. While the CV resource states required for this approach can be generated deterministically and scalably using Gaussian operations, these architectures still require DV resources—specifically, non-Gaussian states such as GKP qubits—to be generated on-demand and deterministically. This requirement imposes infeasible hardware demands given current technological capabilities, as the generation of high-quality non-Gaussian states typically involves probabilistic processes with low success rates.

The second class of architectures involves generating entangled resource states composed entirely of bosonic qubits themselves. While such resource states possess inherent resilience to noise due to the properties of the bosonic encoding, they are extremely difficult to produce at scale. The generation of individual bosonic qubits is often probabilistic, and the operations required to combine them into larger entangled states face similar probabilistic limitations. For example, cat-basis and GKP encodings suffer from non-deterministic state generation, while dual-rail encoding additionally requires non-deterministic entangling or "fusion" gates to grow cluster states. These probabilistic elements impose formidable multiplexing requirements that become increasingly prohibitive as the system scales to larger numbers of qubits.

A critical component in many photonic quantum computing architectures is the Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) encoding, which represents qubits as two-dimensional subspaces within the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space of a bosonic mode. GKP qubits offer several advantages that make them particularly suitable for optical implementations. Most importantly, an important class of quantum gates—specifically, the Clifford operations that constitute the majority of operations required for fault-tolerant quantum computation—can be implemented using only Gaussian resources, which are readily available and easily implementable on integrated photonic devices. Additionally, GKP qubits possess intrinsic robustness to common noise sources such as optical losses and small displacements in phase space, with the ability to perform error correction through measurements of stabilizer operators. However, the generation of high-quality GKP states remains a significant challenge, with most proposed methods being either non-deterministic or requiring experimentally challenging conditions such as strong optical nonlinearities or coherent interactions with matter.

Recent advances in photon-number-resolving (PNR) detectors have revitalized interest in post-selection approaches for generating non-Gaussian states, particularly through Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) devices. GBS devices utilize Gaussian operations—displacement, squeezing, and linear interference—followed by photon-counting measurements to herald the preparation of desired non-Gaussian output states. When specific photon-number patterns are observed in the detectors, the unmeasured mode emerges in a state approximating a GKP qubit. While this process is inherently probabilistic, the success probability can be enhanced through multiplexing, where multiple GBS devices are operated simultaneously to increase the likelihood of successful state generation. However, achieving near-deterministic generation of GKP qubits through multiplexing alone requires prohibitively large numbers of devices, especially as the target fidelity increases.

Existing approaches to dealing with the probabilistic nature of GKP state generation have typically treated failed generation attempts as erasure errors, effectively removing those sites from the computational lattice. This approach, while conceptually straightforward, leads to significant overheads and potential connectivity issues as the erasure probability increases. Alternative strategies based on lattice renormalization have been proposed to handle defects in the computational resource state, but these methods often sacrifice computational efficiency or introduce additional complexity. The fundamental challenge remains: how to design an architecture that can accommodate probabilistic sources of non-Gaussian resources while maintaining the scalability and fault tolerance required for practical quantum computation.

Another significant limitation of existing photonic architectures relates to their physical implementation and scalability. Many proposed schemes require increasing optical path lengths, longer time delays, or more precise spectral resolution as the number of qubits grows, leading to exponentially growing losses that ultimately prevent indefinite scaling. Additionally, some architectures require complex three-dimensional waveguide structures or intersecting optical paths that are difficult to fabricate using standard planar photonic technologies. These physical constraints represent substantial barriers to the practical realization of large-scale photonic quantum computers, even if the fundamental challenges of state generation could be overcome.

The present invention addresses these challenges through a novel hybrid approach that combines the best aspects of both CV and DV architectures while mitigating their respective limitations. By introducing a hybrid resource state that incorporates both GKP qubits and readily available squeezed vacuum states, the invention provides a pathway to scalable photonic quantum computation that is compatible with probabilistic state generation sources. The architecture further includes specialized decoding procedures that account for the unique error structure introduced by the hybrid nature of the resource state, enabling effective error correction even in the presence of significant swap-out probabilities. The physical implementation is designed as a planar chip configuration that leverages existing silicon photonics fabrication techniques, ensuring compatibility with mass manufacturing processes while maintaining the local structure of noise essential for fault tolerance.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive architecture for scalable, fault-tolerant photonic quantum computation that overcomes the fundamental limitations of existing approaches through the use of hybrid resource states and specialized decoding procedures. At its core, the invention introduces a novel computational resource state comprising a mixture of Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) encoded qubits and Gaussian squeezed states of light, arranged according to a three-dimensional Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice structure. This hybrid approach represents a significant departure from conventional architectures, which typically require either deterministic sources of non-Gaussian resources or suffer from prohibitive overheads when dealing with probabilistic generation.

The key innovation lies in the treatment of failed GKP state generation attempts. Rather than treating these failures as erasure errors that remove sites from the computational lattice, the invention substitutes the missing GKP qubits with momentum-squeezed vacuum states. These squeezed states, while not providing the same level of error protection as GKP qubits, maintain the entanglement structure of the computational resource state through the application of controlled-Z (CZ) gates. This substitution strategy, referred to as "swap-out," preserves the connectivity of the lattice while introducing a known and manageable form of noise that can be effectively handled through specialized decoding procedures.

The architecture comprises four main modules working in concert to enable scalable quantum computation. The state-preparation module utilizes Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) devices to generate high-quality GKP qubits, albeit with low probability. The multiplexing module boosts the effective generation rate through spatial and temporal multiplexing techniques, while incorporating the crucial swap-out functionality that substitutes squeezed states when GKP generation fails. The computational module implements the deterministic entangling operations required to create the three-dimensional hybrid cluster state, utilizing a combination of time-domain and spatial-domain entanglement generation. Finally, the photonic quantum processing unit (QPU) performs homodyne measurements on the generated resource state, with adaptive measurement settings controlled by fast classical electronics to implement the desired quantum computation.

A critical component of the invention is the specialized two-tier decoding procedure designed to handle the unique error structure of the hybrid resource state. The inner decoder processes the continuous-valued homodyne measurement outcomes, taking into account the correlations introduced by the CZ gates and the locations of swapped-out squeezed states. This decoder converts the analog measurement data into binary qubit outcomes while providing information about the relative likelihood of different error types. The outer decoder then applies conventional qubit-level error correction techniques to the processed syndrome data, but with weights informed by the analog information from the inner decoder and knowledge of the squeezed state locations. This combined approach enables effective error correction even at relatively high swap-out probabilities, with numerical simulations indicating a maximum tolerable swap-out probability of approximately 23.6% in the ideal squeezing limit, and approximately 13.3% for experimentally accessible squeezing levels of 15 dB.

The physical implementation of the architecture is designed as a planar chip configuration that leverages existing silicon photonics fabrication technologies. The system operates in two spatial dimensions plus one temporal dimension, ensuring that each optical mode traverses a path of constant optical depth independent of the total number of qubits. This design eliminates the exponentially growing losses that plague other architectures as they scale to larger systems. The planar structure also ensures that each qubit connects only to a small, constant number of neighboring qubits, preserving the local structure of noise essential for effective fault tolerance. Importantly, the architecture separates the different functional requirements into distinct modules, allowing each to be optimized independently: the state-generation modules prioritize low-loss performance over reconfigurability, the multiplexing modules can tolerate higher losses while requiring programmability, and the computational modules can operate with fairly lossy reconfigurable switches.

The invention provides several significant technological advantages over competing architectures. First, it enables true modularity by separating the different hardware requirements into specialized components that can be fabricated and optimized independently. Second, it imposes minimal cryogenic requirements, with only the state-generation modules potentially requiring cryogenic operation for optimal PNR detector performance, while the remainder of the system operates at room temperature. Third, the architecture supports extremely fast clock speeds limited primarily by homodyne detection timescales, which are significantly faster than photon-counting detectors used in other photonic approaches. This fast operation enables shorter delay lines with correspondingly lower losses, further enhancing scalability.

The hybrid resource state approach of the present invention represents a fundamental breakthrough in photonic quantum computing architecture, effectively synthesizing the scalability advantages of continuous-variable systems with the error-correction capabilities of discrete-variable encodings. By accommodating probabilistic sources of non-Gaussian resources through the innovative swap-out strategy and specialized decoding procedures, the invention provides a practical pathway to large-scale, fault-tolerant quantum computation using existing and near-term photonic technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### INTRODUCTION

The detailed description of the present invention begins with a comprehensive overview of the architectural framework that enables scalable, fault-tolerant photonic quantum computation through the use of hybrid resource states. The invention fundamentally addresses the long-standing challenge in photonic quantum computing: reconciling the probabilistic nature of non-Gaussian state generation with the deterministic requirements of scalable fault-tolerant computation. Traditional approaches have either demanded impossible hardware specifications for deterministic GKP state generation or suffered from prohibitive overheads when attempting to compensate for probabilistic sources through extensive multiplexing. The present invention circumvents these limitations through a novel hybrid strategy that maintains computational integrity even when a significant fraction of the intended GKP qubits must be substituted with readily available squeezed vacuum states.

The architectural foundation rests upon the recognition that while GKP qubits provide superior error-correction capabilities through their intrinsic structure and compatibility with Gaussian operations, squeezed vacuum states can serve as functional substitutes that preserve the essential entanglement structure of the computational resource state. This insight leads to the development of a hybrid RHG lattice where each site is occupied either by a GKP qubit (with probability 1-p₀) or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state (with probability p₀), where p₀ represents the swap-out probability corresponding to the failure rate of GKP state generation. The resulting resource state maintains the topological structure necessary for fault-tolerant computation while accommodating the practical realities of probabilistic state preparation.

The invention's implementation strategy carefully separates the different functional requirements into distinct modules, each optimized for its specific role in the computational pipeline. This modular approach not only simplifies the engineering challenges but also enables the use of different fabrication technologies and operating conditions for each component. The state-preparation module focuses exclusively on generating high-quality quantum states, whether GKP qubits or squeezed vacuum states, without concern for subsequent entanglement or measurement operations. The multiplexing module handles the complex task of boosting generation rates while implementing the crucial swap-out functionality. The computational module creates the three-dimensional entangled resource state through a combination of temporal and spatial entanglement generation, while the photonic QPU performs the final measurements and implements the adaptive control necessary for universal quantum computation.

A critical aspect of the invention is the development of specialized decoding procedures that account for the unique error structure introduced by the hybrid nature of the resource state. Unlike conventional approaches that treat measurement outcomes as simple binary values, the present invention leverages the continuous-valued nature of homodyne measurements to extract maximum information about the underlying quantum state. The two-tier decoding approach—comprising an inner decoder that processes analog measurement data and an outer decoder that implements qubit-level error correction—enables effective error correction even at relatively high swap-out probabilities. This decoding strategy represents a significant advancement over previous approaches, which either ignored the analog information available in homodyne measurements or failed to account for the correlated noise structure introduced by swapped-out squeezed states.

The physical implementation of the architecture as a planar chip configuration represents another key innovation. By operating in two spatial dimensions plus one temporal dimension, the invention ensures that optical path lengths remain constant regardless of system size, eliminating the exponentially growing losses that have limited the scalability of other photonic architectures. The planar structure also preserves the local nature of noise, which is essential for effective fault tolerance, while enabling fabrication using established silicon photonics technologies. This combination of architectural innovation and practical implementation considerations positions the present invention as a viable pathway to large-scale, fault-tolerant photonic quantum computation.

### Overview of the System Configuration

The system configuration of the present invention comprises four distinct but interconnected modules that work in concert to enable scalable, fault-tolerant photonic quantum computation. Each module serves a specific function in the computational pipeline and is optimized for its particular role, allowing for independent development and optimization while maintaining seamless integration within the overall architecture. The modular design not only simplifies the engineering challenges associated with large-scale quantum computing but also enables the use of different fabrication technologies and operating conditions appropriate for each component's requirements.

The first module, designated as the state-preparation module, is responsible for generating the fundamental quantum states that populate the computational resource lattice. This module utilizes Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) devices to produce high-quality GKP qubits, which serve as the primary computational units in the architecture. Each GBS device consists of an interferometer network that processes displaced squeezed vacuum states, followed by photon-number-resolving (PNR) detectors on all but one output mode. When specific photon-number patterns are observed in the detectors, the remaining unmeasured mode emerges in a state approximating a GKP qubit. While this process is inherently probabilistic, with typical success probabilities ranging from 0.1% to 2% depending on the target fidelity and available resources, it represents the most promising near-term approach to generating non-Gaussian states using existing photonic technologies.

The second module, the multiplexing module, addresses the probabilistic nature of GKP state generation by implementing spatial and temporal multiplexing techniques to boost the effective generation rate. This module comprises an array of GBS devices operating in parallel, connected through a network of active optical switches that can route successfully generated states to the appropriate output channels. The switching network is implemented using binary tree configurations of 2×2 crossbar switches, which can operate in either "bar" mode (implementing the identity operation) or "cross" mode (implementing a SWAP operation). These switches can be realized through variable-transmissivity beam splitters or Mach-Zehnder interferometers with controllable phase shifters, enabling fast reconfiguration based on the detection outcomes from the PNR detectors.

A crucial innovation in the multiplexing module is the implementation of the swap-out functionality, which substitutes momentum-squeezed vacuum states when GKP generation fails. This is accomplished through an additional switch at the output of the multiplexing network that can select between the output of the GBS array and a dedicated source of momentum-squeezed vacuum states. The decision to perform a swap-out is made based on the collective detection outcomes from all GBS devices in the array—if none of the devices successfully generates a GKP qubit, the system automatically substitutes a squeezed vacuum state. This approach ensures that every site in the computational lattice is populated with a valid quantum state, preserving the connectivity and entanglement structure essential for fault-tolerant computation.

To address the timing mismatch between the high repetition rates possible with optical pulse generation and the slower response times of PNR detectors, the multiplexing module incorporates time-to-space demultiplexing techniques. These techniques use optical delay lines and additional switching networks to distribute high-frequency pulse trains across multiple spatial channels, effectively reducing the repetition rate seen by each PNR detector to within its operational limits. For example, a 1024 MHz pulse train can be stepped down to 8 MHz by mapping every 128 consecutive pulses to 128 separate spatial outputs, requiring seven layers in a binary tree configuration with 127 active elements. Optical buffer delays, implemented through low-loss optical fiber or integrated waveguides, provide sufficient time for the multiplexer routing configuration to be actuated based on the PNR detector readout signals.

The third module, the computational module, is responsible for creating the three-dimensional entangled resource state that serves as the substrate for measurement-based quantum computation. This module implements the deterministic entangling operations required to generate the hybrid RHG lattice structure through a combination of temporal and spatial entanglement generation. The temporal entanglement is created using optical delay lines and controlled-Z (CZ) gates, which entangle successive optical pulses as they circulate through the delay line. Each incoming pulse from the multiplexing module interacts with the previously stored pulse through a CZ gate before being stored in the delay line itself, creating a one-dimensional cluster state that extends in the temporal direction.

The spatial entanglement is implemented through a two-dimensional array of these temporal cluster state generators, interspersed with additional state-preparation modules and connected by nearest-neighbor arrays of optical CZ gates. The additional state-preparation modules are divided into two sets that operate on alternating clock cycles, with corresponding CZ gates applied during even and odd clock cycles respectively. This staggered approach ensures that the spatial connectivity is properly established without requiring simultaneous operations that might introduce timing conflicts or crosstalk. The resulting three-dimensional lattice structure combines the temporal dimension with two spatial dimensions to create the complete RHG lattice topology required for fault-tolerant computation.

The fourth and final module, the photonic quantum processing unit (QPU), performs the homodyne measurements that implement the actual quantum computation. This module comprises an array of homodyne detector cells, each consisting of a vertical coupler, a local oscillator (LO) channel, a fast phase shifter, a 50/50 beam splitter, and a pair of photodiodes. The phase of the LO determines the quadrature angle along which the measurement is performed, enabling adaptive measurement settings that can be changed from clock cycle to clock cycle based on previous measurement outcomes and the requirements of the quantum algorithm being executed. The photo-currents from the photodiodes are subtracted to implement balanced detection, suppressing LO noise and extracting the quadrature measurement value.

The photonic QPU is controlled by a classical digital-electronic system that calculates the appropriate LO phases for each clock cycle based on the previous measurement outcomes, the input state record from the state-generation module, and the compiled quantum program instructions. This controller must operate at very high clock speeds to keep pace with the optical processing rates, motivating the development of dedicated application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) optimized for this particular computational task. The controller also records the measurement outcomes for subsequent decoding and error correction, maintaining a complete record of the computational history for post-processing and verification purposes.

The integration of these four modules creates a complete computational pipeline that transforms probabilistic GKP state generation into reliable, fault-tolerant quantum computation. The separation of concerns between modules allows each to be optimized independently while maintaining the necessary coordination through well-defined interfaces. This modular approach not only simplifies the engineering challenges but also enables incremental improvements and upgrades to individual components without requiring complete redesign of the entire system.

### Generation of Bosonic Qubits Using Multiplexed Gaussian Boson Sampling (“GBS”) Devices

The generation of bosonic qubits in the present invention is accomplished through a sophisticated implementation of Gaussian boson sampling (GBS) devices combined with advanced multiplexing techniques. This approach represents a significant advancement over previous methods by effectively addressing the fundamental trade-off between state fidelity and generation probability that has historically limited photonic quantum computing architectures. The GBS-based state preparation leverages the non-Gaussian nature of photon-number-resolving (PNR) detectors to herald the preparation of desired non-Gaussian output states, specifically Gottesman-Kitaev-Preskill (GKP) qubits, through post-selection on specific detection patterns.

Each individual GBS device in the architecture consists of a precisely engineered optical circuit that processes N displaced squeezed vacuum states through a general interferometer network, followed by PNR detectors on N-1 of the output modes. The parameters of this circuit—including the number of modes, displacement amplitudes, squeezing strengths, interferometer configuration, and target photon-number patterns—are carefully optimized to maximize the fidelity and success probability of the desired GKP state preparation. For instance, small-scale GBS devices comprising 3-mode interferometers, two PNR detectors capable of resolving up to 7 photons, and three momentum-squeezed vacuum states with up to 12 dB of squeezing have demonstrated the potential to produce |0⟩_GKP states with ∆² = 0.1 (∆_dB = 10 dB) with fidelities ranging from 76% to 96% and corresponding heralding success probabilities from 2.1% down to 0.1%. This fidelity-probability trade-off illustrates the fundamental challenge that the multiplexing architecture must address.

The multiplexing strategy employed in the present invention combines spatial and temporal multiplexing techniques to achieve near-deterministic state generation while maintaining practical hardware requirements. Spatial multiplexing involves operating multiple GBS devices in parallel, with their outputs connected through a network of active optical switches that can route successfully generated states to the appropriate output channels. The number of GBS devices required to achieve a target success probability of 1-p₀ scales logarithmically with the inverse failure probability, specifically as N_GBS = log(p₀)/log(1-p_GBS), where p_GBS represents the success probability of a single GBS device. This logarithmic scaling represents a significant improvement over linear scaling approaches, making high-success-probability state generation feasible even with modest individual device performance.

The switching network implementing spatial multiplexing is constructed as a binary tree of 2×2 crossbar switches, with the depth of the tree determined by the number of GBS devices. For N_GBS devices, the tree depth D should satisfy 2^(D-1) < N_GBS ≤ 2^D, resulting in a total number of switches equal to 2N_GBS - 2. Each switch in the network can operate in either "bar" mode, which implements the identity operation and passes signals straight through, or "cross" mode, which implements a SWAP operation and exchanges the signals between the two output ports. These switching operations can be realized through variable-transmissivity beam splitters or Mach-Zehnder interferometers with controllable phase shifters, enabling fast reconfiguration based on the detection outcomes from the PNR detectors.

Temporal multiplexing addresses the timing mismatch between the high repetition rates achievable with optical pulse generation and the slower response times of state-of-the-art PNR detectors. Transition-edge sensor (TES) based PNR detectors, which currently offer the best number-resolving capabilities, are typically limited to repetition rates of a few megahertz, while optical pulse generation can operate at gigahertz frequencies. To bridge this gap, the invention employs time-to-space demultiplexing techniques that distribute high-frequency pulse trains across multiple spatial channels. For example, to step down a 1024 MHz pulse train to 8 MHz, each group of 128 consecutive pulses is mapped to 128 separate spatial outputs, requiring seven layers in a binary tree configuration with 127 active elements. This approach effectively reduces the repetition rate seen by each PNR detector to within its operational limits while maintaining the overall system throughput.

An alternative temporal multiplexing approach involves interleaving pulses from multiple GBS devices operating at the TES-compatible repetition rate. In this scheme, k different GBS devices generate pulses at the slower rate, but these pulses are switched into a single spatial mode at different arrival times separated by the faster clock cycle period. This approach eliminates the need for fast feed-forward switches at the cost of requiring additional squeezers and linear optical elements in the GBS devices themselves. The choice between these temporal multiplexing strategies can be made based on specific hardware considerations and performance requirements.

A critical innovation in the multiplexing architecture is the implementation of the swap-out functionality, which ensures that every site in the computational lattice is populated with a valid quantum state regardless of GKP generation success. When none of the GBS devices in the multiplexing array successfully produces a GKP qubit, an additional switch at the output of the multiplexing network automatically substitutes a momentum-squeezed vacuum state. This substitution preserves the entanglement structure of the computational resource state while introducing a known and manageable form of noise that can be effectively handled through specialized decoding procedures. The momentum-squeezed vacuum states used for swap-outs are generated by dedicated sources that can operate deterministically and with high quality, ensuring consistent performance across all computational sites.

The complete multiplexing architecture also incorporates optical buffer delays to provide sufficient time for the switching network to be reconfigured based on PNR detector readout signals. Assuming the readout time is limited by the rise time of TES output voltage traces, a few nanoseconds of buffer delay can be implemented using several meters of low-loss optical fiber or integrated waveguide length. This buffering ensures that the heralded outputs from successful GBS devices arrive at the spatial multiplexers at the appropriate time for correct routing, maintaining the temporal coherence essential for subsequent entanglement operations.

The combination of spatial and temporal multiplexing with the innovative swap-out functionality creates a robust state generation system that can accommodate the probabilistic nature of GKP state preparation while maintaining the deterministic requirements of scalable fault-tolerant computation. This approach represents a fundamental breakthrough in photonic quantum computing architecture, effectively transforming the challenge of probabilistic state generation from a limiting factor into a manageable parameter that can be optimized through system design choices.

### Time Domain Generation of 1D Clusters

The time domain generation of one-dimensional cluster states in the present invention represents a critical component of the overall architecture, enabling the creation of extended entangled resource states through efficient use of optical components and delay lines. This approach leverages the temporal degrees of freedom of light to generate cluster states that extend indefinitely in the time domain, providing a scalable foundation for the three-dimensional computational lattice required for fault-tolerant quantum computation. The time-domain cluster state generation utilizes a feedback loop configuration that repeatedly applies controlled-Z (CZ) gates between successive optical pulses, creating entanglement that propagates through time rather than requiring extensive spatial resources.

The core mechanism for time-domain cluster state generation consists of an optical circuit that receives input states from the multiplexing module and processes them through a combination of active switching elements, CZ gates, and optical delay lines. The first optical pulse entering the circuit is directed into an optical delay line whose length is precisely calibrated to match the temporal separation between successive input pulses. This delayed pulse returns to the interferometer at the exact moment when the next input pulse arrives, allowing both pulses to interact through a CZ gate implemented via beam splitters and squeezers. After this interaction, the originally delayed pulse is routed out of the system while the newly arrived pulse is stored in the delay line, ready to interact with the subsequent input pulse. This cyclic process continues for each incoming pulse, creating a one-dimensional cluster state that extends in the temporal direction.

The optical implementation of this time-domain cluster state generator requires several key components working in precise synchronization. Fast actively switchable beam splitters control the routing of optical pulses between the input/output paths and the delay line, with switching times that must be synchronized to the optical clock cycle. Controllable phase shifters ensure proper phase relationships between interacting pulses, while inline squeezers provide the necessary resources for implementing the CZ gates. The optical delay line itself must be phase-stable and precisely matched to the clock period, making integrated photonic implementations particularly advantageous for maintaining the required stability and precision.

The CZ gate implementation in the time-domain cluster state generator follows the standard optical prescription for continuous-variable controlled-phase operations. Specifically, the CZ gate e^(iq⊗q) can be decomposed into a sequence of single-mode squeezers sandwiched between beam splitters, with the squeezing parameters and beam splitter transmissivities carefully chosen to achieve the desired interaction strength. This decomposition enables the deterministic implementation of CZ gates using only Gaussian resources, which are readily available in integrated photonic platforms. The deterministic nature of these Gaussian operations contrasts sharply with the probabilistic nature of the initial GKP state generation, highlighting the architectural advantage of separating non-Gaussian state preparation from entanglement generation.

The clock speed of the time-domain cluster state generation is ultimately limited by the detection and processing times of the final homodyne measurements, rather than by the state preparation or entanglement generation processes themselves. Homodyne detection can operate at significantly faster rates than photon-number-resolving detectors used in the GBS devices, with modern implementations capable of gigahertz bandwidths. This speed advantage enables the time-domain cluster state generator to operate at correspondingly high clock rates, facilitating the rapid generation of extended cluster states while minimizing the accumulation of decoherence effects.

The time-domain approach to cluster state generation offers several significant advantages over purely spatial implementations. First, it dramatically reduces the physical footprint required for generating long cluster states, as the same optical components are reused for each time step rather than requiring separate components for each spatial site. Second, it ensures that each optical mode traverses a path of constant optical depth regardless of the total length of the cluster state, eliminating the exponentially growing losses that would occur in purely spatial implementations as the system scales to larger sizes. Third, it naturally accommodates the temporal structure of the input pulse train from the multiplexing module, providing a seamless interface between state preparation and entanglement generation.

The integration of time-domain cluster state generation with the multiplexing module creates a powerful synergy that enhances the overall system performance. The multiplexing module provides a steady stream of high-quality quantum states—either GKP qubits or momentum-squeezed vacuum states—while the time-domain generator efficiently converts this stream into an entangled resource state. The swap-out functionality of the multiplexing module ensures that every time step in the cluster state is populated with a valid quantum state, preserving the connectivity and entanglement structure essential for subsequent computation. This combination of probabilistic state preparation with deterministic entanglement generation represents a fundamental breakthrough in photonic quantum computing architecture.

The time-domain cluster state generator also provides a natural framework for implementing error mitigation strategies during the entanglement generation process. By monitoring the homodyne measurement outcomes from previous time steps, the system can adaptively adjust the parameters of subsequent CZ gates or implement real-time error correction protocols. This capability becomes particularly important when dealing with the hybrid resource states generated by the present invention, where the presence of swapped-out squeezed states introduces correlated noise that must be carefully managed throughout the entanglement generation process.

The successful implementation of time-domain cluster state generation in the present invention demonstrates the feasibility of creating extended entangled resource states using practical photonic technologies. By combining this temporal approach with subsequent spatial entanglement operations, the architecture achieves the three-dimensional lattice structure required for fault-tolerant quantum computation while maintaining the scalability and manufacturability essential for practical implementation.

### GKP Cluster in 2+1 Dimensions

The extension of the one-dimensional time-domain cluster states into a full three-dimensional computational lattice represents a critical advancement in the present invention, enabling the implementation of fault-tolerant quantum computation through the creation of a hybrid GKP cluster state in 2+1 dimensions. This three-dimensional structure combines the temporal dimension generated through time-domain techniques with two spatial dimensions created through a carefully orchestrated array of entangling operations, resulting in a complete Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice topology that supports universal, fault-tolerant measurement-based quantum computation.

The construction of the 2+1 dimensional cluster state begins with a two-dimensional spatial array of time-domain cluster state generators, each producing a one-dimensional chain of entangled modes extending in the temporal direction. These individual chains are then interconnected through additional controlled-Z (CZ) gates applied in the two spatial dimensions, creating the full three-dimensional entanglement structure required for fault-tolerant computation. The spatial connectivity is implemented through a nearest-neighbor array of optical CZ gates that connect adjacent chains in the spatial array, with careful attention paid to the timing and coordination of these operations to ensure proper lattice formation.

A key innovation in the spatial entanglement generation is the use of staggered operation cycles that alternate between even and odd clock cycles to establish the complete spatial connectivity without introducing timing conflicts or crosstalk. Additional state-preparation modules are interspersed throughout the spatial array and divided into two sets that operate on alternating clock cycles. Correspondingly, the spatial CZ gates are also divided into two sets that are applied during even and odd clock cycles respectively. This staggered approach ensures that each spatial connection is properly established while maintaining the temporal coherence essential for the overall lattice structure.

The resulting three-dimensional lattice exhibits the characteristic topology of the RHG model, which consists of alternating primal and dual sheets of two-dimensional cluster states that encode the surface code. This special foliated structure is what enables fault tolerance in the measurement-based quantum computation framework, as error detection and decoding involve multiple mutually entangled layers simultaneously. The RHG lattice serves as an ideal candidate for the present invention because it not only supports universal measurement-based quantum computation but also demonstrates high fault-tolerant computational error thresholds, typically around 1% for conventional qubit implementations.

The hybrid nature of the cluster state—comprising both GKP qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states—introduces unique considerations in the three-dimensional lattice formation. Both GKP qubits and squeezed vacuum states can be entangled through the same CZ gates used in conventional CV cluster state generation, ensuring that the swap-out functionality does not disrupt the fundamental entanglement structure. However, the presence of swapped-out squeezed states does introduce correlated noise into the lattice, which must be carefully accounted for in both the lattice formation process and subsequent error correction procedures.

The physical implementation of the 2+1 dimensional cluster state generator leverages the planar nature of integrated photonic technologies to create a scalable and manufacturable architecture. The entire system can be fabricated on a single planar chip using established silicon photonics processes, with the spatial dimensions implemented through waveguide networks and the temporal dimension maintained through integrated optical delay lines. This planar configuration ensures that each qubit connects only to a small, constant number of neighboring qubits, preserving the local structure of noise essential for effective fault tolerance while enabling mass production using existing semiconductor fabrication techniques.

The clock speed of the three-dimensional cluster state generation remains limited primarily by the homodyne detection timescales, which can operate at gigahertz frequencies, significantly faster than the photon-number-resolving detectors used in the state preparation modules. This speed advantage enables the rapid generation of large three-dimensional lattices while minimizing the accumulation of decoherence effects. ### Generation of a Hybrid Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (“RHG”) Lattice

The generation of a hybrid Raussendorf-Harrington-Goyal (RHG) lattice in the present invention represents the culmination of the architectural innovations described thus far, creating a complete computational resource state that enables fault-tolerant, universal quantum computation while accommodating the probabilistic nature of GKP state generation. The hybrid RHG lattice combines the topological structure of the conventional RHG model with the unique properties of the present invention's swap-out functionality, resulting in a resource state that maintains computational integrity even when a significant fraction of sites are occupied by momentum-squeezed vacuum states rather than GKP qubits.

The conventional RHG lattice consists of a three-dimensional structure formed by alternating primal and dual sheets of two-dimensional cluster states, which together implement the surface code for fault-tolerant quantum computation. Each sheet contains data qubits and ancilla qubits arranged in a checkerboard pattern, with stabilizer measurements performed through the entanglement structure of the cluster state. The foliated nature of this lattice—where multiple layers are mutually entangled—enables effective error detection and correction through the correlation of measurement outcomes across different layers. The present invention preserves this essential topological structure while modifying the physical implementation to accommodate the hybrid nature of the resource state.

In the hybrid RHG lattice of the present invention, each site in the conventional lattice is occupied either by a GKP qubit (with probability 1-p₀) or a momentum-squeezed vacuum state (with probability p₀), where p₀ represents the swap-out probability corresponding to the failure rate of GKP state generation. This substitution strategy maintains the connectivity and entanglement structure of the original lattice while introducing a known and manageable form of noise that can be effectively handled through specialized decoding procedures. The momentum-squeezed vacuum states, while not providing the same level of error protection as GKP qubits, preserve the essential properties required for measurement-based quantum computation through their compatibility with the same CZ gates used to entangle GKP qubits.

The physical generation of the hybrid RHG lattice follows the same principles as the conventional RHG lattice but incorporates the time-domain and spatial entanglement techniques described previously. The temporal dimension is created through the cyclic application of CZ gates between successive optical pulses in the time-domain cluster state generators, while the two spatial dimensions are established through the nearest-neighbor array of optical CZ gates connecting adjacent chains in the spatial array. The staggered operation cycles—alternating between even and odd clock cycles for different sets of state-preparation modules and CZ gates—ensure proper lattice formation without timing conflicts.

A critical aspect of the hybrid RHG lattice generation is the preservation of the foliated structure that enables fault tolerance. The alternating primal and dual sheets are maintained through careful coordination of the measurement patterns and entanglement operations, with the swap-out functionality applied uniformly across both sheet types. This ensures that the error detection and correction capabilities of the surface code implementation remain intact, even in the presence of swapped-out sites. The knowledge of which sites contain GKP qubits versus momentum-squeezed vacuum states is recorded throughout the generation process and made available to the subsequent decoding procedures, enabling informed error correction decisions.

The hybrid RHG lattice exhibits several unique properties that distinguish it from conventional implementations. First, the presence of swapped-out sites introduces correlated noise into the lattice, as the momentum-squeezed vacuum states impart random displacements onto their neighboring modes through the action of the CZ gates. This correlated noise structure requires specialized decoding procedures that can account for the joint effects of multiple swapped-out sites, rather than treating each site independently. Second, the hybrid nature of the lattice enables a more flexible approach to error correction, as the system can leverage the superior error-correction capabilities of GKP qubits while maintaining computational functionality through the swapped-out sites.

The physical implementation of the hybrid RHG lattice leverages the planar nature of integrated photonic technologies to create a scalable and manufacturable architecture. The entire lattice can be generated on a single planar chip using established silicon photonics processes, with the spatial dimensions implemented through waveguide networks and the temporal dimension maintained through integrated optical delay lines. This planar configuration ensures that each qubit connects only to a small, constant number of neighboring qubits, preserving the local structure of noise essential for effective fault tolerance while enabling mass production using existing semiconductor fabrication techniques.

The successful generation of the hybrid RHG lattice represents a significant achievement in photonic quantum computing, demonstrating the feasibility of creating the complex entangled resource states required for fault-tolerant computation while accommodating the practical realities of probabilistic state preparation. The combination of topological error correction with the innovative swap-out functionality creates a robust computational substrate that can scale to large numbers of qubits while maintaining the fault tolerance essential for practical quantum computation.

### Passive Version of System Configuration

The passive version of the system configuration represents an important alternative implementation of the present invention that seeks to eliminate the need for active transformations in the computational module, thereby simplifying the hardware requirements and potentially improving overall system performance. In the standard configuration described previously, the computational module requires active optical elements such as inline squeezers and displacements to implement the controlled-Z (CZ) gates necessary for entanglement generation. While these active transformations are feasible with current technology, they introduce additional complexity, potential sources of error, and increased power consumption that could impact the scalability and reliability of large-scale implementations.

The passive configuration addresses these challenges by replacing the active transformations with passive optical elements that do not require external control or power input during operation. This approach draws inspiration from macronode-based architectures that have been developed for continuous-variable quantum computing, where complex entangled states can be generated using only passive linear optical elements such as beam splitters and phase shifters. The key insight enabling this passive implementation is that the hybrid nature of the resource state—combining GKP qubits with momentum-squeezed vacuum states—provides sufficient flexibility to accommodate the constraints imposed by passive optical networks.

In the passive configuration, the entanglement structure of the hybrid RHG lattice is generated through carefully designed interferometer networks that replace the active CZ gates used in the standard configuration. These interferometer networks consist of fixed beam splitters and phase shifters arranged in specific patterns that reproduce the essential entanglement properties of the CZ gates while operating entirely through passive linear optical transformations. The design of these networks requires sophisticated optimization techniques to ensure that the resulting entanglement structure matches the requirements of the RHG lattice topology while accommodating the hybrid nature of the resource state.

The passive configuration maintains the same four-module architecture as the standard implementation but modifies the computational module to eliminate active elements. The state-preparation module continues to generate GKP qubits and momentum-squeezed vacuum states through the same GBS and multiplexing techniques, while the multiplexing module implements the same swap-out functionality to handle probabilistic state generation. The photonic QPU remains unchanged, performing homodyne measurements with adaptive settings controlled by fast classical electronics. Only the computational module is modified to use passive optical networks instead of active transformations.

A significant advantage of the passive configuration is the potential for improved stability and reduced error rates, as passive optical elements are generally less susceptible to environmental fluctuations and control errors than their active counterparts. Additionally, the elimination of active elements reduces the power consumption and thermal management requirements of the computational module, which could be particularly beneficial for large-scale implementations. The passive nature of the optical network also simplifies the fabrication process, as it eliminates the need for integrated control electronics and associated interconnects.

However, the passive configuration also introduces new challenges that must be addressed through careful system design. The fixed nature of the passive optical networks means that the entanglement structure is predetermined and cannot be adapted during operation, potentially limiting the flexibility of the computational model. Additionally, the design of interferometer networks that accurately reproduce the required entanglement properties while accommodating the hybrid resource state requires sophisticated optimization techniques and may result in more complex optical circuits than the active implementation.

The passive configuration represents an important direction for future development of the present invention, offering a pathway to simplified hardware implementations that could enhance the scalability and reliability of large-scale photonic quantum computers. While the standard active configuration provides greater flexibility and potentially higher performance in the near term, the passive configuration offers a compelling alternative for applications where simplicity, stability, and power efficiency are paramount considerations.

## Technological Advantages

### Modularity

The modularity of the present invention represents a fundamental architectural advantage that significantly enhances the practicality and scalability of photonic quantum computing implementations. By carefully separating the different functional requirements of quantum computation into distinct, specialized modules, the invention enables independent optimization of each component while maintaining seamless integration within the overall system. This modular approach addresses several key challenges that have historically limited the scalability of photonic quantum computing architectures, including conflicting hardware requirements, fabrication complexity, and system integration difficulties.

The four-module architecture—comprising state-preparation, multiplexing, computational, and photonic QPU modules—each serves a specific function with well-defined interfaces that facilitate independent development and optimization. The state-preparation module focuses exclusively on generating high-quality quantum states, whether GKP qubits or momentum-squeezed vacuum states, without concern for subsequent entanglement or measurement operations. This specialization allows the module to be optimized for low-loss performance and high-fidelity state generation, potentially using non-reconfigurable circuits with integrated photon-number-resolving (PNR) detectors that can be fabricated using specialized processes.

The multiplexing module handles the complex task of boosting generation rates while implementing the crucial swap-out functionality, requiring a different set of hardware characteristics. This module can tolerate higher optical losses while prioritizing programmability and fast switching capabilities, enabling the use of different fabrication technologies and materials optimized for these requirements. The computational module implements the deterministic entangling operations required to create the three-dimensional hybrid cluster state, with requirements that differ again from the previous modules. This module can operate with fairly lossy reconfigurable switches while maintaining the precision necessary for proper entanglement generation.

The photonic QPU module performs the final homodyne measurements and implements the adaptive control necessary for universal quantum computation, with yet another distinct set of requirements. This module benefits from room-temperature operation and can leverage existing silicon photonics fabrication techniques for the homodyne detector arrays and phase shifters, while requiring sophisticated classical control electronics for real-time feedback and adaptation.

This modular separation of concerns enables several significant advantages. First, it allows each module to be fabricated using the most appropriate technology for its specific requirements, rather than forcing compromises that attempt to satisfy all requirements simultaneously. For example, the state-preparation module might benefit from specialized low-loss materials and cryogenic operation for optimal PNR detector performance, while the computational and QPU modules can operate at room temperature using standard silicon photonics processes. Second, it facilitates incremental improvements and upgrades to individual components without requiring complete redesign of the entire system, accelerating the development cycle and enabling rapid incorporation of technological advances.

The modularity also enhances system reliability and maintainability by isolating potential failure modes to specific modules. If a particular component fails or requires maintenance, only that module needs to be addressed rather than the entire system. This isolation also simplifies testing and characterization, as each module can be validated independently before integration into the complete system. Additionally, the modular approach enables flexible system configurations that can be tailored to specific applications or performance requirements by adjusting the relative sizes or capabilities of different modules.

The well-defined interfaces between modules also facilitate system integration and scaling. As the system grows to accommodate larger numbers of qubits, the modular architecture ensures that the complexity of each individual component remains manageable, while the interfaces provide clear pathways for expanding connectivity and coordination. This scalability is particularly important for fault-tolerant quantum computation, which requires large numbers of physical qubits to implement even modest logical computations.

The modularity of the present invention represents a significant departure from previous photonic quantum computing architectures, which often attempted to integrate all functions into a single monolithic structure. This integrated approach frequently led to conflicting requirements and compromised performance, as no single technology could optimally satisfy all the diverse needs of quantum computation. By embracing modularity, the present invention provides a practical pathway to large-scale, fault-tolerant photonic quantum computation that leverages the strengths of different technologies while mitigating their individual limitations.

Similarly, the photonic QPU, comprising homodyne detector arrays and associated classical control electronics, functions effectively at room temperature using standard silicon photonics fabrication techniques.

This minimal cryogenic approach provides several significant advantages over competing architectures. Third, it facilitates easier integration with classical control electronics, which can operate at room temperature alongside the photonic components, simplifying the overall system architecture.

The room-temperature operation of most system components also enables the use of established silicon photonics fabrication techniques, which are optimized for room-temperature processing and operation. This compatibility with existing semiconductor manufacturing processes provides a clear pathway to mass production and rapid scaling, leveraging the extensive infrastructure and expertise already developed for classical integrated circuits. The ability to fabricate most system components using standard room-temperature processes also simplifies testing and characterization, as components can be validated under normal operating conditions rather than requiring specialized cryogenic test environments.

The modular cryogenic approach also provides flexibility in system design and operation. As room-temperature PNR detector technology advances, the cryogenic requirements can be gradually eliminated entirely, transitioning to a fully room-temperature system. Even in the interim, the localized nature of the cryogenic requirements enables flexible system configurations that can be optimized for specific applications or performance requirements. For example, multiple state-preparation modules can share a single cryogenic environment, or different modules can be operated at different temperatures based on their specific requirements.

The minimal cryogenic requirements of the present invention represent a fundamental advantage that positions photonic quantum computing as a practical and scalable platform for large-scale quantum computation. By leveraging the inherent room-temperature operation of photonic systems while addressing the specific cryogenic needs of PNR detectors through a modular approach, the invention provides a pathway to quantum computing systems that are significantly more accessible, reliable, and cost-effective than competing platforms.

### Homodyne Detection Sets Timescales

The homodyne detection timescales of the present invention represent a critical technological advantage that enables unprecedented clock speeds and operational efficiency in photonic quantum computing implementations. Unlike many competing photonic architectures that are limited by the relatively slow response times of photon-number-resolving (PNR) detectors or threshold detectors, the present invention leverages the significantly faster timescales of homodyne detection to set the fundamental clock rate of the entire system. This speed advantage translates directly into enhanced computational throughput, reduced decoherence effects, and improved overall system performance.

Homodyne detection, which measures the quadrature amplitudes of optical fields through interference with a local oscillator, can operate at gigahertz bandwidths using modern photodiode technology and electronic amplification circuits. This represents a substantial speed advantage over PNR detectors, which are typically limited to megahertz repetition rates due to the physical constraints of their detection mechanisms. In the present invention, this speed difference is strategically exploited by separating the slow PNR detection processes—confined to the state-preparation module—from the fast homodyne detection processes that drive the computational clock in the photonic QPU module.

The computational timescale of the entire system is ultimately determined by the homodyne detection bandwidth rather than the PNR detector limitations, enabling clock speeds that are orders of magnitude faster than would be possible with PNR-limited architectures. This fast operation has several important consequences for system performance and scalability. First, it enables the rapid generation and processing of large three-dimensional cluster states, facilitating high-throughput quantum computation. Second, it reduces the accumulation of decoherence effects during computation, as quantum states spend less time in vulnerable intermediate states. Third, it enables shorter optical delay lines in the time-domain cluster state generators, which correspondingly incur lower optical losses and improve overall system fidelity.

The fast homodyne detection timescales also enhance the effectiveness of the adaptive measurement and feedback mechanisms essential for measurement-based quantum computation. The classical control electronics in the photonic QPU can process measurement outcomes and update local oscillator phases at gigahertz rates, enabling real-time adaptation to measurement results and error correction decisions. This rapid feedback capability is crucial for implementing complex quantum algorithms and maintaining fault tolerance in the presence of noise and errors.

The speed advantage of homodyne detection also impacts the design and optimization of other system components. The multiplexing module, for instance, can leverage the timing differences between PNR detection and homodyne detection through time-to-space demultiplexing techniques that distribute high-frequency pulse trains across multiple spatial channels. This approach effectively bridges the speed gap between state preparation and computation, ensuring that the system can maintain high throughput despite the slower PNR detector limitations.

The homodyne detection timescales also influence the physical implementation of the system, particularly in the design of optical delay lines and switching networks. Shorter delay lines enabled by faster clock speeds reduce the physical footprint required for time-domain operations, facilitating more compact and efficient implementations. Additionally, the fast switching requirements for the multiplexing and computational modules can be relaxed somewhat, as the system has more time relative to the computational clock to perform necessary routing and configuration operations.

The strategic use of homodyne detection to set the computational timescale represents a fundamental innovation in photonic quantum computing architecture. By decoupling the slow state preparation processes from the fast computational processes, the present invention achieves a unique combination of high fidelity and high throughput that addresses a critical challenge in scalable quantum computing. This speed advantage, combined with the other technological benefits of the invention, positions photonic quantum computing as a leading platform for practical, large-scale quantum computation.
