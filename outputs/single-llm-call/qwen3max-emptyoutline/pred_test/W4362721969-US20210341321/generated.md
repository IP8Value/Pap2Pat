# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of microfluidic sensing and, more specifically, to on-chip flow rate measurement devices for both liquid and gaseous media. The invention provides a novel integrated sensor architecture that combines a thin, mechanically robust membrane—preferably fabricated from silicon nitride—with a microwave resonator configured as a coplanar waveguide (CPW) to transduce fluid-induced mechanical deformations into measurable electrical signals. This configuration enables highly sensitive, real-time detection of fluid flow rates across a broad dynamic range without requiring moving parts, external optical components, or assumptions about the thermal or chemical properties of the fluid. The invention is particularly suited for applications in lab-on-a-chip systems, organ-on-a-chip platforms, digital microfluidics, precision drug delivery, and biomedical microreactors, where localized, accurate, and rapid flow monitoring is essential for maintaining experimental fidelity, cellular viability, and process control. Unlike conventional thermal or Coriolis-based flow sensors, the disclosed device operates independently of fluid composition, exhibits fast mechanical recovery, and is compatible with standard microfabrication processes, thereby enabling scalable, monolithic integration with existing microfluidic circuits. The invention further encompasses a dual-mode sensing mechanism: a quasi-static mode based on continuous deformation of the membrane under steady flow, and a dynamic mode based on the periodic pulsation of the membrane under constant pressure-driven flow, wherein the pulsation frequency serves as an additional, highly sensitive indicator of flow rate, especially at low flow regimes. The technical field thus spans microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), microwave engineering, fluid dynamics, and biomedical instrumentation, with particular emphasis on the convergence of mechanical compliance, electromagnetic resonance, and microfluidic transport phenomena.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION (PRIOR ART)

Accurate and localized flow rate measurement in microfluidic systems has long been recognized as a critical enabler for advanced applications in biotechnology, diagnostics, and chemical synthesis. In organ-on-a-chip platforms, for instance, precise control over nutrient and drug delivery is essential to mimic physiological conditions and assess cellular responses; deviations in flow can lead to hypoxia, nutrient depletion, or inconsistent pharmacological exposure, thereby compromising experimental validity. Similarly, in digital microfluidics, the generation of droplets with defined size and composition relies on fine-tuned flow control, necessitating real-time feedback mechanisms. Despite these needs, conventional approaches to flow sensing in microfluidics remain limited by several fundamental drawbacks. Syringe and peristaltic pumps, commonly used to drive flow, suffer from inherent mechanical delays, pulsations, and poor responsiveness to rapid changes, making closed-loop control challenging. While commercial flow sensors—such as thermal mass flow meters—are available, they are typically macroscopic, off-chip components that cannot provide localized measurements within individual microchannels. Their integration adds dead volume, increases system complexity, and disrupts the miniaturization ethos of microfluidic design.

Thermal flow sensors, which infer flow rate from the convective cooling of a heated element, represent one of the most widely adopted on-chip solutions due to their simplicity and lack of moving parts. However, their accuracy is critically dependent on the thermal properties of the fluid, particularly its specific heat capacity and thermal conductivity. In applications involving complex or variable fluid compositions—such as cell culture media, blood analogs, or multi-component reagent mixtures—unaccounted heat transfer pathways and fluctuating thermal properties introduce significant errors, rendering these sensors unreliable. Moreover, thermal sensors often require calibration for each new fluid type, limiting their versatility in dynamic or multi-assay environments.

Coriolis flowmeters, though offering high accuracy and independence from fluid properties, are generally unsuitable for microfluidic integration due to their reliance on oscillating fluid channels and the detection of inertial forces perpendicular to the flow direction. The fabrication complexity, power consumption, and physical footprint of Coriolis devices make them impractical for chip-scale deployment, especially in systems requiring multiple parallel flow sensors. Additionally, the small channel diameters typical of microfluidics reduce the Coriolis force to levels that are difficult to detect without sophisticated signal processing.

Passive mechanical approaches have also been explored. Capacitive sensors have been used to measure drag-induced deflections in microchannels, but they often require direct contact with the fluid or complex electrode geometries that compromise channel integrity. Cantilever-based sensors, wherein fluid flows through perforated structures causing measurable deflection, have demonstrated sensitivities down to 1 nL/min. However, forcing fluid through mechanical apertures introduces flow resistance, potential clogging, and non-uniform shear stress, which can be detrimental in biological applications. Furthermore, such designs alter the native flow path, potentially disrupting laminar flow profiles essential for many microfluidic functions.

Radio-frequency (RF) and microwave-based sensors have emerged as promising alternatives due to their non-invasive nature and high sensitivity to minute mechanical changes. One reported approach employed a thin polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) layer whose deflection under flow was detected via a proximal microwave sensor, achieving a sensitivity of 0.5 µL/min. However, the elastomeric nature of PDMS results in viscoelastic relaxation, leading to slow mechanical recovery times on the order of minutes. This hysteresis severely limits the temporal resolution and repeatability of measurements, making such sensors inadequate for dynamic or high-throughput applications. Moreover, PDMS is prone to swelling in organic solvents and exhibits poor long-term stability under repeated mechanical stress, raising concerns about durability and biocompatibility.

Optical methods, including particle image velocimetry (PIV) and interferometric displacement sensing, offer high spatial resolution but require bulky external optics, precise alignment, and transparent substrates, which restrict their use in opaque or multilayered chip architectures. They are also susceptible to interference from bubbles, particulates, or refractive index variations in the fluid.

In summary, the prior art lacks a flow sensing solution that is simultaneously: (i) integrable at the chip scale without disrupting native flow paths; (ii) insensitive to fluid composition; (iii) capable of measuring both liquid and gas flows with the same device; (iv) mechanically robust with fast recovery; (v) highly sensitive across a wide dynamic range; and (vi) compatible with standard semiconductor fabrication processes. The absence of such a universal on-chip flow sensor has impeded the development of truly autonomous, closed-loop microfluidic systems capable of adaptive flow control in response to real-time biological or chemical feedback. The present invention addresses these longstanding limitations by introducing a novel architecture that leverages the mechanical compliance of a ceramic thin-film membrane coupled with the high sensitivity of microwave resonant circuits to achieve unprecedented performance in microfluidic flow sensing.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a microfluidic flow rate sensor comprising a thin, mechanically compliant membrane formed as part of a microfluidic channel wall, and a microwave resonator directly integrated onto the membrane surface, wherein fluid flow through the channel induces mechanical deformation of the membrane, which in turn modulates the electrical characteristics of the resonator, enabling precise and real-time determination of flow rate. The membrane is preferably composed of silicon nitride, a ceramic material selected for its exceptional mechanical strength, chemical inertness, and rapid elastic recovery, which collectively enable reliable operation across diverse fluidic environments—including both liquids and gases—without degradation or hysteresis. The microwave resonator is configured as a coplanar waveguide (CPW) with a narrowed gap region overlapping the membrane, such that any deflection of the membrane alters the capacitance between the signal and ground electrodes, thereby shifting the resonant frequency of the circuit. This frequency shift is monitored using a phase-locked loop (PLL)-based electronic readout system, which tracks resonance changes with high temporal resolution and sub-kilohertz precision.

In a first operational mode, the sensor functions in a quasi-static regime, wherein steady flow causes a sustained deflection of the membrane, resulting in a measurable offset in the resonant frequency proportional to the flow rate. This mode provides continuous, real-time flow monitoring with a demonstrated short-term sensitivity of approximately 0.5 µL/min for aqueous flows and linear response over a wide pressure range for gaseous flows, up to at least 4 bar. The sensor’s insensitivity to fluid thermal properties eliminates the need for recalibration when switching between different media, a significant advantage over thermal sensors.

In a second operational mode, the invention exploits a previously unreported dynamic phenomenon: under constant pressure-driven flow, the thin membrane exhibits self-sustained periodic pulsations, characterized by regular transitions between distinct deformation states. The period of these pulsations—referred to herein as the pulse interval—is inversely related to the flow rate, with higher flow rates producing shorter intervals and thus higher pulsation frequencies. This dynamic mode offers enhanced sensitivity at very low flow rates (e.g., below 1 µL/min), where quasi-static deflections may be minimal, and enables flow quantification through time-domain analysis of the resonator’s phase response. The microwave resonator serves as an integrated transducer for these oscillations, converting mechanical pulsations into periodic phase modulations that can be readily detected and analyzed using standard RF instrumentation.

The invention further includes a method of fabricating the sensor using standard semiconductor processing techniques, beginning with a silicon wafer having a buried oxide layer and a top silicon nitride layer. A backside etch defines a cavity beneath the nitride layer, forming a free-standing membrane. Photolithography and metal deposition are then used to pattern the CPW resonator directly on the membrane, followed by bonding of a PDMS-based microfluidic channel aligned over the active sensing region. The entire process is compatible with batch fabrication and co-integration with other electronic or fluidic components on the same chip.

By combining the mechanical robustness of silicon nitride with the high sensitivity of microwave resonance, the present invention overcomes the key limitations of prior art flow sensors. It provides a universal, on-chip solution capable of high-precision, composition-independent flow measurement for both liquid and gas phases, with fast response times, excellent durability, and dual-mode operability that extends its utility across a broad spectrum of flow regimes—from nanoliter-per-minute biological perfusion to high-pressure gas delivery. The invention thus enables a new class of intelligent microfluidic systems capable of autonomous, adaptive flow control based on real-time, localized sensing.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a microfluidic flow rate sensor that integrates a thin, mechanically compliant membrane with a microwave resonator to achieve high-sensitivity, real-time flow measurement independent of fluid composition. The following detailed description sets forth the structure, operation, fabrication, and performance characteristics of the preferred embodiment, while also encompassing alternative configurations and operational modes that fall within the scope of the claimed invention.

The core of the sensor is a free-standing membrane formed from a 220 nm thick layer of silicon nitride (Si₃N₄), which serves as a portion of the wall of a microfluidic channel. Silicon nitride is selected for its superior mechanical properties compared to polymeric alternatives such as PDMS: it exhibits high Young’s modulus (~250 GPa), excellent tensile strength, low creep, and rapid elastic recovery, enabling the membrane to withstand repeated deflections without permanent deformation or fatigue. Additionally, its chemical inertness ensures compatibility with a wide range of aqueous and organic solvents, as well as gaseous media, making the sensor universally applicable across diverse microfluidic contexts. The membrane is typically rectangular in shape, with dimensions ranging from 600 µm to 3000 µm in length and 300 µm to 1000 µm in width, although other geometries (e.g., circular, elliptical) may be employed depending on the desired sensitivity and flow range. Larger membranes provide greater compliance and thus higher sensitivity to low flow rates, while smaller membranes offer faster response times and suitability for higher flow regimes.

Integrated directly onto the surface of the silicon nitride membrane is a coplanar waveguide (CPW) microwave resonator. The CPW consists of a central signal electrode flanked by two grounded electrodes, all lying in the same plane on the membrane surface. The gap between the signal and ground electrodes is designed to narrow significantly over the membrane region—typically from 400 µm in the feed lines to 20 µm over the active sensing area—to enhance the electric field concentration and thus the sensitivity of the capacitance to membrane deflection. The entire CPW structure is fabricated from a 100 nm thick gold layer, chosen for its high electrical conductivity and compatibility with standard photolithographic patterning. The resonator is designed to exhibit a characteristic impedance of 50 Ω to match standard RF measurement equipment, and its fundamental resonant frequency typically falls within the range of 2 GHz to 4 GHz, depending on the physical dimensions and layout.

The operational principle of the sensor relies on the electromechanical coupling between fluid flow and microwave resonance. When fluid flows through the microchannel above the membrane, hydrodynamic pressure exerts a force on the membrane surface, causing it to deflect downward (or upward, depending on channel orientation). This deflection changes the distance between the signal and ground electrodes of the CPW, thereby altering the distributed capacitance per unit length of the transmission line. Since the resonant frequency f₀ of a CPW resonator is inversely proportional to the square root of the effective capacitance (f₀ ∝ 1/√C), any change in capacitance results in a measurable shift Δf in the resonance frequency. This shift is directly correlated with the magnitude of the flow-induced pressure drop across the membrane, which in turn is related to the volumetric flow rate Q via fluid dynamic principles (e.g., Hagen-Poiseuille law for laminar flow).

To detect these resonance shifts with high precision, the sensor is interfaced with a custom electronic readout system based on a phase-locked loop (PLL) architecture. A microwave signal generator sweeps or tracks the resonant frequency, while a directional coupler samples the reflected signal from the CPW. The phase of this reflected signal is compared to a reference in a phase detector, and the resulting error signal is fed into a proportional-integral (PI) controller that adjusts the generator frequency to maintain a constant phase (e.g., 0 degrees). Under this feedback condition, the output voltage of the PI controller becomes a direct measure of the resonance frequency shift. This PLL-based approach enables real-time tracking of resonance changes with sub-kilohertz resolution and millisecond-scale update rates, far exceeding the capabilities of traditional frequency sweep methods.

In practice, the sensor demonstrates two distinct operational modes. In the quasi-static mode, observed under steady flow conditions, the membrane adopts a stable deflected state, and the resonance frequency settles to a new equilibrium value proportional to the flow rate. Experimental data show that for deionized water flows ranging from 0 to 120 µL/min, the frequency shift increases monotonically with flow rate, with a short-term noise floor of approximately 1 kHz corresponding to a flow sensitivity of 0.5 µL/min. For gaseous flows, the sensor exhibits a nearly linear response to applied pressure over a range of 20 mbar to 1000 mbar, and can withstand pressures up to 4 bar without failure, as verified by high-speed video imaging.

In the dynamic mode, a remarkable phenomenon emerges under constant pressure-driven flow: the membrane undergoes self-sustained periodic pulsations, transitioning rhythmically between two or more metastable deformation states. These pulsations arise from a nonlinear fluid-structure interaction, wherein the deflected membrane temporarily enlarges the channel cross-section, reducing hydraulic resistance and causing a transient increase in flow rate, which in turn alters the pressure distribution and triggers a snap-back to the original state. This cycle repeats at a characteristic frequency that depends solely on the average flow rate. Optical microscopy confirms that these pulsations are intrinsic to the system and do not require external actuation or cyclic pumping mechanisms. The microwave resonator detects these oscillations as periodic modulations in the phase response, with the pulse interval (time between consecutive pulses) decreasing as flow rate increases. For example, at 5 µL/min, the pulse interval is approximately 14 seconds, while at 10 µL/min, it shortens to about 7 seconds. This inverse relationship enables highly sensitive flow quantification at ultra-low rates (e.g., 0.4–3.5 µL/min), where quasi-static deflections are minimal. Three different sensor geometries were tested to span the full operational range: a large membrane (1.5 mm × 0.3 mm) for sub-microliter flows, a medium membrane (0.8 mm × 0.35 mm) for mid-range flows (5–50 µL/min), and a wide-channel device (3 mm × 1 mm membrane, 1.3 mm channel width) for high flows up to 120 µL/min. In all cases, the pulsation frequency increased monotonically with flow rate, confirming the robustness of this secondary sensing mechanism.

Fabrication of the sensor begins with a commercially available silicon-on-insulator (SOI)-like wafer consisting of a 500 µm thick silicon handle layer, a 2 µm thick buried silicon dioxide (SiO₂) layer, and a 220 nm thick top silicon nitride layer. Photolithography is performed on the backside of the wafer to define an etch window corresponding to the desired membrane area. Inductively coupled plasma (ICP) etching is used to pattern the silicon substrate, followed by a wet etch in potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution, which selectively removes silicon but stops at the SiO₂ layer. Subsequent removal of the exposed SiO₂ (e.g., via buffered oxide etch) releases the silicon nitride membrane. On the front side, another photolithographic step aligns the CPW pattern to the membrane, followed by electron-beam or thermal evaporation of a 100 nm gold layer and lift-off to form the electrodes. Finally, a microfluidic channel is fabricated in polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) using standard soft lithography: a negative photoresist (e.g., SU-8) mold is created on a silicon wafer, PDMS (10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio) is poured and cured at 80°C, and the resulting channel replica—typically 300 µm wide and 150 µm deep—is plasma-bonded to the sensor chip, precisely aligned over the membrane and CPW structure.

The resulting device is compact, robust, and fully integrable with existing microfluidic platforms. It requires no moving parts, optical access, or fluid-specific calibration, and operates reliably in both liquid and gas phases. Its dual-mode capability—quasi-static for high-flow, continuous monitoring and dynamic for ultra-sensitive, low-flow detection—makes it uniquely versatile. Applications include real-time perfusion control in organ-on-a-chip systems, feedback-regulated droplet generation in digital microfluidics, leak detection in gas microchannels, and adaptive dosing in point-of-care diagnostic devices. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in microfluidic sensing technology, enabling a new generation of intelligent, self-regulating lab-on-a-chip systems.
