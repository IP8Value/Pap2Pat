# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of hemodynamic monitoring in critically ill patients, and more particularly to systems, methods, and apparatus for detecting, characterizing, and correcting signal distortions in arterial pressure waveforms caused by dynamic artifacts introduced by fluid-filled catheter-transducer systems. The invention further pertains to the accurate derivation of clinically significant hemodynamic parameters—including systolic arterial pressure, pulse pressure variation, and the maximum rate of left ventricular pressure rise (dP/dt)—from arterial pressure signals that may be subject to overdamping or attenuation due to mechanical and fluid dynamic properties of the measurement system. This invention is especially relevant in intensive care unit (ICU) settings following major cardiovascular interventions such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or valve replacement surgery, where precise real-time hemodynamic assessment is critical for guiding fluid resuscitation, vasoactive drug administration, and overall circulatory support. The invention addresses a long-standing but underappreciated source of clinical error: the degradation of arterial waveform fidelity due to non-ideal frequency response characteristics of standard fluid-filled pressure monitoring lines, which can lead to systematic overestimation or underestimation of key cardiovascular metrics. By providing a robust framework for identifying periods of signal distortion and applying physics-based corrections to restore waveform integrity, the invention enhances the reliability of continuous hemodynamic monitoring and supports more accurate, individualized therapeutic decision-making in high-acuity patient populations.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution to the problem of inaccurate hemodynamic parameter estimation resulting from distorted arterial pressure waveforms in critically ill patients monitored via radial artery catheters connected to fluid-filled tubing systems. Conventional monitoring practices assume that the arterial pressure signal faithfully represents intravascular pressure dynamics; however, this assumption is frequently violated due to mechanical resonances, damping effects, and compliance mismatches inherent in the catheter-transducer assembly. These physical phenomena manifest as either overdamping—characterized by reduced systolic pressure, elevated diastolic pressure, and preserved mean arterial pressure—or attenuation—marked by a uniform reduction in systolic, diastolic, and mean pressures. Both conditions introduce significant bias into derived parameters such as pulse pressure variation (PPV), a key predictor of fluid responsiveness, and dP/dt, a surrogate marker of myocardial contractility. The invention overcomes these limitations through a multi-stage analytical process that begins with continuous acquisition of raw arterial pressure data via a dual-channel transducer system capable of simultaneous reference and test signal recording. During an initial calibration phase within the first hour post-admission to the intensive care unit, three standardized fast-flush maneuvers are performed to excite the natural frequency and damping ratio of the fluid column, thereby enabling system identification of the transfer function governing signal distortion. Subsequently, the entire monitoring record is scanned using algorithmic pattern recognition to detect episodes of overdamping or attenuation based on predefined morphological and statistical criteria applied to systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressure trends. For each identified distortion episode, the invention applies an inverse filtering technique derived from the calibrated transfer function to reconstruct an undistorted pressure waveform. This corrected waveform is then used to recalculate hemodynamic parameters with improved physiological fidelity. Validation studies involving thirty post-cardiac surgery patients demonstrated that uncorrected signals systematically overestimated systolic arterial pressure by an average of 5.0 mmHg (4.3%) and dP/dt by 134 mmHg/sec (24%), while introducing clinically meaningful discrepancies in PPV values. The invention thus ensures that therapeutic interventions—such as volume expansion, inotrope initiation, or vasopressor titration—are guided by hemodynamic data that accurately reflect the patient’s true cardiovascular status rather than artifacts of the measurement apparatus. Furthermore, the invention includes automated alerting mechanisms that notify clinicians when signal quality falls below acceptable thresholds, thereby promoting proactive maintenance of monitoring line integrity. In summary, the invention transforms conventional arterial pressure monitoring from a passive observational tool into an intelligent, self-correcting diagnostic platform that actively compensates for system-induced waveform distortions to deliver trustworthy hemodynamic insights at the bedside.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The detailed description of the present invention encompasses the technical architecture, operational methodology, clinical implementation protocols, and validation evidence supporting a novel system for detecting, characterizing, and correcting distortions in arterial pressure waveforms arising from the dynamic properties of fluid-filled catheter-transducer monitoring systems. This invention is grounded in the recognition that the arterial pressure signal recorded at the bedside is not a direct representation of intravascular pressure but rather the output of a second-order mechanical system comprising the catheter, fluid column, stopcocks, and pressure transducer. Like any physical system with mass, compliance, and resistance, this assembly exhibits resonant frequencies and damping characteristics that can amplify or attenuate specific components of the pressure waveform, particularly during rapid pressure changes such as those occurring in systole. When the natural frequency of the fluid column approaches the harmonic frequencies present in the arterial pulse—typically between 10 and 30 Hz—the system may become underdamped, leading to oscillatory overshoot and ringing artifacts that artificially inflate systolic pressure and the maximum rate of pressure rise (dP/dt). Conversely, excessive friction, air bubbles, or kinks in the tubing increase damping, causing overdamping characterized by blunted systolic peaks, elevated diastolic troughs, and preservation of mean arterial pressure due to the integral nature of mean pressure calculation. A third category, attenuation, occurs when overall signal amplitude is uniformly reduced—often due to partial occlusion or low transducer gain—resulting in proportional decreases across all pressure components. These distortions are not merely academic concerns; they have direct clinical consequences. For instance, an overestimated dP/dt may falsely suggest preserved myocardial contractility in a failing heart, delaying necessary inotropic support. Similarly, an artifactually elevated pulse pressure variation (PPV)—a dynamic index used to predict whether a patient will benefit from fluid administration—could lead to inappropriate volume loading in a patient who is already hypervolemic, exacerbating pulmonary edema or right heart strain. The present invention addresses these risks through an integrated hardware-software platform designed to continuously assess signal quality and apply corrective algorithms in real time or retrospectively.

At the core of the invention is a dual-head pressure transducer system, such as the FloTrac™ sensor manufactured by Edwards Lifesciences (Irvine, CA, USA), which enables simultaneous acquisition of two independent arterial pressure signals from the same radial artery catheter. One channel serves as the primary clinical monitoring stream, while the second functions as a research-grade reference channel dedicated to signal integrity analysis. This redundancy is essential because it allows the system to compare the morphology of the two waveforms and detect discrepancies indicative of distortion without requiring external calibration against a gold-standard measurement (which is often impractical in ICU settings). The transducer system is connected via standard fluid-filled tubing to a 20-gauge radial artery catheter inserted percutaneously using Seldinger technique, with the transducer zeroed to atmospheric pressure at the phlebostatic axis prior to data collection. Continuous pressure data are sampled at a minimum frequency of 100 Hz to capture high-frequency components of the arterial waveform necessary for accurate dP/dt computation and distortion detection. Data acquisition begins immediately upon patient admission to the intensive care unit following elective cardiac surgery—such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve replacement or repair, or combined procedures—and continues for a minimum duration of 18 hours to capture the full spectrum of potential signal degradation events during the critical early recovery period.

A key innovation of the invention lies in its structured calibration protocol performed during the first hour of monitoring. Within this initial window, three standardized "fast-flush" maneuvers are executed by briefly opening the flush device (typically containing pressurized saline at 300 mmHg) for approximately one second. This transient pressure impulse excites the natural frequency and damping ratio of the fluid column, generating a characteristic oscillatory decay pattern known as the "flush test" response. By analyzing the frequency and decay envelope of these oscillations using Fourier transform and logarithmic decrement methods, the system identifies the transfer function H(s) that mathematically describes how the monitoring apparatus distorts the true arterial pressure signal P_true(t) to produce the observed signal P_obs(t):  
P_obs(t) = H(s) * P_true(t)  
where * denotes convolution in the time domain. Once H(s) is characterized, its inverse H⁻¹(s) can be constructed and applied as a digital filter to reverse the distortion. Critically, the invention assumes that the dynamic characteristics of the fluid column remain stable over short intervals (e.g., 10-minute windows), allowing piecewise correction even if gradual changes occur over longer periods due to temperature shifts, clot formation, or tubing movement. This assumption is validated by empirical observation that mechanical properties of the monitoring line do not change abruptly in the absence of overt disconnection or occlusion.

Following calibration, the invention implements a two-tiered signal quality assessment algorithm. The first tier operates continuously in real time, scanning the raw pressure waveform for morphological red flags such as loss of dicrotic notch, excessive smoothing of the systolic upstroke, or abnormal harmonics in the frequency spectrum. The second tier performs retrospective batch analysis of the entire recorded dataset to identify discrete episodes of overdamping (Ov) or attenuation (At) based on quantitative pressure criteria. Specifically, an Ov episode is defined as a sustained period (≥30 seconds) during which systolic arterial pressure (sAP) decreases by more than 5 mmHg from baseline, diastolic arterial pressure (dAP) increases by more than 3 mmHg, and mean arterial pressure (mAP) remains within ±2 mmHg of its pre-episode value. An At episode is defined as a concurrent reduction in sAP, dAP, and mAP by more than 10% relative to baseline, indicating uniform signal attenuation. Baseline values are established during the first 10 minutes of stable monitoring after transducer zeroing and flush test completion. Episodes are timestamped, duration-tagged, and stored in a metadata log linked to the corresponding waveform segment.

For each identified distortion episode, the invention retrieves the most recent transfer function estimate (from the nearest preceding 10-minute calibration window) and applies the inverse filter H⁻¹(s) to the raw pressure data within that episode. The filtering process is implemented in the digital domain using finite impulse response (FIR) or infinite impulse response (IIR) filter structures optimized for minimal phase distortion and computational efficiency. The corrected waveform P_corr(t) is then subjected to standard hemodynamic parameter extraction algorithms: systolic and diastolic pressures are identified as local maxima and minima within each cardiac cycle; pulse pressure is computed as sAP – dAP; PPV is calculated as [(PP_max – PP_min) / PP_mean] × 100 over a respiratory cycle (typically 20–30 seconds in mechanically ventilated patients); and dP/dt_max is determined by numerical differentiation of the pressure waveform during early systole, usually between the foot of the pulse and the dicrotic notch. All derived parameters are stored in parallel with their uncorrected counterparts, enabling direct comparison and audit trail generation.

Clinical validation of the invention was conducted in a prospective observational study involving thirty adult patients admitted to the ICU following elective cardiac surgery. The cohort included sixteen patients who underwent CABG, eleven who received valve surgery (aortic or mitral replacement/repair), and three who had combined procedures. Mean patient age was 69 ± 13 years, with common comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease. Fourteen patients required noradrenaline infusion for vasopressor support, eight received dobutamine for inotropic augmentation, and nine underwent protocolized volume expansion based on initial hemodynamic assessment. Continuous arterial pressure monitoring was initiated immediately upon ICU arrival using the dual-head transducer system, and data were recorded for a minimum of 18 hours per patient. Three fast-flush tests were performed within the first hour to enable system identification, and three 10-minute analysis windows were selected during this period under the assumption of quasi-stationary system dynamics.

Analysis of the full monitoring records revealed a surprisingly high prevalence of signal distortion episodes. Seventeen out of thirty patients (57%) experienced at least one episode of overdamping or attenuation, with ten patients (33%) having two or more episodes and eight patients (27%) enduring five or more distinct distortion events. Episode durations ranged from less than one minute to over six hours, with seven episodes lasting longer than 20 minutes and three exceeding one hour in duration. These findings underscore the dynamic and intermittent nature of signal degradation, which cannot be captured by sporadic manual checks or single-point calibrations. During the first hour of monitoring—when clinical decisions regarding fluid management and vasoactive therapy are most critical—the uncorrected (raw) signals exhibited systematic biases compared to the corrected waveforms. Systolic arterial pressure was overestimated by an average of 5.0 ± 1.4 mmHg (P < 0.0001), corresponding to a relative error of 4.3 ± 0.9%, with individual errors ranging from negligible (0.3 mmHg) to clinically substantial (5.9 mmHg or 15.9%). This overestimation arises primarily from underdamped oscillations that create artificial peaks during the systolic upstroke, a phenomenon exacerbated by the high-frequency content of post-cardiac surgery waveforms which often exhibit increased vascular stiffness and reflected waves.

More concerning was the impact on derived parameters. The maximum rate of left ventricular pressure rise (dP/dt_max), widely used as a non-invasive surrogate for myocardial contractility, was overestimated by 134 ± 47 mmHg/sec (P < 0.0001), representing a 24 ± 6% relative error. Given that normal dP/dt_max values range from 800 to 1200 mmHg/sec in healthy adults and may fall below 600 mmHg/sec in severe heart failure, an artificial inflation of 134 mmHg/sec could misclassify a patient with moderate systolic dysfunction as having near-normal contractility, potentially delaying life-saving interventions. Pulse pressure variation (PPV), a cornerstone of goal-directed fluid therapy in mechanically ventilated patients, also showed meaningful discrepancies: raw PPV averaged 9.5 ± 7.3% compared to 10.0 ± 7.8% for corrected PPV, with individual differences ranging from -2.6% to +4.3%. While the mean difference appears small, the direction and magnitude of error were highly variable across patients, meaning that some individuals would be falsely categorized as fluid responders (PPV > 13%) while others would be incorrectly deemed non-responders. In a population where fluid overload carries significant morbidity, such misclassification could directly influence outcomes.

The invention mitigates these risks through its integrated correction framework. By applying the inverse transfer function derived from the flush test, the system effectively "deconvolves" the artifact introduced by the monitoring line, restoring waveform morphology to closely approximate the true intravascular pressure. Post-correction validation confirmed that systolic pressure, dP/dt, and PPV values aligned with expected physiological ranges and correlated better with other markers of cardiac performance such as echocardiographic ejection fraction and central venous oxygen saturation. Moreover, the invention includes a user interface module that displays both raw and corrected waveforms side-by-side on the bedside monitor, with color-coded alerts indicating active distortion episodes and the degree of parameter adjustment applied. Clinicians can toggle between views to assess the impact of correction on their therapeutic decisions, fostering transparency and trust in the technology.

From an implementation standpoint, the invention is compatible with existing ICU infrastructure. The dual-head transducer requires only a standard radial artery catheter and conventional pressure tubing, with no additional invasive procedures. Data processing can occur either on a dedicated bedside processor or via cloud-based analytics, depending on hospital IT architecture. Software updates can be delivered remotely to refine the distortion detection algorithms based on accumulating clinical evidence. Importantly, the invention does not replace clinician judgment but augments it by providing higher-fidelity data upon which to base decisions. Future iterations may incorporate machine learning models trained on large waveform databases to predict impending distortion episodes before they manifest (e.g., based on subtle changes in tubing resonance), enabling preemptive recalibration or line replacement.

In conclusion, the present invention represents a paradigm shift in arterial pressure monitoring by acknowledging and actively compensating for the inherent limitations of fluid-filled transduction systems. Through rigorous system identification, real-time distortion detection, and physics-based waveform correction, it ensures that hemodynamic parameters used to guide critical care interventions reflect true patient physiology rather than measurement artifacts. This not only improves the accuracy of individual parameter estimates but also enhances the reliability of dynamic indices like PPV that depend on precise waveform morphology. As healthcare systems increasingly adopt data-driven approaches to critical care, the invention provides a foundational layer of signal integrity assurance that is essential for the safe and effective use of advanced hemodynamic monitoring technologies.
