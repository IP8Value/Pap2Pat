# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The identification of molecular interactions that underlie complex biological phenotypes, such as cancer, remains a central challenge in modern biomedical research. Traditional approaches to gene interaction network inference have largely focused on reconstructing global regulatory or functional relationships from a single set of gene expression data—typically derived from either healthy or diseased tissues—using methodologies rooted in Bayesian networks, pairwise mutual information, or graphical Gaussian models. While these methods have yielded valuable insights into general cellular machinery, they are inherently limited when the goal is to isolate interactions that are specifically and causally associated with a particular disease state. Such phenotype-specific interactions represent a distinct class of biological relationships: those that emerge, disappear, or change in strength only in the context of a pathological condition, thereby offering direct mechanistic clues about disease etiology and potential therapeutic targets.

A critical limitation of conventional network inference techniques lies in their inability to distinguish between interactions that are constitutive to normal cellular function and those that are rewired or uniquely activated in disease. One might consider applying standard network inference separately to healthy and diseased expression datasets and then comparing the resulting topologies to identify differences. However, this approach is fraught with methodological pitfalls. The inferred networks are highly sensitive to sample size, data noise, and the heuristic algorithms used for structure learning, which often employ greedy search strategies prone to local optima. Consequently, observed differences between the "normal" and "diseased" networks may reflect technical artifacts rather than true biological rewiring. Moreover, even if genuine topological differences exist, attributing them directly to the phenotype requires assumptions that are rarely justified without additional experimental validation.

An alternative strategy involves augmenting the network with an explicit "phenotype node"—for example, a binary variable representing cancer presence—and inferring edges between this node and individual genes. While this can successfully identify genes whose expression is correlated with the disease, it fails to capture the cooperative interplay among genes that jointly influence the phenotype. In such a model, the relationships between genes themselves remain unexamined in the context of the disease; the network reveals which genes are individually associated with cancer but not how pairs or groups of genes interact synergistically to produce or sustain the pathological state. This is a significant shortcoming, as biological systems often rely on combinatorial logic—such as the co-activation of transcription factors, the formation of protein complexes, or the convergence of signaling pathways—where the joint activity of multiple components is necessary for a functional outcome.

The problem is further compounded by the nature of gene expression data itself. Microarray and RNA-seq technologies generate continuous, high-dimensional measurements that reflect the nuanced regulation of gene activity. Many existing methods simplify this complexity by binarizing expression values—classifying genes as simply "on" or "off"—which discards valuable quantitative information and introduces arbitrary thresholds that can distort biological interpretation. A more faithful approach must operate directly on continuous expression levels while still capturing the essence of cooperative gene behavior relevant to the phenotype.

What, then, constitutes a proper criterion for identifying gene-gene interactions that are specifically associated with a disease? Correlation between a gene pair’s joint expression and the phenotype is insufficient, as this correlation may arise merely from the additive effects of each gene’s individual association with the disease. True cooperative interaction implies that the pair provides more information about the phenotype than the sum of its parts—that is, their combined effect is greater than what would be expected from their independent contributions. This concept aligns with the intuitive notion of synergy, where two or more agents interact to produce an outcome that none could achieve alone. In biological terms, such synergy might manifest as the requirement for two transcription factors to bind adjacent sites on a promoter to activate an oncogene, or the necessity of both a kinase and its substrate being present for a pro-survival signal to propagate. In these cases, neither gene alone is strongly predictive of the disease, but their co-occurrence is highly indicative.

To formalize this idea, information theory offers a rigorous framework. Specifically, the measure of synergy—defined as the mutual information between a gene pair and the phenotype minus the sum of the mutual information between each gene and the phenotype individually—quantifies the extent to which the joint expression of two genes provides non-redundant, cooperative information about the disease. A positive synergy value indicates that the pair acts in concert in a way that is specifically linked to the phenotype, beyond their individual effects. This metric thus serves as a principled figure of merit for identifying phenotype-specific interactions.

However, computing synergy from continuous gene expression data presents a non-trivial computational challenge. Traditional information-theoretic estimators require discrete probability distributions, which are not directly available from continuous measurements. Prior work addressed this by binarizing expression data, but this approach sacrifices resolution and introduces bias. A more robust solution must estimate entropy and mutual information directly from continuous data without discretization, while preserving the interpretability and theoretical guarantees of the information-theoretic framework.

Accordingly, there exists a clear need for a novel computational methodology that (1) operates directly on continuous gene expression data, (2) quantifies the synergistic interaction between gene pairs with respect to a binary phenotype, (3) distinguishes cooperative effects from independent contributions, and (4) produces a statistically validated network of phenotype-specific interactions that can guide biological discovery. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a dendrogram-based approach to estimate conditional entropy from continuous expression profiles, enabling the accurate computation of synergy and the construction of a "synergy network" that reveals gene-gene interactions uniquely associated with a disease state such as cancer.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel computational methodology for identifying gene-gene interactions that are specifically and cooperatively associated with a binary phenotype, such as the presence or absence of a disease like cancer, using continuous gene expression data without reliance on prior biological knowledge. The core innovation lies in the development of a dendrogram-based technique to estimate information-theoretic quantities—specifically conditional entropy, mutual information, and synergy—directly from continuous expression values, thereby overcoming the limitations of prior binarization-based approaches. This methodology enables the construction of a "synergy network," wherein nodes represent genes and edges represent statistically significant synergistic interactions between gene pairs with respect to the phenotype.

In accordance with the invention, synergy is defined as the difference between the mutual information provided by a gene pair about the phenotype and the sum of the mutual information provided by each gene individually. A positive synergy value indicates that the joint expression of the two genes provides more information about the phenotype than would be expected from their independent contributions, suggesting a cooperative biological interaction specifically linked to the disease state. To compute this synergy from continuous data, the invention employs a clustering-based estimation of conditional entropy. Specifically, for any given set of genes, a dendrogram is constructed using a hierarchical clustering algorithm (e.g., UPGMA) applied to the joint expression profiles of the samples. The conditional entropy of the phenotype given the gene expression is then estimated as the average entropy of the clusters formed by cutting the dendrogram at various heights, integrated over a biologically meaningful range of distances. This approach generalizes the discrete entropy calculation used in binarized data and remains backward-compatible with it, while fully leveraging the information contained in continuous expression measurements.

The invention further provides a statistically rigorous framework for assessing the significance of observed synergies. This includes permutation-based null models that preserve either the marginal associations of genes with the phenotype or the joint structure of gene expression within samples, allowing for the estimation of empirical p-values. Additionally, the invention incorporates false discovery rate (FDR) correction to account for the massive number of gene pairs tested during exhaustive search, ensuring that only robust, non-spurious interactions are included in the final synergy network.

The methodology is computationally efficient, enabling exhaustive pairwise analysis across tens of thousands of genes in a feasible timeframe through parallelization. It is also extensible to higher-order interactions (e.g., triplets of genes) and applicable to other types of biomolecular data, including protein expression and post-translational modifications.

In one embodiment, the invention is applied to prostate cancer gene expression data, revealing a synergy network centered on key genes such as RBP1 (cellular retinol-binding protein-1) and PTGDS (prostaglandin D2 synthase), which interact synergistically with numerous partner genes—many of which are involved in oxidative stress response and ribosomal function. These findings support the hypothesis that prostate cancer is associated with oxidative damage coupled with impaired apoptosis, and highlight the potential of synergy networks to uncover novel disease mechanisms and combinatorial therapeutic targets.

Thus, the present invention provides a powerful, general-purpose tool for phenotype-specific interactome inference, offering unique insights that complement traditional gene network approaches and advancing the goal of precision medicine through the identification of cooperative molecular drivers of disease.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for identifying synergistic gene-gene interactions associated with a binary phenotype using continuous gene expression data. The method comprises several key steps: (1) preprocessing of gene expression data, (2) estimation of conditional entropy via dendrogram-based clustering, (3) computation of mutual information and synergy, (4) statistical validation of synergistic interactions, and (5) construction of a synergy network. Each of these steps is described in detail below.

Initially, a dataset of gene expression measurements is obtained from a collection of biological samples, wherein each sample is labeled according to a binary phenotype of interest—for example, "cancer" versus "non-cancer." The expression data may be generated using any high-throughput technology, such as microarrays or RNA sequencing, and should be appropriately normalized to ensure comparability across samples. In a preferred embodiment, Robust Multi-array Average (RMA) normalization is applied to microarray data to reduce technical noise and batch effects. The resulting dataset is represented as a matrix, where rows correspond to genes and columns correspond to samples, with each entry containing the continuous expression level of a gene in a particular sample.

For any subset of genes—initially considered as single genes or pairs—the method proceeds to estimate the conditional entropy of the phenotype given the expression levels of those genes. Conditional entropy, denoted H(C|G₁,…,Gₙ), quantifies the uncertainty in predicting the phenotype C when the expression levels of genes G₁ through Gₙ are known. Lower conditional entropy indicates higher predictive power. In the case of discrete (e.g., binarized) expression data, conditional entropy can be computed directly from the joint frequency distribution of expression states and phenotype labels. However, for continuous data, a different approach is required.

The invention introduces a dendrogram-based estimator for conditional entropy. Specifically, for a given set of n genes, the joint expression profiles of all samples are treated as points in an n-dimensional space. A hierarchical clustering algorithm, such as Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA), is applied to these points to construct a dendrogram. The choice of distance metric is critical; in a preferred embodiment, the Chebyshev distance (i.e., the maximum absolute difference across any dimension) is used. This ensures that the scale of distances remains consistent regardless of the number of genes considered, facilitating meaningful comparisons between single-gene and multi-gene entropies.

The dendrogram is then used to define a continuum of cluster partitions. At any height D above the leaves (samples), a horizontal cut through the dendrogram yields a partition of the samples into disjoint clusters. For each cluster, the entropy of the phenotype distribution within that cluster is computed as h(Q) = -Q log₂ Q - (1-Q) log₂(1-Q), where Q is the proportion of samples in the cluster that belong to the positive class (e.g., cancer). The entropy of the entire partition is the weighted average of the cluster entropies, with weights proportional to cluster sizes.

Rather than selecting a single arbitrary height D, the invention integrates the partition entropy over a range of biologically meaningful distances, from 0 to a cutoff value D*. This integration smooths out discontinuities that occur when clusters merge at specific dendrogram nodes and captures information across multiple scales of similarity. The resulting integrated conditional entropy is defined as:

H*(C|G₁,…,Gₙ) = (1/D*) ∫₀^D* H(C|G₁,…,Gₙ; D) dD,

where H(C|G₁,…,Gₙ; D) is the partition entropy at height D. The parameter D* represents a threshold of biological significance; clusters separated by distances greater than D* are considered biologically distinct and are not merged. In practice, D* is chosen based on the scale of the normalized expression data (e.g., D* = 1.5 for RMA-normalized log-scale data), and the method is robust to moderate variations in this parameter.

Once conditional entropy is estimated, mutual information I(G₁,…,Gₙ; C) is computed as the difference between the entropy of the phenotype alone, H(C), and the conditional entropy:

I(G₁,…,Gₙ; C) = H(C) - H(C|G₁,…,Gₙ).

Mutual information measures the total reduction in uncertainty about the phenotype due to knowledge of the gene expression levels. It is non-negative and reaches its maximum when the gene expression perfectly predicts the phenotype.

For gene pairs (n=2), synergy is then calculated as:

Syn(G₁, G₂; C) = I(G₁, G₂; C) - [I(G₁; C) + I(G₂; C)].

Equivalently, synergy can be expressed in terms of conditional entropies:

Syn(G₁, G₂; C) = H(C|G₁) + H(C|G₂) - H(C|G₁, G₂) - H(C).

A positive synergy value indicates that the joint expression of G₁ and G₂ provides more information about C than the sum of their individual contributions, implying a cooperative interaction specifically related to the phenotype. Negative synergy (redundancy) occurs when the genes provide overlapping information.

The method is applied exhaustively to all possible gene pairs in the dataset. Given the large number of genes (e.g., ~12,000), this results in tens of millions of pairwise computations. To manage computational load, the algorithm is parallelized across multiple processing units, with each unit handling a subset of gene pairs. Efficient implementation of the UPGMA clustering and entropy integration allows the entire analysis to be completed in approximately one hour on a 200-node computing cluster.

To assess the statistical significance of observed synergies, the invention employs permutation testing. Two types of permutations are used to generate null distributions. In Permutation A, the phenotype labels of the samples are randomly shuffled, breaking any association between gene expression and the phenotype while preserving the correlation structure among genes within each sample. In Permutation B, the expression values of each gene are independently shuffled within each phenotype class, preserving the marginal association of each gene with the phenotype but destroying any joint interactions between genes. Both permutation schemes are performed multiple times (e.g., 100 iterations), and for each iteration, the maximum synergy across all gene pairs is recorded.

The distribution of maximum synergies from the permuted data is modeled using the Gumbel (type-I extreme value) distribution, which describes the limiting distribution of maxima from independent samples. Maximum likelihood estimation is used to fit the location and scale parameters of the Gumbel distribution. The p-value for the highest synergy observed in the real data is then computed as the probability under the fitted null distribution of observing a synergy value at least as large as the observed one. This p-value is extremely small (e.g., <10⁻¹⁵) for top-scoring pairs, indicating high statistical significance.

For the full set of gene pairs, false discovery rate (FDR) correction is applied to control the expected proportion of false positives among the reported synergistic interactions. A large number of synergy scores (e.g., 10⁸) are sampled from the permuted datasets to estimate the empirical null distribution. For each real gene pair with synergy score Sᵢ, a raw p-value is estimated as the fraction of permuted scores that are greater than or equal to Sᵢ. The FDR-adjusted p-value is then computed using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure:

P̂ᵢ^FDR = min_{j ≥ i} (N/j * P̂ⱼ),

where N is the total number of gene pairs tested, and the scores are sorted in descending order. Gene pairs with FDR-adjusted p-values below a chosen threshold (e.g., α = 0.05) are deemed statistically significant and included in the synergy network.

The synergy network is a graph where nodes represent genes and edges represent statistically significant synergistic interactions. Visualization of this network reveals hubs—genes that participate in many synergistic pairs—which may serve as key regulators or biomarkers of the phenotype. In the context of prostate cancer, the invention identifies RBP1 and PTGDS as central hubs. RBP1 (cellular retinol-binding protein-1) is known to be downregulated in several cancers and functions as an inhibitor of the PI3K/Akt survival pathway; its downregulation promotes cell survival by inhibiting apoptosis. PTGDS (prostaglandin D2 synthase) is also linked to apoptosis and PI3K downregulation. The synergy network shows that both RBP1 and PTGDS form high-synergy pairs with numerous partner genes, many of which are involved in oxidative stress response (e.g., EEF1B2, FTL, UQCRH) and ribosomal function (e.g., ribosomal proteins and translation factors).

The molecular logic underlying these interactions follows a consistent pattern: prostate cancer tends to occur when RBP1 (or PTGDS) is expressed at low levels and its partner gene is expressed at high levels. This Boolean-like relationship is not captured by traditional correlation-based methods, which would miss the conditional dependence. The synergy network thus reveals combinatorial rules that govern disease state, offering insights into potential therapeutic strategies that target multiple components simultaneously.

The invention is validated using an independent prostate cancer dataset. Although the smaller sample size limits statistical power, the qualitative patterns of joint expression for top synergistic pairs are preserved, supporting the reproducibility of the findings. Additionally, mislabeled samples identified in the original dataset (e.g., sample T39 consistently appearing as an outlier) are corroborated by their anomalous position in scatter plots, demonstrating the method's sensitivity to data quality.

The methodology is not limited to gene pairs; it can be extended to higher-order interactions using the multivariate definition of synergy. For example, the synergy of a triplet of genes is defined as:

Syn(G₁, G₂, G₃; C) = I(G₁, G₂, G₃; C) - [I(G₁, G₂; C) + I(G₁, G₃; C) + I(G₂, G₃; C)] + [I(G₁; C) + I(G₂; C) + I(G₃; C)].

This extension can uncover more complex cooperative modules but requires larger sample sizes for reliable estimation.

Furthermore, the invention is applicable beyond gene expression. Any continuous biomarker data—such as protein abundance, phosphorylation states, or metabolite concentrations—can be analyzed using the same framework, enabling the construction of multi-omic synergy networks.

In summary, the present invention provides a robust, statistically sound, and computationally feasible method for uncovering phenotype-specific gene interactions from continuous expression data. By focusing on synergy rather than mere correlation, it reveals cooperative biological mechanisms that are invisible to conventional approaches, thereby advancing our understanding of complex diseases and informing the development of combination therapies.
