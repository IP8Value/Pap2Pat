# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The development of effective therapeutics for central nervous system (CNS) disorders remains one of the most formidable challenges in modern medicine. Despite significant investment in neuroscience research and drug development, the attrition rate of CNS drug candidates remains exceptionally high, with only approximately 8% of compounds entering Phase I clinical trials ultimately achieving regulatory approval. This failure is largely attributable to inadequate preclinical models that fail to recapitulate the complex physiological and pathological features of the human brain, particularly the blood-brain barrier (BBB)—a highly selective interface that tightly regulates molecular and cellular trafficking between the systemic circulation and the central nervous system. Traditional in vitro models, including two-dimensional (2D) monolayer cultures of brain endothelial cells, have provided foundational insights into BBB biology but suffer from critical limitations: they often rely on immortalized cell lines that do not reflect the phenotypic and functional characteristics of primary human cells, lack the three-dimensional architecture essential for proper cell-cell interactions, and omit key cellular components of the neurovascular unit (NVU), such as pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons. These omissions severely compromise the predictive validity of such models for assessing drug permeability, neurotoxicity, and disease mechanisms.

More advanced in vitro BBB models have emerged in recent years, incorporating co-cultures of primary human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs) with astrocytes and pericytes—three cell types known to be essential for inducing and maintaining BBB integrity. These tri-culture systems have demonstrated improved expression of tight junction proteins (e.g., ZO-1, claudin-5), adherens junctions (e.g., VE-cadherin), and functional transporters (e.g., P-glycoprotein, GLUT1), thereby better mimicking the restrictive nature of the human BBB. However, even these improved models fall short of capturing the full complexity of the human cortical microenvironment. The NVU in vivo is not merely a vascular structure but a dynamic, multicellular ecosystem wherein endothelial cells interact not only with pericytes and astrocytes but also with microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons—each contributing to BBB homeostasis, immune surveillance, metabolic support, and neural function. For instance, microglia modulate tight junction expression and respond to inflammatory stimuli, while neurons influence the expression of BBB-specific enzymes and transporters. Oligodendrocytes, though primarily associated with myelination, also participate in metabolic coupling and may affect barrier function through paracrine signaling. The absence of these cell types in existing models limits their utility in studying neuroinflammatory diseases, neurodegenerative disorders, and the off-target effects of therapeutics that may impact non-endothelial brain cells after crossing the BBB.

Moreover, current models are poorly suited for modeling adult-onset neurological conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and ischemic stroke—conditions that predominantly affect aging populations and involve complex interactions among all NVU cell types. Many preclinical studies rely on rodent-derived cells or non-human systems that do not fully replicate human-specific gene expression profiles, transporter kinetics, or immune responses. The advent of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology has enabled the derivation of human neurons, microglia, and oligodendrocytes from patient-specific sources, opening the door to personalized disease modeling and drug screening. Yet, integrating these iPSC-derived cells into a cohesive, physiologically relevant 3D organoid that includes a functional BBB has remained an unmet challenge. Prior attempts to generate brain organoids have focused on neural tissue development but have largely failed to incorporate a vascularized, barrier-forming endothelium that envelops the parenchymal core—a structural feature critical for modeling drug penetration and neurotoxicity.

Additionally, there is a pressing need for models that can simulate pathological conditions such as hypoxia, which occurs during ischemic stroke and traumatic brain injury. Hypoxia disrupts BBB integrity by altering the expression and localization of junctional proteins, leading to vasogenic edema and neuronal damage. Existing 2D models have demonstrated hypoxia-induced changes in barrier function, but they lack the spatial and cellular complexity necessary to study how hypoxia affects the integrated response of the entire NVU. A 3D model that recapitulates the multicellular architecture of the human cortex and responds to hypoxic insult with physiologically relevant changes in junctional protein distribution would represent a significant advance in stroke modeling and therapeutic development.

Furthermore, the ability to assess the charge selectivity and size exclusion properties of the BBB in a human-relevant context is crucial for predicting whether a drug candidate can reach its target in the brain. Small, lipophilic molecules like MPTP readily cross the BBB, whereas hydrophilic or charged molecules such as MPP+ and mercury(II) ions are typically excluded. Validating these selective permeability properties in a model that includes all major NVU cell types would provide a robust platform for early-stage drug screening, reducing late-stage clinical failures due to poor brain penetration or unexpected neurotoxicity.

In summary, the field lacks a comprehensive, human-derived, three-dimensional in vitro model of the neurovascular unit that incorporates all six major cell types of the adult human cerebral cortex—endothelial cells, pericytes, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons—in a spatially organized architecture that supports the formation of a functional, charge-selective blood-brain barrier. Such a model would enable more accurate prediction of drug permeability, neurotoxicity, and disease mechanisms, thereby accelerating the development of safe and effective CNS therapeutics.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, three-dimensional human neurovascular unit (NVU) organoid model that recapitulates the multicellular composition, spatial organization, and functional properties of the adult human cerebral cortex, including a physiologically relevant blood-brain barrier (BBB). This organoid model comprises six distinct human cell types: human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs), human brain vascular pericytes (HBVPs), human astrocytes (HAs), human microglia (HMs), human oligodendrocytes (HOs), and human neurons (HNs). Critically, the endothelial cells self-organize to form an outer layer that encapsulates the inner parenchymal core composed of the remaining five cell types, thereby mimicking the in vivo anatomical arrangement of the BBB surrounding brain tissue. The organoid is fabricated using a staged assembly method that enhances endothelial coverage and promotes the formation of functional tight and adherens junctions, resulting in a barrier that exhibits charge selectivity, expresses key transport proteins (e.g., P-glycoprotein and GLUT1), and responds dynamically to physiological and pathological stimuli.

The invention further encompasses methods for producing these organoids, which involve the initial formation of a neural-glial core from astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and neurons, followed by the addition of endothelial cells and pericytes to coat the surface of the core. This staged approach ensures robust endothelial enwrapment and improves the integrity of the BBB compared to single-step self-assembly methods. The organoids maintain high cell viability for at least 21 days in culture, allowing for long-term studies of drug toxicity, disease progression, and cellular responses.

Additionally, the invention provides methods of use for the NVU organoid model in a variety of applications, including but not limited to: (1) screening drug candidates for their ability to cross the BBB; (2) assessing neurotoxicity and glial toxicity of compounds that penetrate or bypass the BBB; (3) modeling neurological diseases such as ischemic stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease in a human-relevant context; (4) evaluating the effects of hypoxia and other pathological insults on BBB integrity and NVU function; and (5) enabling personalized medicine approaches through the use of patient-derived iPSCs to generate disease-specific organoids.

The organoid model demonstrates functional BBB properties, including low permeability to large molecules such as IgG under baseline conditions and increased permeability upon exposure to barrier-disrupting agents like histamine. It also exhibits charge selectivity, as evidenced by the exclusion of positively charged mercury(II) ions and the hydrophilic metabolite MPP+, while permitting the passage of the lipophilic prodrug MPTP. Under hypoxic conditions, the organoid recapitulates key pathological features of ischemic stroke, including the disruption of tight junction proteins (ZO-1, claudin-5) and adherens junction proteins (β-catenin, VE-cadherin), thereby validating its utility as a model for cerebrovascular disease.

In sum, the present invention overcomes the limitations of prior art models by providing a physiologically accurate, human-derived, multicellular 3D organoid that integrates all major components of the neurovascular unit and supports the formation of a functional, responsive blood-brain barrier. This platform represents a transformative tool for CNS drug discovery, toxicology, and disease modeling.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### 1. Blood, Brain Barrier Models and Methods of Making the Same.

The present invention provides a three-dimensional human neurovascular unit (NVU) organoid that accurately models the structural and functional characteristics of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in the adult human cerebral cortex. This organoid is composed of six distinct human cell types: human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs), human brain vascular pericytes (HBVPs), human astrocytes (HAs), human microglia (HMs), human oligodendrocytes (HOs), and human neurons (HNs). The spatial organization of these cells within the organoid mirrors the in vivo architecture of the NVU, wherein HBMECs and HBVPs localize to the periphery of the organoid to form a continuous, barrier-forming layer that encapsulates an inner core comprising HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs. This configuration enables the formation of a functional BBB that restricts the paracellular diffusion of molecules and exhibits selective permeability based on size, charge, and lipophilicity.

The organoid is fabricated using a staged assembly method that significantly enhances the coverage and integrity of the endothelial layer compared to conventional single-step self-assembly techniques. In the first stage, a neural-glial core is formed by combining HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs in a defined ratio—typically 15% HAs, 5% HMs, 15% HOs, and 20% HNs, by cell count—and culturing the mixture in a hanging drop plate for 48 hours under conditions that promote spheroid formation. The culture medium during this stage consists of a 50:50 mixture of astrocyte medium (without growth supplements) and neural maintenance medium, supplemented with heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and rat tail collagen I to support cell adhesion and matrix deposition. After the neural-glial core has formed, HBMECs and HBVPs are added in a second stage at a ratio of 30% HBMECs and 15% HBVPs, respectively, relative to the total cell population. The addition of these vascular cells 48 hours after core formation allows them to preferentially migrate to the outer surface of the spheroid, where they establish close associations with one another and with the underlying astrocytic processes, thereby promoting the maturation of tight and adherens junctions.

The resulting organoids exhibit high cell viability for at least 21 days in vitro, as confirmed by live-dead staining assays, with no significant differences in viability between the core and the periphery. Immunofluorescent staining demonstrates that HBMECs express the endothelial marker CD31 and localize uniformly to the organoid surface, forming a near-complete coating that is markedly superior to that achieved by single-step assembly methods. Furthermore, the endothelial cells express key BBB-associated proteins, including the tight junction proteins zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and claudin-5, the adherens junction proteins vascular endothelial (VE)-cadherin and β-catenin, the efflux transporter P-glycoprotein (P-gp), and the glucose transporter GLUT1. The proper localization of β-catenin at the plasma membrane, rather than in the nucleus, indicates stabilization of adherens junctions and supports the transcriptional upregulation of claudin-5, both of which are essential for BBB maturation.

The barrier function of the organoid is validated through permeability assays using fluorescently labeled molecules. For example, when incubated with FITC-conjugated IgG (approximately 150 kDa), untreated organoids show minimal fluorescence in the core, indicating restricted paracellular transport. In contrast, organoids pre-treated with histamine—a known mediator of transient BBB opening—exhibit significantly increased IgG penetration into the core, demonstrating the dynamic responsiveness of the barrier. Similarly, the organoid exhibits charge selectivity: mercury(II) chloride, which dissociates in aqueous media to yield positively charged Hg²⁺ ions, causes substantial cytotoxicity in BBB-deficient organoids (lacking HBMECs, HBVPs, and HAs) but minimal toxicity in BBB-intact organoids, confirming the barrier’s ability to exclude hydrophilic cations. Likewise, the lipophilic prodrug MPTP crosses the BBB and induces neurotoxicity in intact organoids, whereas its hydrophilic, positively charged metabolite MPP+ does not, unless the BBB is pharmacologically disrupted with histamine.

The organoid can be produced using primary human cells or induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived cells, enabling both general applicability and patient-specific disease modeling. HBMECs, HBVPs, and HAs are typically sourced from commercial providers of primary human cells, while HMs, HOs, and HNs are derived from human iPSCs using established differentiation protocols. The use of iPSC-derived cells allows for the incorporation of genetic backgrounds associated with neurological disorders, facilitating the creation of isogenic or patient-matched organoids for mechanistic and therapeutic studies.

In summary, the method of making the NVU organoid involves: (a) preparing a suspension of HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs in a defined ratio; (b) culturing the suspension in hanging drop plates for 48 hours to form a neural-glial core; (c) adding HBMECs and HBVPs to the core; and (d) continuing culture in a composite medium that supports the survival and function of all six cell types. The resulting organoid constitutes a physiologically relevant, human-derived model of the BBB and adjacent brain parenchyma, suitable for a wide range of biomedical applications.

### 2. Methods of Use.

The human neurovascular unit (NVU) organoid of the present invention serves as a versatile platform for numerous applications in drug discovery, toxicology, and disease modeling. One primary method of use involves the screening of drug candidates for their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB). By exposing the organoid to a test compound and measuring its concentration in the parenchymal core—via mass spectrometry, fluorescence, or functional assays—researchers can determine whether the compound penetrates the BBB, a critical factor in the development of CNS therapeutics. Compounds that fail to cross the barrier can be chemically modified or formulated with delivery enhancers, while those that do cross can be further evaluated for efficacy and safety.

A second method of use entails the assessment of neurotoxicity and glial toxicity. Because the organoid contains all major cell types of the human cortex, it enables the detection of cell-type-specific toxic effects that may be missed in simpler models. For instance, a compound that crosses the BBB may selectively damage dopaminergic neurons (as indicated by loss of tyrosine hydroxylase expression) or induce reactive astrogliosis (as shown by upregulation of GFAP), providing early warnings of potential adverse effects. Toxicity can be quantified using assays such as ATP production, lactate dehydrogenase release, or caspase activation, and can be correlated with barrier integrity to distinguish between direct neurotoxicity and secondary effects due to BBB disruption.

A third method of use is the modeling of neurological diseases. The organoid can be subjected to pathological insults such as hypoxia to simulate ischemic stroke. When cultured under 0.1% oxygen for 24 hours, the organoid exhibits significant downregulation and disorganization of tight and adherens junction proteins, including ZO-1, claudin-5, β-catenin, and VE-cadherin, mirroring the vascular leakage observed in stroke patients. This model can be used to test neuroprotective agents that stabilize the BBB or promote recovery. Additionally, by incorporating iPSC-derived cells from patients with Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, or multiple sclerosis, the organoid can be used to study disease-specific mechanisms, such as amyloid-beta accumulation, alpha-synuclein propagation, or demyelination, in a human-relevant, multicellular context.

A fourth method of use involves the evaluation of BBB modulation strategies. The organoid responds predictably to known barrier-altering agents: histamine increases permeability, while anti-inflammatory cytokines may enhance barrier function. This allows researchers to screen for compounds that can temporarily open the BBB for drug delivery or strengthen it in neuroinflammatory conditions.

Finally, the organoid supports personalized medicine approaches. By generating organoids from a patient’s own iPSCs, clinicians can test individual responses to CNS drugs, predict susceptibility to neurotoxicity, or model rare genetic disorders. This capability is particularly valuable for optimizing treatment regimens in heterogeneous diseases such as epilepsy or glioblastoma.

In all these methods, the organoid’s human origin, multicellular complexity, and functional BBB provide a level of physiological relevance unmatched by prior models, thereby improving the translation of preclinical findings to clinical outcomes.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Example 1 demonstrates the successful assembly and characterization of a four-cell-type organoid comprising human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMECs), human brain vascular pericytes (HBVPs), human astrocytes (HAs), and human neuronal cells (HCN-2). The cells were labeled with distinct fluorescent dyes and mixed in a ratio of 1:1:5:6 (HBMEC:HBVP:HA:HCN-2), respectively. The mixture was cultured in a hanging drop plate for 96 hours to facilitate self-assembly into spheroids. Confocal microscopy revealed that HBMECs and HBVPs localized to the periphery of the organoid, forming an outer layer, while HAs were positioned just beneath this layer and HCN-2 neurons formed the inner core. This spatial organization recapitulates the in vivo arrangement of the neurovascular unit, wherein endothelial and pericyte cells constitute the vascular wall and neurons reside in the parenchyma.

To assess barrier formation, the organoids were immunostained for zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), a canonical tight junction protein essential for BBB integrity. Confocal imaging confirmed the presence of ZO-1 at the interfaces between adjacent HBMECs, indicating the establishment of functional tight junctions. However, gaps in ZO-1 staining were observed, suggesting incomplete endothelial coverage—a limitation of the single-step self-assembly method.

Barrier functionality was further evaluated using a permeability assay with FITC-labeled IgG. Untreated organoids exhibited minimal IgG fluorescence in the core, consistent with a restrictive barrier. In contrast, organoids pre-treated with 4.5 mM histamine for 15 minutes before IgG exposure showed significantly increased fluorescence throughout the core, demonstrating that the barrier is dynamically responsive to physiological mediators of permeability. These results confirm that the four-cell-type organoid forms a vascular-like barrier with functional tight junctions that can be modulated by external stimuli, laying the groundwork for more complex, six-cell-type models.

### Example 2. Human Cortex Model with Spheroid Culture System

Example 2 describes the construction and validation of a six-cell-type human cortical organoid containing HBMECs, HBVPs, HAs, human microglia (HMs), human oligodendrocytes (HOs), and human neurons (HNs). The organoids were generated using the staged assembly method: first, HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs were combined in a ratio of 15:5:15:20 and cultured in hanging drop plates for 48 hours to form a neural-glial core. Then, HBMECs and HBVPs were added at ratios of 30% and 15%, respectively, and the culture was continued for an additional 48 hours before transfer to 96-well plates for long-term maintenance.

Cell viability was assessed on days 5, 7, 10, and 21 using calcein AM (live cells, green) and ethidium homodimer-1 (dead cells, red). Confocal imaging of Z-stacked slices showed uniform viability throughout the organoid, with over 90% live cells at all time points, confirming the compatibility of all six cell types in long-term co-culture.

Immunofluorescent staining verified the identity and distribution of each cell type: CD31-positive HBMECs formed a continuous outer layer; PDGFRβ-positive pericytes were interspersed among the endothelial cells; GFAP-positive astrocytes extended processes inward; Iba1-positive microglia were distributed throughout the parenchyma; O4 and MBP-positive oligodendrocytes indicated the presence of both progenitor and mature stages; and MAP2-positive neurons formed a dense network in the core, with a subset expressing tyrosine hydroxylase, confirming dopaminergic identity. Additionally, the organoids expressed functional BBB markers: P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and GLUT1 were detected in the endothelial layer, indicating active efflux and nutrient transport capabilities, respectively.

These results demonstrate that the staged assembly method produces a viable, multicellular human cortical organoid that accurately represents the cellular diversity and spatial organization of the adult human brain, providing a robust platform for downstream applications.

### Example 3: Bioprint Functional Cortical Tissue

Although the primary focus of the invention is on self-assembled spheroids, Example 3 explores the adaptation of the six-cell-type composition to a three-dimensional bioprinting platform to generate functional cortical tissue with enhanced structural control. A bioink was formulated by suspending the six cell types—HBMECs, HBVPs, HAs, HMs, HOs, and HNs—in a hydrogel matrix composed of fibrinogen and Matrigel, which supports cell viability and promotes endothelial tubulogenesis. The bioink was loaded into a multi-nozzle bioprinter, and a concentric printing strategy was employed: the neural-glial core (HAs, HMs, HOs, HNs) was printed first, followed by a surrounding shell of HBMECs and HBVPs. Post-printing, the constructs were cultured in a composite medium identical to that used for spheroids.

After 14 days, the bioprinted tissues exhibited high cell viability (>85%) and organized cellular architecture. CD31 staining revealed a confluent endothelial layer at the periphery, while ZO-1 and claudin-5 were detected at endothelial junctions, confirming barrier formation. Permeability assays with FITC-IgG showed low baseline penetration, which increased upon histamine treatment, validating functional BBB properties. Moreover, the bioprinted tissues supported neuronal network activity, as evidenced by calcium imaging showing synchronized bursts of activity in the core.

This example demonstrates that the six-cell-type formulation is compatible with advanced biofabrication techniques, enabling the creation of scalable, architecturally defined cortical tissues for high-throughput screening or implantation studies.

### Example 4. Three-Dimensional Bioprinting of the Human Neurovascular Unit

Example 4 extends the bioprinting approach to create a more complex, vascularized model of the human neurovascular unit. Using a sacrificial bioink composed of Pluronic F-127, a branched vascular network was printed within a chamber, which was then filled with the six-cell-type bioink. After gelation, the Pluronic was removed by cooling, leaving behind perfusable channels. HBMECs and HBVPs were seeded into these channels to form a lumen, while the surrounding matrix contained the neural-glial core.

After 21 days of culture under perfusion, the construct developed a hierarchical vascular network lined with CD31-positive endothelial cells expressing ZO-1 and claudin-5. The parenchymal region contained all four neural cell types, with microglia adopting ramified morphologies and oligodendrocytes ensheathing neuronal processes. When perfused with FITC-dextran (70 kDa), the vascular network retained the tracer, indicating intact barrier function, whereas leakage occurred upon histamine administration.

This example illustrates the potential to scale the invention into a perfusable, vascularized neurovascular unit model that can be integrated with microfluidic systems for dynamic drug delivery and real-time monitoring of barrier function, representing a significant advancement toward organ-on-a-chip applications.
