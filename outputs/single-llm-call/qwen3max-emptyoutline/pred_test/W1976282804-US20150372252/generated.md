# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

Organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) have emerged as a cornerstone technology in modern display and lighting applications due to their intrinsic advantages, including self-emissive properties, wide viewing angles, fast response times, and the potential for flexible and lightweight form factors. Among the various OLED architectures, phosphorescent OLEDs (PHOLEDs) have garnered significant attention because they can theoretically achieve 100% internal quantum efficiency by harvesting both singlet and triplet excitons through the use of organometallic emitters such as iridium complexes. Despite these promising characteristics, the practical performance of PHOLEDs is often limited by inefficient light extraction, as a substantial portion of the generated photons—typically estimated at 70–80%—remains trapped within the device due to waveguide modes, substrate modes, and surface plasmon polaritons at metal-organic interfaces. This inherent inefficiency has spurred extensive research into optical engineering strategies aimed at enhancing light outcoupling without compromising device stability or color purity.

One such strategy involves the integration of microcavity structures into OLED architectures. A microcavity is formed by placing an emissive layer between two reflective surfaces, typically a highly reflective back electrode and a partially reflective front electrode, thereby creating a resonant optical cavity that selectively amplifies emission at specific wavelengths aligned with the cavity’s resonant modes. Microcavity OLEDs offer several compelling advantages: they can produce highly saturated colors with narrow emission spectra, thereby expanding the color gamut of display systems; they can direct emitted light preferentially along the normal viewing direction, improving luminance efficiency for front-facing applications; and, under optimal conditions, they can enhance the overall photon outcoupling efficiency by constructively interfering with the radiative decay rate of excitons. These benefits have led to the widespread adoption of microcavity designs in high-end active-matrix OLED (AMOLED) displays, particularly for green and red subpixels where color purity and efficiency are critical.

However, despite the theoretical promise and commercial deployment of microcavity OLEDs, significant challenges remain in achieving consistent and predictable performance across the full visible spectrum, particularly for blue-emitting devices. Empirical studies have revealed a marked discrepancy in the degree of luminance enhancement achievable in red, green, and blue microcavity OLEDs. For instance, while green microcavity PHOLEDs have demonstrated current efficiency enhancements of up to two-fold relative to non-cavity counterparts, similar improvements have not been consistently observed in blue devices. This inconsistency cannot be fully explained by differences in photon outcoupling alone, as both quantum efficiency (the ratio of emitted photons to injected electrons) and current efficiency (luminance per unit current, measured in cd/A) are influenced by additional factors, most notably the spectral sensitivity of the human eye as defined by the photopic luminosity function. The photopic luminosity function, standardized by the Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage (CIE), peaks at 555 nm (green) and declines sharply toward the blue and red ends of the visible spectrum. Consequently, even if a microcavity device emits the same number of photons as a reference device, its perceived luminance—and thus its current efficiency—can vary significantly depending on the spectral alignment of its emission peak with the photopic curve.

This interplay between optical resonance, outcoupling efficiency, and photopic response introduces a complex optimization landscape that has not been adequately addressed in prior art. Conventional approaches to microcavity design have predominantly focused on maximizing reflectivity and minimizing absorption in the semitransparent electrode, often using thin metal layers such as silver or magnesium-silver alloys. While these materials offer reasonable transparency and conductivity, they suffer from high optical losses due to their large extinction coefficients and the excitation of surface plasmon modes at organic-metal interfaces, which dissipate energy as heat rather than light. Alternative approaches employing dielectric distributed Bragg reflectors (DBRs)—multilayer stacks of alternating high- and low-refractive-index materials—have shown promise due to their high reflectivity and low absorption in targeted wavelength bands. However, the successful implementation of DBR-based microcavities requires precise control over both the reflectivity spectrum of the DBR and the optical cavity length, which together determine the resonant wavelength and quality factor (Q-factor) of the cavity. Moreover, the cavity length must be tuned not only to match the peak emission of the phosphor but also to align with the maximum of the photopic luminosity function when optimizing for luminance output, especially in green devices.

The challenge is further compounded in blue-emitting PHOLEDs, where the need for deep blue color coordinates (e.g., CIE y < 0.20) often necessitates shifting the emission peak to shorter wavelengths (e.g., below 470 nm), away from the region of higher photopic sensitivity. This spectral shift can result in a paradoxical situation: although the microcavity may enhance the number of outcoupled photons at the desired blue wavelength, the corresponding drop in photopic luminosity may negate or even reverse the gains in current efficiency. In extreme cases, as demonstrated in recent studies, blue microcavity devices exhibit no improvement—or even a degradation—in current efficiency despite measurable enhancements in external quantum efficiency, solely due to the unfavorable positioning of the emission peak relative to the photopic curve. This phenomenon underscores a critical gap in the prior art: the lack of a systematic framework that jointly optimizes photon outcoupling, cavity resonance, and photopic luminosity across the red, green, and blue color channels.

Furthermore, existing microcavity OLEDs often employ single-emitting-layer architectures, which may suffer from exciton quenching at electrode or charge transport layer interfaces, imbalanced charge injection, and broad emission spectra that reduce color purity. While double-emitting-layer (DEL) or multi-emitting-layer structures have been proposed to confine the exciton recombination zone and improve charge balance, their integration with microcavity optics has not been thoroughly explored, particularly in the context of DBR-based substrates. The synergistic combination of DEL architectures with precisely tuned microcavities could potentially overcome multiple limitations simultaneously—enhancing both internal quantum efficiency through better exciton management and external quantum efficiency through optimized optical resonance—but such an approach has not been realized in a manner that accounts for the full interplay of optical, electrical, and perceptual factors.

In summary, the background art reveals a fragmented understanding of microcavity effects in PHOLEDs, characterized by ad hoc design choices, inconsistent performance across color channels, and a failure to holistically consider the role of photopic luminosity in determining practical luminance output. There remains a pressing need for a comprehensive and systematic approach to microcavity OLED design that integrates high-reflectivity, low-loss optical components with precisely controlled cavity lengths and advanced emitting layer architectures, all calibrated to maximize not just photon count but also perceptually relevant luminance. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing a novel class of microcavity phosphorescent OLEDs that achieve unprecedented levels of current efficiency and color saturation through the coordinated optimization of DBR reflectivity, cavity length, and emitting layer structure, with explicit consideration of the photopic luminosity function in the design process.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel class of microcavity organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) that achieve significantly enhanced current efficiency and color saturation by systematically optimizing the interplay between optical resonance, photon outcoupling, and photopic luminosity. Unlike conventional approaches that focus solely on maximizing reflectivity or narrowing emission spectra, the invention recognizes that the practical luminance output of a microcavity OLED is governed by a tripartite relationship among the number of outcoupled photons, the spectral position of the emission peak, and the sensitivity of the human eye as defined by the standard photopic luminosity function. By integrating high-reflectivity, low-absorption distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) substrates with precisely tuned cavity lengths and advanced emitting layer architectures, the invention achieves record-breaking current efficiencies—particularly in the green channel—while maintaining or improving color purity across the red, green, and blue spectrum.

Central to the invention is the use of indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated DBR substrates composed of alternating quarter-wave stacks of titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and silicon dioxide (SiO₂), engineered to exhibit peak reflectivities at 610 nm, 550 nm, and 474 nm for red, green, and blue devices, respectively. These DBR substrates provide superior optical performance compared to traditional semitransparent metal electrodes, eliminating parasitic absorption losses and suppressing surface plasmon modes that degrade efficiency. The cavity length—the total optical path length between the reflective back electrode and the DBR—is meticulously controlled by adjusting the thicknesses of hole transport, emitting, and electron transport layers to align the resonant emission peak with both the intrinsic emission spectrum of the phosphorescent dopant and, critically, the maximum of the photopic luminosity function at 555 nm for green devices. This dual alignment ensures that enhancements in photon outcoupling are translated directly into gains in perceived luminance.

The invention further discloses that the enhancement in current efficiency does not always correlate directly with the enhancement in external quantum efficiency due to variations in photopic luminosity. For example, in green microcavity OLEDs, a device with a resonant peak at 550 nm—coinciding with the DBR reflectivity maximum and the photopic peak—achieves a 140% enhancement in current efficiency, which exceeds the 101% enhancement in integrated photon output, solely due to the favorable spectral positioning. Conversely, in blue devices, forcing the emission peak to shorter wavelengths to achieve deep blue color coordinates (e.g., CIE y < 0.20) results in a significant reduction in photopic luminosity, which can offset or negate gains in photon outcoupling, leading to minimal or no improvement in current efficiency despite measurable quantum efficiency enhancements. This insight forms the basis of a new design principle: the optimization of microcavity OLEDs must account for photopic luminosity as a primary objective function, not merely as a secondary consideration.

To further amplify performance, the invention incorporates a double-emitting-layer (DEL) architecture in conjunction with the microcavity structure. The DEL consists of two adjacent emissive layers—TCTA doped with a green phosphor and CBP doped with the same phosphor—engineered to confine the exciton recombination zone at the interface between the layers. This confinement minimizes exciton quenching at charge transport interfaces and improves charge balance, thereby increasing internal quantum efficiency. When combined with a DBR substrate tuned to 555 nm, the DEL microcavity OLED achieves a maximum current efficiency of 224 cd/A, representing the highest reported value for a single-emitting-unit phosphorescent OLED without additional light extraction techniques. The addition of a macrolens to extract substrate-guided modes further boosts the total external quantum efficiency to an estimated 50%, demonstrating the scalability of the approach.

The invention also provides a method for designing and fabricating microcavity OLEDs that includes the steps of: (1) selecting a DBR substrate with a reflectivity peak matched to the target emission color and, for green devices, aligned with the photopic luminosity maximum; (2) determining an optimal cavity length that positions the resonant emission peak at the intersection of the phosphor’s emission spectrum, the DBR reflectivity peak, and the photopic sensitivity curve; (3) fabricating the OLED stack with precise layer thicknesses to achieve the target optical path length; and (4) optionally incorporating a DEL structure to enhance internal quantum efficiency. This method enables the reproducible fabrication of high-efficiency, high-color-purity microcavity OLEDs across the visible spectrum, with particular efficacy in the green channel where photopic sensitivity is highest.

In summary, the present invention overcomes the limitations of prior art by introducing a holistic design framework that unifies optical, electrical, and perceptual considerations in microcavity OLED development. The resulting devices exhibit unprecedented current efficiencies, expanded color gamuts, and improved angular color stability, making them ideally suited for next-generation AMOLED displays, high-dynamic-range imaging, and energy-efficient solid-state lighting.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

### METHODS AND MATERIALS

The present invention is embodied in a series of microcavity organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs) fabricated on specialized substrates and structured with precise layer thicknesses to optimize both photon outcoupling and photopic luminosity. The foundational component of the invention is the use of indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) substrates, which serve as the bottom reflective electrode and define the optical characteristics of the microcavity. These DBR substrates are constructed from two pairs of quarter-wave optical stacks comprising alternating layers of titanium dioxide (TiO₂) and silicon dioxide (SiO₂). The thickness of each layer is calculated based on the target wavelength and the refractive indices of the materials to ensure constructive interference at the desired reflectivity peak. Specifically, for red-emitting devices, the DBR is engineered to exhibit a reflectance maximum at 610 nm; for green-emitting devices, at 550 nm; and for blue-emitting devices, at 474 nm. This spectral tuning is achieved by adjusting the physical thickness of the TiO₂ and SiO₂ layers during deposition, taking into account the dispersion of their refractive indices across the visible spectrum. The resulting peak reflectivities are approximately 65% for red, 75% for green, and 62% for blue DBR substrates, providing a high-reflectivity, low-absorption mirror that minimizes optical losses compared to conventional semitransparent metal electrodes.

Prior to OLED fabrication, the DBR substrates undergo a surface treatment to enhance hole injection and improve film morphology. Specifically, the substrates are subjected to ultraviolet ozone (UVO) treatment for 15 minutes in ambient air. This process cleans the ITO surface, increases its work function, and improves wettability for subsequent solution processing. Following UVO treatment, a 25-nanometer-thick layer of poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)-polystyrenesulfonic acid (PEDOT:PSS), specifically the AI 4803 formulation, is deposited via spin-coating and then baked at 180°C for 15 minutes in air. This layer serves as the hole injection layer (HIL), facilitating efficient hole transport from the ITO anode into the subsequent organic layers.

The organic stack is then constructed through sequential thermal evaporation under high vacuum conditions (typically below 1×10⁻⁶ Torr) to ensure high purity and precise thickness control. The hole transport layer (HTL) consists of 1,1-bis[(di-4-tolylamino)phenyl]cyclohexane (TAPC), with a thickness ranging from 20 to 45 nanometers, depending on the target cavity length for the specific color channel. For green-emitting devices, the emissive layer (EML) is formed by co-evaporating 4,4′-N,N′-dicarbazole-biphenyl (CBP) as the host material with 7 weight percent tris(2-phenylpyridine)iridium (Ir(ppy)₃) as the phosphorescent dopant, resulting in a layer thickness between 20 and 30 nanometers. For red-emitting devices, the EML comprises two sublayers: a 15-nanometer-thick layer of 3,5′-N,N′-dicarbazolebenzene (mCP) doped with 15 weight percent iridium(III)bis[2-methyldibenzo-(f,h)quinoxaline](acetylacetonate) (Ir(MDQ)₂(acac)), followed by a 15-nanometer-thick layer of 2,2′,2″-(1,3,5-benzenetriyl)tris(1-phenyl-1H-benzimidazole) (TPBi) also doped with 15 weight percent Ir(MDQ)₂(acac). For blue-emitting devices, the EML is a 20-nanometer-thick layer of mCP doped with 5 weight percent iridium(III) bis[(4,6-difluorophenyl)pyridinato-N,C²′]picolinate (FIrpic).

The electron transport layer (ETL) is selected based on the emission color to ensure compatibility with the triplet energy levels of the host materials and to facilitate balanced charge transport. For green and red devices, the ETL is composed of tris[3-(3-pyridyl)-mesityl]borane (3TPYMB), with a thickness ranging from 45 to 60 nanometers. For blue devices, the ETL is 3,5,3′,5′-tetra-3-pyridyl-[1,1′;3′,1″]terphenyl (B3PyPB), deposited to a thickness of 45 to 55 nanometers. Both materials are chosen for their high electron mobility and sufficiently high triplet energy to prevent exciton quenching. An electron injection layer (EIL) of 1 nanometer of lithium fluoride (LiF) is then deposited, followed by a 100-nanometer-thick aluminum cathode, which serves as the highly reflective back mirror of the microcavity.

In certain embodiments, a double-emitting-layer (DEL) architecture is employed to further enhance device performance. In this configuration, the green EML is replaced by two adjacent layers: a first layer of 4,4′,4″-tris(carbazol-9-yl)triphenylamine (TCTA) doped with Ir(ppy)₃ and a second layer of CBP doped with Ir(ppy)₃. The TCTA layer has a hole mobility of approximately 3×10⁻⁴ cm²/V·s and a very low electron mobility of 1×10⁻⁸ cm²/V·s, while CBP exhibits balanced hole and electron mobilities of 3×10⁻⁴ and 2×10⁻³ cm²/V·s, respectively. This mobility mismatch confines the exciton recombination zone to the TCTA/CBP interface, reducing quenching losses and improving charge balance. The DBR substrate for DEL devices is tuned to a reflectivity peak of 555 nm to maximize alignment with the photopic luminosity function.

Device characterization is performed using a suite of standardized techniques. Current-luminance-voltage (J-L-V) characteristics are measured using a Keithley Series 2400 source meter in conjunction with a Keithley Series 6485 picoammeter and a calibrated Newport silicon photodiode. Luminance is cross-verified using a Konica Minolta LS-100 luminance meter. Electroluminescence (EL) spectra are acquired using an Ocean Optics HR4000 spectrometer, with measurements taken at a fixed current density of 0.1 mA/cm² to ensure comparability across devices. To assess the contribution of substrate modes to total light output, a macrolens is attached to the substrate using an index-matching gel, and angular emission profiles are recorded to integrate intensity over all viewing angles. The photopic luminosity of each device is calculated by integrating the product of the normalized EL spectrum S(λ) and the standard CIE photopic luminosity function V(λ) over the visible spectrum (380–780 nm), with V(λ) normalized to unity at 555 nm. External quantum efficiency (EQE) is derived from luminance and current density data using established conversion formulas, while current efficiency is reported directly in candelas per ampere (cd/A).

This detailed fabrication and characterization protocol enables the precise control and evaluation of microcavity effects, allowing for the systematic optimization of OLED performance in accordance with the principles of the present invention.
