# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Prostate cancer (CaP) remains one of the most prevalent malignancies among men worldwide and represents a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in the United States. Despite advances in early detection and therapeutic interventions, significant disparities persist in disease incidence, progression, and outcomes across different racial and ethnic populations. Notably, men of African ancestry—particularly African American (AA) men—experience a disproportionately higher burden of prostate cancer compared to their Caucasian American (CA) counterparts. Epidemiological data consistently demonstrate that AA men are diagnosed with prostate cancer at younger ages, present with more aggressive disease features, and suffer significantly higher rates of recurrence and mortality following treatment. These disparities have long been attributed to a complex interplay of socioeconomic, environmental, and healthcare access factors; however, an increasing body of evidence suggests that intrinsic biological and genetic differences may also play a critical role in driving the observed clinical heterogeneity.

Historically, the molecular characterization of prostate cancer has been heavily skewed toward populations of European ancestry, with the majority of large-scale genomic studies—including those conducted by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)—predominantly enrolling CA patients. As a result, the foundational understanding of recurrent genomic alterations in prostate cancer—such as the TMPRSS2-ERG gene fusion, PTEN deletions, SPOP mutations, and CHD1 loss—has been largely derived from CA cohorts. While these alterations are now recognized as hallmark events in prostate tumorigenesis, emerging research indicates that their prevalence and functional significance may differ substantially across ancestral groups. For instance, multiple studies have reported that the TMPRSS2-ERG fusion, which occurs in approximately 50% of CA prostate cancers, is markedly less frequent in AA tumors, with estimates ranging from 20% to 30%. Similarly, PTEN loss—a key driver of PI3K/AKT pathway activation and disease progression—has been observed at lower frequencies in AA men, particularly in early-stage disease. These findings challenge the assumption that molecular mechanisms of prostate carcinogenesis are uniform across populations and underscore the urgent need for ancestry-informed genomic analyses.

The biological basis for these population-specific differences remains incompletely understood. One hypothesis posits that germline genetic variation influences somatic mutation landscapes through mechanisms such as differential DNA repair capacity, chromatin architecture, or androgen receptor (AR) signaling dynamics. Another possibility is that distinct evolutionary pressures or founder effects in ancestral populations have shaped the repertoire of targetable genomic vulnerabilities in prostate epithelial cells. Regardless of the underlying etiology, the clinical implications are profound: diagnostic biomarkers, prognostic indicators, and therapeutic targets validated primarily in CA populations may lack sensitivity or specificity when applied to AA patients, potentially contributing to suboptimal care and persistent health inequities.

Recent technological advances in next-generation sequencing (NGS), particularly whole-genome sequencing (WGS), have enabled comprehensive, unbiased interrogation of the entire cancer genome, including non-coding regions, structural variants, and complex rearrangements that are often missed by exome or targeted panel approaches. WGS offers unprecedented resolution for identifying population-specific genomic signatures and uncovering novel driver events that may be enriched in underrepresented groups. In this context, the systematic comparison of WGS data from well-matched AA and CA prostate cancer cohorts represents a powerful strategy for elucidating ancestry-associated molecular distinctions and discovering new biomarkers with potential clinical utility.

Prior to the present invention, no study had performed deep whole-genome sequencing of primary prostate tumors from AA and CA men with matched normal controls to systematically compare the full spectrum of somatic alterations—including single nucleotide variants (SNVs), insertions and deletions (indels), copy number variations (CNVs), and structural variations (SVs)—in early-stage disease. Furthermore, while focal deletions on chromosome 3q13.31 involving the LSAMP (Limbic System-Associated Membrane Protein) locus had been sporadically reported in other cancers such as osteosarcoma and acute myeloid leukemia, their role in prostate cancer—and particularly their association with African ancestry and aggressive clinical behavior—had not been previously recognized or validated. The absence of such knowledge has hindered the development of precision oncology approaches tailored to the unique genomic landscape of AA prostate cancer.

This patent application arises from the unexpected discovery that recurrent deletions and rearrangements at the LSAMP locus on chromosome 3q13.31 constitute a novel, ancestry-enriched genomic alteration in prostate cancer that is strongly associated with African ancestry and rapid disease progression. Contrary to the prevailing paradigm that emphasizes PTEN loss and ERG fusions as dominant early drivers, the present invention reveals that AA prostate cancers frequently bypass these canonical pathways and instead harbor LSAMP inactivation as a key tumorigenic event. This finding not only redefines the molecular taxonomy of prostate cancer but also provides a foundation for new diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic strategies specifically designed for AA men—a population historically underserved by precision medicine initiatives.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and compositions for the diagnosis, prognosis, and risk stratification of prostate cancer in men of African ancestry based on the detection of genomic alterations at the LSAMP locus on chromosome 3q13.31. Specifically, the invention discloses that recurrent deletions, rearrangements, or other inactivating alterations affecting the LSAMP gene are significantly enriched in prostate tumors from African American (AA) men compared to Caucasian American (CA) men and are strongly associated with aggressive disease features, including biochemical recurrence, advanced pathological stage (pT3), and metastatic progression. These findings establish LSAMP locus alterations as a novel ancestry-specific biomarker for prostate cancer with high clinical utility in AA populations.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for diagnosing or assessing the risk of aggressive prostate cancer in a subject of African ancestry, comprising detecting the presence of a deletion, rearrangement, or other genomic alteration within the LSAMP locus on chromosome 3q13.31 in a biological sample obtained from the subject. The biological sample may be a tumor tissue specimen, blood sample, urine sample, or other bodily fluid containing circulating tumor DNA. The genomic alteration may encompass partial or complete deletion of the LSAMP gene, disruption of LSAMP regulatory elements, or formation of fusion transcripts involving LSAMP and adjacent genes such as ZBTB20 or GAP43. Detection may be performed using any suitable molecular technique, including but not limited to fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based assays, next-generation sequencing (NGS), whole-genome sequencing (WGS), SNP array analysis, or digital droplet PCR (ddPCR).

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for prognosticating prostate cancer in a subject of African ancestry, comprising determining the presence or absence of an LSAMP locus alteration in a prostate tumor sample and correlating the result with clinical outcome. The presence of an LSAMP alteration is indicative of a higher risk of disease recurrence, progression to metastatic disease, or poor response to standard therapies, thereby enabling clinicians to tailor treatment intensity and surveillance strategies accordingly. For example, AA patients with LSAMP-altered tumors may be candidates for earlier intervention with intensified local therapy, systemic adjuvant treatment, or enrollment in clinical trials targeting alternative oncogenic pathways.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a kit for detecting LSAMP locus alterations in prostate cancer samples, comprising at least one probe or primer set specifically designed to interrogate the LSAMP genomic region on chromosome 3q13.31. The kit may include FISH probes targeting the LSAMP gene and a control probe for chromosome 3 centromere, PCR primers for amplifying LSAMP exons or breakpoint regions, or NGS capture probes for enriching the LSAMP locus during sequencing. The kit may further include reagents, buffers, controls, and instructions for use in a clinical or research laboratory setting.

The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions and therapeutic strategies for treating LSAMP-altered prostate cancer in AA men. Given that LSAMP functions as a putative tumor suppressor, its loss may create dependencies on compensatory signaling pathways that can be therapeutically exploited. For instance, LSAMP-deficient tumors may exhibit heightened sensitivity to inhibitors of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, epigenetic modulators, or agents targeting DNA damage repair deficiencies. The invention thus provides a rationale for patient selection in clinical trials of targeted therapies based on LSAMP status, particularly in AA populations where conventional biomarkers like PTEN loss or ERG fusion are less prevalent.

Additionally, the invention includes computational methods and bioinformatics tools for analyzing genomic data to identify LSAMP alterations. These tools may be implemented as software algorithms integrated into clinical decision support systems, enabling automated interpretation of WGS, SNP array, or FISH data to generate LSAMP alteration reports for individual patients. The algorithms may incorporate ancestry-informed thresholds for calling deletions, taking into account population-specific baseline copy number variation at the 3q13.31 locus.

Collectively, the present invention addresses a critical unmet need in prostate cancer management by providing the first ancestry-specific biomarker system tailored to the unique genomic landscape of AA men. By shifting the focus from canonical alterations like PTEN and ERG—which are less relevant in this population—to the newly discovered LSAMP locus, the invention enables more accurate risk stratification, personalized treatment planning, and improved clinical outcomes for a historically disadvantaged patient group.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “African ancestry” refers to individuals who self-identify as being of African descent, including but not limited to African American, Afro-Caribbean, or Sub-Saharan African populations. In the context of genomic analysis, African ancestry may be confirmed objectively through principal component analysis (PCA) of genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data, wherein the subject’s genetic profile clusters with reference populations such as Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria (YRI) or African ancestry in Southwest USA (ASW) from the HapMap or 1000 Genomes projects.

The term “LSAMP locus” refers to the genomic region on chromosome 3q13.31 encompassing the Limbic System-Associated Membrane Protein (LSAMP) gene (Entrez Gene ID: 3973), including its coding exons, introns, promoter regions, enhancers, and adjacent regulatory sequences. The LSAMP locus may extend from approximately base pair 110,000,000 to 112,000,000 on chromosome 3 (GRCh37/hg19 assembly), though the precise boundaries may vary depending on the specific alteration detected. The term includes not only the LSAMP gene itself but also neighboring genes such as ZBTB20, GAP43, and GSK3B that may be co-deleted or involved in fusion events due to the proximity of their genomic locations.

An “LSAMP alteration” or “LSAMP locus alteration” refers to any somatic genomic change that results in the functional inactivation, reduced expression, or dysregulation of the LSAMP gene. Such alterations include, but are not limited to: (i) homozygous or heterozygous deletions of part or all of the LSAMP gene; (ii) chromosomal rearrangements such as translocations, inversions, or duplications that disrupt the LSAMP coding sequence or regulatory elements; (iii) point mutations in LSAMP exons that introduce premature stop codons or splice site defects; and (iv) epigenetic silencing via promoter hypermethylation. In the context of this invention, the most clinically relevant LSAMP alterations are focal deletions and rearrangements detected at the DNA level, as these have been shown to correlate strongly with aggressive disease in AA men.

“Prostate cancer” or “CaP” refers to malignant neoplasms arising from the epithelial cells of the prostate gland, including adenocarcinoma (the most common histological subtype), as well as rarer variants such as ductal adenocarcinoma, small cell carcinoma, or squamous cell carcinoma. The invention is particularly applicable to clinically localized primary prostate cancer (e.g., Gleason score 6 or 7 with primary pattern 3), which represents the majority of newly diagnosed cases in Western countries, but may also extend to advanced or metastatic disease.

“Biological sample” refers to any material derived from a human subject that contains nucleic acids suitable for genomic analysis. Examples include formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue, fresh frozen tissue, peripheral blood, plasma, serum, urine, or circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA). For the purposes of detecting LSAMP alterations, tumor tissue is preferred due to the requirement for paired tumor-normal comparison to distinguish somatic from germline variants, though liquid biopsy approaches may be developed for monitoring disease progression or recurrence.

“Detection” or “detecting” refers to the identification of a specific genomic alteration using a molecular assay. The sensitivity and specificity of detection depend on the methodology employed and the quality of the biological sample. In clinical practice, detection may be qualitative (presence/absence) or quantitative (e.g., percentage of tumor cells with deletion, copy number estimate).

“Aggressive prostate cancer” refers to disease characterized by features associated with poor prognosis, including but not limited to: Gleason score ≥8, pathological stage pT3 or higher, positive surgical margins, lymph node involvement, biochemical recurrence (defined as a rising prostate-specific antigen [PSA] level after definitive treatment), metastatic progression, or cancer-specific mortality. In the context of this invention, LSAMP alterations are specifically associated with biochemical recurrence and pT3 stage in AA men.

“FISH” or “fluorescence in situ hybridization” refers to a cytogenetic technique that uses fluorescently labeled DNA probes to detect and localize specific DNA sequences on chromosomes within intact nuclei. For LSAMP deletion testing, a locus-specific probe labeled with one fluorophore (e.g., CytoOrange) is used in combination with a chromosome 3 centromere control probe labeled with a different fluorophore (e.g., CytoGreen). Deletion is scored when a significant proportion of tumor cells show loss of the LSAMP signal relative to the centromere signal.

“Whole-genome sequencing” or “WGS” refers to a high-throughput sequencing method that determines the complete DNA sequence of an organism’s genome at a single time. In cancer genomics, WGS is typically performed on paired tumor and normal (germline) samples to identify somatic alterations. The invention utilizes WGS data to discover and validate LSAMP alterations, but clinical implementation may rely on more cost-effective targeted methods.

“Ancestry-informed” refers to analytical approaches that take into account population-specific genetic variation when interpreting genomic data. For example, copy number calling algorithms may use ancestry-matched reference panels to reduce false positives due to benign population-specific deletions or duplications.

### Examples

The following examples illustrate the discovery, validation, and clinical application of LSAMP locus alterations as a novel biomarker for aggressive prostate cancer in men of African ancestry. These examples are provided for illustrative purposes only and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention.

**Example 1: Whole-Genome Sequencing of AA and CA Prostate Tumors Reveals Ancestry-Enriched LSAMP Alterations**

Fourteen patients undergoing radical prostatectomy for clinically localized prostate cancer were enrolled in a discovery cohort, including seven self-identified African American (AA) and seven Caucasian American (CA) men. All tumors were histologically confirmed as Gleason score 6 or 7 with primary pattern 3 and contained 80–95% tumor cellularity. Matched normal DNA was obtained from peripheral blood lymphocytes. Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on all 28 samples (14 tumor-normal pairs) to a depth of at least 30× coverage. Somatic variants were identified using a multi-algorithm pipeline that included Strelka for single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels, BreakDancer and Genomatix Mapper for structural variations (SVs), and cn.MOPs and Control-FREEC for copy number variations (CNVs). Principal component analysis (PCA) of germline SNP data confirmed that the self-reported ancestry of all patients aligned with their genetic profiles, with AA patients clustering with YRI/ASW reference populations and CA patients clustering with CEU.

Comprehensive analysis of the WGS data revealed that while many canonical prostate cancer alterations (e.g., SPOP mutations, CHD1 deletions) were present in both groups, three key genomic features showed striking ancestry-specific patterns: (1) TMPRSS2-ERG fusions were less frequent in AA (29%) than CA (56%) tumors; (2) PTEN deletions were virtually absent in AA tumors (0/7) compared to CA tumors (4/7); and (3) alterations at the chromosome 3q13.31 locus were exclusively observed in AA tumors. Specifically, three of seven AA tumors harbored LSAMP locus alterations: two exhibited large deletions (23 Mb and 1 Mb) spanning the ZBTB20-LSAMP region, and one displayed a complex rearrangement resulting in a novel fusion transcript. None of the CA tumors showed any alterations in this region. Notably, all three AA patients with LSAMP alterations experienced disease recurrence (two biochemical recurrences, one metastasis) within five years of surgery, whereas none of the four AA patients without LSAMP alterations recurred during the same period.

**Example 2: Validation of LSAMP Deletion Frequency in the TCGA Cohort**

To confirm the initial WGS findings in an independent dataset, the ancestry of 320 prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD) patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) was re-evaluated using PCA of SNP array data. This analysis stratified the cohort into 41 AA and 279 CA patients. Copy number analysis using ASCAT software revealed that LSAMP-centered deletions were present in 11 of 41 (27%) AA tumors versus 37 of 279 (13%) CA tumors, a statistically significant difference (p = 0.023). This independent validation confirmed that LSAMP deletions are enriched in AA prostate cancer and are not an artifact of the small discovery cohort.

**Example 3: FISH-Based Validation of LSAMP and PTEN Deletions in Tissue Microarrays**

A tissue microarray (TMA) was constructed from radical prostatectomy specimens of 42 AA and 59 CA patients, with a total of 174 and 299 tumor cores, respectively. Interphase FISH assays were performed using custom-designed probes: a 500-kb probe spanning the ZBTB20-LSAMP region and a 600-kb LSAMP-centered probe, each paired with a chromosome 3 centromere control. A separate PTEN locus-specific probe (450 kb) was used with a chromosome 10 centromere control. LSAMP deletion was defined as loss of LSAMP signal in >75% of evaluable tumor cells or focal loss in >50% of cells within a tumor gland cluster. Results showed LSAMP deletion in 26% of AA cases versus 7% of CA cases (p = 0.007), consistent with WGS and TCGA data. In contrast, PTEN deletion was observed in only 15% of AA cases versus 63% of CA cases (p = 1E-06), with an even more pronounced difference in Gleason 6 tumors (7% vs. 53%, p = 0.004).

**Example 4: Clinical Correlation of LSAMP Deletion with Disease Progression in AA Men**

Among the 42 AA patients in the TMA cohort, 11 (26%) harbored LSAMP deletions. Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that LSAMP-deleted tumors were significantly associated with biochemical recurrence (BCR) compared to LSAMP-intact tumors (log-rank p = 0.05). Additionally, LSAMP deletion correlated with advanced pathological stage (pT3 vs. pT2, p = 0.05). Multivariate analysis adjusting for Gleason score, PSA, and surgical margins confirmed that LSAMP deletion was an independent predictor of BCR in AA men (hazard ratio = 3.2, 95% CI: 1.1–9.4). These findings establish LSAMP deletion as a clinically actionable biomarker for risk stratification in AA prostate cancer.

**Example 5: Development of a Clinical FISH Assay for LSAMP Deletion Testing**

A standardized FISH assay was developed for routine clinical use, comprising a dual-color probe set: a CytoOrange-labeled LSAMP locus-specific probe (covering 600 kb centered on the LSAMP gene) and a CytoGreen-labeled chromosome 3 centromere control probe. The assay was validated on 20 known LSAMP-deleted and 20 LSAMP-intact tumors, achieving 95% sensitivity and 98% specificity. The assay protocol includes guidelines for scoring (minimum 100 tumor cells per core, exclusion of stromal and benign epithelial cells) and interpretation (deletion if >75% of tumor cells show single LSAMP signal with two centromere signals). This assay is now being implemented in a prospective clinical trial to guide adjuvant therapy decisions in AA prostate cancer patients.

**Example 6: Therapeutic Implications of LSAMP Loss**

Functional studies in prostate cancer cell lines engineered to harbor LSAMP deletions revealed increased activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway and enhanced sensitivity to AKT inhibitors compared to LSAMP-wildtype cells. Additionally, LSAMP-deficient cells showed elevated levels of DNA damage markers and synthetic lethality with PARP inhibitors. These preclinical data suggest that LSAMP-altered prostate cancers may benefit from targeted therapies against PI3K/AKT or DNA repair pathways, providing a rationale for biomarker-driven clinical trials in AA men selected by LSAMP status.

These examples collectively demonstrate that LSAMP locus alterations represent a novel, ancestry-specific genomic feature of prostate cancer with significant diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic implications for men of African ancestry.
