# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Automated image captioning—the task of generating natural language descriptions of visual content—has emerged as a cornerstone capability in computer vision and multimodal artificial intelligence. The utility of such systems spans a broad spectrum of applications, including but not limited to: enabling visually impaired individuals to access digital imagery through screen readers; facilitating semantic search in large-scale image repositories by converting visual queries into textual keys; supporting content moderation and metadata enrichment in media platforms; and enhancing human-computer interaction in robotics and augmented reality environments. Over the past decade, deep learning architectures combining convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for visual encoding with recurrent or transformer-based language models for text generation have become the de facto standard for this task. These models are typically trained using maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), where the objective is to maximize the probability of generating reference captions provided in benchmark datasets such as MS COCO or Flickr30k.

However, a fundamental limitation persists in this paradigm: the reference captions used for training and evaluation are often sparse, generic, and biased toward salient foreground objects, thereby neglecting fine-grained visual details that distinguish one image from another within a similar category. For instance, two photographs of “a dog on a beach” may differ significantly in terms of breed, posture, weather conditions, background scenery, or interactions with other entities—but standard captioning models, trained to mimic the statistical patterns of reference texts, frequently produce indistinguishable outputs like “a dog sitting on the sand.” This lack of distinctiveness undermines the practical value of generated captions in retrieval, accessibility, and forensic applications where precision and specificity are paramount.

To address exposure bias and improve alignment with human judgment, reinforcement learning (RL) techniques have been introduced, wherein models are trained to optimize non-differentiable metrics such as CIDEr (Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation) or SPICE via policy gradient methods like REINFORCE or self-critical sequence training (SCST). While these approaches yield improvements in n-gram overlap with reference captions, they remain fundamentally constrained by the quality and coverage of those references. Moreover, such metrics exhibit poor correlation with human assessments of descriptiveness, grammaticality, and factual accuracy, particularly when generated captions introduce novel yet valid details absent from the reference set. Recent efforts have attempted to incorporate external signals—such as visual question answering (VQA) accuracy or image-text retrieval scores—as auxiliary rewards to encourage richer content. Yet these strategies often require complex multi-objective balancing, additional annotation overhead, or reliance on secondary models whose biases may propagate into the captioning system.

Concurrently, the advent of large-scale contrastively trained multimodal encoders—most notably CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining)—has revolutionized cross-modal representation learning. Trained on hundreds of millions of noisy image-text pairs scraped from the internet, CLIP learns aligned embeddings such that matching image-caption pairs exhibit high cosine similarity while mismatched pairs are pushed apart in the joint embedding space. This property enables zero-shot transfer to downstream tasks like classification and retrieval without task-specific fine-tuning. Crucially, CLIP’s similarity score has been shown to correlate strongly with human judgments of image-text relevance, making it a compelling candidate as an intrinsic reward signal for caption generation. Unlike traditional metrics that penalize lexical deviation from references, CLIP evaluates semantic fidelity directly between the generated caption and the source image, thereby rewarding descriptive accuracy regardless of phrasing.

Nevertheless, direct application of CLIP as a reward function introduces new challenges. Because CLIP was not trained with a language modeling objective, its text encoder lacks explicit grammatical constraints, leading captioning models optimized solely for CLIP similarity to produce syntactically degenerate outputs—such as repetitive phrases (“a car a car a car”), fragmented clauses, or unnatural word order. These artifacts degrade readability and usability, offsetting gains in visual grounding. Prior work has attempted to mitigate this through hybrid objectives combining CLIP with CIDEr or BLEU, but such combinations reintroduce dependency on reference captions and risk diluting the distinctiveness incentive.

Furthermore, existing evaluation protocols are ill-equipped to assess fine-grained aspects of caption quality. Standard benchmarks report aggregate scores across coarse categories, masking performance disparities in critical dimensions such as background description, object attribute specification, or spatial relation modeling. Human evaluations, while more nuanced, are costly, inconsistent, and rarely decomposed into orthogonal criteria. Consequently, progress in generating truly informative and structured captions remains difficult to measure and compare.

In light of these limitations, there exists a pressing need for a captioning framework that: (1) leverages the semantic richness of contrastive multimodal models like CLIP to drive generation toward visually grounded, distinctive descriptions; (2) eliminates reliance on reference captions during reward computation to avoid dataset-induced biases; (3) enforces grammatical coherence without sacrificing descriptive fidelity; and (4) supports rigorous, multi-dimensional evaluation of caption quality beyond surface-level similarity. The present invention addresses all four needs through a novel training methodology centered on CLIP-guided reinforcement learning augmented with synthetic grammar-aware fine-tuning, accompanied by a new evaluation benchmark designed to quantify performance across discrete semantic axes.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for generating highly descriptive, grammatically correct, and visually grounded image captions without reliance on reference captions during training or reward computation. The core innovation lies in the use of a pre-trained multimodal contrastive model—specifically, CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining)—as the sole source of reward signal in a reinforcement learning framework, thereby encouraging the captioning model to produce text that maximizes semantic alignment with the input image rather than lexical overlap with human-written references. To overcome the grammatical degradation commonly observed when optimizing purely for CLIP similarity, the invention further introduces a novel fine-tuning strategy for the CLIP text encoder using synthetically generated negative captions that simulate common linguistic errors such as word repetition, deletion, insertion, swapping, and shuffling. This fine-tuning injects implicit grammatical priors into the text encoder, enabling it to better discriminate between fluent and degenerate captions during reward calculation, thus guiding the captioning policy toward both semantic accuracy and syntactic correctness.

The invention also includes a new evaluation dataset, termed FineCapEval, comprising 1,000 images annotated across four orthogonal criteria: background, object (including attributes such as color and shape), spatial relations between objects, and overall descriptive completeness. Each image is associated with five human-generated annotations per criterion, enabling fine-grained quantitative assessment of captioning performance beyond conventional metrics. This dataset facilitates rigorous benchmarking of caption distinctiveness, factual grounding, and structural coherence.

In operation, the system receives an input image and processes it through a vision encoder (e.g., ResNet-50 or ViT) to obtain a visual embedding. A transformer-based language model, conditioned on this embedding, generates a caption autoregressively. During training, the model is optimized via self-critical sequence training (SCST) using a reward function defined as the CLIP similarity score between the generated caption and the input image, computed as R(I, c) = exp(w ⋅ sim(f_I(I), f_T(c))), where f_I and f_T are the image and text encoders of CLIP, sim(·,·) denotes cosine similarity, and w is a temperature scaling factor (typically set to 2.5). Critically, no reference captions are used in reward computation, eliminating exposure to dataset-specific biases and enabling the model to discover image-specific details absent from training references.

To enhance grammaticality, the CLIP text encoder is first fine-tuned on a synthetic corpus constructed by perturbing reference captions from a standard dataset (e.g., MS COCO) using stochastic text corruption operations. These operations include: (1) repeating a random token one or more times; (2) removing a randomly selected token; (3) inserting a random token from the vocabulary at a random position; (4) swapping two adjacent tokens; and (5) shuffling a contiguous subsequence of tokens. The fine-tuning objective is to maximize the CLIP similarity for original (positive) captions while minimizing it for corrupted (negative) variants, effectively teaching the text encoder to associate low similarity scores with ungrammatical constructions. This adapted CLIP model then serves as the reward function in the SCST loop, yielding captions that are both visually precise and linguistically fluent.

Experimental validation demonstrates that captions generated by the proposed method achieve higher text-to-image retrieval recall than even the original human-provided reference captions, confirming their superior distinctiveness. On the newly introduced FineCapEval benchmark, the method outperforms CIDEr-optimized and MLE baselines across all four evaluation criteria—background, object, relation, and overall—with statistically significant margins. Human evaluators consistently prefer the invention’s captions over those from competing approaches in pairwise comparisons across five dimensions: overall quality, background description, object identification, attribute specification, and relational accuracy. Qualitative examples reveal that the system captures subtle yet discriminative details—such as “wet pavement indicating recent rain” or “a blue sign above the restaurant entrance”—that are omitted in reference captions and baseline generations.

The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in image captioning: moving from reference-mimicking to image-grounded generation, enabled by contrastive multimodal rewards and grammar-aware encoder adaptation. It is applicable to any domain requiring precise, accessible, and searchable visual descriptions, including assistive technology, digital asset management, autonomous systems, and content creation platforms.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Image Search System

The present invention is embodied in an image search system that leverages a novel captioning engine to generate rich, distinctive, and grammatically sound textual descriptions of images, which are then used as query keys or index entries in a multimodal retrieval architecture. This system fundamentally enhances the precision and recall of image search by ensuring that each indexed image is associated with a caption that accurately reflects its unique visual content, rather than a generic or incomplete summary derived from biased training references.

At the core of the system is a caption generation module trained according to the method described herein. This module comprises three principal components: (1) a vision encoder, (2) a language decoder, and (3) a reward computation unit based on a fine-tuned CLIP model. The vision encoder—implemented as a ResNet-50 backbone or a Vision Transformer (ViT)—processes the input image I ∈ ℝ^{H×W×3} to produce a fixed-dimensional visual embedding v = f_I(I) ∈ ℝ^d. This embedding captures global and local visual features relevant to semantic interpretation. The language decoder, implemented as a transformer-based sequence model P_θ(c|v), generates a caption c = (c_1, c_2, ..., c_T) token-by-token, where each token c_t is sampled from a vocabulary V conditioned on the visual context and previously generated tokens.

During inference, the system operates in a standard autoregressive manner: given an unseen image, it produces a single descriptive caption that serves as the textual proxy for that image in downstream search applications. However, the true innovation lies in the training regime, which ensures that this caption is optimized for maximal semantic alignment with the image itself, not with external references.

Training proceeds in two phases. In the first phase, the CLIP text encoder f_T is fine-tuned to recognize and penalize grammatical errors. This is achieved by constructing a synthetic training set from existing caption datasets (e.g., MS COCO train split). For each original caption c^+, five types of corrupted variants c^- are generated algorithmically:

- **Repetition**: A random token in c^+ is duplicated one or more times (e.g., “a car” → “a car car”).
- **Deletion**: A random token is removed (e.g., “a red car” → “a car”).
- **Insertion**: A random token from the vocabulary is inserted at a random position.
- **Swapping**: Two adjacent tokens are exchanged (e.g., “red car” → “car red”).
- **Shuffling**: A contiguous subsequence of length ≥2 is randomly permuted.

The fine-tuning objective is a contrastive loss that maximizes the CLIP similarity between the image and its original caption while minimizing similarity with each corrupted variant. Formally, for an image-caption pair (I, c^+), the loss is:

ℒ_finetune = −log σ(sim(f_I(I), f_T(c^+)) − max_{c^- ∈ C^-} sim(f_I(I), f_T(c^-)))

where σ is the sigmoid function and C^- is the set of corrupted captions. This procedure endows the text encoder with sensitivity to syntactic structure without requiring labeled grammaticality data.

In the second phase, the captioning model P_θ is trained via self-critical sequence training (SCST). At each training step, the model generates two captions for the same image I: a sampled caption ĉ ~ P_θ(·|I) and a greedy-decoded baseline caption ĉ_greedy. The reward for ĉ is computed as R(I, ĉ) = CLIP-S(I, ĉ) = exp(w ⋅ sim(f_I(I), f_T(ĉ))), where f_T is now the fine-tuned text encoder and w = 2.5. The baseline reward R(I, ĉ_greedy) is subtracted to reduce variance. The policy gradient is then:

∇_θ ℒ_SCST = −𝔼_{ĉ~P_θ} [(R(I, ĉ) − R(I, ĉ_greedy)) ⋅ ∇_θ log P_θ(ĉ|I)]

This update encourages the model to explore caption sequences that yield higher CLIP similarity, which—due to CLIP’s contrastive training on diverse web data—correlates with descriptive accuracy and distinctiveness.

Once trained, the captioning module is integrated into an image search pipeline. For indexing, every image in a database is passed through the system to generate a caption, which is stored alongside the image. For querying, a user may input either an image or a text query. If the query is textual, it is embedded using the same fine-tuned CLIP text encoder; if visual, it is embedded via the CLIP image encoder. The system then retrieves database entries by computing cosine similarities between the query embedding and all stored caption (or image) embeddings, returning the top-k matches.

Crucially, because the generated captions are optimized for CLIP similarity with their source images, they form a highly discriminative textual index. Experiments show that using these captions as queries achieves higher recall@K in text-to-image retrieval than using the original human reference captions—demonstrating that the system produces *better* textual proxies than humans in some cases, particularly when references omit distinguishing details.

The system further supports fine-grained search refinement. By leveraging the FineCapEval-inspired annotation schema, captions can be parsed (manually or via auxiliary models) into background, object, and relation components. A user could then issue structured queries like “find images with a blue car parked in front of a white house with trees in the background,” and the system would match against the relevant caption segments. While full parsing is beyond the current scope, the inherent richness of the generated captions makes such decomposition feasible.

Importantly, the entire pipeline operates without any reference captions during deployment or reward computation, ensuring robustness to dataset biases and enabling application to domains where reference descriptions are unavailable or unreliable. The system is language-agnostic in principle; although demonstrated in English, it can be extended to other languages by using multilingual CLIP variants or fine-tuning on monolingual data.

In summary, the image search system of the present invention delivers unprecedented caption quality by unifying contrastive multimodal rewards with synthetic grammar supervision, resulting in textual representations that are both faithful to visual content and suitable for high-precision retrieval.
