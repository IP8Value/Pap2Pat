## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is a fundamental biological process essential for development, tissue homeostasis, and the elimination of damaged or infected cells. Dysregulation of apoptosis contributes to numerous human diseases, including cancer, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune conditions, and ischemic injuries such as myocardial infarction and stroke. Central to the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway of apoptosis is the BCL-2 protein family, which governs mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP)—the point of no return in the apoptotic cascade. Among this family, the pro-apoptotic effector proteins BAX and BAK are indispensable mediators of MOMP; their activation leads to oligomerization within the mitochondrial outer membrane, resulting in cytochrome c release, caspase activation, and ultimately, cell death.

BAX exists predominantly as an inactive monomer in the cytosol of healthy cells. Upon apoptotic stimuli, BAX undergoes a series of conformational changes that enable its translocation to the mitochondria, insertion into the outer membrane, and oligomerization to form pores. This activation process is tightly regulated by interactions with other BCL-2 family members, particularly the “BH3-only” proteins such as tBID, BIM, and PUMA, which act as direct activators by engaging specific sites on BAX. Structural and biochemical studies have identified at least two critical regulatory regions on BAX: an N-terminal activation site involving helices α1 and α6 and the intervening loop, and a C-terminal hydrophobic groove that sequesters the transmembrane helix α9 in the inactive state. Activation involves displacement of the α1–α2 loop, exposure of the BH3 domain, release of α9, and large-scale refolding that enables membrane integration.

Despite significant advances in understanding BAX regulation, the dynamic nature of its conformational transitions and the lack of high-affinity, conformation-specific molecular probes have impeded detailed mechanistic dissection and therapeutic targeting. Traditional antibodies often fail to recognize transient or cryptic epitopes on intracellular proteins like BAX, and small-molecule modulators of BAX have been challenging to develop due to the protein’s structural plasticity and the absence of well-defined druggable pockets in its inactive state. Although recent efforts have yielded stapled peptides and a few small molecules that modulate BAX activity—either by mimicking activating BH3 domains or by allosterically inhibiting conformational changes—these tools remain limited in scope, potency, or pharmacological utility.

Phage display technology offers a powerful alternative for generating high-affinity binding proteins against challenging targets. Synthetic antibody fragment (Fab) libraries, engineered with controlled diversity in complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), can yield binders with exceptional specificity and affinity, even for conformationally dynamic proteins. Such Fabs can serve not only as research tools to trap and stabilize specific protein states but also as templates for therapeutic development or as competitive reagents in high-throughput screening campaigns for small-molecule modulators.

Prior to the present invention, no high-affinity synthetic antibodies capable of specifically recognizing monomeric, cytosolic BAX and modulating its activation had been reported. Existing conformation-sensitive antibodies, such as 6A7, recognize only activated BAX and are unsuitable for probing the early steps of BAX regulation. Moreover, no Fab or antibody had been demonstrated to bind the N-terminal activation site of BAX with sufficient affinity to competitively inhibit BH3-mediated activation. The absence of such tools has left a critical gap in the ability to interrogate the structural determinants of BAX activation and to develop pharmacological strategies aimed at either promoting BAX activity in cancer or suppressing it in degenerative diseases.

Thus, there remains a long-felt and unmet need for high-affinity, conformation-specific binding agents that target monomeric BAX, inhibit its activation by physiological triggers, and provide structural insights into the mechanisms governing BAX regulation. The present invention addresses this need by providing a novel class of synthetic Fabs that bind with nanomolar affinity to the N-terminal surface of monomeric BAX, block its interaction with activating BH3-only proteins, prevent mitochondrial translocation, and thereby suppress MOMP and apoptosis.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides isolated antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) that specifically bind to monomeric human BAX protein and inhibit its activation. In particular, the invention encompasses a panel of fourteen distinct synthetic Fabs, each comprising a heavy chain variable domain and a light chain variable domain, wherein said Fabs bind to full-length wild-type human BAX with an equilibrium dissociation constant (K_D) of less than 250 nanomolar, as measured by biolayer interferometry. These Fabs were derived from a phage-displayed synthetic antibody library through iterative selection against purified, monomeric BAX under non-denaturing conditions, ensuring recognition of the native, cytosolic conformation of the protein.

A key aspect of the invention is the identification of Fab 3G11, which exhibits high-affinity binding to BAX (K_D ≈ 2.6 nM) and functions as a potent inhibitor of BAX activation. Fab 3G11 binds stoichiometrically in a 1:1 complex with monomeric BAX and recognizes an epitope localized to the N-terminal surface of BAX, encompassing helix α1, helix α6, and the intervening α1–α2 loop—collectively known as the N-terminal activation site. This site is the same region engaged by pro-apoptotic BH3-only proteins such as tBID, BIM, and PUMA to trigger BAX conformational changes. By occupying this site, Fab 3G11 competitively blocks the binding of activating BH3 domains and prevents the structural rearrangements necessary for BAX translocation to the mitochondria and subsequent oligomerization.

The invention further provides methods of using these Fabs to inhibit BAX-mediated mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP). In liposome-based assays that recapitulate the mitochondrial membrane environment, Fab 3G11 and other high-affinity Fabs (e.g., 3E8, 2B1) dose-dependently suppress tBID-induced BAX-dependent release of encapsulated fluorophores, whereas low-affinity binders (e.g., 2A5) show no inhibitory effect. Similarly, in isolated mitochondria from Bak−/− mice, Fab 3G11 inhibits tBID/BAX-induced cytochrome c release in a concentration-dependent manner. Crucially, Fab 3G11 also prevents the translocation of BAX from the cytosol to the mitochondrial membrane, as demonstrated by subcellular fractionation and immunoblotting, indicating that inhibition occurs at the earliest stage of BAX activation.

Structural characterization of the Fab 3G11–BAX interaction was achieved through hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HXMS) and computational docking. HXMS revealed significant protection from deuterium exchange in helices α1 and α6 and the α1–α2 loop upon Fab binding, confirming direct engagement of the N-terminal activation site. Mutagenesis studies validated these findings: substitution of key residues within this epitope—specifically Lys-21 in α1 and Arg-134 in α6—with glutamic acid (K21E, R134E) markedly reduced or abolished Fab 3G11 binding and its inhibitory function in liposomal assays. A structural model of the complex, generated using HADDOCK docking constrained by HXMS data and CDR sequences, shows Fab 3G11 making extensive hydrophobic and polar contacts with the solvent-exposed face of the α1/α6 interface.

Importantly, the Fabs of the invention bind both full-length BAX and a C-terminally truncated variant (BAX ΔC26, lacking the last 26 residues of helix α9) with comparable affinity, indicating that the epitope is independent of the canonical hydrophobic groove and the C-terminal regulatory helix. This distinguishes the mechanism of inhibition from allosteric modulators that target distal sites, such as the BH4 domain of BCL-2 or the viral protein vMIA, which bind to loops between α3–α4 and α5–α6 without overlapping the N-terminal activation site.

The invention thus provides, for the first time, a class of high-affinity protein therapeutics and research reagents that directly target and occlude the N-terminal activation site of monomeric BAX. These Fabs serve as powerful tools for:
(i) probing the structural dynamics of BAX activation;
(ii) validating the N-terminal site as a druggable target for small-molecule discovery;
(iii) developing competitive binding assays to screen for BAX inhibitors or activators;
(iv) modulating apoptosis in experimental models of disease; and
(v) informing the design of next-generation biologics or peptidomimetics for therapeutic intervention in conditions characterized by dysregulated BAX activity.

In one embodiment, the invention provides an isolated Fab comprising a heavy chain variable region having the amino acid sequence of SEQ ID NO:1 and a light chain variable region having the amino acid sequence of SEQ ID NO:2, or variants thereof that retain BAX-binding and inhibitory activity. In another embodiment, pharmaceutical compositions comprising such Fabs, optionally formulated with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, are provided for use in treating diseases involving aberrant apoptosis. Methods of inhibiting BAX activation in a cell or subject by administering an effective amount of a Fab of the invention are also encompassed.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Introduction

The BCL-2 protein family orchestrates the intrinsic pathway of apoptosis through a delicate balance of pro- and anti-apoptotic interactions. Among its members, BAX stands as a pivotal executioner whose activation culminates in mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), the irreversible commitment to cell death. In healthy cells, BAX resides as an inert monomer in the cytosol, its pro-apoptotic potential held in check by structural autoinhibition. Apoptotic signals, conveyed by BH3-only proteins such as tBID, relieve this inhibition through direct binding, triggering a cascade of conformational changes that culminate in BAX oligomerization within the mitochondrial membrane. Despite decades of research, the precise structural transitions that convert cytosolic BAX into its membrane-embedded pore-forming state remain incompletely understood, largely due to the transient and heterogeneous nature of intermediate conformations. This knowledge gap has hindered the development of targeted therapeutics aimed at modulating BAX activity in disease contexts—either to enhance killing in cancer or to preserve cells in neurodegeneration and ischemia.

The present invention overcomes these limitations by leveraging synthetic antibody technology to generate a suite of high-affinity Fabs that selectively recognize and stabilize the monomeric, cytosolic conformation of human BAX. Through rigorous biochemical, biophysical, and functional validation, we demonstrate that these Fabs, particularly Fab 3G11, bind with nanomolar affinity to a previously untargeted epitope on BAX—the N-terminal activation site—and act as potent inhibitors of BAX activation. This site, comprising helices α1 and α6 and the connecting loop, serves as the docking platform for activating BH3-only proteins. By sterically blocking this interface, Fab 3G11 prevents the initiating conformational changes required for mitochondrial translocation and pore formation.

The significance of this discovery lies not only in the provision of novel research tools but also in the validation of the N-terminal activation site as a viable target for pharmacological intervention. Prior attempts to modulate BAX have focused on allosteric sites or the C-terminal groove, yielding compounds with limited efficacy or unclear mechanisms. The Fabs described herein represent the first direct, high-affinity inhibitors of the N-terminal trigger site, offering a new paradigm for BAX modulation. Furthermore, their ability to form stable, stoichiometric complexes with monomeric BAX enables structural studies that were previously intractable, paving the way for rational drug design.

This detailed description outlines the methods used to discover and characterize these Fabs, presents the experimental evidence supporting their mechanism of action, and discusses their broad utility in basic research and therapeutic development. The data collectively establish that synthetic Fabs can be engineered to target dynamic, intracellular protein interfaces with high specificity, opening new avenues for probing and controlling complex biological pathways.

### Materials and Methods

Recombinant human full-length wild-type BAX, truncated BAX (ΔC26, lacking the C-terminal 26 residues corresponding to helix α9), and site-directed BAX mutants (K21E, R134E, K21E/R134E) were cloned into the pTYB1 expression vector (New England Biolabs) such that the BAX coding sequence was fused in-frame at the N terminus to an intein-chitin binding domain tag, using NdeI and SapI restriction sites. Expression constructs were transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 CodonPlus (DE3)-RIPL cells. Transformed cells were grown in Luria broth medium supplemented with appropriate antibiotics at 37 °C until reaching an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.8–1.0. Protein expression was induced by addition of 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), and cultures were incubated at 30 °C for 4 hours with shaking. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 rpm for 25 minutes at 4 °C, and the resulting pellets were flash-frozen or immediately processed.

For purification, cell pellets were resuspended in cold lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Cells were lysed using a microfluidizer at 4 °C, and the lysate was clarified by ultracentrifugation at 45,000 rpm for 1 hour at 4 °C. The supernatant was loaded onto a gravity-flow column packed with chitin beads (New England Biolabs) pre-equilibrated in lysis buffer. After extensive washing with lysis buffer, the column was washed with lysis buffer containing 50 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) to initiate self-cleavage of the intein tag, releasing untagged BAX into solution. Cleavage was allowed to proceed overnight at 4 °C. Eluted BAX was concentrated using a Centricon spin concentrator with a 10-kDa molecular weight cutoff (Millipore) and further purified by size exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated in gel filtration buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM KCl, pH 7.2). Monomeric BAX fractions, eluting at approximately 12 ml, were pooled, concentrated, and used immediately or stored at 4 °C for up to 48 hours to ensure conformational homogeneity.

Phage display selections were performed using a synthetic human Fab library (designated Library F) displayed on the surface of M13 phage. Library F was engineered with restricted diversity: CDR-H1 and CDR-H2 contained primarily tyrosine and serine residues, while CDR-L3 and CDR-H3 incorporated expanded diversity using nine amino acids (Tyr, Ser, Gly, Ala, Phe, Trp, His, Pro, Val) in a defined ratio and variable loop lengths to mimic natural antibody repertoires. Biotinylated or non-biotinylated monomeric BAX (1 µg per well in 100 µl PBS, pH 7.0) was immobilized on 96-well enzyme immunoassay plates (Corning) either by direct adsorption or via streptavidin-biotin coupling after biotinylation using EZ-Link Maleimide-PEG2-Biotin (Thermo Scientific). Three rounds of biopanning were conducted: phage libraries were incubated with immobilized BAX, unbound phage were washed away, and specifically bound phage were eluted, amplified in E. coli TG1 cells, and used as input for subsequent rounds. Output clones from round three were screened by direct phage ELISA; clones exhibiting signals at least tenfold above background (3% BSA control) were sequenced to identify unique Fab candidates.

Positive Fab clones were subcloned into Fab expression vectors by introducing a stop codon and a C-terminal hexahistidine tag upstream of the phage coat protein gene. Fab proteins were expressed periplasmically in E. coli BL21(DE3) grown in low-phosphate media at 30 °C for 18–22 hours. Cells were harvested, lysed with BugBuster reagent (Novagen), and the periplasmic extract was applied to a Ni-NTA affinity column (Qiagen) equilibrated in Tris-buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.0). After washing with TBS containing 20 mM imidazole, Fabs were eluted with 250 mM imidazole, dialyzed into PBS (pH 8.0), and further purified by protein A affinity chromatography (Pierce) to remove contaminants. Elution was performed with 100 mM glycine (pH 2.0), and fractions were immediately neutralized with 1 M Tris (pH 8.0). Final Fab preparations were buffer-exchanged into PBS (pH 7.0), quantified by absorbance at 280 nm using calculated extinction coefficients, and stored at −80 °C.

Binding affinities were assessed by direct ELISA: BAX (1 µg/well) was immobilized on plates, blocked with 3% BSA, and incubated with serial dilutions of Fab. Bound Fab was detected using horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-FLAG antibody (targeting a C-terminal FLAG tag on the Fab light chain), followed by colorimetric development with TMB substrate. EC50 values were derived by fitting data to a four-parameter logistic equation (GraphPad Prism). Competitive ELISAs employed either biotinylated Fab 3G11 (b3G11) competing with unlabeled Fabs for BAX binding, or phage-displayed Fabs competing with soluble Fabs, with detection via streptavidin-HRP or anti-M13-HRP, respectively.

Kinetic analysis was performed using biolayer interferometry (forteBio BLItz). Ni-NTA biosensors were loaded with 10 µg/ml Fab, then dipped into solutions of BAX at concentrations ranging from 30 to 200 nM. Association and dissociation rates were globally fitted to obtain ka, kd, and KD values.

Functional assays included liposome permeabilization: liposomes composed of phosphatidylcholine (48%), phosphatidylinositol (10%), dioleoyl phosphatidylserine (10%), phosphatidylethanolamine (28%), and tetraoleoyl cardiolipin (4%) were prepared by extrusion and loaded with ANTS/DPX fluorophore-quencher pair. Fabs, BAX (400 nM), and tBID (30 nM) were added to liposomes in assay buffer, and fluorescence dequenching (ex 355 nm, em 520 nm) was monitored over 90 minutes at 32 °C. Percent release was calculated relative to maximal release induced by 1% Triton X-100.

Mitochondrial assays used liver mitochondria isolated from Bak−/− mice. For translocation assays, mitochondria (0.75 mg/ml) were incubated with BAX (200 nM), tBID (100 nM), and Fab 3G11, then separated into supernatant and pellet fractions by centrifugation. Pellets were treated with sodium carbonate (pH 11.5) to remove peripherally associated proteins, solubilized in Triton X-100, and analyzed by Western blot for BAX (anti-BAX antibody, Cell Signaling #2772). Cytochrome c release assays used similar conditions with higher mitochondrial concentration (1.5 mg/ml) and longer incubation (60 min), with cytochrome c detected in supernatants using anti-cytochrome c antibody (BD Biosciences #556433).

Structural studies employed NMR spectroscopy: uniformly 15N-labeled BAX was titrated with unlabeled Fab 3G11, and 1H,15N HSQC spectra were acquired at 25 °C on a Bruker 600 MHz spectrometer. Hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HXMS) was performed by diluting BAX or BAX–Fab complexes into D2O buffer, quenching exchange at 10, 100, and 1000 seconds, digesting with immobilized pepsin, and analyzing peptides by LC-MS on an Orbitrap Elite. Deuteration levels were calculated using HDExaminer with back-exchange correction.

Computational docking used the HADDOCK web server. The BAX structure (PDB 1F16) and a homology model of Fab 3G11 were used as inputs. Ambiguous interaction restraints were derived from HXMS-protected BAX residues (α1: 19–45; α6: 123–142) and CDR residues of 3G11 (heavy chain loops 1–3: 26–32, 52–54, 96–101; light chain: 141–148, 165–168, 206–211). Docking generated 1,000 structures, refined in explicit solvent, and clustered based on RMSD.

### Results

Fourteen unique synthetic Fabs were isolated from Library F following three rounds of phage display selection against monomeric human BAX. Sequence analysis revealed high diversity in CDR-H3 and CDR-L3 lengths and compositions, with no significant homology among clones, indicating recognition of multiple epitopes or distinct binding modes on BAX. All Fabs were successfully expressed and purified to homogeneity. Direct ELISA determined EC50 values ranging from 2.3 nM (Fab 2B1) to 250 nM (Fab 2A5), with the majority exhibiting single- or double-digit nanomolar affinity. Biolayer interferometry confirmed these rankings, with Fab 3G11 displaying a KD of approximately 2.6 nM, characterized by slow dissociation (kd = 1.2 × 10−4 s−1).

Competitive ELISA demonstrated that six representative Fabs (3H1, 3G11, 3E8, 2A5, 2D9, 2B1) largely compete for overlapping epitopes on BAX. Biotinylated Fab 3G11 binding was inhibited by all Fabs except 2A5, consistent with 2A5’s low affinity. Similarly, soluble Fabs inhibited phage-displayed Fab binding in a manner correlating with their affinities: high-affinity Fabs (3E8, 2B1) broadly inhibited all phage clones, while 2A5 showed minimal competition. These data indicate that the Fabs recognize a common or proximal region on BAX, with affinity dictating competitive dominance.

Functionally, none of the 14 Fabs induced liposome permeabilization alone, nor did they activate BAX in the absence of tBID. However, when co-incubated with tBID and BAX, high-affinity Fabs (3G11, 3E8, 2B1) potently inhibited tBID-triggered ANTS/DPX release in a dose-dependent manner, achieving near-complete inhibition at 2 µM. In contrast, Fab 2A5 and a negative-control VEGF-specific Fab (YADS1) showed no inhibitory effect even at high concentrations. In isolated Bak−/− mitochondria, Fab 3G11 dose-dependently suppressed tBID/BAX-induced cytochrome c release. Subcellular fractionation revealed that Fab 3G11 also prevented BAX translocation to mitochondria, as evidenced by reduced BAX levels in the mitochondrial pellet fraction.

Size exclusion chromatography confirmed that Fab 3G11 forms a stable 1:1 complex with BAX. NMR titration of 15N-BAX with 3G11 resulted in progressive loss of BAX cross-peak intensity without chemical shift perturbations, indicative of intermediate-to-slow exchange on the NMR timescale and consistent with high-affinity binding. HXMS analysis of the BAX–3G11 complex showed significant protection from deuterium exchange in helices α1 (residues 19–45) and α6 (123–142) and the α1–α2 loop, with modest increased exchange in α7, α8, and α2. Binding affinity to BAX ΔC26 was comparable to full-length BAX, confirming the epitope is independent of helix α9.

HADDOCK docking, guided by HXMS restraints, generated a structural model wherein Fab 3G11 docks onto the N-terminal face of BAX, contacting α1 and α6. Mutagenesis of predicted interface residues Lys-21 (α1) and Arg-134 (α6) to glutamate disrupted Fab binding and inhibitory function: R134E and double mutants completely abrogated inhibition, while K21E partially reduced efficacy. ELISA confirmed reduced binding to these mutants. The epitope overlaps with the binding site for activating BH3 peptides (e.g., stapled BIM), explaining the competitive inhibition mechanism.

### DISCUSSION

The present invention establishes a novel class of synthetic Fabs that target monomeric BAX with high affinity and inhibit its activation by blocking the N-terminal trigger site. This represents a significant advance over prior art, which lacked tools capable of directly engaging this critical regulatory interface. The Fabs, particularly 3G11, provide unprecedented mechanistic insights: by preventing BAX translocation, they demonstrate that N-terminal engagement is essential for the earliest steps of activation, preceding membrane insertion and oligomerization.

Unlike allosteric inhibitors such as BCL-2 BH4 or vMIA peptides, which bind distal sites and indirectly suppress conformational changes, Fab 3G11 acts through direct steric occlusion of the BH3 docking site. This mechanism is analogous to competitive antagonists in receptor pharmacology and validates the N-terminal groove as a druggable pocket. The ability of these Fabs to bind both full-length and ΔC26 BAX suggests they are effective against cytosolic BAX before mitochondrial association, positioning them as ideal probes for early activation events.

Therapeutically, these Fabs offer a blueprint for developing BAX inhibitors in diseases of excessive cell death. While Fabs themselves may not be ideal drugs due to poor cell permeability, they can guide the design of cell-penetrating mimetics or serve as templates for small-molecule screens. Conversely, in cancer, understanding how to disrupt Fab binding could inform strategies to enhance BAX activation.

In summary, the invention provides a transformative set of tools that illuminate BAX regulation and open new therapeutic avenues. The disclosed Fabs are not merely binding reagents but functional modulators with a defined mechanism, filling a critical void in the field of apoptosis research and drug discovery.
