## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of virology and, more specifically, to methods and systems for the production of infectious hepatitis C virus (HCV) particles in vitro. The invention further pertains to recombinant cell lines engineered to produce authentic, replication-competent HCV particles without requiring HCV RNA replication during the production phase, thereby circumventing the emergence of adaptive mutations that compromise viral fitness and infectivity. The invention also encompasses compositions comprising such HCV particles, as well as their use in antiviral screening, vaccine development, studies of viral entry mechanisms, and ex vivo infection models using human liver tissue. The technical domain intersects with molecular biology, cell biology, virology, and biotechnology, particularly in the context of Flaviviridae family viruses and the development of robust platforms for the study of difficult-to-culture pathogens.

## BACKGROUND

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) is a major global health concern, chronically infecting an estimated 2–3% of the world’s population. Persistent HCV infection significantly elevates the risk of developing severe liver pathologies, including cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver failure, necessitating transplantation in advanced cases. Despite its clinical significance, the development of effective therapeutics and prophylactic vaccines has been hampered by substantial challenges in modeling HCV infection in vitro and in vivo. HCV belongs to the genus *Hepacivirus* within the *Flaviviridae* family and exhibits considerable genetic diversity, with at least six major genotypes and numerous subtypes identified. Genotypes 1a and 1b account for over two-thirds of diagnosed infections worldwide and are notably less responsive to older interferon-based therapies compared to other genotypes, underscoring the urgency of developing genotype-specific interventions.

A critical bottleneck in HCV research has been the lack of reliable small animal models. Historically, chimpanzees served as the primary non-human model for HCV infection due to their susceptibility to the virus and the development of liver pathology resembling human disease. However, ethical considerations and regulatory restrictions have drastically limited the availability of chimpanzees for research, effectively halting this avenue. Alternative small animal models, such as humanized mice, have been developed but often fail to fully recapitulate the natural course of human HCV infection, particularly with respect to the complete viral lifecycle and immune responses. Consequently, in vitro systems have become indispensable for studying HCV biology and screening antiviral compounds.

The development of in vitro HCV culture systems has been marked by two key milestones. First, the establishment of subgenomic replicons—engineered HCV RNA molecules lacking the structural genes (core, E1, E2) but retaining the non-structural genes necessary for RNA replication—enabled the study of HCV RNA replication in selected human hepatoma cell lines, most notably Huh-7 and its highly permissive derivative, Huh-7.5. These replicons, initially developed for genotype 1b and later for 1a, provided a powerful platform for identifying host factors and screening inhibitors of viral replication. Second, the discovery of the JFH-1 strain (genotype 2a), which could complete its entire infectious cycle—including RNA replication, particle assembly, and release of infectious virions (termed HCVcc)—in Huh-7.5 cells represented a transformative breakthrough. This system allowed for the production of infectious HCV particles in cell culture for the first time.

However, these existing systems suffer from significant limitations, particularly when applied to the clinically dominant genotype 1 strains. The HCV RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (NS5B) is inherently error-prone, leading to a high mutation rate during replication. When combined with the selective pressures imposed by the in vitro environment—such as adaptations in the host cell line or the need for the virus to replicate efficiently in a non-natural context—this results in the rapid accumulation of cell-adaptive mutations. While these mutations may enhance RNA replication in the specific cell line used, they frequently impair the production of infectious particles, reduce viral fitness, and critically, render the virus non-infectious in more physiologically relevant settings, such as in vivo or in primary human hepatocytes. Indeed, HCV genomes with a consensus sequence known to be highly infectious in chimpanzees often fail to replicate or produce infectious particles in standard Huh-7.5 cell cultures. Attempts to circumvent this issue through the creation of intergenotypic chimeras, where the structural genes of genotype 1 are grafted onto the JFH-1 backbone, have met with partial success but are themselves prone to acquiring compensatory mutations in the structural genes, again compromising the authenticity of the resulting virus.

Furthermore, the fundamental coupling of HCV RNA replication and particle production in all current in vitro systems is a root cause of these problems. Because the viral genome must be replicated by the error-prone NS5B polymerase to serve as a template for structural protein translation and as the packaged RNA, any selective pressure for efficient replication directly influences the genetic makeup of the produced virions. This intrinsic link makes it nearly impossible to produce large quantities of authentic, wild-type HCV particles of genotype 1 in a scalable and reproducible manner. There exists, therefore, a long-felt and unmet need in the art for a novel platform that can decouple the production of infectious HCV particles from the process of HCV RNA replication. Such a system would enable the generation of genetically stable, authentic HCV virions that faithfully represent circulating clinical isolates, thereby providing a superior tool for basic research, drug discovery, and vaccine development.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and robust method for producing infectious hepatitis C virus (HCV) particles in vitro that overcomes the critical limitations of existing systems by completely uncoupling the production of infectious virions from HCV RNA replication. This is achieved through a unique two-component system that leverages a non-hepatic mammalian cell line stably harboring a flavivirus subgenomic replicon to create a permissive intracellular environment for HCV particle assembly and release, while simultaneously utilizing a plasmid-based, cytoplasmic transcription system to generate the HCV genomic RNA template independently of the HCV replicase.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for producing infectious HCV particles. The method comprises providing a producer cell, which is a non-hepatic mammalian cell stably transfected with a flavivirus subgenomic replicon. The flavivirus subgenomic replicon is derived from a member of the *Flaviviridae* family, such as West Nile virus (WNV) or dengue virus, and encodes the non-structural proteins required for RNA replication and membrane rearrangement but lacks the structural genes. In a preferred embodiment, the producer cell is a baby hamster kidney (BHK-21) cell line stably harboring a WNV subgenomic replicon (referred to herein as a BHK-WNV cell). The method further comprises co-transfecting the producer cell with a first plasmid and a second plasmid. The first plasmid comprises a nucleic acid sequence encoding a full-length or partial HCV genome of interest, operably linked to a bacteriophage promoter (e.g., a T7 promoter) at its 5' end and a hepatitis delta virus (HDV) ribozyme sequence at its 3' end. The second plasmid is part of a dual-plasmid bacteriophage polymerase amplification system (e.g., a p2B system), which comprises a first component plasmid encoding a first bacteriophage RNA polymerase (e.g., T7 RNA polymerase) under the control of a second bacteriophage promoter (e.g., an SP6 promoter), and a second component plasmid encoding the second bacteriophage RNA polymerase (e.g., SP6 RNA polymerase) under the control of the first bacteriophage promoter (e.g., a T7 promoter). Following co-transfection, the dual-plasmid system undergoes a reciprocal amplification loop in the cytoplasm, leading to high-level expression of the bacteriophage RNA polymerases. The T7 RNA polymerase then drives the transcription of the HCV genomic RNA from the first plasmid in the cytoplasm, yielding an uncapped RNA with authentic 5' and 3' ends (the latter generated by self-cleavage of the HDV ribozyme). This HCV RNA is translated by the host cell machinery via the HCV internal ribosome entry site (IRES), producing the full complement of HCV proteins, including the structural proteins core, E1, and E2. Crucially, the flavivirus subgenomic replicon in the producer cell has pre-conditioned the cellular environment, inducing membrane rearrangements and modulating host factors that are conducive to the assembly and release of HCV particles. The resulting infectious HCV particles, which encapsidate the plasmid-derived HCV RNA, are then harvested from the cell culture supernatant.

In another aspect, the invention provides a composition comprising infectious HCV particles produced by the aforementioned method. These particles are authentic, as they are derived from a consensus HCV genome sequence (e.g., the H77 strain of genotype 1a) known to be infectious in vivo, and they are free from the cell-adaptive mutations that plague conventional HCVcc systems. The composition can be used for a variety of purposes, including but not limited to, infecting target cells for functional studies, serving as a substrate for neutralizing antibody assays, and acting as a challenge stock in vaccine efficacy testing.

In a further aspect, the invention provides a method for infecting a target cell with HCV. The method involves contacting the target cell with the infectious HCV particles of the invention. The target cell can be a hepatic cell line, such as Huh-7.5 or HepG2-CD81 (a HepG2 cell line engineered to express the human CD81 receptor), or, more physiologically relevant, an ex vivo human liver slice. The invention demonstrates that the particles produced by the BHK-WNV system can successfully infect these diverse target cells, leading to the establishment of a productive HCV infection characterized by the synthesis of viral proteins and replication of the viral RNA.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a method for studying HCV entry into a target cell. This is accomplished by producing HCV reporter particles (HCVrp) using the same BHK-WNV producer system. In this variation, the HCV genome in the first plasmid is modified such that the non-structural genes (NS3 to NS5B) are replaced with a reporter gene, such as the gene encoding SP6 RNA polymerase. Upon entry of the HCVrp into a target cell, the delivered SP6 RNA polymerase-encoding RNA is translated. The target cell is then co-transfected with a reporter plasmid that expresses a detectable signal (e.g., luciferase or EGFP) only in the presence of both T7 and SP6 RNA polymerases. The level of the detectable signal is directly proportional to the efficiency of HCV entry, providing a quantitative and highly specific readout that is independent of subsequent steps in the viral lifecycle, such as RNA replication. This system is a powerful tool for dissecting the molecular mechanisms of HCV entry and for screening entry inhibitors.

The key advantages of the present invention are manifold. First, by eliminating the need for HCV RNA replication during the production phase, the system prevents the accumulation of cell-adaptive mutations, ensuring the genetic integrity and authenticity of the produced virions. Second, it enables the production of infectious particles from HCV genotypes, particularly genotype 1, that are notoriously difficult to propagate in conventional systems. Third, the use of a non-hepatic producer cell (BHK-21) simplifies the production process and avoids the complexities of maintaining highly specialized and often finicky hepatic cell lines. Fourth, the system is highly versatile and can be adapted to produce particles from any HCV sequence of interest, including patient isolates and chimeric constructs. Finally, the ability to produce authentic HCV particles that can infect human liver slices provides a uniquely valuable ex vivo model that closely mirrors the natural infection in human liver tissue, offering unprecedented opportunities for translational research.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is based on the unexpected discovery that a non-hepatic mammalian cell line, specifically baby hamster kidney (BHK-21) cells, which have been stably engineered to harbor a flavivirus subgenomic replicon, can be repurposed as a highly efficient factory for the production of infectious hepatitis C virus (HCV) particles. This finding is counterintuitive because HCV’s natural target is the human hepatocyte, and conventional wisdom held that a hepatic cellular environment was essential for the proper folding, processing, and assembly of HCV structural proteins into infectious virions. The inventors discovered that the presence of a flavivirus subgenomic replicon, such as one from West Nile virus (WNV), fundamentally reprograms the BHK-21 cell, creating a permissive milieu that supports the entire HCV particle production pipeline, from the translation of a plasmid-derived HCV RNA to the release of mature, infectious virions.

The core of the invention is a two-part system: the producer cell and the cytoplasmic transcription/translation module. The producer cell, in its most preferred embodiment, is a BHK-21 cell line that has been stably transfected with a WNV subgenomic replicon. This replicon is a self-replicating RNA molecule that contains the 5' and 3' untranslated regions (UTRs) of the WNV genome, the gene for a selectable marker (e.g., blasticidin resistance or Renilla luciferase for easy monitoring), and the WNV non-structural protein genes (NS1 through NS5) required for RNA replication and the induction of characteristic membrane rearrangements. Critically, it lacks the WNV structural genes (core, prM, E). The persistent replication of this replicon in the BHK-21 cell induces a profound alteration of the cell's cytoplasmic architecture. It leads to the formation of vesicle packets and convoluted membranes, structures derived from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) that are known sites for flavivirus RNA replication and polyprotein processing. This remodeled cellular environment, rich in the necessary lipids, chaperones, and membrane scaffolds, is what unexpectedly proves to be highly conducive to HCV particle morphogenesis.

The second component of the system is designed to deliver the HCV genome and its encoded proteins into this pre-conditioned producer cell without engaging the HCV replication machinery. This is achieved through a sophisticated plasmid-based strategy that exploits the power of bacteriophage RNA polymerases. The system utilizes a dual-plasmid "p2B" system for cytoplasmic amplification of bacteriophage polymerases. A first plasmid in this system, for example, pSL-SP6p/T7pol, contains the gene for T7 RNA polymerase placed downstream of an SP6 promoter and an encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) internal ribosome entry site (IRES). A second plasmid, for example, pCR-T7p/SP6pol, contains the gene for SP6 RNA polymerase placed downstream of a T7 promoter and an EMCV IRES. When these two plasmids are co-transfected into a cell, a small amount of each polymerase is initially translated from the transfected DNA. The T7 polymerase then transcribes the SP6 polymerase gene from the second plasmid, and the SP6 polymerase transcribes the T7 polymerase gene from the first plasmid. This creates a powerful positive feedback loop, leading to an exponential amplification of both T7 and SP6 RNA polymerases in the cytoplasm.

Alongside the p2B system, a third plasmid is co-transfected into the BHK-WNV producer cell. This plasmid is the HCV expression construct. It contains the desired HCV genomic sequence, which can be a full-length consensus sequence (e.g., from the H77 strain of genotype 1a), a bicistronic construct (e.g., containing HCV structural genes and a subgenomic replicon), or a reporter construct (e.g., with a reporter gene replacing the non-structural genes). This HCV sequence is flanked at its 5' end by a T7 promoter and at its 3' end by a hepatitis delta virus (HDV) antigenomic ribozyme sequence. The massively amplified T7 RNA polymerase in the cytoplasm recognizes the T7 promoter on this plasmid and transcribes a full-length, uncapped HCV RNA molecule. The HDV ribozyme at the 3' end of this nascent transcript then catalyzes a self-cleavage event, precisely generating the authentic 3' end of the HCV genome. This resulting HCV RNA, with its correct 5' and 3' termini, is a perfect substrate for translation by the host cell's ribosomes. The HCV internal ribosome entry site (IRES) located in the 5' UTR drives the cap-independent translation of the HCV polyprotein, which is subsequently processed by host and viral proteases into the individual structural (core, E1, E2, p7) and non-structural proteins.

The key innovation is that the HCV RNA is never replicated by the HCV NS5B polymerase in this system; it is a direct, one-to-one product of the T7 polymerase. Therefore, no adaptive mutations can arise. The newly synthesized HCV structural proteins, in the context of the WNV-induced membrane rearrangements, assemble around the very same HCV RNA molecule from which they were translated. This process results in the formation of enveloped, 50–60 nm diameter particles that are released into the cell culture supernatant. These particles, termed HCVbp (HCV bicistronic particles), HCVwt (HCV wild-type particles), or HCVrp (HCV reporter particles) depending on the construct used, are fully infectious.

The harvested particles can be purified using standard techniques, such as ultracentrifugation through a sucrose density gradient. They exhibit a broad buoyant density range (1.05 to 1.20 g/cm³), which is a hallmark of HCV and is thought to reflect their association with host lipoproteins. The infectivity of these particles has been rigorously demonstrated. They can infect permissive hepatic cell lines like Huh-7.5, leading to the synthesis of HCV non-structural proteins (e.g., NS5A) and the replication of the delivered RNA genome. More importantly, they can infect engineered HepG2-CD81 cells and, in a landmark demonstration of their biological relevance, they can establish a productive infection in ex vivo cultured human liver slices, a model that preserves the complex architecture and cellular diversity of the human liver. This ability to infect human liver tissue confirms that the particles produced by this system are authentic and possess the full biological activity of a natural HCV isolate.

The system is also highly adaptable for mechanistic studies. By creating HCV reporter particles (HCVrp) where the viral non-structural genes are replaced by a reporter gene (e.g., SP6 RNA polymerase), the inventors have created a powerful tool to study HCV entry in isolation. The entry of these particles into a target cell delivers the SP6 RNA polymerase mRNA. The target cell is then transfected with a special reporter plasmid that only expresses a detectable signal (e.g., firefly luciferase) if both T7 and SP6 RNA polymerases are present. Since the target cell is not a producer cell, it lacks the T7/SP6 amplification system unless it is provided exogenously. Thus, the luciferase signal is a direct and exclusive readout of successful particle entry and delivery of the reporter RNA, making it an ideal assay for screening entry inhibitors or studying the role of specific host receptors like CD81, SR-B1, and ASGP-R, whose involvement has been confirmed using this very system.

In summary, the illustrative embodiments of the invention provide a complete, modular, and highly effective platform for the production of authentic, infectious HCV particles. This platform decouples particle production from RNA replication, solves the long-standing problem of genotype 1 propagation, and offers a suite of tools for basic virology, drug discovery, and vaccine development.

## EXAMPLES

### Materials and Methods

All cell culture reagents were obtained from standard commercial suppliers. Baby hamster kidney (BHK-21) cells were maintained in Eagle's Minimum Essential Medium (E-MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and GlutaMax-I. The BHK-WNV cell line, a stable clone of BHK-21 cells harboring a West Nile virus (lineage II) subgenomic replicon encoding Renilla luciferase, was propagated in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (D-MEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, GlutaMax-I, and 5 µg/ml blasticidin to maintain selection pressure. Human hepatoma cell lines Huh-7.5 and Huh-7.5-SG 1a rep (which stably harbors a subgenomic replicon of HCV genotype 1a) were cultured as previously described. HepG2 cells were grown in E-MEM with 10% FBS, GlutaMax-I, and a non-essential amino acid mix. All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The plasmid constructs used in this study were meticulously engineered. The p2B dual-plasmid system for cytoplasmic transcription consisted of pSL-SP6p/T7pol and pCR-T7p/SP6pol. The HCV expression plasmids included p684-SG(L+I)-HDV for generating HCV bicistronic particles (HCVbp), p90-T7p/H77FL-HDV for producing wild-type HCV particles (HCVwt) from the consensus H77 strain, and pCMV(-)T7p/HCV-SP6pol-HDV for generating HCV reporter particles (HCVrp). For the production of HCV particles, BHK-WNV cells were seeded in 162-cm² flasks and co-transfected one day later with the relevant HCV expression plasmid and the p2B system using Lipofectamine LTX according to the manufacturer's protocol. The culture medium was harvested three days post-transfection, clarified by low-speed centrifugation, and the virus particles were pelleted by ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g for 3 hours. The resulting pellet was either resuspended for direct use or subjected to sucrose density gradient centrifugation (20–60%) for purification. Infectivity assays were performed by incubating target cells (e.g., Huh-7.5, HepG2-CD81, or human liver slices) with the harvested particles. Infection was assessed by various methods, including RT-qPCR for HCV RNA, immunofluorescence/confocal microscopy for viral protein expression (e.g., NS5A), and a specific entry assay for HCVrp using a dual-bacteriophage polymerase reporter system. Electron microscopy was performed on fixed cells or purified particles to visualize the morphology of the produced virions. Human liver slices were obtained from surgical specimens in compliance with ethical guidelines and were infected ex vivo for up to 10 days before analysis by multifocal confocal microscopy.

### Example 1

The foundational observation of the present invention was the differential effect of flavivirus subgenomic replicons on the release of HCV structural proteins in different cell types. To investigate this, parental BHK-21 cells and BHK-WNV cells (stably harboring a WNV subgenomic replicon) were transiently transfected with a plasmid expressing the HCV genotype 1a structural genes (core, E1, E2, p7) under the control of a cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate early promoter. Cell lysates and culture supernatants were collected 48 hours post-transfection and analyzed by Western blotting for HCV core and E1 proteins. A striking result was observed: while the levels of HCV structural proteins in the cell lysates were comparable between the two cell lines, the amount of these proteins released into the supernatant was dramatically enhanced in the BHK-WNV cells compared to the parental BHK-21 cells. This suggested that the WNV replicon had created a cellular environment that was highly permissive for the assembly and/or release of HCV particles, even in the complete absence of any HCV RNA. In stark contrast, when the same experiment was performed in the human hepatoma cell line Huh-7.5, which is highly permissive for HCV RNA replication, the presence of an HCV subgenomic replicon actually inhibited the release of HCV structural proteins provided in trans. This paradoxical finding highlighted a fundamental difference between the two systems and indicated that the HCV replication machinery itself might be detrimental to the efficient production of particles from a separate RNA source. Furthermore, attempts to establish a stable HCV subgenomic replicon in BHK-21 cells were unsuccessful, reinforcing the notion that the BHK cellular background is not naturally conducive to HCV replication but can be made so for particle production through the introduction of a heterologous flavivirus replicon. This initial set of experiments provided the crucial impetus for developing the BHK-WNV cell as a dedicated producer line for infectious HCV particles.

### Example 2

Building upon the initial observation, a complete model system was devised to produce infectious HCV particles in BHK-WNV cells. The system was designed to generate HCV genomic RNA in the cytoplasm of the producer cell without involving the HCV RNA-dependent RNA polymerase. This was achieved by co-transfecting BHK-WNV cells with two components: (1) the p2B dual-plasmid system for cytoplasmic amplification of bacteriophage RNA polymerases, and (2) a plasmid encoding the HCV genomic sequence of interest, flanked by a T7 promoter at the 5' end and an HDV ribozyme at the 3' end. As a proof of concept, a plasmid encoding a bicistronic HCV RNA was used. This RNA contained the HCV 5' UTR through NS2, followed by the EMCV IRES and a subgenomic replicon of genotype 1a that encodes a selectable marker and the non-structural genes. Three days after transfection, the cells were processed for transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The TEM analysis revealed the presence of large, distinct vesicles in the BHK-WNV cells that were filled with 50–60 nm particles. These vesicles were located in the vicinity of dilated rough endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, suggesting a site of active particle assembly. Immuno-gold labeling with anti-HCV E1 and anti-core antibodies confirmed that these particles were indeed composed of HCV structural proteins. In control BHK-WNV cells transfected with a plasmid lacking the HCV structural genes, the characteristic membrane rearrangements induced by the WNV replicon (vesicle packets and convoluted membranes) were observed, but the large vesicles containing HCV-like particles were entirely absent. This visual evidence strongly supported the hypothesis that the BHK-WNV cell system could successfully assemble and release HCV particles from a plasmid-derived RNA template.

### Example 3

To biochemically characterize the particles produced by the BHK-WNV system, the culture supernatants from transfected cells were harvested and analyzed. Quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed on both the cell lysates and the supernatants to measure the levels of WNV RNA (from the stable replicon) and HCV RNA (from the transfected plasmid). As expected, the BHK-WNV cells contained high levels of WNV RNA regardless of the HCV plasmid transfection. HCV RNA was detected only in cells that had been transfected with the HCV expression plasmid, and its intracellular levels were comparable to those of WNV RNA. Most importantly, a large amount of HCV RNA was found in the culture supernatant, and it was enriched approximately 100-fold relative to the WNV RNA in the same supernatant. This selective enrichment of HCV RNA in the supernatant strongly suggested that it was being packaged into particles, rather than being released as naked RNA. To confirm this, the particulate material from the supernatant was subjected to sucrose density gradient centrifugation. The HCV RNA was found to migrate over a broad buoyant density range of 1.05 to 1.20 g/cm³, which is a well-known characteristic of infectious HCV particles and is attributed to their association with host lipoproteins. Western blot analysis of the gradient fractions confirmed that the HCV structural proteins (core, E1, E2) co-migrated with the HCV RNA across this density range. Control experiments ruled out the possibility that the released material was exosomes or cell debris, and it was confirmed that the released WNV RNA was not associated with infectious particles, as the WNV replicon lacks the structural genes necessary for particle formation. These results collectively demonstrated that the BHK-WNV system was capable of producing bona fide, enveloped HCV particles that selectively packaged the HCV-based RNA from which they were translated.

### Example 4

The ultimate test of the system was to determine whether the produced HCV bicistronic particles (HCVbp) were infectious. To this end, the harvested particles were used to infect the highly permissive Huh-7.5 cell line. The infectivity of individual fractions from the sucrose density gradient was assessed by measuring the ability of the particles to induce HCV RNA replication in the target cells. Using RT-qPCR for the HCV 5' UTR, it was found that HCV RNA levels in the target cells were negligible at day 3 post-infection but increased substantially by day 4, indicating a successful initiation of a productive infection. The peak of infectivity was found to be in the density range of 1.08–1.13 g/cm³, which corresponded to a relatively low peak of HCV RNA, demonstrating that infectivity does not simply correlate with RNA quantity but with the quality of the particle. The infectious titers of the HCVbp in the supernatants were measured to be between 0.6×10⁴ and 2.5×10⁵ TCID50 units/ml, which, while about one log lower than the highly efficient JFH-1 strain, were at least 10-fold higher than those typically achieved with cell culture-adapted genotype 1 strains. Further confirmation of infectivity came from laser-scanning confocal microscopy, which showed the appearance of NS5A-positive patches in the cytoplasm of Huh-7.5 cells infected with HCVbp, but not in uninfected controls. A more direct assay for RNA replication involved feeding the infected cells with a nucleotide analog, 5-bromo-UTP, and detecting its incorporation into newly synthesized RNA using an anti-bromo-uridine antibody. A clear co-localization of the BrU signal with NS5A was observed in HCVbp-infected cells, mirroring the pattern seen in positive control cells bearing an HCV subgenomic replicon, and confirming active HCV RNA replication in the target cells. The infection was also shown to be sensitive to standard antiviral agents (interferon and ribavirin) and could be neutralized by serum from an HCV-cured patient, providing strong evidence for the biological authenticity and specificity of the infection.

### Example 5

To gain deeper insights into the mechanism of HCV entry, a specialized reporter system was developed. HCV reporter particles (HCVrp) were produced by replacing the non-structural genes (NS3 to NS5B) in the HCV expression plasmid with the gene for SP6 RNA polymerase. These HCVrp, when produced in BHK-WNV cells, contain the SP6 RNA polymerase mRNA packaged within an HCV envelope. The entry of these particles into a target cell delivers this mRNA. The target cell is then co-transfected with a reporter plasmid, pT7-SP6p2/EGFPLuc, which expresses a fusion of EGFP and firefly luciferase only when both T7 and SP6 RNA polymerases are present to drive its transcription. Since the target cell (e.g., Huh-7.5) is not a producer cell, it lacks the T7/SP6 amplification system. Therefore, the firefly luciferase signal is a direct and exclusive measure of the successful entry of the HCVrp and the delivery of its RNA cargo. Using this sensitive assay, the dependence of HCV entry on specific host cell surface receptors was quantitatively assessed. Huh-7.5 cells were treated with siRNA pools targeting known HCV entry factors, including SR-B1, CD81, ASGP-R1, ASGP-R2, and claudin-1. A significant reduction in the luciferase signal was observed upon knockdown of SR-B1, CD81, and the ASGP-R subunits, with SR-B1 knockdown being the most effective. In contrast, the same siRNA treatments had little effect on the entry of WNV reporter particles (WNVrp), demonstrating the specificity of the assay for HCV. The critical role of these receptors was further confirmed by showing that pre-incubation of target cells with neutralizing antibodies against CD81 and SR-B1, or pre-incubation of the HCVrp with antibodies against the HCV E2 hypervariable region 1 (HVR-1), which interacts with SR-B1, also potently inhibited entry. This reporter system thus provides a powerful, quantitative, and highly specific tool for dissecting the molecular details of HCV entry and for screening potential entry inhibitors.

### Example 6

The versatility of the BHK-WNV production system was demonstrated by its ability to produce infectious particles from other HCV strains, including the well-characterized JFH-1 strain of genotype 2a and a Con1-JFH1 chimera (genotype 1b/2a). Plasmids encoding the full-length genomic RNA of JFH-1 or the Con1-JFH1 chimera, each under the control of a T7 promoter and followed by an HDV ribozyme, were transfected into BHK-WNV cells. The resulting particles were harvested and used to infect Huh-7.5 cells. RT-qPCR analysis of the target cells revealed a time-dependent increase in viral RNA starting at day 3 post-infection, which was completely abrogated by treatment with interferon and ribavirin. This experiment confirmed that the BHK-WNV system is not limited to genotype 1 constructs but can serve as a universal platform for producing infectious particles from a wide range of HCV sequences, including those that are already known to be highly infectious in conventional systems. This universality underscores the robustness of the underlying principle—the creation of a flavivirus-induced, permissive environment in a non-hepatic cell—that is broadly applicable across the HCV genotypic spectrum.

### Example 7

The most significant validation of the invention's utility was the production of authentic, wild-type HCV particles (HCVwt) from a consensus sequence known to be infectious in vivo. The H77 strain of genotype 1a, which has been shown to cause infection in chimpanzees, was used as the template. A plasmid encoding the full-length H77 consensus sequence was transfected into BHK-WNV cells using the p2B system. The resulting HCVwt particles were harvested and purified by sucrose density gradient centrifugation. Negative staining electron microscopy of the peak infectivity fractions (1.08–1.13 g/cm³) revealed spherical particles of 50–60 nm in diameter, which were not present in control samples. Immuno-gold electron microscopy using serum from an HCV-cured patient confirmed that these particles were recognized as HCV. The true biological relevance of these particles was then tested in an ex vivo human liver slice model. Human liver slices, which maintain the native architecture and cellular complexity of the liver, were incubated with the BHK-WNV-produced HCVwt. After 6–10 days, multifocal confocal microscopy analysis using antibodies against HCV structural proteins revealed specific staining within clusters of hepatocytes in multiple liver lobules. This pattern of infection was indistinguishable from that observed with Huh-7.5-produced JFH-1 HCVcc and was absent in uninfected control slices. To further confirm active viral replication, a version of the HCVwt encoding a tetracysteine tag in NS5A (HCVwt-4cys) was used. Live-cell staining of infected liver slices with a biarsenical dye showed specific fluorescence in periportal and mediolobular areas, directly demonstrating the local synthesis of HCV non-structural proteins in human liver tissue. This experiment conclusively proved that the BHK-WNV system can produce authentic, replication-competent HCV particles that are fully capable of infecting and replicating in a physiologically relevant human tissue model.

### Example 8

Finally, the utility of the BHK-WNV-produced HCVwt for infecting alternative hepatic cell models was explored. Given the limited availability and variability of human liver slices, a more accessible and standardized cell line, HepG2-CD81, was tested. HepG2 cells are of human hepatic origin but are normally poorly permissive for HCV entry due to the lack of CD81 expression. By stably transducing them with a lentivirus encoding human CD81, they become susceptible to HCV infection. These HepG2-CD81 cells were incubated with HCVwt particles of subtypes 1a, 1b, and 2a produced in BHK-WNV cells. RT-qPCR analysis of intracellular HCV RNA over time showed a characteristic pattern: an initial high reading at day 0 (likely due to bound/input virus), a sharp decline over the first 24–48 hours (possibly reflecting non-productive uptake or degradation), and then a subsequent increase, indicating the establishment of a productive infection. This late increase in RNA was completely blocked by the addition of interferon and ribavirin, confirming that it was due to active viral replication. Notably, infection with HCVwt of subtype 1a resulted in higher intracellular RNA accumulation than infection with the corresponding HCVbp, which carries cell-adaptive mutations. This suggests that the authentic, wild-type genome produced by the BHK-WNV system is better suited for replication in this more physiologically relevant cell line, as the adaptive mutations that enhance replication in Huh-7.5 cells may be detrimental in other hepatic contexts. This example highlights the BHK-WNV system as a superior source of authentic HCV for a wide range of in vitro and ex vivo infection models, providing a more accurate representation of natural HCV infection than conventional, mutation-prone HCVcc systems.
