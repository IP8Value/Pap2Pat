# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of pharmaceutical screening and, more particularly, to a deep learning-based high-throughput drug screening system for evaluating the efficacy of nanoformulated drugs. The invention provides a novel computational framework that leverages convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on single-cell image data obtained via imaging flow cytometry to rapidly, accurately, and reliably assess the biological effects of both conventional and nanocarrier-delivered therapeutic agents. The system is specifically engineered to overcome the limitations inherent in traditional in vitro cytotoxicity assays—such as MTT and lactate dehydrogenase release assays—which are prone to interference from the unique physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, including intrinsic absorbance, optical activity, fluorescence, and high surface energy. By utilizing raw image data as input and bypassing manual feature engineering or reliance on structured chemical databases, the invention enables an end-to-end, automated, and highly generalizable platform for early-stage drug efficacy prediction. The technical domain encompasses interdisciplinary integration of nanomedicine, cellular imaging, machine learning, and high-throughput screening technologies, with applications extending from preclinical drug discovery to potential clinical diagnostics and personalized medicine.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The development of novel therapeutics has increasingly turned toward nanotechnology as a means to enhance drug solubility, bioavailability, targeting specificity, and controlled release profiles. Nanoformulated drugs—comprising active pharmaceutical ingredients encapsulated within or conjugated to nanoscale carriers such as lipid nanoparticles, polymeric micelles, inorganic matrices (e.g., layered double hydroxides), or extracellular vesicles—have demonstrated significant promise in improving therapeutic outcomes across oncology, infectious diseases, and regenerative medicine. However, the evaluation of these advanced drug formulations presents substantial methodological challenges that existing screening paradigms are ill-equipped to address. Conventional in vitro assays, long considered the gold standard for cytotoxicity and efficacy assessment, rely heavily on biochemical readouts that are susceptible to confounding artifacts introduced by nanomaterials themselves. For instance, the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay measures mitochondrial reductase activity through the conversion of a yellow tetrazolium salt into a purple formazan product, quantified spectrophotometrically. While widely adopted due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness, this assay is critically compromised when applied to nanoformulated drugs: nanoparticles may adsorb MTT or formazan crystals onto their surfaces, catalyze non-enzymatic reduction, scatter light, or exhibit intrinsic absorbance at the detection wavelength, leading to false-positive or false-negative results. Similarly, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays, which infer membrane integrity based on cytoplasmic enzyme leakage, can be distorted by nanoparticle-induced membrane perturbations unrelated to true cytotoxicity or by direct interactions between nanomaterials and the LDH substrate.

Flow cytometry, another cornerstone of cellular analysis, offers multiparametric single-cell resolution but remains vulnerable to interference from nanoparticle autofluorescence, light scattering anomalies, and complex compensation requirements. Moreover, standard flow cytometry outputs—typically histograms or dot plots derived from aggregated fluorescence intensities—fail to capture subtle morphological or spatial changes in individual cells that may precede overt cell death. These limitations are particularly acute during early time points post-treatment (e.g., 2–6 hours), where metabolic or apoptotic signals are nascent and easily masked by background noise or assay-specific artifacts. Consequently, researchers are often forced to extend incubation periods to 24 hours or longer to achieve measurable signal-to-noise ratios, drastically slowing the drug screening pipeline and increasing resource consumption. This bottleneck is exacerbated by the growing diversity of nanocarrier platforms—each with distinct compositions, morphologies, surface chemistries, and degradation kinetics—which necessitate customized validation protocols to rule out carrier-specific interference, further fragmenting and complicating the evaluation process.

In parallel, machine learning has emerged as a transformative tool in biomedical research, enabling data-driven pattern recognition beyond human perceptual limits. Early efforts in virtual drug screening employed ligand-based or structure-based models that correlated molecular descriptors or docking scores with biological activity. However, these approaches are constrained by the availability of high-quality, labeled chemical libraries and the subjective selection of relevant features, limiting their applicability to novel chemical spaces or complex formulations lacking well-defined structural analogs. More recently, image-based phenotypic screening has gained traction, leveraging high-content microscopy to extract morphological fingerprints of drug-treated cells. Yet, even these methods often depend on hand-crafted features (e.g., nuclear size, granularity, texture metrics) that may overlook biologically relevant but visually imperceptible changes. Deep learning, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), offers a compelling alternative by automatically learning hierarchical representations directly from raw pixel data. CNNs mimic the hierarchical processing of the mammalian visual cortex, wherein early layers detect edges and textures, intermediate layers assemble contours and shapes, and deeper layers encode complex semantic concepts. This end-to-end learning capability eliminates the need for manual feature engineering and allows the model to discover latent biomarkers of drug response that may not correspond to any predefined morphological category.

Despite these advances, no existing system integrates deep learning with high-throughput single-cell imaging in a manner specifically optimized for the unique challenges of nanoformulated drug screening. Prior art either focuses on small-molecule compounds in homogeneous cell populations or relies on assays incompatible with nanomaterial interference. There remains a critical unmet need for a robust, rapid, and interference-resistant platform capable of evaluating diverse nanocarrier-drug combinations under short incubation conditions, thereby accelerating the translation of nanomedicine from bench to bedside. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a purpose-built deep learning architecture trained on imaging flow cytometry (IFC) data, which combines the statistical power of flow cytometry with the spatial resolution of microscopy to generate millions of single-cell images amenable to CNN analysis. By design, the system is agnostic to the chemical nature of the test agent, whether fluorescent, absorbing, particulate, or soluble, and instead learns to interpret cellular responses through visual patterns that correlate with functional outcomes established via orthogonal validation. This approach not only circumvents the pitfalls of traditional assays but also unlocks new dimensions of biological insight through interpretable AI techniques such as class activation mapping, revealing which subcellular regions the model deems most predictive of drug efficacy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a deep learning-based high-throughput drug screening system, herein referred to as DeepScreen, designed specifically for the rapid, accurate, and interference-resistant evaluation of nanoformulated drug efficacy. The system overcomes the limitations of conventional cytotoxicity assays by leveraging convolutional neural networks trained on single-cell image data acquired through imaging flow cytometry. In one aspect, the invention comprises a method for assessing the efficacy of a test compound—including but not limited to small molecules, biologics, and nanoformulated therapeutics—by treating a population of target cells with the compound for a short duration (e.g., 2 to 6 hours), acquiring bright-field and fluorescent channel images of individual cells via imaging flow cytometry, and inputting these multi-channel images into a pre-trained deep neural network that outputs a classification of drug efficacy into discrete categories such as ineffective, low, medium, or high. The neural network is trained using a dataset of labeled single-cell images, where labels are assigned based on reference efficacy measurements obtained from long-duration (e.g., 24-hour) MTT assays or other validated endpoints, thereby enabling the model to learn early visual correlates of eventual biological outcomes.

In another aspect, the invention features a convolutional neural network architecture optimized for single-cell image classification, comprising at least 40 layers arranged in a “network-in-network” topology that incorporates parallel convolutional branches with diverse filter sizes, global average pooling, and 1×1 convolutional output layers, while avoiding fully connected dense layers to reduce overfitting and parameter count. The model is trained using balanced mini-batch sampling, Adam optimization with specific hyperparameters (learning rate of 0.001, beta1 of 0.9, beta2 of 0.999, epsilon of 1×10⁻⁸), and regularization techniques including weight decay, dropout, and batch normalization to ensure generalizability across cell types and drug formulations. The system supports flexible operational modes: in a high-precision configuration, it utilizes both bright-field and Annexin V-APC fluorescence channels (and optionally additional markers such as anti-EGFR-FITC for specific cell lines) to achieve classification accuracies exceeding 96%; in a simplified mode, it operates on bright-field images alone, maintaining acceptable performance (approximately 70% accuracy) for resource-constrained settings.

A further aspect of the invention includes an interpretability module based on class activation mapping (CAM), which generates heatmaps overlaid on input cell images to visualize the spatial regions most influential in the model’s decision-making process. These maps consistently highlight plasma membrane boundaries and other subcellular structures undergoing early drug-induced alterations, providing biological validation of the model’s learned features and offering mechanistic insights into drug action. Critically, the system demonstrates robust anti-interference capabilities, maintaining high classification accuracy even when tested with inherently fluorescent drugs (e.g., curcumin) or diverse nanocarriers (e.g., inorganic layered double hydroxides and lipid-based solid lipid nanoparticles) that confound traditional assays. Experimental validation shows that DeepScreen achieves statistically significant discrimination of drug efficacy at 2- and 6-hour time points—where MTT and standard flow cytometry fail to detect differences—thereby reducing the total screening timeline from days to hours. The invention is applicable to a wide range of cell types, including but not limited to A549 (lung adenocarcinoma) and HEpG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma) cells, and is extensible to emerging nanocarrier platforms such as extracellular vesicles. By automating analysis and eliminating manual intervention, DeepScreen minimizes human error, enhances reproducibility, and establishes a scalable foundation for next-generation nanomedicine development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Example 1 A Deep Learning-Based Quick and Precise High-Throughput Drug Screening System

The present invention is exemplified through the development and validation of DeepScreen, a deep learning-based drug screening system tailored for nanoformulated therapeutics. The system integrates experimental biology, high-throughput imaging, and advanced machine learning to create an end-to-end pipeline for rapid efficacy assessment. The following detailed description outlines the preparation of nanoformulated drugs, acquisition and preprocessing of single-cell image data, architecture and training of the convolutional neural network, performance evaluation against conventional methods, and demonstration of anti-interference and interpretability capabilities.

Initially, two representative nanoformulated drug systems were prepared for testing: (1) etoposide (VP16) loaded into inorganic layered double hydroxide (LDH) nanoparticles, denoted LDH-VP16, and (2) curcumin (Cur) encapsulated within solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), denoted SLN-Cur. LDH nanoparticles were synthesized via co-precipitation, followed by anion exchange to intercalate VP16, resulting in a scroll-like morphology as confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). SLNs were formulated using a hot homogenization technique, yielding spherical particles that effectively entrapped Cur. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) verified successful drug loading: LDH-VP16 exhibited characteristic peaks of both LDH (O—H stretch at 3526 cm⁻¹, NO₃⁻ vibration at 1384 cm⁻¹) and VP16 (C=O stretch at 1610 cm⁻¹, aromatic C=C at 1485 cm⁻¹), with attenuation of the NO₃⁻ peak indicating anion exchange. Similarly, SLN-Cur displayed combined spectral features of SLN (C—H stretches at 2917 and 2849 cm⁻¹) and Cur (C=O at 1628 cm⁻¹, —CH₃ at 1430 cm⁻¹). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) confirmed particle size increases upon drug loading: LDH-VP16 measured 141.1 ± 1.7 nm versus 98.96 ± 0.9 nm for empty LDH, and SLN-Cur measured 150.6 ± 1.9 nm versus 103.2 ± 1.5 nm for empty SLN, consistent with successful encapsulation.

Human cancer cell lines A549 (non-small cell lung carcinoma) and HEpG2 (hepatocellular carcinoma) were cultured in RPMI-1640 or EMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. To establish ground-truth efficacy labels, cells were treated with free VP16, LDH-VP16, free Cur, or SLN-Cur for 24 hours, and viability was assessed via MTT assay. Drug concentrations corresponding to 80%, 50%, and 20% cell viability were identified and designated as low, medium, and high efficacy levels, respectively; untreated cells served as the ineffective control. Subsequently, independent experiments were conducted with shortened incubation times of 2 and 6 hours using these same concentrations. Post-treatment, cells were stained with Annexin V-APC to label phosphatidylserine externalization—a marker of early apoptosis—and, for A549 cells only, with anti-EGFR-FITC to highlight epidermal growth factor receptor expression. Single-cell images were acquired using an Amnis Flowsight imaging flow cytometer, generating a dataset of 103,568 A549 and 115,968 HEpG2 training images, each comprising bright-field and Annexin V-APC channels (plus EGFR-FITC for A549), along with 25,892 and 28,992 corresponding test images from separate biological replicates. Each image was labeled according to the 24-hour MTT-derived efficacy category (ineffective, low, medium, high).

Prior to model input, images underwent standardized preprocessing: single-channel images per cell were concatenated along the channel dimension, resized to 70×70 pixels using bicubic interpolation, and normalized to zero mean and unit variance. The core of DeepScreen is a 46-layer convolutional neural network inspired by the Google Inception architecture but modified to eliminate fully connected layers in favor of a fully convolutional design. The network employs a “network-in-network” motif, wherein multiple parallel convolutional pathways—with varying kernel sizes (e.g., 1×1, 3×3, 5×5) and pooling operations—are concatenated channel-wise at strategic junctions to capture multi-scale features efficiently. Regularization is enforced through batch normalization after each convolutional layer, dropout (rate=0.5) before the final classification layers, and L2 weight decay (coefficient=1×10⁻⁴). The output layer consists of a global average pooling operation followed by a 1×1 convolution that maps feature maps to class logits, enabling seamless integration with class activation mapping.

Training was performed on two NVIDIA GTX 1080Ti GPUs using TensorFlow. To mitigate class imbalance, mini-batches were constructed with equal representation from each efficacy class. The Adam optimizer was configured with a learning rate of 0.001, β₁=0.9, β₂=0.999, and ε=1×10⁻⁸. Training proceeded for 100 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss. The resulting model achieved test accuracies of 0.851 on mixed-cell data, 0.864 on HEpG2, and 0.908 on A549 when using both bright-field and Annexin V-APC channels. Notably, performance remained stable across cell types, confirming cross-line generalizability. When augmented with anti-EGFR-FITC for A549, accuracy rose to 0.966, demonstrating the benefit of cell-type-specific markers. Conversely, using bright-field alone reduced accuracy to 0.705, yet this simplified mode remains viable for high-throughput contexts where staining is impractical.

Comparative analysis against MTT and standard flow cytometry underscored DeepScreen’s advantages. At 2- and 6-hour time points, neither MTT nor flow cytometry detected significant differences between treatment groups known to differ at 24 hours, owing to insufficient signal development and assay interference. In contrast, DeepScreen’s high accuracy at these early time points enabled reliable efficacy ranking within hours rather than days. Furthermore, the system exhibited strong anti-interference properties: accuracy was unaffected by the intrinsic fluorescence of Cur or the distinct morphologies of LDH (scroll-like) versus SLN (spherical), which typically complicate traditional assays. Class activation maps revealed that the model focused predominantly on cell membrane boundaries for correct predictions, aligning with the biological role of Annexin V in detecting phosphatidylserine exposure. Misclassifications correlated with diffuse or off-target attention, validating the model’s biological plausibility.

The invention’s extensibility was demonstrated through theoretical consideration of emerging nanocarriers like extracellular vesicles (EVs), whose membrane-like structure could introduce staining artifacts analogous to fluorescent drugs. Given DeepScreen’s proven resilience to such interference, it is anticipated to perform robustly with EV-based formulations. Additionally, the system’s ability to discern intracellular structural changes suggests potential adaptation to other phenotypic endpoints, such as stem cell differentiation, by incorporating lineage-specific markers (e.g., Oct4, Sox2) into the imaging protocol. In summary, DeepScreen represents a paradigm shift in nanoformulated drug screening—offering speed, precision, automation, and resistance to physicochemical interference—thereby accelerating the development of next-generation nanotherapeutics.
