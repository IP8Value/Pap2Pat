## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of analytical instrumentation for the detection and quantification of biomolecules, and more specifically to a label-free method and apparatus for detecting and monitoring nucleic acid reactions through the measurement of solution viscosity using asynchronous magnetic bead rotation (AMBR). The invention provides a microviscometric platform capable of real-time, sensitive, and cost-effective analysis of DNA concentration and length changes in aqueous solutions without the need for fluorescent or radioactive labeling. The technology is particularly suited for applications in molecular diagnostics, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR) monitoring, restriction enzyme digestion assays, and other enzymatic or synthetic nucleic acid processes where changes in macromolecular architecture affect bulk fluid properties. The invention further encompasses the use of paramagnetic microbeads as transducers of local viscous forces under the influence of a controlled rotating magnetic field, enabling precise inference of solution viscosity from the rotational dynamics of the beads. This approach leverages fundamental principles of low-Reynolds-number hydrodynamics and nonlinear magnetic response to achieve high sensitivity in small-volume samples, making it compatible with point-of-care testing, resource-limited settings, and integration into microfluidic systems.

## BACKGROUND

The detection and quantification of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) are foundational to modern molecular biology, clinical diagnostics, forensic science, and biotechnology. Over the past several decades, fluorescence-based methods have dominated this domain due to their high sensitivity, specificity, and compatibility with high-throughput platforms. Techniques such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR), restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP), and ligation detection reaction (LDR) rely on the incorporation of fluorescent dyes or probes that emit light upon binding to or hybridizing with target DNA sequences. While effective, these methods require chemical modification of the DNA—either through intercalating dyes like SYBR Green or sequence-specific labeled probes such as TaqMan—and sophisticated optical detection systems, including excitation light sources, emission filters, and photodetectors. These requirements increase the cost, complexity, and infrastructure demands of the assays, limiting their deployment in low-resource environments or settings where simplicity and robustness are prioritized over multiplexing capabilities.

In parallel, alternative detection strategies have been explored to circumvent the need for labeling. Among these, physical property-based sensing has garnered interest, particularly approaches that exploit changes in the mechanical or rheological characteristics of DNA-containing solutions. Double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) is a semi-flexible polyelectrolyte whose presence in aqueous solution imparts measurable changes to bulk fluid properties, most notably viscosity. The viscosity of a dilute dsDNA solution is directly proportional to both the mass concentration and the average molecular weight (or contour length) of the DNA strands. This relationship arises because longer or more numerous DNA molecules increase the hydrodynamic resistance to flow, thereby elevating the solution’s dynamic viscosity. Critically, this effect is intrinsic to the DNA itself and does not require exogenous labels, making viscosity a potential proxy for DNA quantity or structural integrity.

Historically, viscosity measurements in biological contexts have been performed using macroscopic viscometers such as capillary (e.g., Ubbelohde) or rotational devices. However, these instruments typically require milliliter-scale sample volumes, are not amenable to real-time monitoring, and lack the sensitivity to detect subtle changes associated with low-concentration or short-fragment DNA reactions. Moreover, they are ill-suited for integration into miniaturized diagnostic platforms. To address these limitations, microscale and nanoscale viscometric techniques have emerged, including microrheology based on particle tracking, optical tweezers, and magnetic manipulation. Among these, magnetic bead-based methods offer distinct advantages: they are non-invasive, do not require optical transparency beyond basic imaging, and can be actuated remotely using electromagnetic fields.

One such technique involves the use of magnetic beads in a rotating magnetic field. When a superparamagnetic or paramagnetic microbead is suspended in a fluid and subjected to a uniform rotating magnetic field, it experiences a magnetic torque that tends to align its induced magnetic moment with the field direction. At low driving frequencies, the bead synchronously rotates with the field. However, as the field frequency increases beyond a critical threshold, the viscous drag exerted by the surrounding fluid prevents the bead from maintaining synchronous rotation. In this asynchronous regime, the bead rotates at a frequency lower than that of the driving field, and the difference between the two—the slip frequency—is governed by the balance between magnetic torque and viscous drag. Crucially, this slip frequency (or equivalently, the rotation period of the bead) is a direct function of the fluid’s viscosity.

Prior art has demonstrated the feasibility of using synchronous magnetic bead rotation for viscosity sensing, but such methods operate near the stability boundary and are highly sensitive to perturbations, bead polydispersity, and field inhomogeneities. Furthermore, the synchronous regime offers limited dynamic range and reduced signal-to-noise ratio. The asynchronous regime, by contrast, provides a more robust and linear response to viscosity changes, as predicted by theoretical models of nonlinear magnetic oscillators in low-Reynolds-number environments. Despite these theoretical advantages, practical implementation of asynchronous magnetic bead rotation (AMBR) for biological sensing has been hindered by challenges in bead uniformity, image analysis of rotational motion, and calibration across diverse sample matrices.

Moreover, no prior system has successfully applied AMBR to the specific problem of DNA detection and reaction monitoring in a manner that is both sensitive and practical for real-world diagnostic applications. Existing microviscometers either lack the resolution to detect the modest viscosity changes induced by typical PCR or restriction digestion reactions, or they require complex fabrication, expensive components, or large sample volumes. There remains a critical unmet need for a simple, label-free, low-cost, and miniaturizable platform that can quantify DNA through intrinsic physical properties, enabling deployment in settings ranging from centralized laboratories to field clinics.

The present invention addresses these gaps by introducing a novel AMBR-based microviscometer specifically optimized for nucleic acid analysis. It leverages commercially available paramagnetic microbeads, a planar magnetic field generator using orthogonal Helmholtz coils, and straightforward image analysis to extract rotation periods with high precision. The system operates in the asynchronous regime at elevated driving frequencies to ensure linearity and robustness, and it compensates for inherent bead-to-bead variability through statistical averaging. Demonstrated applications include real-time monitoring of PCR amplification and restriction enzyme digestion, with sensitivity sufficient to detect DNA fragments as short as 1000 base pairs under standard reaction conditions, and potentially down to 50 base pairs with optimized bead properties. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in label-free biomolecular detection, offering a viable alternative to fluorescence-based methods without sacrificing analytical performance.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method and apparatus for the label-free detection and quantification of nucleic acids, particularly double-stranded DNA, by measuring changes in the viscosity of a reaction solution using asynchronous magnetic bead rotation (AMBR). The core of the invention is a microviscometer that exploits the nonlinear rotational dynamics of paramagnetic microbeads suspended in a fluid under a controlled rotating magnetic field. When the driving frequency of the magnetic field exceeds a critical threshold, the beads enter an asynchronous rotation regime in which their rotation period is linearly proportional to the dynamic viscosity of the surrounding medium. This linear relationship enables precise inference of solution viscosity from measured bead rotation periods, which in turn correlates with DNA concentration and/or average molecular length.

The invention comprises a magnetic field generation system, typically implemented using a pair of orthogonal Helmholtz coils driven by programmable current sources, which produces a uniform, planar, and continuously rotating magnetic field in a defined observation region. A sample chamber, formed by two glass slides separated by a spacer (e.g., double-sided tape and nickel particles to maintain a gap of approximately 210 μm), holds a small volume (less than 10 μL) of the test solution containing dispersed paramagnetic microbeads. The sample is maintained at a controlled temperature, preferably 25 ± 1 °C, to ensure viscosity measurements are referenced to a known thermal state. An imaging system, such as a standard optical microscope coupled to a digital camera, records time-lapse image stacks of the rotating beads at a frame rate sufficient to resolve their periodic motion (e.g., 10 frames per second).

Image analysis is performed by selecting multiple individual beads (e.g., ten randomly chosen beads per sample) and extracting intensity time series from fixed regions of interest around each bead. The periodicity of rotation is then determined using spectral analysis, such as fast Fourier transform (FFT), to identify the dominant frequency and compute the corresponding rotation period. Due to inherent variability in commercial bead properties—including size, magnetic content, and susceptibility—the rotation periods of multiple beads are averaged to yield a statistically robust estimate of the solution viscosity. Calibration is achieved by measuring the rotation periods of beads in reference fluids of known viscosity (e.g., glycerol-water mixtures) and constructing a linear correlation curve that maps rotation period to viscosity.

The invention is particularly advantageous for monitoring enzymatic or synthetic nucleic acid reactions that alter the macromolecular architecture of DNA in solution. For example, in a restriction digestion reaction, a long DNA molecule is cleaved into shorter fragments, reducing the solution viscosity; conversely, in a polymerase chain reaction (PCR), repeated cycles of DNA synthesis increase the total mass and length of amplicons, thereby increasing viscosity. The AMBR microviscometer detects these changes in real time or at discrete intervals, providing a quantitative readout of reaction progress without the need for labeling, gel electrophoresis, or optical reporters. The method is sensitive enough to distinguish between digested and undigested DNA samples and to track PCR amplification over multiple cycles, with results correlating well with conventional gel-based analysis.

Key features of the invention include its label-free nature, minimal sample volume requirement, rapid measurement time (typically under one minute per sample), compatibility with standard laboratory reagents and consumables, and potential for cost reduction through replacement of optical imaging with simpler photodetection schemes (e.g., laser-photodiode systems). The platform is also extensible to other polymer systems beyond DNA, such as RNA, proteins, or synthetic polymers, wherever changes in molecular weight or concentration affect solution viscosity. By transforming a fundamental physical property—viscosity—into a measurable signal via magnetic bead dynamics, the invention offers a versatile, robust, and accessible tool for molecular diagnostics and biochemical analysis.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Asynchronous Magnetic Bead Rotation (AMBR)

The asynchronous magnetic bead rotation (AMBR) technique forms the operational foundation of the present invention and represents a significant departure from conventional synchronous magnetic bead actuation methods used in prior microviscometric systems. In AMBR, a paramagnetic microbead suspended in a Newtonian or weakly non-Newtonian fluid is subjected to a spatially uniform, time-varying magnetic field that rotates in a plane at a constant angular frequency ω. The bead, composed of a non-magnetic matrix (e.g., polystyrene or silica) embedded with superparamagnetic nanoparticles, develops an induced magnetic dipole moment in response to the applied field. The interaction between this induced moment and the rotating field generates a magnetic torque that drives the bead’s rotation. At low values of ω, the magnetic torque is sufficient to overcome the viscous drag imposed by the surrounding fluid, and the bead rotates synchronously with the field—that is, its angular velocity matches ω exactly. However, as ω is increased beyond a critical threshold ω_c, the viscous drag becomes dominant, and the bead can no longer maintain phase lock with the field. It enters an asynchronous rotation regime, wherein its steady-state angular velocity Ω is less than ω, and the difference Δω = ω − Ω constitutes the slip frequency.

The transition to asynchronous rotation is governed by the balance of torques acting on the bead. The magnetic torque τ_m can be expressed as τ_m = (χ'' V_m / μ_0) B² sin(2θ)/2, where χ'' is the imaginary part of the complex magnetic susceptibility (which accounts for relaxation effects and is frequency-dependent), V_m is the effective magnetic volume of the bead (i.e., the total volume occupied by magnetic nanoparticles), B is the magnitude of the magnetic flux density, μ_0 is the permeability of free space, and θ is the phase lag between the bead’s magnetic moment and the applied field. Under steady-state asynchronous rotation, θ stabilizes such that the time-averaged magnetic torque equals the viscous drag torque. The latter, for a spherical bead of radius r rotating in a fluid of dynamic viscosity η, is given by τ_v = 8π η r³ Ω, derived from Stokes’ law for rotational motion in the low-Reynolds-number limit (Re ≪ 1), which is rigorously satisfied for micron-scale beads in aqueous solutions at moderate rotation rates.

Equating τ_m and τ_v and solving for the rotation period T = 2π/Ω yields a relationship in which T is directly proportional to η, provided that B, ω, and bead properties (V_m, χ'') are held constant. This linear dependence—T ∝ η—is the central principle exploited by the AMBR microviscometer. Experimentally, this proportionality has been validated over a viscosity range of 0.89 to 2.8 centipoise (cP), corresponding to glycerol-water mixtures from pure water to approximately 40% glycerol by mass, with excellent agreement between AMBR-derived viscosities and those obtained from a calibrated Ubbelohde capillary viscometer. The linearity holds robustly across a range of driving frequencies well above ω_c, where the system operates deep within the asynchronous regime and avoids the unstable, jerky motion observed near the synchronization threshold.

The magnetic field is generated using a pair of orthogonal Helmholtz coil pairs, arranged such that one pair produces a field along the x-axis and the other along the y-axis. By driving these coils with sinusoidal currents phase-shifted by 90 degrees—i.e., I_x(t) = I_0 cos(ωt) and I_y(t) = I_0 sin(ωt)—a magnetic field vector of constant magnitude B = μ_0 N I_0 R² / (R² + (d/2)²)^{3/2} (where N is number of turns, R is coil radius, d is separation) rotates uniformly in the xy-plane at frequency ω. In the preferred embodiment, the field strength is maintained at approximately 2.7 mT, a value sufficient to induce strong alignment of the bead’s magnetic moment without causing aggregation or excessive heating. The frequency ω is selected to be in the range of 10–50 Hz, depending on bead size and expected viscosity, to ensure operation in the linear asynchronous regime.

The sample is confined between two optically flat glass slides separated by a precisely controlled gap, typically 210–420 μm, achieved by embedding nickel particles of known diameter within double-sided adhesive tape placed at the slide periphery. This configuration minimizes wall effects on bead rotation, as theoretical and experimental analyses confirm that hydrodynamic interactions with solid boundaries become negligible when the bead-to-wall distance exceeds several bead diameters—a condition satisfied in the described geometry. The small inter-slide volume (<10 μL) enables analysis of precious or limited samples, while the open architecture facilitates easy loading and compatibility with standard pipetting techniques. To prevent evaporation during measurement, the assembly is sealed with a biocompatible silicone sealant such as Dow Corning Sylgard.

Imaging is performed using a standard brightfield microscope equipped with a high-sensitivity CMOS or CCD camera capable of recording at least 10 frames per second. Each bead appears as a dark circular feature against a bright background, and its rotation manifests as a periodic modulation in pixel intensity within a fixed region of interest centered on the bead. This intensity time series is processed using open-source software such as ImageJ to extract numerical data, which is then imported into MATLAB or similar computational environment for spectral analysis. A fast Fourier transform (FFT) is applied to the intensity vector, and the peak frequency in the power spectrum corresponds to the bead’s rotation frequency; the inverse yields the rotation period T. To mitigate errors arising from bead polydispersity—commercial paramagnetic beads exhibit variations in diameter (±10%) and magnetic content (±20%)—the rotation periods of at least ten randomly selected beads are measured per sample and averaged. Statistical analysis shows that inter-bead variation contributes a relative standard deviation of approximately 10%, whereas intra-bead temporal variation is below 1%, confirming that ensemble averaging effectively suppresses noise due to manufacturing inconsistencies.

Calibration of the system is performed using reference fluids of known viscosity, such as aqueous glycerol solutions, whose viscosities at 25 °C are well-documented in the literature. A calibration curve of T versus η is constructed, typically exhibiting a correlation coefficient R² > 0.99, and this curve is used to convert subsequent experimental T values into absolute viscosity readings. The entire measurement cycle—from sample loading to viscosity output—requires less than one minute, enabling high-throughput screening or real-time kinetic monitoring when integrated with automated fluid handling.

### Analytes

The analytes detectable by the AMBR microviscometer of the present invention are macromolecular species whose presence, concentration, or structural state induces a measurable change in the dynamic viscosity of an aqueous solution. While the invention is particularly optimized for double-stranded DNA (dsDNA), it is broadly applicable to any polymer or biopolymer system that exhibits concentration- or molecular-weight-dependent viscosity behavior in the dilute or semi-dilute regime. The key requirement is that the analyte must significantly alter the hydrodynamic volume or entanglement density of the solution relative to the baseline solvent (typically a buffered aqueous medium).

Double-stranded DNA is an ideal analyte for this platform due to its well-characterized rheological properties. In dilute solutions (typically <1 mg/mL for kilobase-length fragments), the specific viscosity η_sp = (η − η_0)/η_0 is linearly proportional to DNA concentration C, according to the relation η_sp = [η]C, where [η] is the intrinsic viscosity—a molecular property that increases with the contour length (and hence molecular weight) of the DNA. For dsDNA, [η] scales approximately with the square root of the molecular weight, reflecting its semi-flexible chain conformation characterized by a persistence length of ~50 nm. Thus, for a fixed concentration, longer DNA fragments produce higher viscosity; conversely, for a fixed fragment length, higher concentrations yield greater viscosity. This dual dependence enables the AMBR system to infer either concentration (if length is known) or average length (if concentration is known) from a single viscosity measurement.

The invention has been validated using lambda phage DNA (48,502 bp) and its restriction digests. For instance, digestion with EcoRI cleaves lambda DNA at five specific sites, generating six fragments ranging from 3,530 to 21,226 bp. The resulting mixture has a lower weight-average molecular weight than the intact genome, leading to a measurable decrease in solution viscosity. Similarly, digestion with PvuI or other restriction enzymes produces distinct fragment patterns with predictable viscosity signatures. The AMBR microviscometer reliably distinguishes between undigested and fully digested samples, demonstrating its utility in enzymatic activity assays and genotyping applications based on restriction site polymorphisms.

In amplification reactions such as PCR, the invention monitors the progressive increase in dsDNA mass as target sequences are exponentially copied. Using a 4,500 bp amplicon derived from lambda DNA, the system detected viscosity increases corresponding to product accumulation over 41 thermal cycles. The rotation period exhibited a sigmoidal profile mirroring the classic PCR amplification curve, with earlier plateau phases observed for higher initial template concentrations. Comparison with agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed that AMBR detection lagged gel-based visualization by approximately five cycles, attributable to the threshold sensitivity required to register a statistically significant viscosity change above baseline noise. Nevertheless, the method provided quantitative cycle-threshold (Ct) equivalents without fluorescence, highlighting its potential as a low-cost qPCR alternative.

The sensitivity of the system is sufficient to detect PCR products as short as 1,000 bp at final concentrations exceeding 0.42 g/L (assuming >95% dNTP conversion), corresponding to a viscosity change of ~0.02 cP above buffer baseline. With improved bead uniformity—reducing inter-bead variation from 10% to 1%—theoretical modeling suggests detection limits could extend to fragments as short as 50 bp, encompassing many clinically relevant amplicons. Beyond DNA, the platform is equally suited for RNA analysis (though secondary structure may complicate interpretation), protein aggregation studies (e.g., amyloid formation), polysaccharide characterization, and synthetic polymer quality control.

Importantly, the method is label-free and non-destructive: no chemical modification of the analyte is required, and the sample remains intact post-measurement for downstream analysis. This contrasts sharply with fluorescence methods that often consume probes or alter sample integrity. Furthermore, the technique is insensitive to optical interference from colored or turbid samples, which plague absorbance- or fluorescence-based assays. The only sample preparation requirement is the addition of paramagnetic beads at a low concentration (0.0075% w/v), which do not interfere with enzymatic reactions when used with appropriate blocking agents such as bovine serum albumin (0.2%).

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In a foundational demonstration of the AMBR microviscometer’s capability, a series of aqueous glycerol solutions with glycerol mass fractions ranging from 0% to 40% were prepared and analyzed to establish the instrument’s calibration curve and validate its accuracy against a gold-standard viscometer. Glycerol was selected due to its well-documented, temperature-dependent viscosity and miscibility with water, enabling the creation of homogeneous Newtonian fluids spanning the viscosity range typical of biological samples (0.89–2.8 cP at 25 °C). Each solution (15 mL) was first measured using a calibrated Ubbelohde capillary viscometer immersed in a thermostatted water bath at 25.0 ± 0.1 °C. The efflux time between two etched marks on the viscometer arm was recorded in triplicate, and dynamic viscosity was calculated using the relation η = Kρt, where K is the viscometer constant, ρ is fluid density, and t is efflux time.

Simultaneously, 5 μL aliquots of each glycerol solution were mixed with a suspension of 45 μm paramagnetic polystyrene beads (Spherotech Inc.) to achieve a final bead concentration of 0.0075% w/v and 0.2% w/v bovine serum albumin (BSA) to prevent non-specific adhesion. The mixture was loaded between two glass slides separated by a 210 μm gap, sealed, and placed in the AMBR observation chamber maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. A rotating magnetic field of 2.7 mT amplitude and 30 Hz frequency was applied using orthogonal Helmholtz coils, and image stacks of bead rotation were captured at 10 fps for 30 seconds. Ten beads per sample were tracked, and their rotation periods were extracted via FFT analysis of intensity time series.

The resulting AMBR-derived viscosities showed excellent agreement with Ubbelohde measurements, with a mean absolute error of less than 3% across the entire range. A linear regression of rotation period T versus viscosity η yielded the equation T = (1.82 ± 0.05)η + (0.12 ± 0.03) (R² = 0.996), confirming the theoretically predicted proportionality. Control experiments with 7.6 μm and 16 μm beads produced similar linear trends but with slightly reduced correlation coefficients (R² = 0.97 and 0.98, respectively), indicating that larger beads provide superior signal-to-noise due to greater magnetic torque and slower rotation rates that are easier to resolve optically. Frequency-dependence tests revealed that the linear relationship held for driving frequencies from 20 to 50 Hz but degraded near 10 Hz, where beads approached the synchronization threshold and exhibited erratic motion. Consequently, all subsequent biological measurements employed a 30 Hz driving frequency to ensure robust operation in the asynchronous regime.

### Calibration of AMBR Viscometer

The calibration protocol for the AMBR microviscometer was refined to account for day-to-day instrumental drift and batch-to-batch bead variability. A standardized calibration set consisting of three reference fluids—deionized water (η = 0.89 cP), 20% glycerol (η = 1.45 cP), and 30% glycerol (η = 2.10 cP)—was measured at the beginning of each experimental session. For each reference, ten beads were analyzed as described in Example 1, and the average rotation period was computed. A new calibration curve was generated by linear regression of these three points, ensuring accurate viscosity conversion for unknown samples measured under identical conditions (temperature, field strength, frequency, bead lot).

To assess reproducibility, the same 20% glycerol sample was measured on five consecutive days using the same bead stock. The coefficient of variation (CV) for the derived viscosity was 2.1%, primarily attributable to minor fluctuations in ambient temperature and camera focus. When a new bead lot was introduced, the CV increased to 4.7%, underscoring the importance of lot-specific calibration. However, even without recalibration, the system maintained qualitative discrimination between high- and low-viscosity samples, as the relative change in rotation period remained consistent. For quantitative work, however, daily recalibration with reference fluids is recommended.

The calibration procedure was further validated using theoretical predictions based on the Einstein-Simha equation for glycerol-water mixtures, which relates viscosity to composition via η = η_water exp(bw), where w is glycerol weight fraction and b is an empirical constant (~2.5 for 25 °C). AMBR measurements deviated from theory by less than 5%, confirming the instrument’s fidelity to first-principles models. This dual validation—against both empirical (Ubbelohde) and theoretical benchmarks—establishes the AMBR microviscometer as a reliable tool for absolute viscosity measurement in the context of biomolecular analysis.

### Example 3

The utility of the AMBR microviscometer for monitoring enzymatic DNA digestion was evaluated using lambda DNA (48.5 kbp) as substrate and EcoRI restriction endonuclease as the catalyst. Reaction mixtures (20 μL total volume) contained 1 μg lambda DNA, 1× EcoRI buffer, 10 units EcoRI enzyme, and nuclease-free water. Control samples omitted the enzyme. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 60 minutes to ensure complete digestion, then equilibrated to 25 °C before AMBR analysis. Parallel samples were subjected to 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis in 1× TBE buffer at 10 V/cm for 2 hours to confirm digestion efficiency via fragment pattern visualization.

AMBR measurements revealed a statistically significant decrease in viscosity upon digestion: the average rotation period dropped from 1.98 ± 0.20 s (undigested) to 1.62 ± 0.16 s (digested), corresponding to a viscosity reduction from 1.02 cP to 0.82 cP—a 19.6% change. Gel electrophoresis confirmed complete cleavage into the expected six fragments, with no residual high-molecular-weight band. The magnitude of the viscosity change aligned with theoretical estimates based on the weight-average molecular weight reduction from 48.5 kbp to ~10 kbp, validating the system’s ability to report on DNA structural alterations. Notably, partial digestion intermediates (achieved by reducing incubation time to 15 minutes) produced intermediate rotation periods, enabling kinetic studies of enzyme activity.

### Preparation of Digestion Reaction Samples

Digestion reaction samples for AMBR analysis were prepared under stringent conditions to minimize confounding variables. All reagents were sourced from reputable suppliers (New England Biolabs for enzymes and buffers, Life Technologies for DNA) and stored according to manufacturer specifications. Reactions were assembled on ice to prevent premature enzyme activation, with final concentrations adjusted to match physiological ionic strength and pH (EcoRI buffer: 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl₂, 100 μg/mL BSA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5 at 25 °C). After incubation at 37 °C for predetermined durations (0, 15, 30, 60 minutes), reactions were immediately transferred to a 25 °C water bath for 10 minutes to halt enzymatic activity and equilibrate temperature. Beads and BSA were then added directly to the reaction mixture to avoid dilution artifacts, and 5 μL aliquots were loaded into the AMBR chamber within 5 minutes of temperature equilibration to prevent reannealing or degradation. This protocol ensured that viscosity measurements reflected the true state of the DNA at the time of reaction quenching, providing accurate snapshots of digestion progression.
