## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of virology, molecular biology, and therapeutic intervention for viral diseases, particularly coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2). More specifically, the invention provides novel methods and compositions for the prevention and treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection and its associated complications, including coagulopathy and thrombosis, by targeting a newly identified cellular receptor for SARS-CoV-2—liver/lymph node–specific ICAM-3 grabbing nonintegrin (L-SIGN, also known as CD209L, CD299, or CLEC4M). The invention further encompasses diagnostic tools and assays for detecting L-SIGN-mediated SARS-CoV-2 infection, as well as pharmaceutical compositions comprising agents that block the interaction between the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and L-SIGN. The technical field also includes the identification of specific high-mannose–type N-glycans on the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein that are essential for binding to L-SIGN, thereby enabling the development of glycan-targeted therapeutics and vaccines. Additionally, the invention addresses the pathophysiological role of L-SIGN-expressing liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) and lymph node lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) in the systemic manifestations of severe COVID-19, particularly in the dysregulation of coagulation factors such as von Willebrand factor (vWF) and coagulation factor VIII (FVIII). The invention thus bridges virology, glycobiology, vascular biology, and clinical medicine to provide a comprehensive approach to understanding and mitigating the multiorgan complications of SARS-CoV-2 infection beyond the canonical angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor pathway.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the etiological agent of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), has caused a global pandemic with significant morbidity and mortality. While the majority of infected individuals remain asymptomatic or experience mild symptoms, a substantial subset of patients develops severe disease characterized by acute respiratory distress syndrome, multiorgan dysfunction, and coagulopathy. The latter is a particularly concerning complication, with up to 71.4% of critically ill patients exhibiting thrombotic events and coagulopathy, which are strongly associated with poor prognosis and increased mortality. These coagulopathic manifestations are marked by elevated plasma levels of D-dimer, von Willebrand factor (vWF), and coagulation factor VIII (FVIII), suggesting a profound dysregulation of the hemostatic system. Although these abnormalities are often attributed to systemic inflammation and sepsis-like responses, the precise mechanisms by which SARS-CoV-2 triggers endothelial dysfunction and procoagulant activity remain incompletely understood.

The primary cellular receptor for SARS-CoV-2 entry is angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2), which is expressed on alveolar epithelial type II cells in the lung, as well as on epithelial cells in the kidney, intestine, and heart. Binding of the viral spike protein to ACE2 facilitates viral entry and subsequent replication, leading to direct cytopathic effects in these tissues. However, despite the widespread endothelial involvement observed in severe COVID-19—including evidence of endothelial activation and injury—multiple studies have failed to detect significant ACE2 expression in vascular endothelial cells. This discrepancy has raised questions about whether direct viral infection of endothelial cells contributes to the coagulopathy and thrombosis seen in patients, or whether these phenomena are solely secondary to systemic inflammation.

Concurrently, research into the structural features of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein has revealed that it is heavily glycosylated, forming a so-called “glycan shield” that helps the virus evade host immune surveillance. Mass spectrometry and structural analyses have identified both complex-type and high-mannose–type N-linked glycans on the spike protein, with specific glycosylation sites implicated in modulating spike conformation, receptor binding, and immune evasion. Notably, the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the spike protein contains an N-glycosylation site at Asn343, which has been shown to influence the open conformation of the RBD and its accessibility to ACE2. The presence of high-mannose glycans on the spike protein also raises the possibility that SARS-CoV-2 may exploit lectin receptors—proteins that recognize specific carbohydrate structures—for cellular entry, particularly in cell types that do not express ACE2.

In this context, liver/lymph node–specific ICAM-3 grabbing nonintegrin (L-SIGN) emerges as a compelling candidate. L-SIGN is a C-type lectin receptor that mediates calcium-dependent binding to high-mannose glycans and is known to function as an alternative entry receptor for several viruses, including Ebola virus, Marburg virus, hepatitis C virus, and human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1). Previous studies have demonstrated that the closely related SARS-CoV (which caused the 2003 SARS outbreak) can utilize L-SIGN for cellular entry, independent of ACE2. Given the high sequence homology between SARS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 spike proteins, it is plausible that SARS-CoV-2 might also interact with L-SIGN. However, prior to the present invention, it was unknown whether L-SIGN serves as a functional receptor for SARS-CoV-2, where it is expressed in human tissues relevant to COVID-19 pathogenesis, and whether such interaction contributes to the disease’s systemic manifestations.

Moreover, the tissue distribution of L-SIGN has been a subject of debate. Earlier reports suggested expression on various blood endothelial cells, including those in the lung, but more recent single-cell RNA sequencing studies have questioned this, showing minimal or no ACE2 expression in vascular endothelium and inconsistent detection of L-SIGN. This uncertainty has hindered efforts to determine whether direct viral infection of endothelial cells plays a role in COVID-19 coagulopathy. Additionally, while animal models of SARS-CoV-2 infection have been developed, they often fail to recapitulate the coagulopathic features of human disease, possibly due to species-specific differences in L-SIGN expression—since L-SIGN is encoded only in humans and great apes, not in commonly used laboratory animals.

Thus, there exists a critical need to identify alternative receptors for SARS-CoV-2 that could explain the virus’s tropism for non-epithelial cells, particularly endothelial cells involved in coagulation regulation. There is also a need for therapeutic strategies that target these alternative entry pathways to prevent or mitigate the systemic complications of severe COVID-19. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by definitively establishing L-SIGN as a functional receptor for SARS-CoV-2, mapping its expression to specific endothelial cell populations in human liver and lymph nodes, demonstrating direct viral infection of these cells, and linking this interaction to the upregulation of procoagulant factors. Furthermore, the invention provides the first evidence that blocking the L-SIGN–spike interaction can inhibit SARS-CoV-2 infection, thereby offering a novel therapeutic avenue for the treatment of severe COVID-19.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious discovery that liver/lymph node–specific ICAM-3 grabbing nonintegrin (L-SIGN, also known as CD209L, CD299, or CLEC4M) functions as a cellular receptor for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), mediating viral entry into specific endothelial cell populations in a manner distinct from the canonical angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) pathway. The invention further establishes that L-SIGN binds to high-mannose–type N-glycans on the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein in a calcium-dependent fashion, with particular dependence on the glycan moiety at Asn343 within the receptor-binding domain (RBD). Critically, the invention identifies that L-SIGN is highly expressed on liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) and lymph node lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs) in human tissues, but is absent from most other vascular endothelial cells, including those in the lung, heart, kidney, and peripheral blood vessels. This restricted expression pattern explains why SARS-CoV-2 can directly infect specific endothelial compartments without requiring ACE2, which is undetectable in these cells.

The invention demonstrates that authentic SARS-CoV-2 virus infects L-SIGN–expressing LSECs ex vivo, and that this infection correlates with increased expression of procoagulant proteins, including von Willebrand factor (vWF) and coagulation factor VIII (FVIII)—key mediators of the coagulopathy observed in severe COVID-19. Moreover, LSECs isolated from liver autopsy samples of patients who died from COVID-19 exhibit elevated L-SIGN expression, intracellular SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein, loss of the quiescence marker Lyve-1, and heightened vWF production, collectively indicating L-SIGN–mediated endothelial activation and dysfunction. Importantly, the invention shows that L-SIGN–mediated infection is non-productive, meaning it does not result in significant viral replication or progeny release, but instead appears to trigger cellular activation pathways that contribute to systemic inflammation and coagulopathy.

The invention further provides methods for inhibiting SARS-CoV-2 infection by blocking the interaction between the viral spike protein and L-SIGN. Specifically, the invention discloses that anti–L-SIGN monoclonal antibodies, mannan (a competitive inhibitor of mannose-binding lectins), and recombinant L-SIGN/Fc fusion proteins can dose-dependently inhibit both pseudotyped and authentic SARS-CoV-2 infection in L-SIGN–expressing cells. These findings establish L-SIGN as a therapeutically targetable receptor and offer novel strategies for preventing or treating the endothelial complications of severe COVID-19.

Additionally, the invention encompasses diagnostic and prognostic applications based on the detection of L-SIGN expression levels in liver or lymph node biopsies, or the presence of SARS-CoV-2 in LSECs, as biomarkers for severe disease progression and coagulopathic risk. The invention also includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising L-SIGN antagonists, such as neutralizing antibodies, glycan mimetics, or soluble L-SIGN decoys, formulated for systemic administration to patients at risk of or suffering from severe COVID-19. In summary, the invention redefines the understanding of SARS-CoV-2 tropism by identifying L-SIGN as a key endothelial receptor, elucidates a direct mechanistic link between viral infection and coagulopathy, and provides actionable therapeutic and diagnostic tools to combat this aspect of the pandemic.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is based on the unexpected and groundbreaking discovery that liver/lymph node–specific ICAM-3 grabbing nonintegrin (L-SIGN) serves as a functional cellular receptor for severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), enabling viral entry into specific endothelial cell populations that do not express the canonical receptor angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2). This finding resolves a long-standing paradox in COVID-19 pathogenesis: how SARS-CoV-2 induces endothelial dysfunction and coagulopathy despite the apparent absence of ACE2 on vascular endothelial cells. The invention provides a comprehensive molecular, cellular, and pathological framework demonstrating that L-SIGN, through its recognition of high-mannose–type N-glycans on the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, mediates direct infection of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) and lymph node lymphatic endothelial cells (LECs), leading to their activation and the release of procoagulant factors such as von Willebrand factor (vWF) and coagulation factor VIII (FVIII).

In one embodiment, the invention provides a method for detecting or confirming SARS-CoV-2 infection in LSECs or LECs by assaying for the presence of viral components, such as nucleocapsid protein or viral RNA, in conjunction with the expression of L-SIGN. This method is particularly useful in postmortem or biopsy analyses of liver or lymph node tissues from patients with severe or fatal COVID-19, where conventional respiratory sampling may be insufficient to explain systemic complications. Immunohistochemical or immunofluorescent staining using validated anti–L-SIGN antibodies (e.g., Origene TA810067 or TA810055) in combination with anti–SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid antibodies (e.g., Invitrogen MA1-7404) enables the co-localization of viral antigen within L-SIGN–positive endothelial cells, providing definitive evidence of direct infection.

In another embodiment, the invention discloses that the interaction between SARS-CoV-2 and L-SIGN is mediated by high-mannose–type N-glycans on the viral spike protein, with a critical contribution from the glycosylation site at Asn343 in the receptor-binding domain (RBD). This was demonstrated through a series of mutagenesis and binding assays using recombinant spike/Fc fusion proteins and pseudotyped lentiviral particles. Specifically, mutation of Asn343 to glutamine (SpikeN343Q/Fc) abolished binding to L-SIGN–expressing cells without affecting ACE2 binding, indicating that the high-mannose glycan at this site is selectively required for L-SIGN recognition. Additional mutagenesis of other high-mannose–bearing sites (Asn61, Asn122, Asn234) revealed that Asn61 and Asn122 also contribute to L-SIGN–mediated infection, whereas Asn234 does not, highlighting the specificity of the glycan–lectin interaction. The dependence on high-mannose structures was further confirmed by sensitivity to endoglycosidase H (Endo-H) digestion and enhancement of binding following treatment with kifunensine, a mannosidase I inhibitor that increases surface high-mannose glycan expression.

The calcium dependence of this interaction was established through inhibition experiments using the calcium chelator EGTA, which abolished spike/Fc binding to L-SIGN–expressing cells and blocked pseudotyped virus infection. Moreover, substitution of the conserved Glu-Pro-Asn (EPN) motif in the carbohydrate recognition domain of L-SIGN with alanines (L-SIGNAAA) or with a galactose-binding QPD motif (L-SIGNQPD) completely abrogated spike binding and viral entry, confirming that L-SIGN functions as a canonical mannose-binding C-type lectin in the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

A critical aspect of the invention is the precise anatomical localization of L-SIGN expression in human tissues. Through rigorous immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent analysis of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded normal and pathological human samples—including liver, lung, heart, kidney, intestine, and lymph nodes—the invention demonstrates that L-SIGN is robustly expressed on LSECs and lymph node LECs, but is absent from blood endothelial cells in all examined vascular beds, including hepatic central veins, portal veins, hepatic arteries, high endothelial venules, and arterioles. This finding contradicts earlier reports suggesting broader endothelial expression and clarifies that L-SIGN–mediated infection is restricted to specialized endothelial compartments. Notably, ACE2 was undetectable in LSECs and lymph node LECs using multiple validated antibodies, confirming that L-SIGN operates as an independent entry pathway in these cells.

The invention further validates L-SIGN as a receptor for authentic SARS-CoV-2 virus, not just pseudotyped particles. Primary human LSECs transduced to express L-SIGN were susceptible to infection with live SARS-CoV-2 (USA-WA1/2020 strain), as evidenced by intracellular nucleocapsid protein detection 48 hours post-infection. However, viral titers in the supernatant, as measured by 50% tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) assay, indicated non-productive infection—i.e., no significant viral amplification occurred. This suggests that L-SIGN–mediated entry leads to viral degradation or limited replication, consistent with the antigen-presenting and immunomodulatory functions of LSECs. Nevertheless, even non-productive infection was sufficient to induce a procoagulant phenotype, as LSECs from COVID-19 autopsy livers showed significantly increased vWF expression and a trend toward higher FVIII levels, along with loss of Lyve-1, a marker of endothelial quiescence.

Therapeutically, the invention provides multiple strategies to block L-SIGN–mediated SARS-CoV-2 infection. Anti–L-SIGN monoclonal antibodies (e.g., R&D Systems MAB162) inhibited pseudotyped virus infection in a dose-dependent manner. Similarly, mannan, a polysaccharide composed of β-mannose units, competitively blocked spike binding and infection. Recombinant L-SIGN/Fc fusion proteins, acting as soluble decoy receptors, also potently inhibited infection. Crucially, these inhibitors did not affect vesicular stomatitis virus G (VSV-G) pseudotyped control virus infection, confirming specificity. Moreover, mannan treatment of L-SIGN–transduced LSECs reduced authentic SARS-CoV-2 infection, demonstrating translational potential.

The invention also contemplates pharmaceutical compositions comprising any of these L-SIGN antagonists, formulated for intravenous or subcutaneous administration, for the prevention or treatment of severe COVID-19, particularly in patients exhibiting signs of coagulopathy or elevated D-dimer levels. Diagnostic kits may include anti–L-SIGN antibodies and SARS-CoV-2 detection reagents for use in histopathology or flow cytometry to assess endothelial infection status. Additionally, the invention supports the development of glycan-based vaccines or therapeutics that mask or modify the high-mannose epitopes on the spike protein to prevent L-SIGN engagement.

In conclusion, the detailed description of the invention encompasses the molecular mechanism of L-SIGN–spike interaction, the cellular and tissue specificity of L-SIGN expression, the pathological consequences of L-SIGN–mediated infection in endothelial cells, and the therapeutic and diagnostic applications arising from this knowledge. This integrated approach provides a robust foundation for combating the systemic manifestations of SARS-CoV-2 infection beyond the respiratory tract.
