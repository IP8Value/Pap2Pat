# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Glaucoma represents a leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting millions of individuals across diverse populations and age groups. The disease is characterized by progressive optic nerve damage that is typically associated with elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), although other contributing factors such as vascular insufficiency and genetic predisposition also play significant roles in its pathogenesis. Despite advances in medical and surgical management, the primary therapeutic objective in glaucoma remains the reduction and stabilization of IOP to prevent or slow the progression of visual field loss. Current treatment modalities include pharmacologic agents administered as topical eye drops, laser-based interventions, and various forms of surgical procedures, each tailored to the severity and subtype of glaucoma presented by the patient.

Pharmacologic therapy, often initiated as first-line treatment, involves the use of medications that either reduce aqueous humor production or enhance its outflow through the trabecular meshwork or uveoscleral pathways. However, long-term adherence to topical regimens is frequently compromised due to side effects, cost, and the necessity for multiple daily applications. When medical therapy fails to achieve adequate IOP control or when disease progression continues despite optimal medical management, surgical intervention becomes necessary. Among the available surgical options, trabeculectomy has historically served as the gold standard for adult primary open-angle glaucoma, involving the creation of a fistula between the anterior chamber and the subconjunctival space to facilitate aqueous drainage. While effective in many cases, trabeculectomy is associated with a significant risk of complications, including hypotony, choroidal effusion, endophthalmitis, and notably, failure due to postoperative scarring and fibrosis at the filtration site.

In response to these limitations, glaucoma drainage implants (GDIs) were developed to provide a more controlled and predictable means of aqueous outflow, particularly in patients with secondary glaucomas, pediatric glaucomas, neovascular glaucoma, or those who have experienced prior trabeculectomy failure. GDIs typically consist of a silicone or polypropylene tube connected to a plate or reservoir that is implanted in the episcleral space, allowing aqueous humor to drain from the anterior chamber into a subconjunctival bleb. Although GDIs have demonstrated improved success rates in high-risk populations compared to trabeculectomy, their long-term efficacy remains constrained by the body’s natural healing response, which leads to the formation of a fibrotic capsule around the implant plate. This encapsulation significantly impedes fluid drainage over time, resulting in elevated IOP and potential surgical failure.

More recently, minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries (MIGS) have emerged as a promising alternative for patients with mild to moderate glaucoma, offering a favorable safety profile and faster recovery times. MIGS procedures generally involve the implantation of micro-scale devices that enhance aqueous outflow through existing physiological pathways, such as the trabecular meshwork or suprachoroidal space. While these approaches have shown efficacy in reducing medication burden and modestly lowering IOP, they are not designed for advanced disease and may still be subject to fibrotic occlusion, particularly in anatomically complex or inflamed eyes.

The common denominator among all current surgical strategies—trabeculectomy, GDIs, and MIGS—is the host’s fibrotic response to the presence of foreign material or surgically created tissue disruption. This fibrotic encapsulation, driven by the activation of fibroblasts, deposition of extracellular matrix proteins, and chronic low-grade inflammation, ultimately compromises the patency of the newly created drainage pathway. Numerous attempts have been made to mitigate this response, including the intraoperative and postoperative use of antifibrotic agents such as mitomycin-C and 5-fluorouracil. While these agents can improve short-term outcomes, they are associated with serious complications, including thin-walled blebs, bleb leaks, and endophthalmitis, thereby limiting their utility in routine clinical practice. Corticosteroids, though safer, offer only modest antifibrotic effects and are insufficient to ensure long-term patency in many cases.

Material modifications to existing implants have also been explored, with flexible silicone plates demonstrating reduced fibrosis compared to rigid polypropylene counterparts, likely due to better mechanical compatibility with surrounding ocular tissues. Additionally, novel device designs incorporating drug-eluting coatings, amniotic membrane wraps, or ferrofluid-based valves have been tested with varying degrees of success. However, even these advanced iterations continue to provoke significant fibrotic reactions, underscoring the need for a fundamentally different approach to implant design—one that addresses the root causes of the foreign body response rather than merely attempting to suppress its downstream consequences.

Recent insights from the field of neural engineering have revealed that the mechanical and geometric properties of an implant play a critical role in determining the magnitude of the host’s inflammatory and fibrotic responses. Specifically, it has been demonstrated that ultra-flexible, porous, and cellular-scale architectures can dramatically reduce chronic tissue reactions by minimizing mechanical mismatch with native tissue and preserving local cellular and vascular networks. These principles, originally applied to brain-computer interfaces and cortical probes, suggest that similar design strategies could be leveraged to create next-generation ocular implants that evade fibrotic encapsulation. In the context of glaucoma surgery, such an implant would need to not only integrate seamlessly with the dynamic, curved surface of the sclera but also maintain sufficient structural integrity and fluidic conductance to support long-term aqueous drainage.

Accordingly, there exists a compelling and unmet need in the art for a novel glaucoma drainage device that overcomes the limitations of current implants by fundamentally re-engineering the interface between the device and the host tissue. Such a device must be capable of sustaining aqueous outflow while simultaneously minimizing the biological triggers of fibrosis—namely, mechanical stress, disruption of native tissue architecture, and chronic immune activation. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a microfluidic meshwork-based drainage platform that embodies the principles of biomimetic design, ultra-flexibility, and minimal invasiveness, thereby offering a transformative solution to the persistent challenge of fibrotic failure in glaucoma surgery.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel glaucoma drainage device comprising a microfluidic meshwork configured to replace the conventional solid plate of existing glaucoma drainage implants. This microfluidic meshwork is engineered to minimize fibrotic encapsulation and promote long-term patency of aqueous humor outflow by virtue of its unique structural and mechanical properties. Specifically, the meshwork consists of an interconnected network of microfluidic channels having dimensions on the order of individual cells, fabricated from biocompatible materials to form an ultra-thin, highly flexible sheet that conforms to the curvature and movement of the ocular surface upon implantation. The invention is predicated on the discovery that such a design significantly reduces the foreign body response typically elicited by rigid or solid implants, thereby preserving the integrity of surrounding tissue architecture and preventing the formation of obstructive fibrotic capsules.

In one embodiment, the microfluidic meshwork has an overall area of approximately 7 mm × 7 mm, a thickness of about 4 micrometers, and a periodic grid structure with a pitch of 100 micrometers. The individual microfluidic channels within the meshwork possess an outer diameter of approximately 20 micrometers and an inner lumen diameter of about 8 micrometers, dimensions selected based on finite element simulations to ensure adequate aqueous humor drainage at clinically relevant pressure gradients (e.g., 2 μL/min at 10 mmHg). The meshwork is fabricated using photolithographic techniques on a silicon wafer substrate, followed by release and sterilization, resulting in a freestanding, porous membrane that is both mechanically robust and biologically inert.

The invention further encompasses methods of manufacturing the microfluidic meshwork, including the use of negative photoresists such as SU-8 to define channel walls, sacrificial layers (e.g., LOR 5A and AZ1505) to form luminal spaces, and a nickel-releasing layer to facilitate detachment from the fabrication substrate. The resulting device is compatible with standard ophthalmic surgical procedures and can be integrated with a conventional drainage tube to form a complete glaucoma drainage implant, or used independently as a standalone drainage platform in certain clinical scenarios.

Additionally, the invention provides methods of use, wherein the microfluidic meshwork is implanted in the episcleral space of the eye, typically in the supratemporal quadrant, without the need for suturing due to its inherent flexibility and conformability. Upon implantation, the meshwork integrates with the surrounding tissue without disrupting cellular or vascular networks, thereby avoiding the mechanical irritation and chronic inflammation that lead to fibrosis. Preclinical studies in a rabbit model have demonstrated that the microfluidic meshwork elicits minimal fibrotic capsule formation—averaging only 1.3 ± 0.6 μm in thickness after three months—compared to conventional Ahmed glaucoma valve plates, which induced capsules averaging 89 ± 6.6 μm in thickness under identical conditions. These results represent a greater than 98% reduction in fibrosis and underscore the superior biocompatibility of the inventive meshwork.

Importantly, the invention also recognizes that optimal performance requires the meshwork to remain in a single, unfolded layer during implantation. Histological analysis revealed that regions where the meshwork was inadvertently folded or stacked during surgery exhibited localized inflammatory cell accumulation, albeit in less than 10% of the total implant area. This observation highlights the importance of proper surgical technique and suggests that future iterations may incorporate structural reinforcements or deployment aids to ensure monolayer placement.

In summary, the present invention offers a paradigm-shifting approach to glaucoma drainage by replacing the traditional solid implant plate with a biomimetic, ultra-flexible microfluidic meshwork that actively resists fibrotic encapsulation. By addressing the fundamental biomechanical and biological drivers of implant failure, this technology has the potential to significantly improve the long-term success rates of glaucoma surgery across a broad spectrum of patient populations, including those with refractory, pediatric, or secondary forms of the disease.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed to them below, unless otherwise indicated by context.

“Aqueous humor” refers to the clear, watery fluid produced by the ciliary body that fills the anterior and posterior chambers of the eye, providing nutrients to the avascular lens and cornea and maintaining intraocular pressure.

“Fibrotic encapsulation” or “fibrotic capsule” denotes the pathological process whereby fibroblasts deposit dense collagenous tissue around a foreign body, such as an implanted medical device, leading to the formation of a thick, avascular sheath that can impede device function.

“Microfluidic meshwork” means a porous, sheet-like structure composed of an interconnected network of microscale fluid-conducting channels, wherein the channels have dimensions comparable to those of mammalian cells (typically 5–50 μm in diameter) and are arranged in a periodic or quasi-periodic lattice.

“Ultra-flexible” describes a material or structure that exhibits a bending stiffness sufficiently low to allow it to conform to the natural curvature and dynamic movements of biological tissues without inducing mechanical stress or deformation in the surrounding environment. In the context of the present invention, ultra-flexibility is achieved through a combination of nanoscale thickness (e.g., ≤10 μm) and low elastic modulus materials.

“Glaucoma drainage implant (GDI)” refers to any surgically implanted device designed to lower intraocular pressure by facilitating the outflow of aqueous humor from the anterior chamber to an external reservoir, typically located in the subconjunctival or episcleral space.

“Episcleral space” is the anatomical region between the sclera (the white outer coat of the eye) and the overlying conjunctiva, commonly used as the implantation site for glaucoma drainage devices.

“Biocompatibility” signifies the ability of a material to perform with an appropriate host response in a specific application, meaning that it does not elicit significant inflammation, toxicity, or immune rejection when placed in contact with living tissue.

“Photolithography” is a microfabrication process that uses light to transfer a geometric pattern from a photomask to a light-sensitive chemical photoresist on a substrate, enabling the precise definition of microscale features.

“Monolayer configuration” refers to the arrangement of the microfluidic meshwork as a single, continuous sheet without overlapping or folding, which is essential for maximizing biocompatibility and minimizing inflammatory responses.

### Glaucoma Drainage Meshwork

The core component of the present invention is the glaucoma drainage meshwork, a revolutionary departure from conventional solid-plate glaucoma implants. Unlike traditional devices that present a rigid, impermeable barrier to surrounding tissues, the inventive meshwork is a porous, fluid-permeable membrane engineered to mimic the structural and mechanical properties of native ocular tissue. This biomimetic design enables seamless integration with the episcleral environment, thereby circumventing the cascade of events that culminate in fibrotic encapsulation.

The meshwork is constructed as a two-dimensional lattice of microfluidic channels, each serving as a conduit for aqueous humor drainage. The channels are arranged in a square or hexagonal grid with a periodicity of approximately 100 micrometers, ensuring uniform fluid distribution across the entire implant surface. Each channel comprises a tubular wall formed from a biocompatible polymer—preferably a medical-grade silicone or polyimide derivative—with an outer diameter of about 20 micrometers and an inner lumen diameter of approximately 8 micrometers. These dimensions are not arbitrary; they are the result of extensive computational modeling using finite element analysis to balance two competing requirements: sufficient hydraulic conductance to achieve clinically meaningful IOP reduction, and minimal physical footprint to avoid perturbing local cellular processes.

The overall geometry of the meshwork is optimized for ocular implantation. In the preferred embodiment, the device measures 7 millimeters by 7 millimeters, a size that accommodates the typical episcleral pocket created during GDI surgery while avoiding excessive tissue coverage that could provoke unnecessary immune surveillance. The thickness of the entire meshwork is remarkably thin—only 4 micrometers—rendering it orders of magnitude more flexible than conventional silicone plates, which are typically 200–500 micrometers thick. This extreme thinness, combined with the low elastic modulus of the constituent polymer, allows the meshwork to drape effortlessly over the scleral surface, adapting to its convex curvature and accommodating the subtle pulsations and movements inherent to the living eye.

Critically, the porosity of the meshwork is not merely a passive feature but an active design element that preserves the continuity of the local tissue microenvironment. By occupying only a small fraction of the total volume (less than 10% solid material), the meshwork leaves ample space for resident fibroblasts, immune cells, and capillaries to maintain their normal spatial relationships and functional interactions. This stands in stark contrast to solid implants, which act as physical barriers that sever cellular networks and trigger wound-healing responses. Moreover, the cellular-scale dimensions of the channels prevent the infiltration of large inflammatory aggregates while permitting the free diffusion of cytokines, growth factors, and small molecules, thereby maintaining homeostatic signaling.

The material composition of the meshwork is selected to ensure long-term stability in the ocular milieu. Medical-grade silicones are preferred due to their proven track record in ophthalmic implants, excellent biocompatibility, and resistance to degradation in aqueous environments. Alternative materials such as parylene-C, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based hydrogels, or bioresorbable polymers may also be employed depending on the desired lifetime and mechanical properties. Surface modifications, including plasma treatment or covalent grafting of anti-fouling moieties (e.g., zwitterionic polymers), can further enhance biocompatibility by reducing protein adsorption and macrophage adhesion.

In operation, aqueous humor entering the meshwork via an attached drainage tube (not part of the present claims but envisioned for full device integration) distributes radially through the interconnected channel network, dissipating pressure gradients and promoting even fluid dispersion across the implant surface. This distributed drainage mechanism prevents the formation of high-pressure focal points that could otherwise stimulate localized fibrosis. Furthermore, because the meshwork lacks sharp edges or abrupt topographical features, it avoids the mechanical irritation that often initiates foreign body giant cell formation and subsequent collagen deposition.

The design also incorporates fail-safes against surgical mishandling. For instance, the meshwork may include radiopaque markers or colored fiducials (e.g., a limbal suture anchor point) to aid intraoperative visualization and positioning. Future embodiments may integrate self-expanding frames or shape-memory elements to ensure consistent monolayer deployment, addressing the observed issue of inflammatory hotspots in folded regions.

### Methods of Making

The fabrication of the glaucoma drainage meshwork employs advanced microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) techniques adapted from semiconductor manufacturing, ensuring high precision, reproducibility, and scalability. The process begins with a standard silicon wafer, which serves as a rigid, flat substrate for photolithographic patterning. A thin layer of nickel is first deposited onto the wafer using electroplating or sputtering; this nickel layer functions as a sacrificial release layer that will later enable the detachment of the completed meshwork.

Next, a negative photoresist—specifically, SU-8 2005 or a similar epoxy-based resist—is spin-coated onto the nickel surface to a thickness of approximately 4 micrometers. The wafer is then soft-baked to remove solvent, exposed to ultraviolet light through a photomask defining the channel wall pattern, and post-exposure baked to crosslink the exposed regions. Development removes the unexposed resist, leaving behind a raised network of SU-8 walls that correspond to the future microfluidic channels.

To create the internal lumens of the channels, a bilayer sacrificial resist system is employed. First, a lift-off resist (LOR 5A) is spin-coated and baked, followed by a thin layer of positive photoresist (AZ1505). This stack is patterned using a second photomask aligned to the SU-8 walls, such that the AZ1505 defines narrow strips running along the center of each SU-8 wall. After development, the LOR 5A beneath the AZ1505 is selectively dissolved in a mild solvent, creating undercut profiles that will later form the channel interiors.

The entire structure is then encapsulated with a final conformal coating of the same SU-8 or an alternative biocompatible polymer, sealing the sacrificial resist within. Subsequent thermal curing solidifies the polymer matrix. The wafer is then immersed in a solvent (e.g., ethyl lactate) that dissolves the LOR/AZ1505 stack, leaving behind hollow microchannels embedded within the SU-8 framework. Finally, the nickel release layer is etched away using a ferric chloride solution, freeing the meshwork from the silicon substrate.

The released meshwork is thoroughly rinsed in deionized water and phosphate-buffered saline to remove residual chemicals, then stored in sterile buffer until ready for use. Prior to implantation, the device undergoes autoclave sterilization at 121°C for 15 minutes, a process validated to preserve structural integrity and biocompatibility.

This fabrication method offers several advantages: it enables sub-micron feature resolution, ensures batch-to-batch consistency, and is compatible with roll-to-roll processing for mass production. Moreover, the use of established photolithographic tools allows for rapid iteration and customization of meshwork geometry to suit different clinical needs—for example, varying channel density for high- versus low-flow applications.

### Methods of Use

The glaucoma drainage meshwork is intended for surgical implantation in patients requiring long-term IOP control, particularly those at high risk for fibrotic failure with conventional devices. The procedure follows standard GDI surgical protocols with minor modifications to accommodate the meshwork’s unique physical properties.

Under general or local anesthesia, the surgeon accesses the supratemporal quadrant of the eye through a conjunctival peritomy. The underlying Tenon’s capsule and episcleral tissue are dissected to create a pocket approximately 6 millimeters posterior to the limbus. Unlike rigid plates that require fixation with non-absorbable sutures (e.g., 9-0 nylon), the ultra-flexible meshwork is simply laid into the pocket without suturing, relying on its conformability and tissue adhesion to maintain position. In some cases, a single 10-0 nylon suture may be placed at the limbal edge of the meshwork solely for intraoperative identification and postoperative monitoring, not for mechanical anchoring.

If integrated into a full GDI system, a silicone drainage tube is connected to the meshwork prior to implantation and inserted into the anterior chamber via a paracentesis. However, in the context of the present invention—which focuses on the meshwork itself—the device may be implanted independently to assess biocompatibility, as demonstrated in the experimental examples.

Postoperatively, patients receive standard antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory prophylaxis, including topical antibiotics (e.g., polymyxin) and corticosteroids (e.g., prednisolone acetate), tapered over several weeks. The absence of significant fibrosis eliminates the need for aggressive antifibrotic agents like mitomycin-C, thereby reducing complication risks.

Long-term, the meshwork remains integrated within the episcleral tissue, with histological evidence showing near-complete absence of capsule formation even after three months. Its monolayer configuration is critical; surgeons must take care to avoid folding or stacking the meshwork during placement, as multilayer regions may provoke localized inflammation. Training modules and specialized insertion tools are envisioned to standardize deployment.

The method is applicable across glaucoma subtypes, including primary open-angle, pseudoexfoliative, uveitic, and pediatric glaucomas, where fibrosis is a major determinant of surgical outcome. By decoupling drainage function from fibrotic liability, this approach promises durable IOP control with fewer interventions.

## EXPERIMENTAL EXAMPLES

### Example 1: A Novel Flexible Microfluidic Meshwork to Reduce Fibrosis in Glaucoma Surgery

To evaluate the biocompatibility and antifibrotic potential of the inventive microfluidic meshwork, a controlled preclinical study was conducted in a well-established rabbit model of glaucoma implantation. Three healthy Albino New Zealand rabbits (12–14 weeks old, 2–3 kg) were enrolled under Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval at the University of California, San Francisco, in strict accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Each rabbit underwent bilateral implantation in a randomized, contralateral design: one eye received the microfluidic meshwork (test group), and the fellow eye received the plate component of a commercially available Ahmed Glaucoma Valve (AGV PF7 model, no tube attached) as the control. This design eliminated inter-animal variability and provided direct comparison under identical physiological conditions.

Surgical implantation was performed under an operating microscope. Anesthesia was induced with intramuscular ketamine (50 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), maintained with inhaled isoflurane (2–4%). After povidone-iodine preparation, a 6–0 Vicryl suture was passed through the supratemporal limbus to rotate the globe inferiorly. Conjunctival peritomy was performed at the limbus, followed by blunt dissection of the episcleral plane to create a pocket 6 mm posterior to the limbus. The AGV PF7 plate was secured with 9–0 nylon sutures, while the microfluidic meshwork was placed without sutures due to its conformability. A 10–0 nylon suture was affixed to the limbal edge of the meshwork for identification. The conjunctiva was closed with 8–0 Vicryl. Subconjunctival cefazolin (0.1 mL) was administered, and postoperative care included polymyxin drops (once daily for 7 days) and prednisolone acetate 1% (three times daily, tapered every 3 days).

Animals were monitored on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, 14, and monthly thereafter for signs of infection, inflammation, or erosion. No adverse events were observed in any eye throughout the 3-month study period.

At 3 months, rabbits were euthanized, and exenteration was performed to harvest the entire orbital contents. Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE). A blinded pathologist measured fibrous capsule thickness at the implant-tissue interface.

Results demonstrated a dramatic reduction in fibrosis with the microfluidic meshwork. The mean capsule thickness beneath the AGV PF7 plate was 89 ± 6.6 μm, consistent with historical controls. In stark contrast, the meshwork group exhibited nearly absent encapsulation, with a mean thickness of only 1.3 ± 0.6 μm—a statistically significant difference (P = 0.002, paired t-test). Histology revealed dense collagenous tissue surrounding the AGV plate, whereas the meshwork was integrated within loose connective tissue with minimal inflammatory infiltrate.

Notably, in regions where the meshwork had folded during surgery (<10% of total area), mild inflammatory cell accumulation was observed, confirming the importance of monolayer placement. No such findings occurred in single-layer regions.

These results validate the core hypothesis: an ultra-flexible, cellular-scale microfluidic meshwork can effectively evade fibrotic encapsulation by minimizing mechanical mismatch and preserving tissue architecture. This represents a foundational advance toward next-generation glaucoma implants with enhanced longevity and safety.
