# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of automatic speech recognition (ASR) and, more particularly, to systems and methods for enhancing the performance of ASR models when decoding speech produced by individuals suffering from neurological speech disorders such as aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria. The invention specifically leverages non-invasive neural signals—namely electroencephalography (EEG)—synchronized with acoustic speech signals to generate supplementary acoustic representations that are integrated into deep learning-based ASR architectures. This integration enables a significant improvement in decoding accuracy for distorted, fragmented, or otherwise atypical speech patterns that conventional ASR systems fail to transcribe reliably. The technical domain encompasses interdisciplinary fields including computational neuroscience, signal processing, machine learning, natural language processing, and assistive communication technologies. The invention is particularly relevant to the development of next-generation voice-enabled assistive devices, speech prosthetics, and therapeutic tools designed to support individuals recovering from stroke, traumatic brain injury, or other neurological conditions that impair speech production and comprehension. By bridging neural correlates of speech articulation with traditional acoustic modeling, the invention advances the state of the art in robust, inclusive speech recognition capable of serving populations historically excluded from mainstream voice interface technologies.

## BACKGROUND

Automatic speech recognition (ASR) has become a foundational technology in modern human-computer interaction, powering virtual assistants such as Apple’s Siri, Amazon’s Alexa, and Samsung’s Bixby. These systems rely on deep neural networks trained on large corpora of normative speech—i.e., speech produced by neurotypical individuals with intact motor and linguistic capabilities. However, this design paradigm inherently excludes millions of individuals worldwide who suffer from acquired or developmental speech disorders, including aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria. Aphasia, typically resulting from stroke or head trauma, disrupts the brain’s ability to comprehend or formulate language, leading to fragmented, anomalous, or absent speech output. Apraxia of speech arises from impaired motor planning, causing inconsistent articulatory errors despite intact musculature. Dysarthria, stemming from neurological damage to the motor-speech system, results in slurred, slow, or imprecise articulation due to weakened or poorly coordinated speech muscles. Collectively, these disorders produce speech that is acoustically degraded, temporally irregular, and lexically sparse—characteristics that severely degrade the performance of standard ASR systems.

Prior efforts to adapt ASR for disordered speech have largely focused on collecting specialized speech datasets and fine-tuning acoustic models accordingly. For instance, the AphasiaBank corpus has enabled training of acoustic models tailored to aphasic speech, yet even these customized systems report phoneme error rates (PER) exceeding 75% in severe cases, translating to word error rates (WER) often above 90%. Similar limitations plague attempts to recognize apraxic and dysarthric speech, where vocabulary coverage remains narrow and accuracy unacceptably low for practical deployment. These shortcomings stem from the fundamental mismatch between the statistical assumptions embedded in conventional ASR pipelines—optimized for fluent, canonical speech—and the highly variable, non-stationary nature of disordered utterances.

Concurrently, advances in cognitive neuroscience have demonstrated that neural signals encode rich information about speech perception and production. Electrocorticography (ECoG) studies have shown that cortical activity can predict phonetic content with high fidelity, but ECoG requires invasive intracranial electrode placement, limiting its clinical applicability. In contrast, electroencephalography (EEG) offers a non-invasive alternative, capturing electrical activity from the scalp with millisecond temporal resolution—sufficient to track the rapid dynamics of speech, which unfolds at approximately 150 words per minute. Although EEG suffers from poor spatial resolution and low signal-to-noise ratio compared to ECoG, recent work has established that specific EEG-derived features—such as root mean square, zero-crossing rate, kurtosis, power spectral entropy, and moving window averages—correlate with speech-related neural processes. Notably, these features have been used to enhance isolated word recognition in healthy speakers, albeit on extremely limited vocabularies (e.g., four words and five vowels).

Despite these promising findings, no prior art has successfully integrated EEG-derived representations into ASR systems for individuals with speech disorders. Existing approaches either treat neural and acoustic modalities in isolation or fail to model their synergistic relationship during speech production. Moreover, previous studies have not addressed the practical challenges of real-world deployment, such as artifact contamination (e.g., electromyographic interference from facial muscles), high-dimensional feature spaces, or the need for computationally efficient inference. Crucially, there remains a gap in methodologies that can generalize across both isolated and continuous speech recognition tasks while maintaining compatibility with standard ASR architectures like connectionist temporal classification (CTC) models or recurrent neural network transducers.

The present invention addresses these deficiencies by introducing a novel deep learning framework that fuses EEG-derived acoustic representations with traditional Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) to significantly improve ASR performance for aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria speech. Unlike prior art, the invention does not require re-engineering of the core ASR model; instead, it augments input features through a pre-trained regression module that maps EEG features to pseudo-acoustic representations. This modular design ensures broad applicability across different ASR paradigms while leveraging the complementary information carried by neural signals—information that reflects intended speech even when acoustic output is incomplete or distorted. Furthermore, the invention incorporates robust preprocessing pipelines for EEG artifact removal, nonlinear dimensionality reduction via kernel principal component analysis (KPCA), and strategic sensor selection based on neuroanatomical relevance (e.g., frontal and temporal lobes), thereby optimizing signal quality and computational efficiency. By bridging the gap between neural intention and acoustic realization, the invention enables a new class of inclusive, high-performance speech recognition systems capable of restoring voice to those whose speech has been compromised by neurological injury.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for significantly improving the accuracy of automatic speech recognition (ASR) for individuals with neurological speech disorders—including aphasia, apraxia, and dysarthria—by integrating non-invasive electroencephalography (EEG) signals with conventional acoustic features during the training and inference phases of deep learning-based ASR models. The core innovation lies in a two-stage deep learning architecture wherein a regression model first learns a mapping from high-dimensional EEG features to acoustic representations, specifically Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs), and subsequently, these EEG-derived acoustic representations are concatenated with actual MFCCs extracted from the user’s speech to form an enriched multimodal input for the ASR model. This approach capitalizes on the fact that EEG signals capture neural correlates of speech intention and articulatory planning, which remain partially intact even when the resulting acoustic output is severely degraded due to motor or linguistic impairments. By supplementing the acoustic stream with this neural-derived proxy of intended speech, the ASR model gains access to latent linguistic structure that would otherwise be lost in distorted utterances, thereby achieving substantial reductions in word error rate (WER) and improvements in sentence-level accuracy.

In one embodiment, the invention comprises the following key components: (1) a synchronized data acquisition system that records speech, EEG, and optionally electromyography (EMG) signals from subjects with speech disorders during structured speaking tasks; (2) a signal preprocessing pipeline that filters EEG data, removes EMG artifacts via linear regression, and extracts five discriminative time-domain and spectral features per EEG channel; (3) a nonlinear dimensionality reduction module employing kernel principal component analysis (KPCA) with a polynomial kernel to compress the high-dimensional EEG feature space into a compact, informative representation; (4) a gated recurrent unit (GRU)-based regression model trained to predict 13-dimensional MFCC vectors from the reduced EEG features using mean squared error loss; and (5) a deep ASR model—configured either as an isolated sentence classifier or a continuous character-level CTC decoder—that receives concatenated inputs of true MFCCs and EEG-predicted MFCCs during both training and inference. The ASR model is trained end-to-end using standard optimization techniques, with early stopping to prevent overfitting, and may incorporate external language models during decoding for continuous speech tasks.

Experimental validation demonstrates that the proposed method improves isolated speech recognition accuracy by more than 50% compared to baseline systems using MFCCs alone, and achieves statistically significant reductions in WER for continuous speech recognition on a vocabulary of over 1,700 sentences. Notably, the invention maintains efficacy even when using only EEG sensors from neuroanatomically relevant regions (frontal and temporal lobes) or when processing truncated speech segments—scenarios that mimic real-world challenges such as long pauses or incomplete utterances common in disordered speech. Furthermore, ablation studies confirm that EEG-derived features contribute meaningfully beyond what can be achieved with EMG or acoustic features alone, underscoring the unique value of neural signals in capturing speech intent. The invention is designed to be modular and compatible with existing ASR frameworks, requiring no modification to the core recognition architecture beyond input feature augmentation. A publicly released dataset of synchronized speech-EEG recordings from nine individuals with aphasia, apraxia, or dysarthria further supports reproducibility and future research. Ultimately, the invention enables the development of robust, accessible speech prosthetics and therapeutic feedback tools that empower individuals with speech disorders to interact effectively with voice-driven technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a deep learning-based system and method for enhancing automatic speech recognition (ASR) performance in the context of disordered speech—specifically, speech affected by aphasia, apraxia, or dysarthria—through the integration of non-invasive neural signals captured via electroencephalography (EEG). The following detailed description sets forth the structural components, operational procedures, and algorithmic innovations that constitute the preferred embodiment of the invention, though it will be understood by those skilled in the art that various modifications and alternative implementations remain within the scope of the claims.

The system begins with a multimodal data acquisition setup designed to simultaneously record speech, EEG, and electromyography (EMG) signals from subjects diagnosed with one or more of the target speech disorders. Data collection occurs in a controlled clinical environment, such as a speech therapy session at a specialized rehabilitation center. Each subject performs two primary tasks: (1) reading aloud pre-selected English sentences displayed on a computer screen, and (2) listening to pre-recorded audio prompts of the same sentences and then repeating them verbally. These tasks are chosen to elicit both self-initiated and imitated speech, capturing a range of articulatory behaviors relevant to therapeutic assessment. During each trial, speech is recorded using a high-fidelity mono-channel microphone sampled at 16 kHz, ensuring sufficient bandwidth for phonetic analysis. Concurrently, EEG signals are captured using a 29-channel wet-electrode cap conforming to the international 10–20 system for standardized scalp placement. The EEG amplifier, such as the Brain Products ActiChamp, samples neural activity at 1,000 Hz to preserve temporal fidelity. Additionally, two EMG sensors are affixed near the masseter and frontalis muscles to monitor facial and jaw movements that may introduce myoelectric artifacts into the EEG stream. This synchronized tri-modal recording yields a comprehensive dataset linking neural activity, muscular effort, and acoustic output during disordered speech production.

Following acquisition, raw signals undergo a rigorous preprocessing pipeline to enhance signal quality and extract discriminative features. The EEG data are first bandpass-filtered using a fourth-order infinite impulse response (IIR) filter with cutoff frequencies of 0.1 Hz and 70 Hz to eliminate slow drifts and high-frequency noise. A 60 Hz notch filter is applied to suppress power line interference. EMG artifacts, which can dominate EEG signals during articulation, are mitigated through a linear regression technique: for each EEG channel, the contaminated signal \( x_{\text{EEG}}(t) \) is modeled as \( x_{\text{EEG}}(t) = s(t) + \alpha \cdot x_{\text{EMG}}(t) + \epsilon(t) \), where \( s(t) \) is the artifact-free neural signal, \( x_{\text{EMG}}(t) \) is the reference EMG recording, \( \alpha \) is a regression coefficient estimated via ordinary least squares, and \( \epsilon(t) \) represents residual noise. The cleaned EEG is then segmented into epochs aligned with speech utterances, and five time-domain and spectral features are computed per channel at a frame rate of 100 Hz to match the temporal resolution of subsequent acoustic feature extraction. These features include: (i) root mean square (RMS), capturing signal energy; (ii) zero-crossing rate (ZCR), reflecting signal frequency content; (iii) moving window average over 100 ms windows, indicating local trends; (iv) kurtosis, measuring deviation from Gaussianity; and (v) power spectral entropy, quantifying spectral complexity. This feature set, previously validated in studies of speech perception and production, yields a 145-dimensional vector per time step (29 channels × 5 features).

To address the curse of dimensionality and nonlinear structure inherent in the EEG feature space, the invention employs kernel principal component analysis (KPCA) for dimensionality reduction. Prior to KPCA, features are normalized by subtracting the mean and scaling to unit variance across the training set. A polynomial kernel of degree three is selected based on empirical evaluation of reconstruction error and downstream ASR performance. The number of retained components is determined by analyzing the cumulative explained variance curve; in the preferred embodiment, 10 components are sufficient to preserve over 95% of the relevant information while drastically reducing computational load. This yields a compact 10-dimensional EEG embedding that retains neurophysiologically meaningful patterns related to speech articulation.

Parallel to EEG processing, the speech signal is converted into standard acoustic features. Specifically, 13-dimensional Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) are extracted at 100 Hz using a 25 ms Hamming window with 10 ms overlap, consistent with typical ASR frontends. These MFCCs serve as the baseline acoustic representation for both training and testing.

The heart of the invention is a two-stage deep learning architecture. In the first stage, a regression model is trained to map the reduced EEG features to MFCC-like representations. This model comprises a single-layer gated recurrent unit (GRU) with 128 hidden units, followed by a time-distributed dense layer with 13 linear units. The GRU processes the temporal sequence of 10-dimensional EEG embeddings and outputs a predicted MFCC vector at each time step. Training proceeds for 70 epochs using the Adam optimizer and mean squared error (MSE) loss between predicted and ground-truth MFCCs. Early stopping based on validation loss prevents overfitting. The trained regression model thus acts as a neural-to-acoustic translator, converting EEG-derived speech intentions into a format compatible with conventional ASR inputs.

In the second stage, the ASR model itself is trained using a hybrid input comprising both true MFCCs and EEG-predicted MFCCs. Two configurations are supported: isolated and continuous speech recognition. For isolated recognition—where each utterance corresponds to one of 57 predefined English sentences—the ASR model is a GRU-based classifier. It consists of a single GRU layer with 512 hidden units, whose final hidden state is passed through a dropout layer (rate = 0.2) and then a dense layer with 57 linear units (one per sentence label). A softmax activation yields prediction probabilities over the vocabulary. The model is trained for up to 10 epochs with categorical cross-entropy loss, Adam optimization, and batch size 50, again with early stopping. During inference, the input to the GRU is the concatenation of true MFCCs and EEG-predicted MFCCs along the feature dimension, resulting in a 26-dimensional input vector per time step.

For continuous speech recognition—handling open-ended sequences of words—the invention employs a character-level connectionist temporal classification (CTC) model. The encoder is a GRU with 512 hidden units that processes the concatenated 26-dimensional input sequence. The decoder comprises a dense layer with linear activation followed by softmax, producing character probabilities at each time step without requiring explicit alignment between input and output. Training spans 100 epochs with CTC loss, Adam optimizer, and batch size 50. At inference time, a CTC beam search decoder integrates a pre-trained 4-gram language model to resolve ambiguities and improve lexical coherence.

The entire system is evaluated on a dataset comprising 8,854 utterances from nine subjects with clinically diagnosed aphasia, apraxia, or dysarthria. The data are randomly partitioned into 70% training, 10% validation, and 20% test sets with no speaker overlap. Performance metrics include accuracy, F1-score, precision, and recall for isolated recognition, and word error rate (WER) for continuous recognition. Baseline comparisons use MFCCs alone; all proposed variants augment MFCCs with EEG-predicted features.

Results demonstrate that the invention consistently outperforms baselines. For isolated recognition, the addition of EEG-derived features improves accuracy by over 50%, with similar gains in F1-score and recall. Ablation studies confirm that (i) EMG artifact removal provides marginal benefit, (ii) KPCA-based dimensionality reduction is critical for performance, (iii) restricting EEG sensors to frontal and temporal lobes (12 + 4 channels) yields comparable results to full-cap recordings, and (iv) the system remains effective even when only the first half of each utterance is processed—addressing the challenge of fragmented speech. For continuous recognition on a 1,771-sentence test set, the invention reduces WER from a baseline range of 48.58–51.10% to 44.25–47.13% at 95% confidence, with a p-value of 0.0000213 confirming statistical significance. Confusion matrices reveal reduced misclassification among semantically or phonetically similar sentences, indicating that EEG features help disambiguate ambiguous acoustic patterns.

The invention’s modular design ensures compatibility with existing ASR ecosystems: the regression model can be trained offline and deployed as a feature augmentation module without altering the core recognizer. Latency considerations for real-time use are acknowledged, with future work aimed at model compression and edge deployment. Critically, the invention does not require invasive procedures, making it suitable for outpatient and home-based applications. By transforming EEG from a diagnostic tool into an active component of speech recognition, the invention pioneers a new paradigm in assistive communication—one that listens not just to the voice, but to the brain behind it.
