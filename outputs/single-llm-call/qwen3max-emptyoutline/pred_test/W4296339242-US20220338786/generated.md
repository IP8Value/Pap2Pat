## BACKGROUND

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) represents a well-established neuromodulation therapy for a range of neurological and psychiatric disorders, including Parkinson’s disease, essential tremor, dystonia, epilepsy, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. The therapeutic mechanism involves the delivery of high-frequency electrical pulses through chronically implanted electrodes positioned within specific deep brain nuclei. These electrical stimuli modulate pathological neural activity patterns, thereby alleviating motor and non-motor symptoms associated with these conditions. Despite its clinical efficacy, the precise neurophysiological mechanisms underlying DBS remain incompletely understood, which limits the ability to optimize stimulation parameters for individual patients and to develop next-generation adaptive therapies.

A critical barrier to advancing the scientific understanding of DBS—and to realizing closed-loop, biomarker-driven stimulation systems—is the overwhelming contamination of neural recordings by stimulation artifacts. During active DBS, the recorded local field potentials (LFPs) or electrocorticographic signals are dominated by large-amplitude, high-frequency electrical transients generated by the stimulator itself. These artifacts can be orders of magnitude larger than the endogenous neural signals of interest, effectively obscuring them in both the time and frequency domains. Consequently, conventional signal processing techniques fail to recover the underlying neural dynamics that may serve as candidate biomarkers for symptom severity or treatment response.

The problem of artifact removal in DBS is further exacerbated by several practical constraints inherent to real-world clinical and research settings. First, although the nominal stimulation frequency is programmable via the DBS device interface, the actual delivered frequency often deviates slightly due to hardware tolerances, clock drift, or other implementation-specific factors. This discrepancy, though seemingly minor, renders naive assumptions about the artifact frequency insufficient for high-fidelity reconstruction and removal, particularly when using parametric models such as harmonic regression. Second, many implantable DBS systems operate under stringent power budgets, necessitating low sampling rates—typically in the range of 200 to 250 Hz—to conserve battery life and reduce data transmission overhead. At these sampling rates, the true stimulation frequency (often set between 130 and 185 Hz) may exceed the Nyquist limit, causing aliasing that folds the artifact energy into lower frequency bands that overlap precisely with those containing physiologically relevant neural oscillations (e.g., beta band at 13–30 Hz). This spectral overlap complicates frequency-domain filtering approaches and increases the risk of inadvertently removing or distorting genuine neural content.

Third, and perhaps most challenging, is the issue of data discontinuity. Modern wireless-enabled DBS platforms frequently transmit neural data in segmented bursts due to limitations in bandwidth, memory buffering, or duty cycling of sensing circuitry. As a result, the recorded time series is often fragmented into multiple non-contiguous segments separated by gaps of unknown duration. These gaps introduce arbitrary phase shifts between successive data segments relative to the periodic stimulation waveform. Because the artifact is strictly periodic with respect to the stimulation clock—but the recording clock may not be perfectly synchronized—the phase relationship of the artifact across segments becomes unknown and must be jointly estimated along with the artifact’s fundamental frequency and waveform shape. Conventional artifact removal methods, including template subtraction, notch filtering, blanking, and standard Fourier-based techniques, either assume continuous data, ignore phase misalignment, or rely on inaccurate frequency estimates derived from device settings or coarse spectral peaks. None adequately address the combined challenges of unknown frequency, aliasing, and inter-segment phase shifts in a unified framework suitable for real-time operation.

Existing approaches to DBS artifact mitigation fall into several categories. Template-based methods involve averaging artifact waveforms across multiple cycles to construct a representative template, which is then subtracted from the raw signal. However, these methods require long stretches of continuous data without phase interruptions and are sensitive to temporal jitter or amplitude modulation in the artifact. Frequency-domain notch filters can suppress energy at the stimulation frequency and its harmonics but suffer from poor selectivity, especially at low sampling rates where aliased components cluster near neural bands, and they cannot recover signal content that lies beneath the artifact spectrum. Adaptive filtering techniques, such as recursive least squares or Kalman filters, have been explored but often require prior knowledge of the artifact characteristics or struggle with non-stationarities introduced by phase shifts. More recently, model-based harmonic regression has shown promise by representing the artifact as a sum of sinusoids at integer multiples of a fundamental frequency. Yet, as demonstrated in the present work, such methods are highly sensitive to errors in the assumed frequency; even sub-percent deviations can lead to catastrophic reconstruction failures due to phase misalignment over time.

Moreover, while some prior art has attempted to estimate unknown phases between two data segments, these approaches typically proceed sequentially—first estimating the artifact from one segment, then searching over a discrete set of candidate phase offsets corresponding to plausible numbers of missing samples—and do not perform joint optimization over frequency, phase shifts, and harmonic coefficients. This sequential strategy is computationally inefficient, prone to error propagation, and ill-suited for scenarios involving many segments or long gaps. Crucially, none of the existing methods provide a robust, integrated solution capable of simultaneously estimating the true stimulation frequency (to high precision), aligning multiple discontinuous data segments via unknown phase shifts, and reconstructing the full artifact waveform in a manner compatible with real-time embedded implementation.

Accordingly, there exists a compelling need for a novel artifact removal system that overcomes these limitations. Such a system must be capable of operating under low-sampling-rate conditions, handling arbitrarily segmented data with unknown inter-segment timing, accurately estimating the true stimulation frequency without reliance on device-reported values, and performing all computations efficiently enough to support closed-loop neuromodulation. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a unified optimization framework that jointly infers the artifact’s fundamental frequency, inter-segment phase shifts, and harmonic composition through a combination of energy-maximization initialization and iterative least-squares refinement. This approach enables high-fidelity recovery of underlying neural signals even in the presence of aliasing, missing data, and frequency uncertainty, thereby facilitating both offline biomarker discovery and real-time adaptive DBS control.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a computer-implemented method and system for reconstructing and removing periodic stimulation artifacts from segmented neural recordings obtained during deep brain stimulation (DBS), wherein the artifact exhibits an unknown fundamental frequency and unknown phase shifts between data segments due to missing samples or asynchronous recording intervals. The method operates by modeling the observed signal in each segment as the superposition of a periodic artifact, an underlying neural signal, and noise, where the artifact is represented as a finite Fourier series comprising a fundamental frequency and a predetermined number of harmonics. Critically, the invention jointly estimates the artifact’s true fundamental frequency, the relative phase shifts between all data segments, and the harmonic coefficients through a two-stage optimization process that ensures high accuracy and computational efficiency.

In a first aspect, the invention includes an initialization algorithm that identifies robust initial estimates of the artifact frequency and inter-segment phase shifts by maximizing a generalized energy function derived from a continuous-time Fourier transform formulation extended across all data segments. This energy function accounts for unknown phase offsets by treating each segment’s contribution as a complex exponential modulated by a segment-specific phase variable. The maximization is performed numerically using a modified Newton ascent method with random restarts and backtracking line search, applied to a discretized approximation of the energy functional computed via the trapezoidal rule. This initialization stage circumvents the limitations of discrete Fourier transform (DFT)-based frequency estimation, which is constrained to a fixed frequency grid and fails to resolve frequencies lying between bins, especially under low sampling rates or in the presence of aliasing.

In a second aspect, the invention employs an artifact removal algorithm that refines the initial estimates by minimizing a least-squares loss function representing the residual energy after subtracting a parametric artifact model from the observed data. The artifact model is defined as a trigonometric polynomial whose arguments incorporate the estimated fundamental frequency and segment-specific phase shifts. For any fixed frequency and phase configuration, the optimal harmonic coefficients are obtained in closed form via linear least squares, reducing the overall problem to a nonlinear optimization over only the frequency and phase variables. The algorithm applies Newton’s descent method to this reduced objective, leveraging analytical gradients and Hessians derived from the underlying harmonic regression structure. Due to the smoothness and local convexity of the objective near the true parameters, convergence to a high-precision solution is achieved rapidly when initialized properly.

The invention further provides a complete computational framework that handles practical constraints of DBS systems, including uniform sampling at clinically relevant rates (e.g., 200–1000 Hz), segmentation into an arbitrary number of contiguous data blocks, and the presence of aliased artifact components. By requiring only a single tunable parameter—the number of harmonics to include in the artifact model—the system offers simplicity and interpretability: this parameter may be selected based on visual inspection of the spectrogram or fixed offline for a given DBS device configuration, rendering the online artifact removal effectively parameter-free. Experimental validation demonstrates that the method achieves near-machine-precision frequency estimation, accurately reconstructs the artifact waveform even when overlaid with chirp signals or neural-like waveforms, and successfully removes stimulation artifacts from real human LFP recordings containing missing data of unknown duration.

In another embodiment, the invention is implemented on embedded hardware such as digital signal processors (DSPs) or field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) within an implantable DBS device, enabling real-time artifact removal for closed-loop neuromodulation. The computational complexity of both the initialization and refinement stages scales cubically with the number of segments and the number of harmonics, but remains tractable in practice due to typical constraints (e.g., fewer than 20 segments per processing window and fewer than 10 harmonics needed to model common DBS pulse shapes). The resulting clean neural signal can be used to extract biomarkers, trigger adaptive stimulation adjustments, or provide feedback for therapy personalization.

Thus, the invention constitutes a significant advancement over prior art by providing the first unified, optimization-based solution that simultaneously addresses unknown stimulation frequency, inter-segment phase misalignment, and low-sampling-rate aliasing—three interrelated challenges that have previously hindered reliable neural signal recovery during active DBS. The method is generalizable to other periodic interference problems in biomedical signal processing and represents a foundational technology for next-generation adaptive brain-computer interfaces.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

### Computer System

The methods described herein are implemented on a specialized computer system configured to process neural data acquired during deep brain stimulation. This system comprises one or more processors, a memory unit storing executable instructions, and input/output interfaces for receiving raw neural recordings and outputting artifact-corrected signals. In a preferred embodiment, the computer system is integrated within an implantable medical device, such as a next-generation DBS pulse generator equipped with sensing capabilities, though it may also be deployed on external computing platforms for offline analysis or research purposes.

The processor is a low-power microcontroller, digital signal processor (DSP), or application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) optimized for real-time numerical computation. It is capable of executing floating-point or fixed-point arithmetic operations necessary for evaluating trigonometric functions, solving linear systems, and performing iterative optimization. The memory stores program code implementing the artifact removal algorithms, as well as buffers for incoming neural data segments, intermediate variables (e.g., gradient vectors, Hessian matrices), and output signals. The system receives digitized neural recordings via analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) sampling at rates between 200 Hz and 1000 Hz, consistent with current clinical DBS devices. Data may arrive continuously or in discrete packets corresponding to non-overlapping time windows, with potential gaps between packets due to wireless transmission latency, power management protocols, or buffer overflow events.

Upon receipt of a batch of segmented data—comprising n+1 contiguous blocks S₀, S₁, ..., Sₙ, each sampled uniformly at times t = j/fₛ for j = 0, ..., Nᵢ−1—the system executes the artifact removal pipeline. First, it invokes the initialization algorithm to compute preliminary estimates of the artifact’s fundamental frequency ω and phase shifts δ₁, ..., δₙ. These estimates are then passed to the artifact removal algorithm, which performs iterative refinement to minimize the residual sum of squares between the observed data and the parametric artifact model. The final artifact estimate â(t) is subtracted from each segment Sᵢ(t) to yield the recovered neural signal Rᵢ(t) = Sᵢ(t) − â(t), which is either stored locally, transmitted wirelessly to an external receiver, or used immediately for closed-loop control logic (e.g., detecting beta-band power exceeding a threshold to trigger stimulation adjustment).

The software architecture is modular, with distinct routines for energy computation, gradient/Hessian evaluation, linear system solving, and convergence checking. Numerical stability is ensured through the use of modified Cholesky decomposition in the Newton steps to handle potential indefiniteness of the Hessian, and backtracking line search guarantees sufficient decrease in the objective function. Parallelization is supported where feasible—for instance, evaluating the energy function across multiple random initializations in the initialization stage can be distributed across processor cores or vectorized using SIMD instructions. The entire pipeline is designed to complete within a time budget compatible with real-time operation; empirical testing shows processing latencies of less than 2 seconds for 10-second data windows on consumer-grade hardware, suggesting feasibility for embedded deployment with optimized code.

### Period-Based Artifact Reconstruction and Removal for Deep Brain Stimulation

The core innovation of the present invention lies in a period-based artifact reconstruction and removal methodology that models the stimulation artifact as a deterministic, periodic function with unknown period (or equivalently, unknown fundamental frequency) and unknown phase offsets across segmented data. Let the observed neural recording be partitioned into n+1 contiguous segments S₀, S₁, ..., Sₙ, where each segment Sᵢ corresponds to a time interval during which data was successfully acquired without interruption. Due to missing samples—arising from wireless dropouts, device sleep modes, or buffer resets—the temporal alignment of these segments relative to the periodic stimulation waveform is unknown. Consequently, the artifact appearing in segment Sᵢ is a phase-shifted version of the artifact in segment S₀.

Formally, the i-th observed segment is modeled as  
Sᵢ(t) = A(t + δᵢ*/ξ*) + Bᵢ(t) + ηᵢ(t),  
for i = 0, ..., n, where A(·) is a periodic artifact function with unknown true period 1/ξ*, δᵢ* is the unknown true phase shift (expressed in cycles) between segment i and segment 0 (with δ₀* = 0 by convention), Bᵢ(t) is the underlying neural signal of interest, and ηᵢ(t) represents measurement noise. The goal is to estimate A(·) and remove it from each Sᵢ(t) to recover Bᵢ(t) + ηᵢ(t).

To enable tractable computation, the artifact A(·) is approximated by a finite Fourier series:  
a(t | ξ, δ, α₀, αₖ, βₖ, K) = α₀ + Σₖ₌₁ᴷ [αₖ cos(2πk(ξt + δ)) + βₖ sin(2πk(ξt + δ))],  
where ξ is the estimated fundamental frequency, δ is the phase offset, α₀ is a constant (DC) term, and {αₖ, βₖ} are harmonic amplitudes for k = 1 to K. The number of harmonics K is a user-selectable parameter, typically chosen between 3 and 10 based on the expected spectral richness of the DBS pulse shape (e.g., rectangular pulses require more harmonics than sinusoidal ones).

Given this model, the artifact removal problem is cast as a joint optimization over ξ, {δᵢ}, and the harmonic coefficients. Specifically, the system minimizes the total squared residual:  
ℒ(ξ, {δᵢ}, θ) = Σᵢ₌₀ⁿ Σ_{t ∈ Tᵢ} [Sᵢ(t) − a(t | ξ, δᵢ, θ)]²,  
where θ = (α₀, {αₖ, βₖ}) and Tᵢ denotes the set of sample times in segment i. Rather than optimizing over all parameters simultaneously, the invention exploits the fact that for fixed ξ and {δᵢ}, the optimal θ can be found analytically via linear least squares. This reduces the problem to minimizing a scalar-valued function g(ξ, {δᵢ}) defined as the minimal residual achievable for given frequency and phase values.

The function g is smooth and twice differentiable, allowing the use of second-order optimization methods. The artifact removal algorithm employs Newton’s descent: at each iteration, it computes the gradient ∇g and Hessian ∇²g with respect to ξ and {δᵢ}, solves the Newton system ∇²g · Δ = −∇g for the update direction Δ, and applies a step along Δ with backtracking to ensure sufficient decrease. Convergence is declared when the norm of the gradient falls below a tolerance (e.g., 1e−8) or the relative change in g becomes negligible.

This approach contrasts sharply with conventional harmonic regression, which assumes ξ is known a priori. As demonstrated empirically, even minute errors in ξ (e.g., 0.001% relative error) cause harmonic regression to accumulate phase drift over time, leading to large reconstruction errors. By jointly estimating ξ and {δᵢ}, the present method avoids this pitfall and achieves reconstruction fidelity limited only by numerical precision and model adequacy (i.e., choice of K).

### Comparison of PARRM to Conventional Filters

The performance of the present invention—referred to herein as Period-based Artifact Reconstruction and Removal Method (PARRM)—is markedly superior to conventional filtering techniques commonly employed in DBS signal processing. Standard approaches include fixed-frequency notch filters, adaptive template subtraction, and DFT-based frequency estimation followed by harmonic regression. Each of these suffers from fundamental limitations that PARRM overcomes through its joint estimation framework.

Notch filters, whether implemented in analog hardware or digital software, attenuate signal energy within a narrow band around the nominal stimulation frequency (e.g., 130–185 Hz) and its harmonics. However, under low sampling rates (e.g., 250 Hz), the true stimulation frequency may be aliased into lower bands (e.g., a 150 Hz stimulus appears at 100 Hz when fs = 250 Hz), causing the notch to suppress neural oscillations in the beta or gamma ranges. Moreover, notch filters cannot recover signal content that lies spectrally beneath the artifact; they merely attenuate it, resulting in irreversible information loss. PARRM, by contrast, explicitly models and subtracts the artifact, preserving underlying neural dynamics even when they spectrally overlap with the artifact.

Template subtraction methods average artifact waveforms across multiple cycles to build a representative template, which is then aligned and subtracted from each cycle. These methods fail when data is segmented, as the unknown phase shifts prevent consistent alignment across gaps. They also assume stationarity of the artifact shape, which may not hold if stimulation amplitude or impedance changes over time. PARRM does not require cycle-by-cycle alignment; instead, it leverages the global periodicity across all segments simultaneously, making it robust to segmentation and moderate non-stationarities.

DFT-based frequency estimation identifies the artifact frequency as the peak in the magnitude spectrum of the observed signal. However, the DFT is constrained to a discrete frequency grid spaced by fs/N, where N is the number of samples. If the true frequency falls between bins—as is almost always the case—the estimated frequency will be biased, leading to poor harmonic regression performance. For example, with fs = 1000 Hz and N = 10⁴, the frequency resolution is 0.1 Hz, yet a true frequency of 150.6117 Hz will be misestimated as either 150.6 or 150.7 Hz, introducing a relative error exceeding 0.001%. PARRM’s initialization algorithm bypasses the DFT grid entirely by maximizing a continuous energy functional, achieving frequency estimates accurate to machine precision (e.g., relative error < 1e−12%).

Empirical comparisons confirm these advantages. In simulated data with no underlying signal, PARRM reconstructs the artifact with relative RMSE below 1e−10%, whereas DFT-initialized harmonic regression exhibits RMSE above 10%. In the presence of a chirp signal overlapping the artifact frequency, PARRM preserves the chirp with relative RMSE ~5.5%, while notch filtering distorts it irreversibly. In aliased, segmented data mimicking real-world constraints, PARRM removes the artifact completely in the frequency domain, leaving the neural signal intact, whereas conventional methods either fail to converge or introduce spurious oscillations. Thus, PARRM represents a qualitative leap in artifact removal capability, enabling reliable neural signal recovery under conditions where prior art fails.

### Periodic Estimation of Lost Packets From Deep Brain Stimulation Waveform Data

A critical feature of the present invention is its ability to estimate the duration of missing data segments—corresponding to lost packets in wireless DBS systems—from the phase shifts inferred during artifact reconstruction. In many clinical and research DBS platforms, neural data is transmitted intermittently due to bandwidth limitations, power constraints, or protocol design, resulting in a time series composed of disjoint blocks separated by gaps of unknown length. These gaps disrupt the temporal continuity of the periodic stimulation artifact, introducing unknown phase offsets between blocks that must be resolved to enable coherent artifact modeling.

The invention treats each gap as inducing a phase shift δᵢ in the artifact waveform of segment i relative to a reference segment (e.g., segment 0). Since the artifact is periodic with true period T* = 1/ξ*, a time gap of duration Δt corresponds to a phase shift of δ = (Δt / T*) mod 1. Thus, estimating δᵢ allows inference of Δt up to an integer number of periods. While the absolute gap duration cannot be determined without additional constraints (e.g., maximum plausible gap length), the relative phase alignment is sufficient for artifact reconstruction.

During the optimization process, the algorithm simultaneously estimates ξ and all δᵢ by maximizing the coherence of the artifact across segments. Specifically, the energy function E(ω, {δᵢ}) = |ℱ(ω, {δᵢ})|², where ℱ is a generalized Fourier transform summing contributions from all segments with phase corrections, is maximized to find the frequency and phases that best align the artifact energy constructively. This alignment implicitly encodes the missing time intervals: larger gaps produce larger phase shifts, which the algorithm resolves by searching over the continuous interval [0,1) for each δᵢ.

In practice, the estimated phase shifts δ̂ᵢ can be converted to estimated gap durations via Δt̂ᵢ = δ̂ᵢ / ξ̂, where ξ̂ is the estimated fundamental frequency. Although this yields Δt̂ᵢ modulo T̂, the ambiguity can often be resolved by imposing physiological or system-level priors—for instance, if the maximum packet loss duration is known to be less than one stimulation period, the modulo ambiguity vanishes. Even without such priors, the phase estimates enable accurate artifact subtraction, as the harmonic model depends only on the fractional phase, not the absolute time offset.

This capability is essential for real-world applicability, as it eliminates the need for precise timestamp synchronization between the stimulator and recorder—a requirement that is often unmet in commercial DBS systems. By treating missing data as a source of unknown phase rather than irrecoverable corruption, the invention transforms a major obstacle into a solvable parameter estimation problem, thereby enabling robust artifact removal from realistically fragmented neural recordings.

### Experimental Testing of the Period-Based Estimation of the Loss of Packets (PELP)

The efficacy of the present invention was rigorously validated through a series of controlled experiments encompassing synthetic and real-world datasets, designed to stress-test its performance under conditions representative of clinical DBS applications. Four primary test cases were constructed, each progressively introducing greater complexity: (1) a pure artifact with no neural signal and no segmentation; (2) an artifact overlaid with a chirp signal spanning the artifact frequency; (3) an aliased artifact with simulated neural activity, additive noise, and randomly inserted gaps; and (4) a human LFP recording from an OCD patient undergoing chronic DBS, artificially segmented to simulate packet loss.

In Example 1, a 10,000-sample artifact at 150.6117 Hz was generated using five harmonics and sampled at 1000 Hz. With no underlying signal or phase shifts (n=0), the algorithm successfully estimated the frequency with relative error 3.77×10⁻¹⁴% and reconstructed the artifact with relative RMSE 1.79×10⁻¹⁰%. The recovered signal was indistinguishable from zero (RMSE 3.01×10⁻¹⁰), confirming perfect artifact removal in the ideal case.

Example 2 introduced a chirp signal sweeping from 0 to 500 Hz, with amplitude 1/15th that of the artifact. Despite spectral overlap at the artifact frequency, PARRM preserved the chirp with relative RMSE 5.55% and removed >50 dB of artifact power, demonstrating resilience to concurrent neural activity.

Example 3 simulated worst-case clinical conditions: sampling at 250 Hz (causing aliasing of the 150.6117 Hz artifact to ~99.39 Hz), ten 250-sample segments separated by random-length gaps, underlying neural signal modeled as random sinusoidal snippets, and Gaussian noise. The artifact was reconstructed with 5.55% relative RMSE, and the neural signal recovered with 11.06% relative RMSE—visually near-perfect despite the challenging conditions. Spectral analysis confirmed complete removal of artifact harmonics.

Example 4 utilized real LFP data from a Summit RC+S DBS system (Medtronic) recorded at 1000 Hz during 150.6 Hz stimulation. After artificial segmentation into ten 250-sample blocks, PARRM estimated a frequency of 150.6093 Hz (consistent with device settings) and removed visible artifact bands in the spectrogram. Comparison with a "No DBS" baseline suggested successful recovery of physiological signal content.

Across all tests, the algorithm converged reliably using the proposed initialization, required only K=5 harmonics, and executed in under 2 seconds per dataset on standard hardware. These results confirm that PARRM meets the demanding requirements of real-time, embedded DBS applications, even under severe aliasing, noise, and data fragmentation.

### Period-Based Estimation of Electrical Stimulation Artifacts in the Presence of Phase Shifts

The ability of the present invention to handle unknown phase shifts between data segments is central to its utility in real-world DBS scenarios. Phase shifts arise whenever the temporal continuity of the recording is broken relative to the periodic stimulation clock, which occurs routinely due to wireless data transmission protocols, memory buffering constraints, or asynchronous sensing and stimulation subsystems. Unlike prior methods that either ignore phase misalignment or handle it heuristically, the invention incorporates phase shifts as explicit optimization variables within a unified mathematical framework.

Mathematically, the phase shift δᵢ for segment i represents the fractional offset (in cycles) of the artifact waveform in that segment relative to a reference segment. Because the artifact is periodic, δᵢ is defined modulo 1, meaning that phase shifts differing by an integer number of cycles are indistinguishable. The optimization problem thus seeks to identify the set of phases {δᵢ} that, together with the fundamental frequency ξ, maximize the coherence of the artifact across all segments.

This is achieved through the energy maximization initialization step, which defines a generalized Fourier transform ℱ(ω, {δᵢ}) that sums the complex exponentials e⁻²πi(ωt + δᵢ) weighted by the observed signal in each segment. Maximizing |ℱ|² encourages alignment of the artifact components across segments, as constructive interference occurs only when ω matches the true frequency and {δᵢ} match the true phase offsets. The subsequent refinement step then fine-tunes these estimates by minimizing the residual error in the time domain.

Critically, the invention does not assume that phase shifts correspond to integer numbers of missing samples—a common simplification in prior art that fails when clock drift or variable gap lengths are present. Instead, it treats δᵢ as continuous variables, enabling sub-sample resolution of phase alignment. This is essential for high-fidelity artifact reconstruction, as even small phase errors accumulate over multiple cycles and corrupt the harmonic model.

Experimental results confirm that the method accurately recovers phase shifts even when gaps are long and numerous. In Example 3, with ten segments separated by random gaps, the algorithm successfully aligned all segments to a common artifact phase, enabling complete removal of stimulation artifacts despite severe fragmentation. Similarly, in the human LFP data (Example 4), the estimated phases allowed coherent reconstruction across artificial gaps, yielding a clean neural signal.

This phase-aware approach distinguishes the invention from all previous artifact removal techniques and constitutes a key enabler for reliable neural decoding in next-generation adaptive DBS systems, where continuous, uninterrupted data streams cannot be guaranteed. By transforming phase uncertainty from a liability into a solvable parameter, the invention ensures robust performance across the full spectrum of real-world recording conditions.
