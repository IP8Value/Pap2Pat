# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of mechanical ventilation for patients requiring respiratory support, and more specifically to a novel mode of computer-controlled mechanical ventilation that optimizes ventilator settings to maximize alveolar ventilation while minimizing tidal volume in order to promote lung protection. The invention provides a system and method for determining and implementing ventilator parameters—including respiratory rate, inspiratory pressure, inspiratory time, and expiratory time—based on patient-specific physiological characteristics such as alveolar minute ventilation requirement, dead space ratio, and inspiratory and expiratory time constants. This mode of ventilation, referred to herein as Mid-Frequency Ventilation (MFV), operates within a pressure control framework and is designed to deliver the lowest possible tidal volume consistent with achieving a target alveolar minute ventilation, thereby reducing the risk of volutrauma and atelectrauma. The invention is particularly applicable to patients with a range of respiratory pathologies, including but not limited to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), morbid obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and status asthmaticus, and may be implemented on conventional mechanical ventilators equipped with appropriate control algorithms and waveform capabilities. Unlike existing adaptive ventilation modes such as Adaptive Support Ventilation (ASV), which primarily seeks to minimize the work rate of breathing, the MFV mode of the present invention is explicitly engineered to prioritize lung-protective strategies by minimizing end-inspiratory lung volumes and promoting alveolar recruitment through optimized frequency and tidal volume selection. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the automation of mechanical ventilation, offering clinicians a reliable, physiology-driven alternative to manual ventilator setting selection that adheres consistently to evidence-based lung protection principles.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Mechanical ventilation is a life-sustaining intervention employed in intensive care units worldwide to support or replace spontaneous breathing in critically ill patients. The primary objectives of mechanical ventilation include ensuring adequate gas exchange, minimizing the work of breathing, and preventing ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI). Over the past several decades, substantial research has demonstrated that inappropriate ventilator settings—particularly those involving high tidal volumes and excessive airway pressures—can exacerbate lung injury through mechanisms such as volutrauma (overdistension of alveoli), barotrauma (injury from high pressure), and atelectrauma (cyclic opening and closing of alveoli). In response, the concept of "lung-protective ventilation" has emerged as a cornerstone of modern critical care practice, emphasizing the use of low tidal volumes (typically 4–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight), limitation of plateau pressures, and application of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) to maintain alveolar patency.

Despite widespread adoption of these principles, the selection of optimal ventilator settings remains a complex and often subjective process that depends heavily on clinician experience, institutional protocols, and real-time interpretation of patient data. Human variability in decision-making can lead to deviations from recommended guidelines, especially in challenging clinical scenarios such as severe obstructive or restrictive lung disease. To address this inconsistency, manufacturers have developed automated ventilation modes that use computer algorithms to select and adjust ventilator parameters based on measured or estimated respiratory system characteristics. One such commercially available mode is Adaptive Support Ventilation (ASV), which employs an "optimum targeting scheme" designed to minimize the work rate of breathing that a patient would experience if breathing unassisted with the selected tidal volume and frequency. ASV calculates ventilator settings using inputs such as predicted body weight, percent minute ventilation, and estimates of respiratory system mechanics, including alveolar minute ventilation requirement, dead space volume, and expiratory time constant. While ASV has been shown to provide adequate ventilation across a variety of clinical conditions and includes embedded safety rules to mitigate risks such as hypoventilation, air trapping, and volutrauma, its primary optimization goal is not explicitly aligned with lung protection. Consequently, in certain pathophysiological states—particularly those involving obstructive lung disease—ASV may select settings that differ significantly from clinician preferences and potentially compromise lung-protective objectives.

Moreover, the reliance on human judgment introduces additional challenges. Even experienced clinicians may struggle to balance competing physiological demands, especially when managing patients with mixed or atypical respiratory pathologies. For instance, in status asthmaticus, the priority is to minimize dynamic hyperinflation by prolonging expiratory time, often at the expense of allowing permissive hypercapnia. In contrast, in ARDS, the focus is on minimizing tidal volume and plateau pressure to prevent alveolar overdistension. These divergent goals require nuanced adjustments to ventilator settings that are not always intuitive or consistently applied. Studies have shown considerable inter-clinician variability in initial ventilator setup, with some practitioners selecting tidal volumes outside the recommended lung-protective range or failing to adequately adjust respiratory rate in response to changes in dead space or compliance. This variability underscores the need for a more standardized, physiology-driven approach to ventilator management—one that systematically incorporates known principles of respiratory mechanics and gas exchange into the selection of ventilator parameters.

Existing automated modes, while useful, do not fully address this need. ASV, for example, bases its calculations on a model that minimizes the theoretical work of breathing, which may not always correspond to the lowest possible tidal volume or the most favorable distribution of lung volumes. Furthermore, ASV does not explicitly account for the relationship between frequency, tidal volume, and alveolar ventilation in a manner that prioritizes minimization of end-inspiratory volume—a key determinant of volutrauma. There remains a significant gap in the art for a ventilation mode that directly optimizes for lung protection by maximizing alveolar ventilation efficiency while simultaneously minimizing tidal volume and associated lung stress. Such a mode would need to integrate real-time or estimated patient-specific parameters—including dead space ratio, inspiratory and expiratory time constants, and alveolar minute ventilation requirement—into a mathematical model that identifies the combination of frequency and tidal volume yielding the highest alveolar ventilation for a given inspiratory pressure above PEEP. Critically, this mode must be implementable on conventional mechanical ventilators without requiring specialized hardware, thereby ensuring broad clinical applicability.

The development of such a system is further motivated by the increasing sophistication of ventilator technology and the growing demand for closed-loop systems that reduce cognitive load on clinicians while improving adherence to best practices. However, any new automated ventilation mode must also engender trust among healthcare providers, which requires transparency in its underlying logic, consistency in its outputs, and demonstrable superiority—or at least non-inferiority—in achieving clinically relevant outcomes compared to expert human judgment. Prior attempts to evaluate computerized ventilation strategies have often been limited by methodological constraints, such as the use of fixed comparator settings that do not reflect real-world clinical decision-making, or the absence of direct comparisons between algorithm-derived and clinician-selected parameters under identical physiological conditions. A robust validation framework is therefore essential to demonstrate the physiological rationale and practical benefits of any novel targeting scheme.

In summary, the background art reveals a clear need for an automated ventilation mode that transcends the limitations of current approaches by explicitly optimizing for lung protection through the dual objectives of maximizing alveolar ventilation and minimizing tidal volume. Such a mode must be grounded in sound respiratory physiology, adaptable to diverse clinical scenarios, and capable of delivering consistent, guideline-concordant settings without relying on variable human input. The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing Mid-Frequency Ventilation (MFV), a novel targeting scheme that leverages a mathematical model of pressure control ventilation to determine the optimal frequency and tidal volume for a given patient’s respiratory system characteristics and ventilation goals.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel mode of mechanical ventilation, termed Mid-Frequency Ventilation (MFV), which utilizes a computer-implemented targeting scheme to automatically select and deliver ventilator settings that maximize alveolar minute ventilation while minimizing tidal volume, thereby promoting lung protection. The invention is based on the recognition that for a given inspiratory pressure above PEEP, there exists a specific combination of respiratory frequency and tidal volume that yields the highest possible alveolar ventilation, and that this combination inherently results in lower tidal volumes and reduced end-inspiratory lung volumes compared to conventional or other automated ventilation strategies. The MFV mode operates within a pressure control continuous mandatory ventilation framework and determines optimal settings by integrating patient-specific physiological parameters—including alveolar minute ventilation requirement, dead space ratio, and inspiratory and expiratory time constants—into a mathematical model that identifies the frequency and tidal volume pair that maximizes alveolar ventilation efficiency.

In accordance with the invention, a mechanical ventilator is configured to receive or estimate the necessary patient parameters, either through direct input by a clinician, automatic measurement via integrated sensors, or derivation from standard clinical data such as arterial blood gases and predicted body weight. The ventilator then executes a computational algorithm that solves for the optimal respiratory rate and corresponding inspiratory time that, when combined with a fixed inspiratory pressure above PEEP, produces the target alveolar minute ventilation with the lowest feasible tidal volume. This optimization process ensures that the delivered ventilation is both efficient in terms of gas exchange and protective in terms of minimizing mechanical stress on the lungs. The resulting ventilator settings—characterized by higher frequencies in normal and restrictive lung conditions and appropriately adjusted frequencies in obstructive diseases—consistently achieve lower end-inspiratory volumes and reduced peak and mean airway pressures compared to both human-selected settings and those generated by existing adaptive modes such as Adaptive Support Ventilation (ASV).

The invention further encompasses a method for implementing MFV on a conventional mechanical ventilator capable of delivering pressure-controlled breaths with a sharply rectangular pressure waveform, which is essential for maintaining consistent inspiratory pressure throughout the breath cycle and thereby enabling accurate tidal volume delivery at high frequencies. The method includes the steps of: (a) determining the patient’s alveolar minute ventilation requirement based on clinical indicators such as arterial pH and PaCO₂; (b) estimating the dead space volume, typically as a fixed fraction of predicted body weight (e.g., 2.2 mL/kg); (c) obtaining or estimating the inspiratory and expiratory time constants of the respiratory system, which may be derived from measurements of resistance and compliance; (d) inputting these parameters into the MFV optimization algorithm; (e) calculating the optimal respiratory rate and inspiratory time that maximize alveolar ventilation for a specified inspiratory pressure above PEEP; and (f) configuring the ventilator to deliver pressure-controlled mandatory breaths at the calculated rate and inspiratory time. The PEEP level, while not calculated by the MFV algorithm itself, may be set separately by the clinician based on standard clinical considerations and is incorporated into the overall pressure profile.

A key advantage of the present invention is its ability to consistently apply lung-protective principles across a wide spectrum of respiratory pathologies without relying on variable human judgment. In restrictive disorders such as ARDS and morbid obesity, MFV selects higher respiratory rates and significantly lower tidal volumes than typical clinician choices, resulting in reduced end-inspiratory volumes and increased end-expiratory volumes—thereby mitigating both volutrauma and atelectrauma. In obstructive conditions like COPD and status asthmaticus, MFV adjusts frequency and inspiratory time to accommodate prolonged expiratory time constants, avoiding excessive air trapping while still minimizing tidal volume. Importantly, the invention achieves these benefits while maintaining equivalent or superior alveolar ventilation compared to alternative strategies, demonstrating that lung protection need not come at the cost of inadequate gas exchange.

The present invention also provides a system for comparing and validating automated ventilation strategies against human performance using a lung simulator programmed with realistic respiratory system characteristics. This system enables objective evaluation of ventilation outcomes—including tidal volume, lung volumes, airway pressures, and auto-PEEP—under controlled conditions, thereby facilitating the development and refinement of targeting schemes like MFV. By demonstrating that MFV consistently delivers lower tidal volumes and end-inspiratory volumes than both clinician-selected settings and ASV, while maintaining target alveolar ventilation, the invention establishes a new standard for automated, lung-protective mechanical ventilation. The disclosed mode is implementable on existing ventilator platforms with appropriate software modifications, making it readily translatable to clinical practice and offering a reliable, evidence-based alternative to manual ventilator management.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for mechanical ventilation that employs a novel targeting scheme—Mid-Frequency Ventilation (MFV)—to automatically determine and deliver ventilator settings that maximize alveolar minute ventilation while minimizing tidal volume, thereby promoting lung protection. The following detailed description sets forth the principles, components, and operational procedures of the invention, illustrating how it overcomes the limitations of prior art ventilation strategies and provides a consistent, physiology-driven approach to ventilator management.

At the core of the invention is a mathematical model that describes the relationship between ventilator settings, respiratory system mechanics, and alveolar ventilation. Alveolar minute ventilation (VA) is defined as the product of the effective alveolar tidal volume (VT − VD) and the respiratory rate (f), where VT is the total tidal volume and VD is the physiologic dead space volume. The goal of MFV is to maximize VA for a given inspiratory pressure above PEEP (ΔP), which constrains the maximum achievable tidal volume based on the compliance of the respiratory system. The model incorporates patient-specific parameters including the alveolar minute ventilation requirement (derived from clinical assessment of acid-base status), the dead space ratio (VD/VT or VD as a fixed volume), and the inspiratory and expiratory time constants (τ_insp and τ_exp), which are determined by the resistance (R) and compliance (C) of the respiratory system (τ = R × C). These parameters are used to solve for the optimal respiratory rate (f_opt) and corresponding inspiratory time (T_insp) that yield the highest possible VA without exceeding the ΔP limit.

The implementation of MFV begins with the acquisition of necessary patient data. The alveolar minute ventilation requirement is typically inferred from arterial blood gas analysis: for instance, a patient with respiratory acidosis (elevated PaCO₂ and low pH) requires a higher VA than one with normal gas exchange, while a patient with respiratory alkalosis (low PaCO₂ and high pH) may require a lower VA. The dead space volume (VD) is commonly estimated as 2.2 mL per kilogram of predicted body weight (PBW), a value supported by physiological literature and used consistently across clinical scenarios to isolate the effect of ventilator settings. Respiratory system resistance and compliance—which define the time constants—are either measured directly by the ventilator (if equipped with such capabilities) or estimated based on established values for specific disease states (e.g., low compliance and normal resistance in ARDS; high resistance and normal or increased compliance in COPD). These inputs are then fed into the MFV optimization algorithm.

The algorithm operates under the assumption of pressure control continuous mandatory ventilation (PC-CMV), wherein all breaths are time-triggered, pressure-limited, and time-cycled. In this mode, the inspiratory pressure (P_insp) is held constant above PEEP for a specified inspiratory time (T_insp), and the resulting tidal volume is determined by the respiratory system’s compliance and the duration of inspiration. The MFV algorithm calculates the T_insp that, for a given f, allows the inspiratory pressure to be maintained long enough to achieve the tidal volume required for the target VA, while respecting the constraint that the expiratory time (T_exp) must be sufficient to prevent air trapping (i.e., T_exp ≥ 3–5 × τ_exp in obstructive disease). Crucially, the algorithm identifies the f that maximizes VA, which mathematically occurs at a mid-range frequency—not too low (which would require large VT to meet VA, increasing volutrauma risk) and not too high (which would increase dead space ventilation and reduce efficiency). This “mid-frequency” optimum is dynamically adjusted based on the patient’s τ_insp and τ_exp, ensuring that the duty cycle (T_insp / [T_insp + T_exp]) remains appropriate for the underlying pathophysiology.

For example, in a patient with normal lungs (compliance ≈ 70 mL/cmH₂O, resistance ≈ 10 cmH₂O/L/s), the MFV algorithm might select a respiratory rate of 25–30 breaths per minute with a short T_insp (e.g., 0.8–1.0 seconds), resulting in a tidal volume of approximately 3.5–4.5 mL/kg PBW—significantly lower than the 6–8 mL/kg typically chosen by clinicians. In ARDS (compliance ≈ 30 mL/cmH₂O, resistance ≈ 15 cmH₂O/L/s), the algorithm increases the rate further (e.g., 35–40 breaths/min) to compensate for reduced compliance, yielding tidal volumes of 4–6 mL/kg while maintaining adequate VA. In obstructive disease such as COPD (compliance ≈ 80 mL/cmH₂O, resistance ≈ 25 cmH₂O/L/s), the algorithm reduces the rate (e.g., 10–14 breaths/min) and prolongs T_exp to allow complete exhalation, even if this means slightly higher tidal volumes, thereby avoiding dynamic hyperinflation. In all cases, the inspiratory pressure is set to the minimum necessary to achieve the target VT, which is inherently lower than that used in volume control or other pressure control modes due to the optimized frequency selection.

The ventilator hardware must be capable of delivering a sharply rectangular pressure waveform to ensure that the inspiratory pressure is maintained consistently throughout T_insp, especially at high frequencies. This requirement is met by certain commercial ventilators, such as the Dräger Evita XL, which features rapid pressure rise times and precise flow control. The circuit configuration uses standard adult ventilator tubing without humidification to minimize compressible volume effects, and all connections are leak-tested prior to use. Data from the ventilator—such as flow, pressure, and volume waveforms—are sampled at high frequency (e.g., 500 Hz) to accurately measure tidal and lung volumes, including end-inspiratory volume (EIV) and end-expiratory volume (EEV), which are critical indicators of lung stress and recruitment.

Validation of the MFV mode is performed using a lung simulator programmed with the resistance and compliance values corresponding to specific clinical scenarios. The simulator replicates passive respiratory mechanics, allowing direct comparison of ventilation outcomes between MFV, ASV, and clinician-selected settings under identical physiological conditions. In such studies, MFV consistently demonstrates lower tidal volumes (3.5–9.9 mL/kg vs. 6.1–8.3 mL/kg for clinicians) and reduced EIV (1–30% lower) across normal and restrictive pathologies, while maintaining equivalent alveolar ventilation. In obstructive disease, MFV avoids excessive air trapping by selecting appropriate rates and duty cycles, resulting in auto-PEEP levels only slightly higher than other modes (≈2 cmH₂O) but with significantly lower peak inspiratory pressures. Mean airway pressure is comparable across modes, indicating that oxygenation is not compromised.

The invention further includes a user interface that allows clinicians to input the target alveolar minute ventilation (either directly or via PaCO₂/pH targets) and PEEP level, while the MFV algorithm handles the calculation of rate, T_insp, and ΔP. Safety features may include upper limits on respiratory rate, minimum expiratory times, and alarms for excessive auto-PEEP or inadequate ventilation. The system is designed to operate in a closed-loop fashion, continuously adjusting settings if new patient data becomes available, though in its initial implementation, it is intended for use in passive, paralyzed patients where respiratory effort does not confound the model.

In summary, the detailed embodiment of the invention provides a robust, automated ventilation strategy that systematically applies lung-protective principles by optimizing the trade-off between frequency and tidal volume to maximize alveolar ventilation efficiency. By leveraging a physiology-based mathematical model and standard ventilator hardware, MFV offers a reliable, consistent alternative to manual setting selection, reducing variability and improving adherence to evidence-based practice in the management of mechanically ventilated patients.
