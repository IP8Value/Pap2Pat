# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of food science and analytical chemistry, specifically to methods for the authentication, differentiation, and quality assessment of animal-derived meat products based on their volatile organic compound (VOC) profiles. More particularly, the invention relates to a novel methodology for distinguishing between different strains or breeds of donkey meat through the rapid, non-destructive, and highly sensitive detection of strain-specific VOC biomarkers using gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS) coupled with multivariate statistical analysis. The invention further encompasses systems, kits, and analytical protocols designed to implement this methodology in commercial, regulatory, or research settings for the purposes of meat traceability, fraud prevention, quality control, and consumer protection. The technical domain integrates principles from analytical instrumentation, chemometrics, food chemistry, and bioinformatics to provide a robust, reproducible, and scalable solution for the characterization of raw meat based on its intrinsic volatile metabolome without requiring extensive sample preparation or destructive processing. This approach is especially relevant in the context of high-value specialty meats such as donkey meat, where economic incentives may drive mislabeling or substitution, and where sensory attributes—particularly flavor—are critical determinants of market value and consumer acceptance. The invention thus resides at the intersection of food authenticity testing, precision agriculture, and advanced analytical diagnostics, offering a scientifically grounded and technically feasible means to verify the genetic origin of donkey meat products through objective chemical fingerprinting.

## BACKGROUND

Meat flavor is a complex sensory attribute governed by a dynamic interplay of volatile and non-volatile compounds that collectively define the olfactory and gustatory experience of consumption. Among these, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are paramount, as they are directly responsible for the characteristic aromas perceived during eating and significantly influence consumer preference, product acceptability, and market value. In recent years, donkey meat has emerged as a premium protein source in China and other regions due to its favorable nutritional profile—being low in fat and calories while rich in high-quality protein and polyunsaturated fatty acids—and its distinctive taste, which has garnered increasing consumer interest. Indeed, donkey meat constitutes over 80% of the economic output of the donkey farming industry, underscoring its commercial significance and the need for reliable methods to ensure its authenticity and quality. However, the rising demand and premium pricing of donkey meat have also heightened the risk of adulteration, mislabeling, or substitution with cheaper alternatives, thereby necessitating robust analytical tools for verification.

Traditional methods for VOC analysis in meat, such as gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) and gas chromatography–olfactometry–mass spectrometry (GC–O–MS), have been widely employed to identify and quantify flavor compounds. These techniques, while highly sensitive and capable of detecting over 1,000 distinct VOCs across various chemical classes—including aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, esters, and carboxylic acids—are inherently limited by practical constraints. Specifically, they require labor-intensive sample pre-treatment procedures such as solvent extraction, distillation, or thermal desorption, which not only prolong analysis time but also risk altering the native VOC profile through degradation, oxidation, or loss of labile compounds. Moreover, the complexity of data interpretation and the need for skilled operators render these methods less suitable for routine, high-throughput screening in industrial or regulatory environments.

In contrast, gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS) has emerged as a promising alternative for rapid VOC profiling. GC–IMS combines the separation power of gas chromatography with the high sensitivity and selectivity of ion mobility spectrometry, enabling the detection of trace-level volatiles directly from headspace samples without any chemical pre-treatment. The technique operates under ambient pressure and utilizes soft ionization (typically via tritium β-radiation), preserving molecular integrity and allowing for the simultaneous measurement of both retention time and drift time—two orthogonal parameters that enhance compound identification. Furthermore, GC–IMS instruments are compact, relatively low-cost, and amenable to automation, making them ideal for deployment in slaughterhouses, processing facilities, or customs checkpoints where real-time meat authentication is required.

Despite these advantages, the application of GC–IMS to the authentication of donkey meat has remained unexplored until now. While prior studies have successfully used GC–IMS coupled with chemometric models to differentiate beef, mutton, and chicken with over 98% accuracy, and to discern variations in cured pork products based on breed or aging time, no such methodology exists for donkey meat. This gap is particularly significant given that donkey meat exhibits strain-dependent differences in sensory and compositional properties. For instance, SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) are two prominent strains of Dezhou donkeys in China, known to differ in growth characteristics and meat quality, yet no objective chemical markers have been established to reliably distinguish their meat products. Previous GC–MS analyses have identified aldehydes—particularly hexanal—as dominant VOCs in donkey meat, suggesting a lipid oxidation-driven flavor profile, but these findings pertain primarily to cooked samples and do not address raw meat differentiation between strains.

Compounding this challenge is the biological complexity underlying VOC formation. Volatiles in raw meat largely originate from enzymatic and oxidative degradation of lipids, especially polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), which yield aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols as primary oxidation products. The specific profile of these compounds is influenced by the animal’s genetics, diet, age, sex, and post-mortem handling—all of which can vary even within the same species. Recent lipidomic studies have revealed subtle but significant differences in glycerolipid and glycerophospholipid composition between SF and WT donkey meats, despite similar overall fatty acid profiles. These structural lipid differences may modulate the availability of free fatty acid precursors for oxidation, thereby shaping strain-specific VOC signatures. However, without a direct analytical link between these lipidomic variations and the resulting volatile metabolome, such hypotheses remain speculative.

Consequently, there exists a critical unmet need for a rapid, accurate, and non-invasive method to authenticate donkey meat by strain, leveraging its intrinsic VOC fingerprint. Such a method would not only support fair trade and consumer transparency but also enable breeders to validate premium claims associated with specific donkey strains. The integration of GC–IMS with advanced multivariate statistical techniques—such as principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA)—offers a powerful framework for this purpose, as these tools can extract meaningful patterns from high-dimensional spectral data, identify discriminatory biomarkers, and construct predictive classification models. Yet, to date, no study has systematically applied this integrated approach to donkey meat, nor has it established a validated panel of differential VOCs capable of serving as chemical barcodes for strain identification.

Thus, the background art lacks a comprehensive, standardized, and scientifically validated methodology for the strain-level authentication of donkey meat based on VOC profiling. Existing techniques are either too slow, too destructive, or insufficiently specific for practical implementation. The present invention addresses this deficiency by introducing a novel analytical workflow that combines GC–IMS with chemometrics to generate reproducible, interpretable, and actionable insights into the volatile signature of donkey meat, thereby enabling reliable differentiation between SF and WT strains and laying the foundation for broader applications in meat traceability and quality assurance.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and robust method for the authentication and differentiation of donkey meat derived from distinct genetic strains, specifically SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) donkeys, based on the comprehensive profiling of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) using gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS) integrated with multivariate statistical analysis. The invention overcomes the limitations of conventional analytical techniques by enabling rapid, non-destructive, and highly sensitive detection of strain-specific VOC biomarkers directly from raw meat samples without the need for chemical pre-treatment or extensive sample preparation. Central to the invention is the discovery that the VOC profiles of SF and WT donkey meats exhibit statistically significant and reproducible differences in the relative abundances of specific aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, and heterocyclic compounds, which collectively form a unique chemical fingerprint for each strain.

In one aspect, the invention discloses a method comprising the steps of: (a) obtaining a raw donkey meat sample; (b) analyzing the headspace volatiles of said sample using GC–IMS under defined instrumental conditions, including a capillary column (e.g., MXT-5, 15 m × 0.53 mm × 1.0 μm), carrier gas flow programming, drift tube temperature and voltage settings, and automated headspace sampling at controlled temperature and agitation; (c) generating a topographic plot and fingerprint spectrum representing the VOC profile of the sample; (d) processing the spectral data using chemometric software to perform unsupervised and supervised pattern recognition analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA); and (e) identifying the strain origin of the sample based on the presence, absence, or relative intensity of a panel of seventeen differential VOCs that serve as diagnostic biomarkers.

The seventeen differential VOCs include, but are not limited to: tetrahydrofuran, hexanal (monomer and dimer forms), pentan-1-ol (monomer and dimer), 3-octenal, oct-1-en-3-ol, pentanal (monomer and dimer), (E)-hept-2-enal, 2-butanol, 2-methyl-1-propanol, benzaldehyde, ethanol, isopropyl alcohol, acetone, and 2-pentanone (monomer). Of these, ten VOCs—namely 2-butanol, 2-methyl-1-propanol, benzaldehyde, ethanol, isopropyl alcohol, acetone, and 2-pentanone-m—are consistently found at significantly higher levels in SF donkey meat compared to WT, while seven VOCs—including tetrahydrofuran, hexanal-m, pentan-1-ol-d, pentan-1-ol-m, 3-octenal, oct-1-en-3-ol, pentanal-d, (E)-hept-2-enal, pentanal-m, and hexanal-d—are markedly elevated in WT donkey meat. The identification of these compounds is achieved through comparison of retention indices and drift times against reference standards and proprietary GC–IMS databases, ensuring high-confidence annotation.

In another aspect, the invention provides a system for implementing the aforementioned method, comprising a GC–IMS instrument equipped with an automated headspace sampler, a data processing unit loaded with chemometric analysis software (e.g., MetaboAnalyst 5.0 or equivalent), and a reference database containing the VOC fingerprints and biomarker profiles of authenticated SF and WT donkey meat samples. The system is configured to automatically classify unknown samples by comparing their VOC profiles against the reference database using validated OPLS-DA models, thereby delivering a strain assignment with high accuracy and minimal operator intervention.

Additionally, the invention encompasses a kit for donkey meat authentication, which includes calibration standards for the seventeen differential VOCs, protocol guidelines for sample handling and GC–IMS operation, and software modules for real-time data interpretation and reporting. The kit is designed for use in commercial laboratories, regulatory agencies, or meat processing facilities to support quality control, anti-fraud measures, and supply chain transparency.

The invention further provides a method for verifying the authenticity of donkey meat products in commerce by applying the disclosed VOC profiling technique to detect mislabeling or adulteration. By establishing a clear chemical distinction between SF and WT strains—two economically significant varieties—the invention enables stakeholders to enforce labeling standards, protect brand integrity, and ensure consumer confidence in premium donkey meat products.

Overall, the invention represents a significant advancement in food authentication technology by delivering a rapid, reliable, and scientifically rigorous solution for strain-level differentiation of donkey meat. It leverages the synergy between cutting-edge analytical instrumentation and advanced data analytics to transform complex VOC data into actionable intelligence, thereby addressing a critical gap in the current food safety and traceability landscape.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is described in detail below with reference to specific embodiments, which are provided for illustrative purposes and are not intended to limit the scope of the claims. It will be understood by those skilled in the art that various modifications, substitutions, and equivalents may be made without departing from the spirit and scope of the invention.

In accordance with the invention, a method for differentiating donkey meat from SanFen (SF) and WuTou (WT) strains is implemented through a standardized analytical workflow that begins with sample collection and culminates in strain classification based on volatile organic compound (VOC) biomarkers. The method is predicated on the empirical observation that raw donkey meat from these two strains exhibits distinct VOC profiles that can be reproducibly detected, quantified, and interpreted using gas chromatography–ion mobility spectrometry (GC–IMS) coupled with multivariate statistical modeling.

The first step involves the procurement of fresh donkey meat samples. Preferably, the longissimus dorsi muscle is excised from animals slaughtered under standardized conditions to minimize confounding variables related to stress, diet, or post-mortem handling. Animals should be of comparable age (e.g., two years), sex-balanced (equal numbers of males and females), and raised under identical environmental and nutritional regimes to isolate genetic effects on VOC composition. Immediately after slaughter, muscle tissue is flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C to preserve the native volatile profile until analysis. This cryogenic preservation prevents microbial spoilage and enzymatic degradation that could otherwise alter VOC concentrations.

For GC–IMS analysis, a representative subsample (typically 1.5 grams) is transferred to a 20-mL headspace vial and equilibrated at 60°C for 15 minutes under continuous agitation at 500 rpm. This incubation facilitates the release of volatiles into the headspace without inducing thermal degradation, as the temperature remains well below the boiling points of most target compounds. An automated headspace sampler then injects 500 µL of the vapor phase into the GC–IMS instrument. The GC column, preferably a mid-polarity MXT-5 capillary column (15 m length, 0.53 mm internal diameter, 1.0 µm film thickness), provides adequate separation of complex VOC mixtures based on volatility and polarity. The carrier gas, ultra-high-purity nitrogen (≥99.999%), is delivered according to a programmed flow rate: 2 mL/min for the first 2 minutes, ramped to 20 mL/min between 2–10 minutes, and further increased to 100 mL/min from 10–20 minutes. This gradient optimizes resolution while minimizing analysis time.

The ion mobility spectrometer operates in positive ion mode using tritium (³H) ionization, which generates protonated molecular ions ([M+H]⁺) with minimal fragmentation. The drift tube, maintained at 60°C, and the IMS chamber at 45°C, ensure stable ion transmission. A constant electric field of 5 kV is applied across the 9.8-cm drift region, and purified nitrogen serves as the drift gas at 150 mL/min. Under these conditions, ions are separated based on their collision cross-section with the buffer gas, yielding a second dimension of separation orthogonal to GC retention time. The resulting data are rendered as a three-dimensional topographic plot (intensity vs. retention time vs. drift time) and a two-dimensional fingerprint map where each peak corresponds to a specific VOC or its monomer/dimer forms.

VOC identification is performed by matching experimental retention indices (RIs) and drift times (DTs) against those of authentic standards and entries in the NIST 2014 mass spectral library and the manufacturer’s GC–IMS compound database. RI calibration is achieved using a homologous series of C4–C9 n-ketones analyzed under identical conditions. Compounds are reported with suffixes “-d” and “-m” to denote dimeric and monomeric species, respectively, which commonly arise due to clustering with water or other polar molecules in the ionization region.

Following data acquisition, spectral preprocessing is conducted using dedicated software plugins (e.g., Reporter and Gallery Plot from G.A.S.). Peak alignment, baseline correction, and normalization are applied to ensure comparability across samples. The processed dataset is then subjected to multivariate statistical analysis using platforms such as MetaboAnalyst 5.0. Principal component analysis (PCA) is first employed as an unsupervised method to visualize inherent clustering among samples and assess technical reproducibility. Samples from SF and WT strains typically segregate along principal components 1 and 2, confirming systematic differences in VOC composition.

To enhance discrimination and identify predictive features, supervised models including PLS-DA and OPLS-DA are constructed. OPLS-DA, in particular, separates predictive variation (related to strain) from orthogonal (unrelated) variation, improving model interpretability. Model validity is confirmed via permutation testing: if the Q² intercept of the regression line is negative and all permuted Q² values fall below the original, overfitting is ruled out. In the disclosed embodiment, the OPLS-DA model achieves excellent separation with R² = 0.71 and Q² = −0.26, indicating robustness.

Differential VOCs are selected based on two criteria: variable importance in projection (VIP) score >1 and statistical significance (p < 0.05, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test). This dual-filter approach yields a refined biomarker panel of seventeen compounds. Heatmap analysis further validates these findings by illustrating consistent upregulation or downregulation patterns across biological replicates.

The final step involves strain classification. An unknown sample is analyzed under the same protocol, and its VOC profile is projected onto the validated OPLS-DA model. If its coordinates fall within the SF cluster, it is classified as SanFen; if within the WT cluster, as WuTou. Alternatively, a decision rule based on the ratio of key biomarkers (e.g., ethanol/hexanal) may be implemented for simpler binary classification.

The invention also contemplates the development of a portable or benchtop GC–IMS system integrated with cloud-based analytics for field deployment. Such a system would allow real-time authentication at points of sale or import inspection. Furthermore, the biomarker panel may be expanded to include additional donkey breeds or crossbred populations as more data become available.

In summary, the detailed embodiments of the invention provide a complete, end-to-end solution for donkey meat strain authentication that is rapid (analysis time <30 minutes per sample), non-destructive, highly accurate, and suitable for routine use in both research and commercial settings. By anchoring classification on objective chemical evidence rather than subjective sensory evaluation, the invention sets a new standard for meat traceability and quality assurance in the emerging donkey meat industry.
