# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of spintronics has undergone a transformative evolution in recent decades, driven by the discovery and exploitation of chiral magnetic textures that arise from antisymmetric exchange interactions. Among these interactions, the Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI) stands as a cornerstone phenomenon enabling the stabilization of noncollinear spin structures such as magnetic skyrmions, spin spirals, and chiral domain walls. Originally proposed by Dzyaloshinskii and later formalized microscopically by Moriya, the DMI emerges exclusively in systems lacking inversion symmetry and is fundamentally rooted in the interplay between spin-orbit coupling and broken spatial symmetry. In bulk materials, this condition is satisfied by noncentrosymmetric crystal structures, as exemplified by the B20-type transition metal silicides and germanides, which host helical spin orders and skyrmion lattices. However, in thin-film and multilayer architectures—central to modern spintronic device engineering—the requisite symmetry breaking occurs naturally at interfaces, where the structural inversion asymmetry along the surface normal direction gives rise to interfacial DMI.

This interfacial DMI has become a focal point of both fundamental research and technological development due to its ability to dictate the chirality and stability of magnetic domain walls and topological spin textures. The energy associated with the DMI is expressed as −**D**<sub>ij</sub> ⋅ (**S**<sub>i</sub> × **S**<sub>j</sub>), where **D**<sub>ij</sub> is the DMI vector and **S**<sub>i</sub>, **S**<sub>j</sub> are localized spin moments. The sign of **D**<sub>ij</sub> determines whether the resulting spin texture is right-handed or left-handed, while its magnitude governs the characteristic length scale of the texture, such as the diameter of a skyrmion or the width of a domain wall. Consequently, precise control over the DMI—both in sign and strength—is essential for designing functional spin-orbitronic devices that rely on the deterministic manipulation of chiral magnetism.

Historically, experimental efforts to engineer strong interfacial DMI have concentrated on coupling ferromagnetic layers (typically 3d transition metals like Co or Fe) with adjacent heavy metals possessing large atomic numbers, such as Pt, Pd, Ir, Ta, or W. These systems operate within the framework of the Fert-Levy model, wherein the DMI arises from hybridization between the 3d orbitals of the ferromagnet and the 5d orbitals of the heavy metal near the Fermi level. The strength of this interaction correlates strongly with the spin-orbit coupling constant of the heavy metal, leading to significant DMI values in systems like Co/Pt or Co/Ir. More recently, attention has expanded to include metal/oxide interfaces, where voltage-tunable DMI has been observed and attributed to Rashba-type electric fields induced by charge redistribution at the interface. These findings have opened avenues for electric-field control of magnetic chirality, a critical requirement for low-power memory and logic applications.

Despite these advances, a fundamental gap remains in understanding the role of light elements—particularly those with low atomic numbers—in generating DMI. Conventional wisdom suggests that such elements, lacking strong spin-orbit coupling, should contribute negligibly to the DMI. However, theoretical predictions have challenged this assumption, proposing that chemisorbed species with high electronegativity, such as oxygen, can induce substantial DMI through mechanisms distinct from heavy-metal hybridization. Specifically, the large electronegativity difference between oxygen (Pauling electronegativity χ = 3.44) and 3d ferromagnets (χ ≈ 1.90) drives significant charge transfer upon chemisorption, creating a strong internal electric field and a surface dipole moment. This electrostatic asymmetry, combined with orbital hybridization between oxygen’s p-orbitals and the metal’s d-orbitals, can generate a sizable DMI even in the absence of heavy elements. Recent theoretical work has further linked the magnitude of the DMI to the electric dipole moment at the interface, suggesting that adsorbates capable of inducing large dipoles may serve as potent sources of chiral interactions.

Experimental validation of this hypothesis has been limited. While hydrogenation-induced skyrmion formation in Fe/Ir(111) and DMI at the Co/graphene interface have been reported, direct evidence of oxygen-induced DMI at room temperature in well-defined ultrathin film systems has remained elusive. Moreover, the quantitative comparison of oxygen-induced DMI with conventional heavy-metal interfaces has not been systematically established. This knowledge gap is particularly significant given the potential implications for sustainable spintronics: oxygen is abundant, inexpensive, and compatible with existing semiconductor fabrication processes, making it an attractive alternative to scarce and costly noble metals like Pt or Ir.

Prior art in magnetic imaging and DMI quantification has relied heavily on techniques such as spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy (SPLEEM), scanning electron microscopy with polarization analysis (SEMPA), and Brillouin light scattering. These methods enable direct visualization of domain wall chirality and allow for the extraction of DMI strength through thickness-dependent studies of magnetic anisotropy and spin texture transitions. A key strategy involves identifying the critical thickness at which domain walls transition from chiral Néel to achiral Bloch configurations—a signature directly tied to the competition between DMI and dipolar interactions. By calibrating known DMI contributions in multilayer stacks and then introducing an unknown interface, researchers can infer the DMI of the new interface by observing shifts in the chirality transition point. This comparative approach has successfully quantified DMI at interfaces such as graphene/Co and Ru/Co.

However, existing platforms lack the tunability required to unambiguously determine the sign and magnitude of weak or competing DMI contributions. Most systems exhibit a fixed DMI handedness dictated by a single dominant interface, limiting their utility in probing subtle interfacial effects. There is thus a pressing need for a versatile, tunable platform wherein the effective DMI can be continuously adjusted across zero—from left-handed to right-handed—enabling clear discrimination of additional DMI contributions from adsorbates or capping layers. Such a platform would not only facilitate the discovery of new DMI sources but also provide a robust methodology for quantifying their strength relative to established benchmarks.

In summary, while the foundational principles of interfacial DMI are well established, the exploration of light-element-induced DMI—particularly from chemisorbed oxygen—remains underexplored experimentally. The absence of a tunable, calibrated system for measuring small DMI contributions has hindered progress in this area. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel multilayer architecture that enables precise control over the effective DMI through spacer layer thickness, thereby providing a sensitive probe for quantifying the DMI induced by atomic oxygen chemisorption at room temperature. This approach not only validates theoretical predictions regarding electronegativity-driven DMI but also opens new pathways for designing heavy-metal-free chiral spintronic devices.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for inducing, controlling, and quantifying a large Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI) at room temperature through the chemisorption of atomic oxygen onto a ferromagnetic surface, without reliance on heavy metals or external electric fields. Central to this invention is a tunable multilayer platform comprising a tungsten substrate, a palladium spacer layer of precisely controlled thickness, and a perpendicularly magnetized Ni/Co bilayer, wherein the effective DMI of the oxygen-free stack can be continuously adjusted from right-handed (W-like) to left-handed (Pd-like) by varying the Pd thickness. This tunability enables the unambiguous detection and quantification of the DMI contribution arising from chemisorbed oxygen, which is found to be sufficiently strong to reverse the chirality of magnetic domain walls and tailor the internal structure of magnetic skyrmions.

The invention discloses that chemisorbed oxygen, when adsorbed in sub-monolayer coverage (up to 0.29 monolayers) on the Ni surface of the Ni/Co/Pd/W(110) multilayer, induces a right-handed DMI of magnitude approximately 0.63 ± 0.26 meV/atom at full monolayer coverage. This value is comparable to DMI strengths observed at conventional ferromagnet/heavy metal interfaces such as Co/Ir or Ni/W, and significantly exceeds that of other light-element interfaces like graphene/Co. The oxygen-induced DMI originates from the strong electronegativity difference between oxygen and the 3d ferromagnet, leading to charge transfer, p-d orbital hybridization, and the formation of a surface electric dipole moment, consistent with recent theoretical predictions linking DMI to interfacial dipole fields.

The invention further demonstrates that the oxygen-induced DMI can be used to deterministically control the chirality of magnetic textures. In systems initially exhibiting left-handed domain walls due to a thick Pd spacer, incremental oxygen exposure drives a complete chirality transition to right-handed Néel walls via an intermediate achiral Bloch state. Similarly, in isotropic [Co/Ni]<sub>3</sub>/Cu(111) films, oxygen chemisorption transforms left-handed hedgehog skyrmions into Bloch-type configurations, illustrating the capacity to engineer the winding number and domain wall type of topological spin textures at room temperature. These effects are reversible and controllable through oxygen dosing, offering a pathway for dynamic chirality manipulation.

A key aspect of the invention is the use of spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy (SPLEEM) to directly image and quantify domain wall chirality as a function of both Pd spacer thickness and oxygen coverage. By mapping the two-dimensional parameter space of Pd thickness (d<sub>Pd</sub>) and oxygen coverage (d<sub>O</sub>), the invention establishes a calibration curve that allows the oxygen-induced DMI to be extracted from the shift in the chirality transition boundary. This methodology provides a generalizable framework for quantifying unknown DMI contributions at any new interface added to a tunable DMI platform.

The invention also extends to applications in spintronics and magneto-ionics, wherein the oxygen-induced DMI can stabilize chiral magnetic order in ultrathin films up to approximately 3 nm thick, sufficient for practical device integration. Furthermore, the compatibility of oxygen chemisorption with voltage-controlled ion migration suggests synergies with emerging magneto-ionic technologies, where electric fields could modulate oxygen coverage and thereby dynamically tune magnetic chirality. The use of earth-abundant oxygen as a DMI source offers a sustainable and cost-effective alternative to rare noble metals, enhancing the scalability and environmental profile of chiral spintronic devices.

In summary, the invention comprises: (1) a tunable multilayer platform for DMI engineering; (2) a method for inducing large, controllable DMI via oxygen chemisorption; (3) a quantitative technique for measuring adsorbate-induced DMI using SPLEEM and thickness-dependent chirality analysis; and (4) applications in chirality-controlled magnetic memory, logic, and neuromorphic computing devices that leverage the oxygen-mediated DMI for heavy-metal-free operation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a novel method and apparatus for generating, controlling, and utilizing a large Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI) at room temperature through the chemisorption of atomic oxygen on a ferromagnetic surface. This approach leverages the intrinsic properties of oxygen—specifically its high electronegativity and ability to form strong chemical bonds with 3d transition metals—to create a robust interfacial DMI without requiring heavy elements or complex heterostructures. The invention is implemented using a carefully engineered multilayer stack that provides a tunable baseline DMI, enabling precise quantification and application of the oxygen-induced effect.

The core of the invention resides in a multilayer structure grown epitaxially on a W(110) single-crystal substrate under ultrahigh vacuum (UHV) conditions. The stack consists of a Pd spacer layer of variable thickness (d<sub>Pd</sub>), followed by a Ni/Co bilayer that exhibits perpendicular magnetic anisotropy (PMA). The W(110) substrate serves a dual purpose: it provides a template for high-quality, layer-by-layer growth of the overlying films, and it contributes a right-handed DMI due to the broken inversion symmetry at the W/Co interface. The Pd spacer layer, when grown on W(11), forms a pseudomorphic fcc film with excellent crystallinity, as confirmed by low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) and real-time intensity oscillations during deposition. Critically, Pd itself induces a left-handed DMI at the Pd/Co interface, opposite in sign to that of W. By varying d<sub>Pd</sub> from sub-monolayer to several monolayers, the effective DMI of the entire stack—arising from the superposition of W-like and Pd-like contributions—can be tuned continuously from right-handed to left-handed, passing through a chirality-neutral point where the net DMI vanishes. This tunability is experimentally verified using spin-polarized low-energy electron microscopy (SPLEEM), which directly images the chirality of magnetic domain walls in the Ni/Co layer.

Domain walls in perpendicularly magnetized films adopt either Néel or Bloch configurations depending on the balance between DMI and dipolar interactions. In the absence of DMI, dipolar forces favor Bloch walls, where the magnetization rotates in the plane of the film. When DMI is present, it stabilizes chiral Néel walls, with the sense of rotation (clockwise or counterclockwise) determined by the sign of the DMI vector. In the Ni/Co/Pd/W(110) system, the uniaxial magnetic anisotropy induced by epitaxial strain aligns domain walls along the W[001] direction, simplifying the analysis of chirality. SPLEEM measurements resolve the three-dimensional magnetization vector (**M**<sub>x</sub>, **M**<sub>y</sub>, **M**<sub>z</sub>) by acquiring images with the incident electron beam polarized along orthogonal axes. From these, the angle α between the domain wall magnetization vector and the wall normal is extracted, with α = 0° and 180° corresponding to right- and left-handed Néel walls, respectively. Histograms of α reveal sharp peaks whose position shifts with d<sub>Pd</sub>, confirming the sign reversal of the effective DMI near d<sub>Pd</sub> = 2.5 monolayers (ML).

This tunable platform is then employed to investigate the DMI induced by chemisorbed oxygen. High-purity oxygen is introduced into the UHV chamber via controlled dosing, resulting in atomic oxygen adsorption on the Ni surface without oxide formation, as verified by the absence of NiO-related LEED spots and x-ray photoemission signatures. Oxygen coverage (d<sub>O</sub>) is calibrated using established kinetics models and corroborated by work function measurements, which show a linear increase of ~0.7 eV with coverage up to 0.29 ML—consistent with the formation of a surface dipole due to charge transfer from Ni to O. SPLEEM imaging of the same sample before and after oxygen exposure reveals a systematic shift in domain wall chirality toward the right-handed configuration. Notably, in samples with thick Pd spacers (e.g., d<sub>Pd</sub> = 2.83 ML), where the bare stack exhibits strong left-handed chirality, oxygen exposure drives a complete transition to right-handed Néel walls at d<sub>O</sub> = 0.24 ML. In contrast, samples with thin Pd spacers (d<sub>Pd</sub> < 2.0 ML), which are inherently right-handed, show no chirality reversal upon oxygen adsorption, confirming that the oxygen-induced DMI is consistently right-handed.

To quantify the oxygen-induced DMI, the invention exploits the linear relationship between DMI strength and Pd thickness in the vicinity of the chirality transition. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations confirm that the DMI varies smoothly with d<sub>Pd</sub>, allowing interpolation between discrete thicknesses. The DMI change ΔD associated with a Pd thickness increment of 1 ML is determined to be 0.41 ± 0.17 meV/atom from independent measurements of the Co/Pd/W system. Experimentally, it is found that adding 0.24 ML of oxygen produces the same chirality shift as increasing d<sub>Pd</sub> by 0.37 ML. Therefore, the DMI per monolayer of oxygen is calculated as (0.41 ± 0.17 meV/atom × 0.37) / 0.24 = 0.63 ± 0.26 meV/atom. This value is validated by cross-comparison with other systems: in a [Co/Ni]<sub>3</sub>/Cu(111) film—which lacks heavy metals and exhibits left-handed chirality due to intrinsic effects—0.21 ML of oxygen induces a transition to achiral Bloch walls, consistent with the extracted DMI magnitude.

The invention further demonstrates the utility of oxygen-induced DMI in tailoring topological spin textures. In the Ni/Co/Pd/W(110) system, magnetic bubbles (cylindrical domains with reversed magnetization) undergo a continuous evolution from left-handed to right-handed chirality as oxygen coverage increases, with intermediate states exhibiting mixed or achiral character. In isotropic [Co/Ni]<sub>3</sub>/Cu(111) films, where skyrmions form spontaneously, oxygen chemisorption transforms left-handed hedgehog skyrmions into Bloch-type configurations, effectively altering their topological winding number and current-driven dynamics. These results establish oxygen as a powerful tool for engineering the internal structure of magnetic solitons at room temperature.

The physical origin of the oxygen-induced DMI is attributed to the strong electronegativity difference between O and Ni/Co, which drives electron transfer from the metal to oxygen, creating a surface dipole moment. This dipole generates an effective Rashba-like electric field that, combined with p-d orbital hybridization, breaks inversion symmetry and activates the DMI mechanism. The observation of a large work function change upon oxygen adsorption provides direct experimental evidence for this dipole, supporting recent theoretical models that link DMI to interfacial electric fields.

From a practical standpoint, the invention enables the design of heavy-metal-free spintronic devices that rely on chiral magnetism. The oxygen-induced DMI is sufficient to stabilize chiral domain walls in films up to ~3 nm thick, encompassing typical [Co/Ni] multilayer stacks used in magnetic tunnel junctions. Moreover, because oxygen coverage can be modulated via gas dosing or, in solid-state devices, through voltage-driven ion migration, the DMI can be dynamically tuned in operando. This capability is particularly valuable for reconfigurable logic, neuromorphic computing, and racetrack memory applications where chirality control dictates device functionality.

In conclusion, the detailed embodiment of the invention encompasses: (1) the fabrication of a W(110)-based multilayer with tunable DMI via Pd thickness control; (2) the chemisorption of atomic oxygen under UHV to induce a right-handed DMI; (3) the use of SPLEEM for real-space, vectorial magnetization imaging and chirality quantification; (4) the calibration of oxygen-induced DMI through comparative thickness analysis; and (5) the application of this effect to manipulate skyrmion and domain wall chirality for next-generation spintronic devices. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in DMI engineering, replacing scarce heavy metals with abundant, tunable oxygen chemistry to achieve robust chiral magnetic control at room temperature.
