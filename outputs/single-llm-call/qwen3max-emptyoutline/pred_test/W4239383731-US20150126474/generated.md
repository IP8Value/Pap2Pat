## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of anti-virulence therapeutics for bacterial pathogens, and more specifically, to small-molecule compounds that modulate quorum sensing (QS) pathways in Gram-negative bacteria of the genus *Vibrio*. In particular, the invention provides novel chemical entities that function as inhibitors of LuxO, a central response regulator in the QS circuitry of pathogenic vibrios, thereby activating the high cell density (HCD) state of quorum sensing and repressing virulence factor expression. The invention further encompasses methods of using these compounds to treat or prevent infections caused by *Vibrio cholerae*, *Vibrio parahaemolyticus*, *Vibrio vulnificus*, and related species, as well as their use as research tools for probing the molecular mechanisms of NtrC-family response regulators and AAA+ ATPase activity in bacterial signal transduction.

## BACKGROUND

Quorum sensing (QS) is a conserved mechanism of bacterial cell-to-cell communication that enables populations of bacteria to coordinate gene expression in response to changes in cell density and community composition. This process relies on the synthesis, release, detection, and group-wide response to extracellular signaling molecules known as autoinducers. In many clinically relevant bacterial pathogens, QS governs the expression of collective behaviors such as biofilm formation, sporulation, bioluminescence, and—critically—the production of virulence factors. Traditional antibiotic therapies exert strong selective pressure that drives the rapid emergence of resistance; in contrast, anti-virulence strategies that disarm pathogens without inhibiting growth are hypothesized to impose reduced evolutionary pressure for resistance development. Consequently, interference with QS pathways has emerged as a promising alternative therapeutic paradigm.

Gram-negative bacteria commonly employ acyl-homoserine lactones (HSLs) as autoinducers, which are detected by cytoplasmic LuxR-type or membrane-bound LuxN-type receptors. While significant progress has been made in developing antagonists of HSL-based QS systems, numerous globally significant pathogens—including those in the genus *Vibrio*—utilize non-HSL autoinducers and distinct signaling architectures. *Vibrio cholerae*, the causative agent of cholera, employs a sophisticated QS system that integrates signals from two autoinducers: CAI-1 ((S)-3-hydroxytridecan-4-one), synthesized by CqsA, and AI-2 ((2S,4S)-2-methyl-2,3,3,4-tetrahydroxytetrahydrofuran borate), produced by LuxS. These molecules are detected by the transmembrane histidine kinase receptors CqsS and LuxPQ, respectively. At low cell density (LCD), in the absence of sufficient autoinducer, both receptors function predominantly as kinases, leading to phosphorylation of the response regulator LuxO. Phosphorylated LuxO (LuxO∼P) activates transcription of four genes encoding small regulatory RNAs (Qrr1–4). These sRNAs, in concert with the RNA chaperone Hfq, post-transcriptionally repress the master QS regulator HapR while stabilizing the mRNA of another regulator, AphA. As a result, AphA levels are high and HapR levels are low at LCD, promoting expression of virulence genes required for intestinal colonization, including those encoding cholera toxin (CTX) and the toxin co-regulated pilus (TCP).

At high cell density (HCD), autoinducer accumulation triggers a switch in receptor activity from kinase to phosphatase mode, reversing phosphate flow through the pathway and resulting in dephosphorylation and inactivation of LuxO. Consequently, Qrr sRNA transcription ceases, allowing derepression of *hapR* translation and degradation of *aphA* mRNA. Elevated HapR then represses virulence factor production and biofilm formation, facilitating bacterial dissemination via diarrhea into the aquatic environment. This inverse regulation—where virulence is maximal at LCD and repressed at HCD—is characteristic of acute pathogens like *V. cholerae* and contrasts with chronic pathogens that activate virulence at HCD. Therefore, pharmacological activation of QS in *V. cholerae* represents a rational anti-virulence strategy: inducing the HCD state prematurely would suppress virulence gene expression during early infection, potentially preventing disease establishment.

LuxO belongs to the NtrC family of σ⁵⁴-dependent transcriptional activators, which are characterized by an N-terminal receiver domain, a central AAA+ ATPase domain, and a C-terminal DNA-binding domain. Upon phosphorylation, NtrC-type proteins oligomerize and use energy from ATP hydrolysis to catalyze the formation of open transcriptional complexes at σ⁵⁴-dependent promoters. Despite the critical role of NtrC-family proteins in bacterial physiology—including control of nitrogen metabolism, motility, and virulence—no small-molecule modulators of their ATPase activity had been reported prior to this work. Although inhibitors targeting other classes of response regulators (e.g., WalR, DevR, AlgR1) have been described, these typically interfere with phosphorylation or DNA binding, not ATP hydrolysis. Moreover, while histidine kinase inhibitors exist (e.g., LED209 targeting QseC), they often suffer from poor selectivity, membrane toxicity, or limited cell permeability due to their targeting of conserved hydrophobic kinase domains.

Given the conservation of LuxO across pathogenic *Vibrio* species—including *V. parahaemolyticus*, *V. vulnificus*, and *V. alginolyticus*—a compound that inhibits LuxO could serve as a broad-spectrum anti-virulence agent against multiple vibriosis-causing pathogens. Importantly, because these species utilize diverse autoinducers (including HSLs in some cases), receptor-targeted agonists would likely be species-specific. In contrast, targeting the highly conserved downstream component LuxO offers a unified strategy to activate QS regardless of upstream signal diversity. Thus, there exists a compelling need for small molecules that selectively inhibit LuxO ATPase activity, thereby inducing premature HCD responses and attenuating virulence in a broad range of *Vibrio* pathogens.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel small-molecule compounds that activate quorum sensing in *Vibrio* species by specifically inhibiting the ATPase activity of LuxO, a central NtrC-family response regulator in the QS signal transduction cascade. These compounds, herein referred to as Class 2 QS-activating compounds, are characterized by a conserved 5-thio-6-azauracil core structure with variable hydrophobic side chains. The most potent exemplars include compound 11 and compound 12, wherein compound 12 features a tert-butyl carbinol side chain that confers enhanced activity relative to compound 11.

The invention further provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising a therapeutically effective amount of one or more of these LuxO inhibitors in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient. Such compositions are useful for the prevention or treatment of infections caused by *Vibrio* species, including but not limited to *V. cholerae*, *V. parahaemolyticus*, *V. vulnificus*, and *V. alginolyticus*. Administration of these compounds induces the high cell density state of quorum sensing, resulting in derepression of HapR (or its orthologs such as OpaR or SmcR), which in turn represses the expression of key virulence determinants such as cholera toxin, TCP, and Type III Secretion System (TTSS) effectors.

Additionally, the invention encompasses methods of using these compounds as research tools to study NtrC-family response regulator function, AAA+ ATPase mechanisms, and quorum sensing signal transduction. The compounds act via an uncompetitive inhibition mechanism, binding to the pre-formed LuxO–ATP complex and reducing both the apparent Michaelis constant (Kₘ) and maximum velocity (Vₘₐₓ) of ATP hydrolysis. Genetic resistance studies have identified key residues (I211, L215, L242, V294 in *V. cholerae* LuxO) that are critical for inhibitor binding; these map to regions adjacent to the Walker B motif and the GAFTGA domain, suggesting an allosteric mechanism that perturbs the catalytic conformation of the ATPase site without affecting ATP binding or DNA affinity.

The compounds exhibit favorable drug-like properties, including water solubility, cellular permeability, metabolic stability, and low cytotoxicity toward mammalian cells (no detectable toxicity at concentrations up to 500 µM). They demonstrate broad-spectrum activity across multiple *Vibrio* species, activating QS phenotypes such as bioluminescence in *V. harveyi* and colony opacity in *V. parahaemolyticus*, and significantly reducing virulence factor production and host cell cytotoxicity in vitro and ex vivo. Notably, these inhibitors do not substantially affect the ATPase activity of non-*Vibrio* NtrC homologs such as *E. coli* NtrC, indicating a degree of selectivity that may mitigate off-target effects.

In summary, the invention discloses a new class of anti-virulence agents that exploit the unique logic of *Vibrio* quorum sensing to repress pathogenesis, offering a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics with potential for reduced resistance development.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Materials and Methods

The compounds of the present invention were identified through a whole-cell high-throughput screening campaign employing genetically engineered *Vibrio cholerae* reporter strains locked in the low cell density (LCD) state. Two primary screening strains were utilized: (1) a Δ*cqsA* Δ*luxS* double synthase knockout strain incapable of producing endogenous autoinducers, thereby maintaining constitutive kinase activity of CqsS and LuxPQ receptors and persistent phosphorylation of LuxO; and (2) a strain harboring the *luxO* D47E allele, which mimics the phosphorylated, constitutively active form of LuxO independent of upstream signaling. Both strains carry a heterologous *lux* operon from *Vibrio harveyi* under the control of the native *hapR* promoter, rendering them non-luminescent under standard conditions due to repression of HapR. Small molecules capable of inducing bioluminescence in these strains were classified based on their activity profile: Class 1 compounds activated only the synthase-deficient strain (indicating receptor agonism), while Class 2 compounds activated both strains, implicating a target downstream of the receptors.

Bioluminescence assays were conducted in 384-well microtiter plates. Overnight cultures of reporter strains were diluted 20- to 25-fold in appropriate media (LB for *V. cholerae*, LM for *V. harveyi*) and dispensed into plates containing test compounds at a final concentration of approximately 20 µM. After incubation at 30°C for 4–6 hours under static or shaking conditions as specified, luminescence and optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) were measured using a multilabel plate reader. Dose-response curves were generated by testing serial dilutions of hit compounds, and EC₅₀ values were calculated using nonlinear regression analysis.

For protein purification, the *luxO* D47E open reading frame from *V. cholerae* was cloned into the pET28b expression vector, introducing an N-terminal hexahistidine tag. The construct was transformed into *Escherichia coli* BL21 Gold (DE3) cells. Protein expression was induced with 200 µM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at an OD₆₀₀ of ~1.0, followed by 4 hours of incubation at 30°C. Cells were lysed by mechanical disruption in lysis buffer (20 mM sodium phosphate pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, 5 mM imidazole), and the soluble fraction was applied to a nickel-charged HiTrap chelating column. His-tagged LuxO D47E was eluted with an imidazole gradient, concentrated, and stored at −80°C in liquid nitrogen-frozen aliquots. Protein concentration was determined by UV absorbance at 280 nm using calculated extinction coefficients.

ATPase activity was measured using a coupled-enzyme spectrophotometric assay. The reaction mixture contained 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), 5 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM NADH, 1 mM phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), 5–20 units/mL pyruvate kinase/lactate dehydrogenase (PK/LDH) enzyme mix, 10 µM LuxO D47E, and varying concentrations of ATP and test compounds. ATP hydrolysis generates ADP, which is converted to pyruvate by PK using PEP; pyruvate is then reduced to lactate by LDH, oxidizing NADH to NAD⁺. The decrease in NADH absorbance at 340 nm (ε = 6220 M⁻¹ cm⁻¹) was monitored over 5 minutes, and initial reaction rates were used to calculate kinetic parameters via Michaelis-Menten and Lineweaver-Burk analyses.

DNA-binding affinity was assessed by electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) and fluorescence anisotropy. For EMSA, purified LuxO D47E was incubated with a ⁶FAM-labeled 200-bp DNA fragment encompassing the *qrr4* promoter region in binding buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 5% glycerol, 0.1 mg/mL BSA). Reactions were resolved on 6% native polyacrylamide gels and visualized by fluorescence imaging. For fluorescence anisotropy, increasing concentrations of LuxO D47E were titrated into a fixed concentration of ⁶FAM-labeled *qrr4* promoter DNA, and anisotropy was measured using a plate reader. Binding constants (K<sub>d</sub>) were derived by fitting data to a one-site binding model.

To identify LuxO mutants resistant to inhibition, random mutagenesis was performed on the *luxO* D47E gene using the GeneMorph II kit, and the mutant library was cloned into a conjugative plasmid (pEVS143). The library was introduced into a *V. cholerae* Δ*luxO* strain carrying the *lux* reporter. Transconjugants were arrayed in 96-well plates containing 100 µM compound 12 and screened for loss of luminescence induction. Resistant clones were retested, and *luxO* alleles were sequenced to identify mutations. Site-directed mutagenesis was used to reconstruct single mutations in otherwise wild-type backgrounds.

Virulence factor expression was analyzed by Western blot. *V. cholerae luxO* D47E cultures were grown in AKI medium under virulence-inducing conditions (static then shaking) in the presence of compound 12. TcpA levels were probed using specific antisera. Similarly, *V. parahaemolyticus luxO** strains were grown in LM medium with Mg²⁺ and oxalate to induce TTSS-1, and VopD secretion was assessed in culture supernatants and cell lysates.

Cytotoxicity was measured using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay. HeLa cells were infected with *V. parahaemolyticus luxO** at a multiplicity of infection of 10, with or without 500 µM compound 12. Supernatants were collected at 2–4 hours post-infection, and LDH activity was quantified using a commercial kit, with percent cytotoxicity calculated relative to Triton X-100-lysed controls.

All chemical syntheses were performed according to standard organic chemistry protocols, and compound structures were confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS).

### Chemical Reactions.

The Class 2 compounds of the present invention are synthesized through a convergent route centered on the 5-thio-6-azauracil heterocyclic core. The general synthetic strategy involves the preparation of appropriately substituted alkyl halides or alcohols bearing branched hydrophobic termini, followed by nucleophilic substitution or coupling to the thiol moiety of a protected 6-azauracil derivative. Specifically, 2,4-bis(trimethylsilyloxy)-5-thio-6-azauracil is first prepared by silylation of 6-azauracil with hexamethyldisilazane in the presence of ammonium sulfate, followed by thiation using Lawesson’s reagent or P₂S₅. The bis-silylated intermediate is then treated with an alkylating agent—such as 1-bromo-3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol (for compound 12) or analogous branched alkyl bromides—in the presence of a mild base like potassium carbonate in anhydrous DMF or acetonitrile at 50–80°C for 12–24 hours. Deprotection is achieved by aqueous workup or mild acid hydrolysis, yielding the final 5-alkylthio-6-azauracil derivatives.

For compound 12, the key step involves the reaction of the bis-silylated 5-thio-6-azauracil with 1-bromo-3,3-dimethylbutan-2-ol. The tertiary alcohol functionality is preserved under the reaction conditions, and the resulting product is purified by silica gel chromatography to afford compound 12 as a white solid in 65% yield. Structural confirmation is obtained by ¹H and ¹³C NMR, which show characteristic signals for the azauracil ring protons (δ ~7.8 ppm, singlet) and the tert-butyl methyl groups (δ ~1.2 ppm, singlet, 9H), along with the methine proton adjacent to the alcohol (δ ~3.9 ppm, multiplet). High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) confirms the molecular formula C₁₀H₁₅N₃O₃S [M+H]⁺.

Analogues with linear or less bulky side chains (e.g., n-propyl, isopropyl, cyclohexylmethyl) are prepared similarly using the corresponding alkyl halides. Structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies reveal that branching near the terminal carbon and the presence of a hydroxyl group enhance potency, with the tert-butyl carbinol side chain of compound 12 providing optimal steric bulk and polarity for LuxO inhibition. All final compounds are >95% pure by HPLC and are stable in aqueous solution for at least 48 hours at 37°C, as confirmed by LC-MS analysis.

## Results

### Investigation of the Targets of the QS Activating Compounds

Following the identification of eight Class 1 and three Class 2 QS-activating compounds from a 90,000-member library, the molecular targets of these molecules were systematically investigated. Class 1 compounds were tested against *V. cholerae* strains lacking either the CqsS or LuxPQ receptor. All eight induced bioluminescence in the Δ*luxPQ* strain but not in the Δ*cqsS* strain, confirming their action as CqsS-specific agonists. These compounds lack structural similarity to the native CAI-1 autoinducer, representing novel chemotypes for CqsS activation.

In contrast, the three Class 2 compounds—designated compounds 11, 12, and a third analogue—activated QS in both the Δ*cqsA* Δ*luxS* and *luxO* D47E screening strains, indicating a target downstream of the membrane receptors. Given their structural homology, sharing a 5-thio-6-azauracil core with variations only in the alkyl side chain, it was hypothesized that they share a common mechanism of action. To pinpoint the target, a transcriptional reporter fusion (*qrr4-gfp*) was introduced into the *luxO* D47E background. Treatment with compound 11 resulted in a ~3-fold reduction in GFP fluorescence, indicating decreased *qrr4* transcription. This suggested that the compound acts on either LuxO or σ⁵⁴, the sigma factor required for *qrr* promoter activity.

To distinguish between these possibilities, the effect of compound 11 on another σ⁵⁴-dependent gene, *vpsR* (involved in biofilm formation), was examined. No significant change in *vpsR* transcription was observed upon compound treatment, strongly implicating LuxO—not σ⁵⁴—as the direct target. This conclusion was further supported by the observation that compound 11 activated QS in strains expressing either wild-type LuxO or the phosphomimetic LuxO D47E, demonstrating that the compound’s activity does not depend on the phosphorylation state of LuxO and thus does not interfere with the kinase/phosphatase steps of the pathway.

### Structure-Activity-Relationship of Class 2 Compounds

A focused SAR study was undertaken to optimize the activity of the Class 2 scaffold. A library of 5-thio-6-azauracil derivatives was synthesized, systematically varying the length, branching, and terminal functional groups of the alkyl side chain. Bioluminescence assays in the *luxO* D47E reporter strain revealed that potency was highly sensitive to side chain architecture. Linear alkyl chains (e.g., n-butyl, n-pentyl) conferred weak activity (EC₅₀ > 50 µM), while branched chains significantly enhanced potency. Introduction of a hydroxyl group at the penultimate carbon further improved activity, with the tert-butyl carbinol side chain (as in compound 12) yielding the most potent analogue, exhibiting a 3-fold lower EC₅₀ (~8 µM) compared to compound 11 (~25 µM).

Notably, removal of the hydroxyl group from compound 12 (yielding the corresponding ether or hydrocarbon) resulted in a marked loss of activity, indicating that hydrogen bonding or polarity at this position is critical. Similarly, replacement of the tert-butyl group with smaller alkyl groups (methyl, ethyl) or cyclic moieties (cyclopropyl) reduced potency. These findings establish that maximal activity requires a bulky, hydrophobic terminus combined with a polar hydroxyl group positioned to interact with a specific pocket in the LuxO protein. The SAR data guided the selection of compound 12 as the lead candidate for subsequent mechanistic and phenotypic studies.

### Class 2 Compounds Inhibit the LuxO ATPase Activity

To determine which biochemical activity of LuxO is inhibited by compounds 11 and 12, three key functions were examined: phosphorylation, DNA binding, and ATP hydrolysis. As noted, both compounds activate QS in strains expressing LuxO D47E, which cannot be phosphorylated, ruling out interference with phosphorylation/dephosphorylation. Electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs) showed that LuxO D47E binds the *qrr4* promoter DNA with equal affinity in the presence or absence of 200 µM compound 12. Quantitative fluorescence anisotropy confirmed this result, yielding identical dissociation constants (K<sub>d</sub> ≈ 300 nM) under both conditions. Thus, DNA binding is unaffected.

In contrast, ATPase assays revealed dose-dependent inhibition of LuxO D47E-catalyzed ATP hydrolysis by both compounds. Michaelis-Menten analysis demonstrated that increasing concentrations of compound 12 decreased both Vₘₐₓ and Kₘ for ATP. Lineweaver-Burk plots of 1/v versus 1/[ATP] yielded parallel lines in the presence and absence of inhibitor, a hallmark of uncompetitive inhibition. This indicates that the compounds bind exclusively to the LuxO–ATP complex, not to free LuxO or free ATP. The degree of ATPase inhibition (% at 50 µM) strongly correlated with QS activation potency (EC₅₀) across a panel of analogues (R² = 0.89), confirming that ATPase inhibition is the primary mechanism of action. By blocking ATP hydrolysis, the compounds prevent LuxO from catalyzing open complex formation at *qrr* promoters, thereby halting Qrr sRNA transcription and allowing premature HapR accumulation.

### A Genetic Screen to Identify LuxO Mutants Resistant to Compound 12

To map the inhibitor binding site, a genetic screen was performed for LuxO D47E mutants that confer resistance to compound 12. A library of randomly mutated *luxO* D47E alleles was introduced into a *V. cholerae* Δ*luxO* strain carrying the *lux* reporter. Colonies that remained dark in the presence of 100 µM compound 12 were isolated, and sequencing revealed four single-amino-acid substitutions: I211F, L215F, L242F, and V294L. All four mutants retained the ability to repress luminescence in the absence of inhibitor, confirming they are functional.

These residues were mapped onto the crystal structure of *Aquifex aeolicus* NtrC1 (PDB: 3M0E), which shares 58% sequence identity with *V. cholerae* LuxO. I211 and L215 reside in the helix containing the conserved GAFTGA motif, which mediates interaction with σ⁵⁴-RNAP. L242 lies immediately upstream of the Walker B motif (D245-E246-L247-C248), which coordinates the catalytic water molecule for ATP hydrolysis. V294 is positioned near the putative catalytic arginine finger (R306). Purified LuxO D47E/I211F protein exhibited ATPase activity that was completely resistant to compound 12, confirming that these mutations disrupt inhibitor binding rather than compensating via enhanced σ⁵⁴ interaction. The clustering of resistance mutations around the Walker B and GAFTGA regions suggests that the inhibitors bind an allosteric pocket adjacent to the ATPase active site, inducing a conformational change that blocks hydrolysis.

### Broad Spectrum Activation of Vibrio QS

The conservation of LuxO across *Vibrio* species prompted evaluation of compound activity in non-*cholerae* species. In *V. harveyi*, which uses a nearly identical QS circuit, the *luxO* D47E mutant is non-luminescent. Treatment with compounds 11 or 12 induced light production by >10,000-fold, confirming cross-species activity. In *V. parahaemolyticus*, QS controls colony morphology via the HapR ortholog OpaR: LCD (translucent) vs. HCD (opaque). A constitutively active *luxO** mutant forms translucent colonies, but addition of compound 12 or 11 switched the phenotype to opaque, indistinguishable from a Δ*luxO* strain. This demonstrates that the compounds inhibit *V. parahaemolyticus* LuxO, derepressing OpaR.

Similar results were obtained with *V. vulnificus* and *V. alginolyticus* (data not shown), confirming that the inhibitors function broadly across the genus. This pan-*Vibrio* activity stems from the high sequence identity (>90%) of the LuxO ATPase domain among these species, particularly at the inhibitor-binding residues identified in the resistance screen. Thus, the compounds represent the first broad-spectrum activators of *Vibrio* QS, capable of overriding diverse upstream autoinducer inputs by targeting a conserved downstream node.

### New Chemical Tools for Controlling Virulence in Vibrios

The therapeutic potential of LuxO inhibitors was evaluated by assessing their impact on virulence factor production. In *V. cholerae luxO* D47E, compound 12 treatment increased HapR levels and concomitantly decreased TcpA (the major subunit of TCP) in a dose-dependent manner, as shown by Western blot. In *V. parahaemolyticus luxO**, compound 12 reduced both intracellular and secreted levels of VopD, a TTSS-1 effector critical for cytotoxicity.

This biochemical attenuation translated to functional protection in a HeLa cell infection model. The *luxO** mutant caused ~100% LDH release (cytotoxicity) by 3 hours post-infection, whereas pre-treatment with 500 µM compound 12 reduced cytotoxicity to ~30% (p < 0.01). At 4 hours, cytotoxicity in the treated group rose to ~100%, likely due to compound degradation or bacterial adaptation, but the delay in host cell lysis demonstrates a significant window of protection. The residual cytotoxicity of the Δ*luxO* control (~60% at 4 hours) reflects TTSS-1-independent killing mechanisms, consistent with prior reports.

Critically, the compounds showed no cytotoxicity toward HeLa cells at concentrations up to 500 µM, and did not inhibit bacterial growth, confirming their anti-virulence (not antibacterial) mode of action. These results validate LuxO inhibition as a viable strategy to disarm pathogenic vibrios and provide proof-of-concept for the development of anti-infectives that exploit the unique inverse QS logic of acute pathogens.

## Discussion Of Results

The present invention establishes a new paradigm for anti-virulence therapy by demonstrating that small-molecule inhibition of LuxO—a central NtrC-family response regulator—can broadly activate quorum sensing and repress virulence across multiple pathogenic *Vibrio* species. The identified compounds, exemplified by compound 12, represent the first chemical probes of NtrC-type ATPase activity and offer several advantages over existing quorum-sensing modulators. Unlike receptor agonists, which are inherently species-specific due to receptor divergence, LuxO inhibitors leverage the high conservation of the downstream signaling node to achieve pan-*Vibrio* activity. This is particularly valuable given the diversity of autoinducers used by vibrios (CAI-1, AI-2, HSLs), which would necessitate multiple receptor-targeted drugs for broad coverage.

The uncompetitive mechanism of inhibition is unprecedented among response regulator modulators and provides key insights into LuxO allostery. By binding the LuxO–ATP complex, the compounds trap the protein in a catalytically inactive conformation, likely by restricting conformational dynamics required for hydrolysis. The resistance mutations map to regions flanking the Walker B motif and the GAFTGA domain—areas known to undergo rearrangement during the ATPase cycle in other AAA+ proteins. This suggests that the inhibitors stabilize a “closed” or “pre-hydrolysis” state that cannot proceed to transition-state formation.

Although the 5-thio-6-azauracil scaffold shows selectivity for *Vibrio* LuxO over *E. coli* NtrC (despite conservation of the resistance residues), future optimization could enhance spectrum or selectivity. The scaffold’s drug-like properties—water solubility, stability, low cytotoxicity—make it an excellent starting point for medicinal chemistry. Potential applications extend beyond anti-infectives: these compounds are powerful tools for dissecting NtrC signaling in diverse bacteria and may inspire inhibitors of eukaryotic AAA+ ATPases, which are implicated in cancer and neurodegeneration.

In conclusion, the LuxO inhibitors disclosed herein validate a novel target class for anti-virulence drug discovery and provide a foundation for developing broad-spectrum therapeutics against vibriosis, a significant global health burden. By converting acute pathogens into a less virulent state without killing them, this approach aligns with the growing emphasis on sustainable antimicrobial strategies that minimize resistance evolution.
