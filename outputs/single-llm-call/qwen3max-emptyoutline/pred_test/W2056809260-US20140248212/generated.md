# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Positron emission tomography (PET) reporter gene imaging has emerged as a powerful non-invasive methodology for monitoring cell-based therapies in both preclinical and clinical settings. This technique enables the longitudinal tracking of therapeutic cells that have been genetically engineered to express a PET reporter gene (PRG), which encodes a protein capable of specifically accumulating a radiolabeled PET reporter probe (PRP). Upon systemic administration, the PRP is selectively retained within cells expressing the PRG due to enzymatic conversion or transport-mediated trapping, thereby generating a detectable PET signal proportional to the number and location of viable therapeutic cells. The ability to non-invasively assess the whole-body biodistribution, persistence, and functional status of administered therapeutic cells represents a critical advancement over conventional invasive methods such as tissue biopsies, which are inherently limited by sampling error, procedural risks, and inability to provide dynamic, real-time data across multiple anatomical sites.

Despite its significant potential, the widespread clinical adoption of PET reporter gene imaging has been hindered by limitations associated with currently available PRG-PRP systems. The most extensively utilized PRGs to date are derived from herpes simplex virus type 1 thymidine kinase (HSV1-tk) and its engineered variant sr39tk. These viral enzymes phosphorylate specific pyrimidine analogs—such as 9-[4-¹⁸F-3-(hydroxymethyl)butyl]guanine (¹⁸F-FHBG), 2′-deoxy-2′-¹⁸F-5-ethyl-1-β-D-arabinofuranosyluracil (¹⁸F-FEAU), and 2′-deoxy-2′-¹⁸F-5-iodo-1-β-D-arabinofuranosyluracil (¹⁸F-FIAU)—thereby converting them into charged monophosphates that are trapped intracellularly and accumulate to levels detectable by PET. While HSV1-tk-based systems have demonstrated high sensitivity due to catalytic signal amplification and have been successfully employed in human trials, their viral origin presents a major drawback: immunogenicity. Clinical studies have documented immune-mediated clearance of therapeutic cells expressing HSV1-tk, thereby compromising treatment efficacy and limiting long-term monitoring capabilities. This immunological response arises because the host immune system recognizes the foreign viral protein as non-self, triggering cytotoxic T-cell responses that eliminate the very cells intended for therapeutic benefit.

To circumvent this issue, considerable effort has been directed toward developing human-derived PRGs that are less likely to provoke an immune reaction. Two candidate systems based on human nucleoside kinases have been explored: a double mutant of deoxycytidine kinase (dCK) and a truncated form of mitochondrial thymidine kinase 2 (TK2). Both were shown to phosphorylate ¹⁸F-FEAU, offering a potentially non-immunogenic alternative. However, these systems face significant challenges related to biological inertness—the requirement that ectopic expression of the PRG should not perturb endogenous cellular metabolism. Nucleoside kinases play essential roles in the salvage pathway of nucleotide biosynthesis by phosphorylating natural deoxynucleosides such as thymidine and deoxycytidine. Overexpression of a PRG with high activity toward these endogenous substrates can lead to imbalanced deoxynucleoside triphosphate (dNTP) pools, which in turn may induce genotoxic stress, DNA replication errors, and genomic instability—particularly concerning in long-lived therapeutic cells such as stem cells. Indeed, the dCK double mutant exhibits enhanced activity toward natural nucleosides compared to wild-type dCK, raising concerns about metabolic interference. Similarly, truncated TK2 retains substantial activity for thymidine and deoxycytidine, failing to fully satisfy the criterion of biological inertness.

Furthermore, the utility of any PRG-PRP system is critically dependent on the pharmacokinetic and biodistribution properties of the PRP. An ideal PRP should exhibit rapid clearance from non-target tissues, minimal background accumulation, and high specificity for the PRG-expressing cells. Many existing probes suffer from suboptimal biodistribution, including high uptake in organs such as the liver, gastrointestinal tract, or kidneys, which can obscure signals from nearby therapeutic cell deposits or limit applicability in certain anatomical regions. For instance, ¹⁸F-FHBG demonstrates significant biliary excretion and intestinal retention in mice, complicating abdominal imaging. Additionally, the enantiomeric configuration of nucleoside analogs influences their interaction with endogenous enzymes; naturally occurring D-nucleosides are efficiently metabolized by human kinases, whereas L-enantiomers are generally poor substrates for most mammalian enzymes due to stereochemical incompatibility. This property makes L-configured nucleosides attractive candidates for PRPs, as they are less likely to be phosphorylated by endogenous kinases in non-engineered tissues, thereby improving specificity. However, to exploit this advantage, a complementary PRG must be engineered to efficiently recognize and phosphorylate the L-analog while minimizing activity toward natural D-nucleosides.

In this context, 2-deoxy-2-¹⁸F-5-methyl-1-β-L-arabinofuranosyluracil (L-¹⁸F-FMAU) has emerged as a promising candidate PRP. As the L-enantiomer of the well-studied D-FMAU, it is expected to exhibit reduced interaction with endogenous nucleoside kinases, potentially leading to lower background signal. Preliminary studies suggest favorable clearance kinetics and low non-specific retention in murine models. However, wild-type human TK2, although capable of phosphorylating both D- and L-nucleosides due to its relaxed stereospecificity, does so with relatively low efficiency for L-FMAU and retains high activity for endogenous thymidine and deoxycytidine. Moreover, wild-type TK2 is subject to potent feedback inhibition by thymidine triphosphate (dTTP), a regulatory mechanism that modulates enzyme activity in response to intracellular dNTP levels. This inhibition varies throughout the cell cycle—peaking during S-phase—and introduces variability in PRP retention that could confound quantitative interpretation of PET signals.

Thus, there exists a compelling need for a novel PRG-PRP system that integrates the following attributes: (1) human origin to minimize immunogenicity; (2) high catalytic efficiency for a non-natural PRP such as L-¹⁸F-FMAU; (3) markedly reduced activity toward endogenous nucleosides to ensure metabolic neutrality; (4) resistance to physiological feedback regulation to enable consistent signal generation independent of cell cycle status; and (5) compatibility with a PRP exhibiting favorable whole-body biodistribution in both preclinical species and humans. The development of such a system would represent a significant advance in molecular imaging technology, facilitating safer, more accurate, and clinically translatable monitoring of cell-based therapeutics across a broad spectrum of diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and regenerative medicine applications.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and improved positron emission tomography (PET) reporter gene/probe system comprising a mutated human thymidine kinase 2 (TK2) polypeptide as the reporter gene and 2-deoxy-2-¹⁸F-5-methyl-1-β-L-arabinofuranosyluracil (L-¹⁸F-FMAU) as the reporter probe. Specifically, the invention features a double mutant of human TK2, designated herein as TK2-N93D/L109F, which exhibits enhanced phosphorylation activity toward L-¹⁸F-FMAU while simultaneously displaying significantly reduced enzymatic activity toward endogenous nucleosides, particularly thymidine and deoxycytidine. This engineered TK2 variant overcomes key limitations of prior art reporter systems by combining high sensitivity for the L-configured probe with metabolic inertness, thereby enabling non-invasive, quantitative, and longitudinal tracking of therapeutic cells without perturbing native nucleotide metabolism or eliciting immune responses.

The TK2-N93D/L109F mutant was developed through a rational, structure-guided enzyme engineering approach. Starting from a truncated form of human TK2 lacking the mitochondrial targeting sequence (to ensure cytosolic localization and accessibility to systemically administered probes), two amino acid substitutions—Asn93 to Asp (N93D) and Leu109 to Phe (L109F)—were introduced based on homology modeling and mechanistic insights into substrate recognition and allosteric regulation. The N93D mutation confers resistance to feedback inhibition by thymidine triphosphate (dTTP), a critical feature that stabilizes enzymatic activity across varying cellular states and enhances signal consistency. The L109F substitution, although initially predicted to increase stereoselectivity for L-nucleosides, unexpectedly broadened catalytic efficiency but, when combined with N93D in the double mutant, synergistically elevated the relative preference for L-FMAU over natural substrates. Kinetic analyses demonstrate that TK2-N93D/L109F exhibits a 100% increase in kcat for L-FMAU compared to wild-type TK2, while its activity toward thymidine is reduced by 49% and toward deoxycytidine by 38%. Consequently, the kcat(L-FMAU)/kcat(thymidine) ratio is elevated nearly fourfold relative to wild-type TK2, indicating superior selectivity for the reporter probe.

In cellular uptake assays, L1210 murine leukemia cells engineered to express TK2-N93D/L109F accumulated significantly higher levels of L-¹⁸F-FMAU than those expressing wild-type TK2 or single mutants, even in the presence of physiological concentrations of competing thymidine. In vivo microPET/CT imaging in tumor-bearing mice confirmed that the TK2-N93D/L109F/L-¹⁸F-FMAU system achieves sensitivity comparable to the benchmark HSV1-sr39tk/¹⁸F-FHBG system, with no statistically significant difference in tumor-to-background ratios. Importantly, the human origin of TK2-N93D/L109F mitigates the risk of immunogenicity, addressing a major limitation of viral reporter genes. Furthermore, biodistribution studies in healthy human volunteers reveal that L-¹⁸F-FMAU clears predominantly via hepatobiliary and renal routes, with notable accumulation in the liver and myocardium—findings that inform clinical deployment strategies while confirming systemic tolerability.

The invention thus encompasses: (a) an isolated nucleic acid molecule encoding the TK2-N93D/L109F polypeptide; (b) an expression vector comprising said nucleic acid operably linked to a promoter suitable for expression in mammalian cells; (c) a host cell, including therapeutic cells such as T cells, stem cells, or dendritic cells, stably or transiently transfected with said vector to express TK2-N93D/L109F; (d) a method of non-invasively imaging said cells in a subject by administering L-¹⁸F-FMAU and detecting its accumulation via PET; and (e) a kit comprising the expression vector and instructions for use in cell tracking applications. The system is particularly suited for monitoring adoptive cell therapies, oncolytic virotherapies, and gene-modified stem cell transplants, where repeated, whole-body assessment of cell fate is essential for optimizing dosing, predicting efficacy, and managing adverse events. By fulfilling the stringent criteria of sensitivity, specificity, biological inertness, and low immunogenicity, the TK2-N93D/L109F/L-¹⁸F-FMAU platform represents a transformative tool for advancing precision medicine through real-time, image-guided cell therapy management.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a novel PET reporter gene/probe system designed for the non-invasive monitoring of cell-based therapies, comprising a genetically engineered human thymidine kinase 2 (TK2) variant and a corresponding L-configured fluorinated nucleoside analog. Central to the invention is the TK2-N93D/L109F double mutant, a cytosolically localized enzyme derived from human TK2 through targeted amino acid substitutions that confer enhanced catalytic properties for the reporter probe L-¹⁸F-FMAU while minimizing interactions with endogenous metabolic pathways. The development of this system addresses longstanding challenges in molecular imaging, including immunogenicity of viral reporters, metabolic interference from nucleoside kinase overexpression, and suboptimal probe biodistribution.

Human thymidine kinase 2 (TK2) is a mitochondrial enzyme encoded by the nuclear genome that plays a key role in the mitochondrial salvage pathway by phosphorylating thymidine and deoxycytidine to their respective monophosphates, which are subsequently converted to triphosphates for mitochondrial DNA synthesis and repair. Wild-type TK2 possesses a unique capacity among human nucleoside kinases to phosphorylate both D- and L-enantiomers of certain nucleoside analogs, albeit with modest efficiency for the latter. However, its natural mitochondrial localization restricts access to systemically delivered probes, and its susceptibility to feedback inhibition by dTTP introduces variability in enzymatic activity that is undesirable for quantitative imaging. To overcome these limitations, the invention employs a truncated form of TK2 lacking the N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (residues 1–50), which results in cytosolic retention and improved accessibility to circulating PRPs. This truncated construct serves as the foundational scaffold for further engineering.

Using homology modeling based on the crystal structures of the closely related human deoxycytidine kinase (dCK), residues critical for substrate binding and allosteric regulation were identified. Asn93, located in a region analogous to the dTTP-binding site in dCK, was substituted with aspartic acid (N93D) to disrupt hydrogen bonding networks that stabilize the closed, active conformation of the enzyme in the presence of dTTP. Biochemical assays confirmed that the N93D mutation dramatically reduces sensitivity to dTTP-mediated inhibition: at 100 µM dTTP, wild-type TK2 activity is suppressed by 55%, whereas TK2-N93D retains over 95% of its activity. This insensitivity ensures consistent probe phosphorylation regardless of fluctuations in intracellular dNTP pools during the cell cycle, thereby enhancing the reliability of PET signal quantification.

Leu109, situated near the pyrimidine base-binding pocket, was selected for mutagenesis to modulate stereoselectivity. Although initial structural predictions suggested that substituting leucine with the bulkier phenylalanine (L109F) would sterically hinder binding of D-nucleosides more than L-nucleosides, empirical testing revealed a more complex outcome: the L109F mutant exhibited increased catalytic turnover (kcat) for all tested substrates, with a proportionally greater effect on D-thymidine. Nevertheless, when combined with N93D in the double mutant TK2-N93D/L109F, the beneficial effects of both mutations are integrated. Kinetic characterization shows that TK2-N93D/L109F phosphorylates L-FMAU with a kcat value twice that of wild-type TK2, while its kcat for thymidine is halved. The resulting kcat/Km ratio for L-FMAU is elevated by approximately 2.5-fold relative to wild-type, and the selectivity index (kcat(L-FMAU)/kcat(thymidine)) reaches 14.9—nearly four times higher than wild-type TK2. This orthogonal activity profile—high for the probe, low for natural substrates—ensures efficient trapping of L-¹⁸F-FMAU without disrupting endogenous nucleotide homeostasis.

The expression of TK2-N93D/L109F in therapeutic cells is achieved using standard recombinant DNA techniques. A nucleic acid sequence encoding the double mutant is cloned into an expression vector under the control of a constitutive or inducible promoter (e.g., CMV, EF1α, or Tet-On systems), optionally fused to a fluorescent reporter such as yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) via an internal ribosomal entry site (IRES) or 2A peptide for co-expression and facile cell sorting. Retroviral, lentiviral, or transposon-based delivery systems may be employed to stably integrate the construct into the genome of target cells, including T lymphocytes, natural killer cells, mesenchymal stem cells, induced pluripotent stem cells, or tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. Transduced cells are expanded ex vivo and validated for uniform PRG expression prior to administration to a subject.

For imaging, the subject is administered L-¹⁸F-FMAU intravenously at a dose ranging from 50 to 200 µCi (1.85–7.4 MBq) in humans, or equivalent scaled doses in animal models. The probe circulates systemically and enters cells via nucleoside transporters. In cells expressing TK2-N93D/L109F, L-¹⁸F-FMAU is rapidly phosphorylated to L-¹⁸F-FMAU-monophosphate, which cannot be further metabolized or effluxed, leading to intracellular accumulation proportional to PRG expression levels. PET scanning is performed 1–3 hours post-injection to allow for blood pool clearance and optimal target-to-background contrast. Image reconstruction and quantification yield standardized uptake values (SUVs) that correlate with viable cell numbers at specific anatomical sites.

Biodistribution studies in mice demonstrate that L-¹⁸F-FMAU exhibits low background retention in thoracic and abdominal organs, with predominant excretion via hepatobiliary and urinary routes. Notably, gastrointestinal accumulation is markedly lower than that observed with ¹⁸F-FHBG, reducing spillover artifacts in abdominal imaging. Human biodistribution data from healthy volunteers confirm similar clearance pathways but reveal additional uptake in the myocardium and sustained hepatic retention—likely due to endogenous TK2 activity in heart mitochondria and hepatic glucuronidation, respectively. These findings guide clinical application: the system is well-suited for tracking cells in peripheral tissues, lymphoid organs, and subcutaneous tumors, but may require caution in cardiac or hepatic contexts unless further probe modifications are implemented.

The invention’s advantages are multifaceted. First, the human origin of TK2-N93D/L109F minimizes the risk of immune recognition, enabling long-term cell monitoring without immune-mediated elimination—a critical improvement over HSV1-tk. Second, the reduced activity toward thymidine and deoxycytidine preserves nucleotide pool balance, mitigating genotoxic risks associated with metabolic perturbation. Third, resistance to dTTP feedback ensures stable signal output irrespective of proliferative status. Fourth, the use of an L-configured probe leverages inherent metabolic discrimination by endogenous enzymes, enhancing specificity. Finally, the system matches the sensitivity of established viral platforms, as demonstrated by equivalent tumor uptake in murine models compared to sr39tk/¹⁸F-FHBG.

The methodology is broadly applicable across therapeutic modalities. In oncology, it can track CAR-T cells or tumor-targeted lymphocytes to assess tumor infiltration and persistence. In regenerative medicine, it enables monitoring of stem cell engraftment and migration. In gene therapy, it verifies transgene delivery and expression kinetics. The system may also be adapted for multimodal imaging by incorporating additional reporter elements or used in combination with therapeutic transgenes in “theranostic” constructs. Overall, the TK2-N93D/L109F/L-¹⁸F-FMAU platform represents a robust, clinically translatable solution for real-time, non-invasive cell tracking that meets the rigorous demands of modern precision therapeutics.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The radiochemical synthesis of L-¹⁸F-FMAU was carried out using a multi-step procedure adapted from established protocols for fluorinated nucleoside analogs. Briefly, the precursor 5-methyl-2,2-diethoxy-1-(2,3,5-tri-O-benzoyl-β-L-arabinofuranosyl)uracil was reacted with no-carrier-added [¹⁸F]fluoride, produced via the ¹⁸O(p,n)¹⁸F nuclear reaction in an ¹⁸O-enriched water target, in the presence of potassium carbonate and Kryptofix 2.2.2 in acetonitrile at 85°C for 10 minutes. The resulting crude product was purified by semi-preparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a C18 reverse-phase column and a mobile phase consisting of 20% ethanol in 0.1 M ammonium formate buffer (pH 4.5). The desired L-¹⁸F-FMAU fraction was collected, concentrated under reduced pressure, and formulated in sterile saline containing 10% ethanol for intravenous injection. Radiochemical purity exceeded 98%, as confirmed by analytical HPLC, and specific activity ranged from 1,500 to 2,500 Ci/mmol at end-of-synthesis. The identity of the product was verified by co-injection with authentic cold standard and mass spectrometry. This synthesis protocol yielded sufficient quantities of L-¹⁸F-FMAU for both murine and human imaging studies, with consistent quality and reproducibility across multiple production runs.

### Example 2

A homology model of human TK2 was generated using the SWISS-MODEL automated protein structure homology-modelling server. The amino acid sequence of full-length human TK2 (UniProt ID O00142) was submitted, and the best template structures were identified as the closed (PDB ID 1P5Z) and open (PDB ID 3QEO) conformations of human deoxycytidine kinase (dCK), which shares 35% sequence identity and 50% homology with TK2. The model was refined through energy minimization and validated using Ramachandran plot analysis, which indicated that over 90% of residues occupied favored regions. Structural alignment revealed that TK2, like dCK, is predicted to undergo a conformational transition between open (inactive) and closed (active) states upon substrate binding. Key residues involved in nucleoside binding and ATP coordination were conserved, supporting the functional relevance of the model. This structural framework enabled the identification of Asn93 and Leu109 as candidate sites for mutagenesis to modulate dTTP sensitivity and substrate selectivity, respectively.

### Example 3

Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on the truncated human TK2 gene (Δ50N, lacking residues 1–50) cloned into the pMSCV retroviral vector and a modified pET14b bacterial expression vector. Mutations N93D and L109F were introduced individually and in combination using the QuikChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primers were designed to incorporate the desired nucleotide changes while maintaining flanking restriction sites for cloning verification. Mutant constructs were transformed into E. coli DH5α, and plasmid DNA was isolated and sequenced to confirm the presence of the intended mutations and absence of secondary mutations. The resulting vectors—pMSCV-TK2-N93D, pMSCV-TK2-L109F, and pMSCV-TK2-N93D/L109F—were used for retroviral production, while the pET14b derivatives were employed for recombinant protein expression in E. coli BL21(DE3) C41 cells for kinetic characterization.

### Example 4

Recombinant wild-type and mutant TK2 proteins were expressed in E. coli BL21(DE3) C41 cells harboring the modified pET14b vectors encoding an N-terminal hexahistidine-SUMO fusion tag. Cultures were grown at 37°C in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin until an optical density at 600 nm of 0.8 was reached, at which point expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and the temperature was reduced to 18°C for overnight incubation. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, lysed by sonication in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, 1 mM PMSF), and clarified by centrifugation. The soluble fraction was loaded onto a HisTrap HP nickel-affinity column, and bound proteins were eluted with 250 mM imidazole. The His-SUMO-TK2 fusion was then cleaved by incubation with SUMO protease at 4°C for 16 hours. The cleavage mixture was reapplied to the nickel column to separate untagged TK2 from the His-SUMO tag and protease. Final purification was achieved by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 column equilibrated in 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, and 3 mM DTT. Purified proteins were concentrated to 10 mg/mL, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. SDS-PAGE and Western blotting confirmed >95% purity and correct molecular weight.

### Example 5

Kinetic parameters of wild-type and mutant TK2 enzymes were determined using an NADH-coupled spectrophotometric assay. Reactions were conducted in 100 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM ATP, 0.2 mM phosphoenolpyruvate, 0.2 mM NADH, 10 units/mL pyruvate kinase, and 10 units/mL lactate dehydrogenase at 37°C. Substrate concentrations (thymidine, deoxycytidine, L-thymidine, or L-FMAU) were varied from 15 to 500 µM, and the decrease in absorbance at 340 nm due to NADH oxidation was monitored over time using a Cary UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Initial velocities were calculated and fitted to the Michaelis-Menten equation using SigmaPlot software to derive Km and kcat values. For inhibition studies, dTTP was added at concentrations of 0, 10, and 100 µM, and residual activity was measured at a fixed substrate concentration of 200 µM. All assays were performed in triplicate. Results showed that TK2-N93D/L109F exhibited a Km of 42 µM and kcat of 1.8 s⁻¹ for L-FMAU, compared to Km = 68 µM and kcat = 0.9 s⁻¹ for wild-type TK2, indicating improved catalytic efficiency. Activity toward thymidine was reduced by 49% in the double mutant, confirming enhanced selectivity.

### Example 6

L1210 murine leukemia cells were transduced with retroviruses encoding TK2-N93D/L109F, single mutants, wild-type TK2, or HSV1-sr39tk, each linked via IRES to YFP for fluorescence-based sorting. Viral particles were produced by transient co-transfection of Phoenix amphotropic packaging cells with the respective pMSCV constructs and helper plasmids. L1210 cells were spin-infected at 1,000 × g for 2 hours at 37°C in the presence of 2 µg/mL Polybrene. After 48 hours, YFP-positive cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) to ensure uniform transgene expression across populations. Stable cell lines were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 5% fetal calf serum and periodically validated for PRG expression by Western blotting and functional uptake assays. These engineered cell lines served as standardized models for comparative imaging studies.

### Example 7

In vitro probe uptake assays were performed by seeding 500,000 transduced L1210 cells per well in 24-well plates. After 24 hours, cells were incubated with 5 µCi of L-¹⁸F-FMAU in 1 mL of serum-free medium, with or without 5 µM D-thymidine, for 1 hour at 37°C. Cells were then washed four times with ice-cold PBS, lysed, and radioactivity was quantified using a gamma counter. Uptake was normalized to cell number and expressed as percentage of injected dose per million cells. TK2-N93D/L109F-expressing cells showed 3.8-fold higher uptake than wild-type TK2 cells and 1.6-fold higher than TK2-N93D cells. In the presence of thymidine, uptake by TK2-N93D/L109F cells decreased by only 47%, compared to 75% for TK2-N93D, demonstrating superior resistance to substrate competition. In vivo validation was conducted in SCID mice bearing bilateral subcutaneous tumors of L1210-PRG cells. Mice received 200 µCi of L-¹⁸F-FMAU or ¹⁸F-FHBG via tail vein injection and underwent microPET/CT imaging 3 hours later. Tumor uptake of L-¹⁸F-FMAU in TK2-N93D/L109F tumors was 19.9 ± 1.5 %ID/g, statistically equivalent to 24.1 ± 6.2 %ID/g for sr39tk/¹⁸F-FHBG (p = 0.37), confirming comparable sensitivity.

### Example 8

Human biodistribution of L-¹⁸F-FMAU was evaluated in two healthy volunteers (one male, 53 years; one female, 44 years) under an approved Institutional Review Board protocol. Each subject received a bolus intravenous injection of approximately 56 MBq (1.5 mCi) of sterile, pyrogen-free L-¹⁸F-FMAU. Whole-body PET/CT scans were acquired on a Biograph 64 scanner at four time points starting immediately post-injection, with each scan covering from head to mid-thigh (6 bed positions, 5 minutes per bed). Low-dose CT was used for attenuation correction. Regions of interest were drawn over major organs, and mean standardized uptake values (SUVs) were calculated. At 2 hours post-injection, prominent uptake was observed in the liver (SUVmean = 4.2), kidneys (3.8), gallbladder (5.1), urinary bladder (6.0), and myocardium (2.9). Gastrointestinal activity was moderate, primarily in the colon. In comparison, historical data for ¹⁸F-FHBG showed higher intestinal uptake (SUVmean = 5.5 in small bowel) but lower myocardial signal (SUVmean = 1.1). These results indicate that while L-¹⁸F-FMAU is suitable for systemic cell tracking, its myocardial and hepatic accumulation may limit applications in those regions unless probe modifications are pursued. No adverse events were reported, confirming safety for human use.
