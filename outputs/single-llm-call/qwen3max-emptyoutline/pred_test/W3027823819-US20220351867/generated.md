# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates generally to the field of forensic science, and more particularly to methods and systems for estimating the time elapsed since the formation of a blood pool at a crime scene. The invention provides a novel approach for determining the age of a blood pool based on the physical and morphological characteristics of its drying process, thereby enabling forensic investigators to reconstruct timelines of events even in the absence of a human body or other traditional temporal indicators. The disclosed method leverages principles of fluid mechanics, thermodynamics, and image analysis to correlate observable features of a partially dried blood pool—specifically, the position and progression of the drying front—with the time since deposition. This technique is grounded in a detailed understanding of the evaporation dynamics of blood pools, which exhibit behavior intermediate between that of discrete liquid drops and large-scale evaporating surfaces such as lakes or ponds. By integrating empirical measurements of blood pool geometry, environmental conditions, and the intrinsic properties of blood, the invention enables accurate estimation of the time of blood deposition with a precision of approximately ±30 minutes under controlled conditions. The method is non-invasive, requires only photographic documentation of the bloodstain, and is designed for practical deployment in real-world forensic investigations.

## TECHNICAL BACKGROUND

Bloodstain pattern analysis (BPA) is a well-established forensic discipline focused on interpreting the location, distribution, and morphology of bloodstains to reconstruct the sequence of events that occurred during a violent incident. While significant advances have been made in analyzing spatter patterns, impact angles, and droplet trajectories, the temporal dimension of blood deposition—specifically, when a blood pool was formed—has remained largely unaddressed by existing forensic methodologies. Current techniques for estimating the time of death or event occurrence rely heavily on postmortem changes such as body temperature (algor mortis), muscle stiffening (rigor mortis), lividity (livor mortis), and entomological evidence. However, these methods are contingent upon the presence of a corpse at the scene. In cases where the body has been removed or is otherwise absent—such as in hit-and-run incidents, staged crime scenes, or clandestine homicides—the ability to determine when blood was deposited becomes critically important for establishing investigative timelines and corroborating or refuting witness statements.

Historically, research into blood drying has concentrated on volume estimation for reconstructive purposes or assessing whether observed blood loss could be fatal. These approaches do not address the temporal aspect of blood pool formation. Moreover, while the drying of individual blood droplets has been studied to some extent—particularly in relation to stain morphology, edge effects, and cracking patterns—the evaporation dynamics of larger blood pools (i.e., volumes significantly exceeding a single drop, typically greater than 0.5 mL) have received minimal scientific attention. This gap is problematic because blood pools behave fundamentally differently from isolated drops due to their greater mass, surface area, and interaction with substrate geometry, all of which influence heat and mass transfer during drying.

Prior art in forensic science lacks any validated method for estimating the age of a blood pool based on its physical state. Existing protocols treat bloodstains as static evidence to be collected and analyzed for DNA or serological markers, but offer no framework for interpreting the dynamic process of desiccation as a clock-like indicator. Meanwhile, in the broader scientific literature, studies on liquid evaporation have largely focused on idealized systems: either microscopic sessile drops governed by capillary forces and contact line pinning, or macroscopic bodies of water (e.g., lakes) where evaporation is modeled as a one-dimensional diffusion process described by Fick’s law. Blood pools occupy an intermediate regime wherein both surface tension and gravitational forces play significant roles, and the liquid-substrate interaction is complicated by the biological complexity of blood itself—a biphasic colloidal suspension comprising plasma (an aqueous matrix containing proteins, electrolytes, and other solutes) and red blood cells (RBCs) that undergo coagulation and gelation upon exposure to air.

It has been previously observed that drying blood pools progress through five distinct morphological stages: (I) coagulation, (II) gelation, (III) rim desiccation, (IV) center desiccation, and (V) final desiccation. During these stages, a visible “drying front”—the boundary between wet, red blood and dried, darkened residue—advances inward from the periphery toward the center. This front is a reproducible and photographically detectable feature, yet no prior work has established a quantitative relationship between its position and the elapsed time since deposition. Furthermore, while analogies have been drawn between blood drying and sol-gel transitions in materials science—where a colloidal sol transforms into a porous gel that subsequently dries through constant-rate and falling-rate periods—these parallels have not been operationalized into a forensic tool.

Compounding the challenge is the sensitivity of evaporation dynamics to multiple variables: ambient temperature, relative humidity, substrate type (e.g., linoleum, wood, tile), initial blood volume, pool shape, and hematocrit level (the volume fraction of RBCs). Traditional evaporation models fail to account for the coupled effects of these parameters in a biologically active fluid that undergoes phase transitions (from liquid to gel to solid) during drying. Consequently, there exists a long-felt but unsolved need in forensic science for a robust, physics-based method to estimate the time of blood pool formation using only non-destructive, field-deployable observations—ideally, standard crime scene photography. The absence of such a method has left investigators without a critical piece of temporal evidence in numerous cases, thereby hindering accurate reconstruction of criminal events.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure provides a method and system for estimating the time elapsed since the formation of a blood pool at a crime scene by analyzing the morphological and geometric characteristics of the drying blood in conjunction with environmental parameters. The invention is based on the discovery that the evaporation dynamics of blood pools follow predictable physical laws that can be modeled using a modified diffusion framework, and that the position of the drying front—the visible boundary between wet and dry regions of the blood pool—serves as a reliable temporal marker when properly calibrated against known variables.

In accordance with the invention, a method is disclosed for determining the time of formation of a blood pool, comprising the steps of: (a) capturing at least one digital image of a partially dried blood pool on a substrate, wherein the image clearly depicts a drying front separating a wet central region from a dried peripheral region; (b) measuring, from the image, the total area (A<sub>i</sub>) and perimeter (P) of the blood pool, as well as the wet area (A<sub>x</sub>) corresponding to the undried portion; (c) estimating the initial height (h) of the blood pool using empirical correlations derived from controlled experiments on the same substrate type; (d) calculating a dimensionless shape factor L* defined as A<sub>i</sub>/(hP); (e) determining the initial mass (m<sub>i</sub>) of the blood pool using the estimated height, total area, and known density of blood; (f) estimating the current mass (m<sub>x</sub>) of the blood pool using a normalized mass-area relationship derived from experimental data, expressed as m<sub>x</sub>/m<sub>i</sub> = 1 − α[1 − (A<sub>x</sub>/A<sub>i</sub>)]<sup>β</sup>, where α and β are empirically determined constants; (g) computing the mass loss δm = m<sub>i</sub> − m<sub>x</sub>; (h) calculating an effective evaporation flux J* using a diffusion-based model that incorporates a characteristic diffusion coefficient D<sub>blood</sub> ≈ 1×10<sup>−9</sup> m<sup>2</sup>s<sup>−1</sup>, adjusted for the shape factor L*, ambient temperature (T), saturation vapor pressure of water (P<sub>w</sub>), atmospheric pressure (P<sub>a</sub>), molecular diameter of water (d), Boltzmann’s constant (k), universal gas constant (R), and molar mass of water (M); and (i) estimating the elapsed time δt since pool formation as δt = δm / (J* A<sub>i</sub>).

The invention further discloses that the diffusion coefficient D<sub>blood</sub> is substantially invariant across blood pools of varying initial masses and shapes when drying occurs under identical environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, substrate), provided that the evaporation rate is normalized using the wet area and the shape factor L*. This invariance enables the creation of reference tables or computational models that map observed drying front positions to elapsed time for specific combinations of environmental parameters.

Additionally, the method accounts for the multi-stage drying behavior of blood, which includes an initial coagulation phase, followed by a constant-rate evaporation period analogous to the constant-rate period (CRP) in gel drying, and subsequent falling-rate periods (FRP1 and FRP2) as desiccation progresses inward. The transition between these stages is marked by changes in the slope of the mass-loss curve, but the core temporal estimation relies on the quasi-linear progression of the drying front during the dominant evaporation phase.

The disclosed system may be implemented using standard forensic photography equipment, with image analysis performed via software capable of segmenting wet and dry regions and computing geometric parameters. Calibration datasets for different substrates, temperatures, and humidity levels can be pre-established through controlled laboratory experiments, allowing field investigators to select the appropriate reference model based on on-scene environmental measurements (e.g., using portable hygrometers and thermometers).

The method achieves a time estimation accuracy of ±30 minutes under controlled conditions and represents the first scientifically validated approach to dating blood pools in the absence of a body. It is particularly advantageous because it is non-destructive, requires no physical sampling of the bloodstain, and leverages existing forensic documentation practices. The invention thus fills a critical gap in forensic timeline reconstruction and provides law enforcement with a new, objective tool for evaluating the chronology of violent incidents.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a method and associated system for estimating the time elapsed since the deposition of a blood pool at a crime scene by analyzing the physical progression of its drying process. The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, including the underlying scientific principles, experimental validation, mathematical modeling, and practical implementation protocols. It will be understood by those skilled in the art that the invention is not limited to the particular configurations described herein, but encompasses all equivalents and modifications that fall within the spirit and scope of the appended claims.

The foundation of the invention lies in the recognition that blood, when deposited as a pool on a solid substrate, undergoes a complex but reproducible drying process governed by the interplay of fluid mechanics, thermodynamics, and biochemical coagulation. Unlike simple liquids such as water, blood is a biphasic colloidal suspension consisting of an aqueous plasma phase (approximately 90–92% water by volume) and dispersed red blood cells (RBCs), which constitute about 36–47% of the total volume in healthy adults (as measured by hematocrit). Upon contact with air and a foreign surface, blood initiates the hemostatic cascade: platelets aggregate to form a temporary plug, and fibrinogen is enzymatically converted into a fibrin mesh that entraps RBCs, resulting in gelation. This transformation from a free-flowing liquid to a viscoelastic gel fundamentally alters the evaporation dynamics compared to pure water or non-coagulating fluids.

Experimental studies conducted by the inventors have demonstrated that drying blood pools progress through five morphologically distinct stages, as previously noted: (S.I) coagulation, (S.II) gelation, (S.III) rim desiccation, (S.IV) center desiccation, and (S.V) final desiccation. Critically, during stages S.III through S.V, a sharp visual boundary—the drying front—emerges and advances radially inward from the pool’s edge. This front corresponds to the interface between hydrated, red-colored blood and dehydrated, darkened residue, and its position over time serves as the primary observable metric for temporal estimation.

To develop a quantitative model linking the drying front to elapsed time, the inventors conducted a series of controlled evaporation experiments using fresh human blood (collected in accordance with ethical guidelines and without anticoagulants to preserve natural clotting behavior) deposited on common forensic substrates including linoleum, varnished oak wood, and ceramic tile. Environmental conditions—temperature (ranging from 22°C to 27°C) and relative humidity (20% to 60%)—were precisely regulated using a glovebox equipped with climate control. High-resolution digital images were captured at regular intervals (every 2–10 minutes) to track the evolution of pool geometry and the drying front. Simultaneously, mass loss was recorded using precision balances to correlate visual changes with quantitative evaporation rates.

A key insight from these experiments is that blood pool evaporation exhibits characteristics intermediate between drop-wise and film-wise evaporation. For small volumes (<0.5 g), behavior resembles that of a sessile drop, dominated by contact line dynamics and Marangoni flows. For larger volumes (>1 g), the system transitions toward gel-like drying, with evaporation initially occurring uniformly across the surface (constant-rate period) followed by diffusion-limited transport through a porous, shrinking matrix (falling-rate periods). This transition is smooth and volume-dependent, but crucially, the evaporation flux—when normalized appropriately—converges to a consistent value across a wide range of pool sizes and shapes under identical environmental conditions.

The normalization procedure involves two critical corrections. First, instead of using the total pool area (which remains constant until late-stage shrinkage), the wet area (A<sub>wet</sub>)—defined as the region still exhibiting liquid characteristics—is used to compute the instantaneous evaporation rate J* = (dm/dt) / A<sub>wet</sub>. Second, a dimensionless shape factor L* = A / (hP) is introduced, where A is the total area, h is the average initial height (estimated as V/A, with V derived from initial mass and blood density ρ ≈ 1060 kg/m³), and P is the perimeter. This shape factor accounts for the influence of pool geometry on evaporation efficiency; for example, elongated pools with high perimeter-to-area ratios dry faster than circular pools of equal volume due to enhanced edge effects.

When J* is further adjusted by multiplying by √L* and scaling with thermodynamic parameters (temperature T, saturation vapor pressure P<sub>w</sub>, atmospheric pressure P<sub>a</sub>, etc.), the resulting quantity collapses to a near-constant diffusion coefficient D<sub>blood</sub> ≈ 1×10<sup>−9</sup> m²/s for pools drying under the same T and RH. This value is consistent with literature reports for colloidal gels and significantly lower than that of blood drops (~10<sup>−8</sup> m²/s), confirming the shift toward diffusion-limited drying in larger pools.

With D<sub>blood</sub> established as a reference parameter, the time estimation algorithm proceeds as follows. From a crime scene photograph showing a visible drying front, the investigator measures A<sub>i</sub> and P using image analysis software (e.g., ImageJ or proprietary forensic tools). The initial height h is not directly measurable but can be estimated from empirical databases correlating h with substrate type—for instance, on smooth tiles, h averages 1.44 ± 0.19 mm for pools >1 g. Using h, A<sub>i</sub>, and ρ, the initial mass m<sub>i</sub> = ρ h A<sub>i</sub> is computed.

Next, the wet area A<sub>x</sub> is segmented from the image (e.g., via color thresholding in the red channel). The current mass m<sub>x</sub> is then inferred from the experimentally derived power-law relationship m<sub>x</sub>/m<sub>i</sub> = 1 − 0.78[1 − (A<sub>x</sub>/A<sub>i</sub>)]<sup>0.16</sup>, which holds across diverse pool geometries when normalized. The mass loss δm = m<sub>i</sub> − m<sub>x</sub> is thus obtained without physical sampling.

The evaporation flux J* is calculated using the rearranged diffusion model:
J* = D<sub>blood</sub> M P<sub>w</sub> / (L<sub>K</sub> R T √L*),
where L<sub>K</sub> = kT / (π d² P<sub>a</sub>) is the Knudsen layer thickness (~10<sup>−7</sup> m for water vapor at 25°C), k is Boltzmann’s constant, d is the molecular diameter of water (~2.65×10<sup>−10</sup> m), R is the gas constant, M is the molar mass of water, and P<sub>w</sub> is obtained from standard vapor pressure tables based on ambient T.

Finally, the elapsed time is δt = δm / (J* A<sub>i</sub>). Substituting all terms yields a closed-form expression:
t<sub>x</sub> = [0.78 R k T² A<sub>i</sub><sup>1/2</sup> h<sup>1/2</sup> ρ (1 − A<sub>x</sub>/A<sub>i</sub>)<sup>0.16</sup>] / [M d² π P<sup>1/2</sup> D<sub>blood</sub> P<sub>w</sub> P<sub>a</sub>].

Validation experiments demonstrated that this method estimates drying time with an error of less than 5% (e.g., predicting 8h18m vs. actual 8h37m) under controlled conditions. Accuracy depends on precise environmental measurement and substrate-specific calibration of h and the mass-area relationship. Future refinements may incorporate machine learning to automate image segmentation and adapt to variable lighting or substrate textures.

The system embodiment comprises: (1) a digital camera for capturing high-resolution images of blood pools; (2) a portable environmental sensor for recording T and RH; (3) a computing device running analysis software that implements the above algorithm; and (4) a database of calibration parameters for common substrates and conditions. The method is non-invasive, rapid, and compatible with standard forensic workflows, offering a groundbreaking capability to date blood evidence independently of biological decay markers.
