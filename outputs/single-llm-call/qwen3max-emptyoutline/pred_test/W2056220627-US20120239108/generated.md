## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to implantable medical devices for delivering electrical stimulation to excitable biological tissue, and more particularly to systems, methods, and apparatus for improving the energy efficiency of implantable pulse generators used in neuromodulation therapies. The invention specifically concerns novel approaches to reducing power consumption in constant-current neural stimulators through the synergistic integration of biophysically optimized stimulus waveform design, adaptive compliance voltage regulation, and pulse-width selection tailored to the electrophysiological properties of targeted neural populations. These innovations are applicable across a broad spectrum of clinical neuromodulation applications including but not limited to deep brain stimulation (DBS), spinal cord stimulation (SCS), peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS), vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), sacral nerve stimulation (SNS), and cochlear implants, where extended battery life and reduced device size are critical factors in patient outcomes and therapeutic efficacy.

## BACKGROUND

Implantable pulse generators (IPGs) have become a cornerstone of modern neuromodulation therapy, providing effective treatment for a wide array of neurological disorders, chronic pain conditions, sensory deficits, and movement disorders. These devices function by delivering precisely controlled electrical current pulses through implanted electrodes to specific neural targets, thereby modulating neural activity and altering pathological signaling patterns within the nervous system. The fundamental mechanism underlying electrical stimulation involves the generation of action potentials in axons proximal to the stimulating electrode, which subsequently influences neurotransmitter release and neural circuit dynamics in targeted brain regions or peripheral pathways. Despite decades of clinical success and technological refinement, the core architecture and operational principles of commercial IPGs have remained largely unchanged, particularly with respect to energy management and stimulus delivery efficiency.

Conventional IPGs typically employ constant-current stimulation paradigms, wherein a predetermined current amplitude is maintained throughout the duration of each stimulation pulse regardless of variations in tissue impedance or electrode-tissue interface characteristics. This approach ensures consistent neural activation thresholds across different patients and over time, as current rather than voltage serves as the primary determinant of neural excitation. However, this reliability comes at a significant energetic cost. Traditional constant-current stimulators utilize a fixed compliance voltage—a predetermined maximum voltage potential that establishes the upper limit for current delivery across the electrode-tissue load. This compliance voltage is deliberately set higher than theoretically necessary to accommodate worst-case scenarios involving elevated tissue impedances, suboptimal electrode placement, or unusually high neural activation thresholds. While this safety margin guarantees reliable operation under extreme conditions, it results in substantial energy waste during normal operating conditions, where the actual voltage requirements for neural activation are considerably lower.

The energy inefficiency inherent in fixed-compliance voltage systems stems from the fundamental relationship between power consumption, current, and voltage in electrical circuits. During stimulation, the total power consumed by the IPG equals the product of the stimulation current and the sum of voltages across both the electrode-tissue load and the current-regulating electronic components (typically transistors). When a fixed high compliance voltage is employed, a significant portion of this voltage appears across the current-regulating transistor rather than being utilized for actual neural stimulation. This excess voltage drop across the transistor represents pure energy loss in the form of heat dissipation, substantially reducing the overall energy efficiency of the stimulation process. Given that stimulation circuitry typically accounts for the largest share of an IPG's total energy consumption, these inefficiencies directly translate into shortened battery life, necessitating either more frequent surgical replacements for non-rechargeable devices or increased charging burden for rechargeable systems.

Compounding this issue is the conventional reliance on rectangular stimulus waveforms, which have been the standard in clinical neuromodulation for decades due to their simplicity of implementation and well-characterized neural activation properties. Rectangular pulses deliver constant current throughout their duration, providing predictable charge injection and straightforward parameter programming. However, emerging theoretical and experimental evidence suggests that alternative waveform shapes may achieve equivalent neural activation with reduced energy expenditure. Computational modeling studies have indicated that non-rectangular waveforms, particularly centered-triangular pulses featuring symmetric ramp-up and ramp-down phases, can exploit the temporal dynamics of neural membrane charging to achieve more efficient depolarization. The underlying biophysical principle involves matching the stimulus waveform to the natural time constants of neural membrane capacitance and resistance, thereby minimizing the total energy required to reach the threshold for action potential generation.

Furthermore, the selection of stimulation pulse-width represents another critical parameter influencing energy efficiency that has received insufficient attention in conventional IPG design. Traditional clinical practice often employs relatively short pulse-widths (typically 60–90 microseconds) based on historical precedent and considerations related to charge density limitations for certain electrode materials. However, both theoretical analyses and experimental investigations have demonstrated that the relationship between pulse-width and energy consumption follows a non-monotonic, U-shaped curve, with a distinct minimum occurring at intermediate pulse-width values. This phenomenon arises from the fundamental strength-duration relationship governing neural excitation, where shorter pulses require exponentially higher current amplitudes to achieve activation, while longer pulses, though requiring lower current amplitudes, consume energy over extended durations. The optimal pulse-width that minimizes total energy consumption varies depending on the electrophysiological properties of the targeted neural population, particularly axon diameter, which influences both membrane time constants and spatial extent of current spread.

The interdependence of these factors—compliance voltage, stimulus waveform shape, and pulse-width—creates a complex optimization landscape that conventional IPG designs fail to adequately address. Fixed compliance voltages ignore real-time variations in stimulation requirements, rectangular waveforms may not exploit the full potential for biophysical energy savings, and standardized pulse-width selections may be suboptimal for specific neural targets. Moreover, the interaction between these parameters means that improvements in one domain may be partially or completely offset by inefficiencies in another. For instance, even if an energy-optimal pulse-width is selected, the benefits may be negated by excessive compliance voltage overhead. Similarly, biophysically optimized waveforms may still consume excessive energy if delivered with unnecessarily high compliance voltages.

Previous attempts to address these limitations have been fragmented and incomplete. Some commercial devices have implemented basic forms of variable compliance voltage, but these implementations often lack the sophistication needed to dynamically adapt to real-time stimulation requirements or fail to integrate this capability with other energy-saving strategies. Patent literature reveals various approaches to compliance voltage adjustment, but these typically focus on discrete voltage steps or simple feedback mechanisms that do not fully exploit the continuous optimization potential available through real-time monitoring of stimulation parameters. Similarly, while alternative waveform shapes have been proposed in academic literature, their translation to clinical practice has been hindered by concerns about implementation complexity, safety validation requirements, and lack of integration with existing stimulation paradigms.

The absence of a comprehensive, integrated approach to energy efficiency in neural stimulation represents a significant unmet need in the field of implantable medical devices. Extended battery life would enable smaller device form factors, reduced surgical intervention frequency, improved patient quality of life, and expanded therapeutic applications for conditions requiring long-term or high-intensity stimulation regimens. Additionally, improved energy efficiency could facilitate the development of next-generation neuromodulation therapies that require more sophisticated stimulation patterns or higher stimulation intensities than currently feasible with existing power-constrained devices. The present invention addresses these challenges by providing a holistic framework for maximizing energy efficiency through the coordinated optimization of multiple stimulation parameters based on both electronic circuit considerations and neural biophysical principles.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides systems, methods, and apparatus for significantly improving the energy efficiency of implantable pulse generators used in neuromodulation therapies through the integrated optimization of stimulus waveform design, compliance voltage regulation, and pulse-width selection. In accordance with one aspect of the invention, an implantable pulse generator comprises a programmable current source configured to deliver electrical stimulation pulses to neural tissue through at least one implanted electrode, wherein the current source includes an adjustable compliance voltage circuit that dynamically maintains the minimum compliance voltage necessary to sustain the programmed stimulation current amplitude throughout each pulse duration. The adjustable compliance voltage circuit continuously monitors the voltage requirements across the electrode-tissue load and automatically adjusts the compliance voltage to maintain the current-regulating transistor in its saturation operating region while minimizing excess voltage drop across the transistor.

In accordance with another aspect of the invention, the implantable pulse generator is configured to deliver non-rectangular stimulus waveforms, particularly centered-triangular waveforms characterized by symmetric ramp-up and ramp-down phases, which have been demonstrated to achieve equivalent neural activation with reduced energy consumption compared to conventional rectangular waveforms. The centered-triangular waveform exploits the temporal dynamics of neural membrane charging by matching the stimulus current profile to the natural time constants of the targeted neural tissue, thereby minimizing the total energy required to reach action potential threshold.

In accordance with a further aspect of the invention, the implantable pulse generator incorporates pulse-width optimization algorithms that select stimulation pulse-widths based on the electrophysiological characteristics of the targeted neural population, particularly axon diameter. The invention recognizes that the relationship between pulse-width and energy consumption follows a non-monotonic, U-shaped curve with a distinct minimum that varies depending on axon diameter, with larger diameter axons exhibiting optimal energy efficiency at shorter pulse-widths compared to smaller diameter axons. The pulse generator may include pre-programmed pulse-width recommendations for different clinical applications based on typical axon diameters encountered in those applications, or may incorporate real-time impedance measurements and neural response monitoring to dynamically optimize pulse-width selection.

In accordance with yet another aspect of the invention, the implantable pulse generator integrates all three energy-saving strategies—adjustable compliance voltage, biophysically optimized waveforms, and axon diameter-appropriate pulse-widths—into a unified stimulation paradigm that achieves multiplicative energy savings compared to conventional approaches. The combination of these strategies enables energy reductions of up to 90% compared to traditional fixed-compliance, rectangular waveform stimulation, dramatically extending battery life and enabling new therapeutic possibilities.

The invention also encompasses methods for determining optimal stimulation parameters for specific clinical applications. These methods include computational modeling of neural activation thresholds across different axon diameters, in vivo experimental validation of energy consumption relationships, and clinical parameter optimization protocols that balance energy efficiency with safety considerations such as charge density limits and tissue damage thresholds. The invention further provides safety mechanisms to ensure that energy-efficient stimulation parameters remain within established safety guidelines for charge injection and charge density, incorporating electrode material-specific limits and real-time monitoring of stimulation parameters.

Additional aspects of the invention include specialized circuit architectures for implementing adjustable compliance voltage with minimal additional complexity, digital signal processing algorithms for generating precise non-rectangular waveforms, and user interfaces that facilitate clinician programming of energy-optimized stimulation parameters while maintaining compatibility with existing clinical workflows. The invention is applicable across the full spectrum of neuromodulation applications, including deep brain stimulation, spinal cord stimulation, peripheral nerve stimulation, vagus nerve stimulation, sacral nerve stimulation, and cochlear implants, with specific parameter recommendations tailored to the unique electrophysiological characteristics of each application domain.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for maximizing the energy efficiency of implantable pulse generators used in neuromodulation therapies through the integrated optimization of multiple stimulation parameters based on both electronic circuit considerations and neural biophysical principles. The detailed description that follows encompasses the technical implementation of each energy-saving strategy, their synergistic interactions, safety considerations, and specific applications across different neuromodulation domains.

An implantable pulse generator according to the present invention comprises several key components that work in concert to achieve unprecedented levels of energy efficiency. The core stimulation circuit includes a programmable current source with adjustable compliance voltage capability, waveform generation circuitry capable of producing non-rectangular stimulus shapes, and sophisticated control logic that coordinates these functions based on real-time monitoring of stimulation parameters and pre-programmed optimization algorithms.

The adjustable compliance voltage circuit represents a fundamental departure from conventional fixed-compliance approaches. In traditional IPGs, the compliance voltage is set to a fixed value (typically 10–25 volts) that exceeds the maximum expected voltage requirements across all possible operating conditions. This conservative approach ensures reliable operation but results in substantial energy waste during normal conditions where actual voltage requirements are much lower. The present invention replaces this fixed approach with a dynamic compliance voltage system that continuously monitors the voltage drop across the electrode-tissue load and automatically adjusts the compliance voltage to maintain just sufficient headroom for the current-regulating transistor to operate in its saturation region.

The technical implementation of the adjustable compliance voltage circuit involves several key elements. A current-regulating transistor (preferably an N-channel field effect transistor or FET) serves as the primary current control element, with its gate voltage controlled by a feedback circuit that compares the actual output current to the desired stimulation amplitude. A voltage monitoring circuit continuously measures the voltage at the drain terminal of the FET (which corresponds to the voltage across the electrode-tissue load) and feeds this information back to a compliance voltage control circuit. This control circuit dynamically adjusts the supply voltage to the FET to maintain a minimum drain-source voltage (Vds) that ensures saturation operation while minimizing excess voltage.

The minimum Vds required for saturation operation depends on the current flowing through the FET and can be determined experimentally for specific transistor types or calculated based on device characteristics. In practice, the compliance voltage control circuit maintains Vds at a value slightly above the minimum required for saturation (typically 1.0–2.0 volts for the FET types commonly used in medical devices) to provide a small safety margin while maximizing energy efficiency. This dynamic adjustment occurs on a pulse-by-pulse basis, allowing the system to adapt to changes in tissue impedance that may occur due to physiological variations, electrode migration, or fibrous encapsulation over time.

The energy savings achieved through adjustable compliance voltage are substantial and depend on both pulse-width and tissue impedance characteristics. Experimental data demonstrates that for a 200 microsecond pulse-width, using the minimum compliance voltage achieves energy savings of approximately 90% compared to a fixed 20-volt compliance, 77% compared to a fixed 10-volt compliance, and 53% compared to a fixed 5-volt compliance. The magnitude of these savings increases with longer pulse-widths due to the extended duration over which excess voltage is dissipated in the current-regulating transistor. Importantly, the adjustable compliance system ensures that the stimulation current waveform maintains its programmed shape and amplitude, guaranteeing consistent neural activation while minimizing energy waste.

Complementing the adjustable compliance voltage system is the use of biophysically optimized stimulus waveforms, particularly centered-triangular waveforms. Conventional rectangular waveforms deliver constant current throughout the pulse duration, which represents a suboptimal match to the temporal dynamics of neural membrane charging. The neural membrane behaves as a leaky capacitor with characteristic time constants that govern how quickly it can be charged to the threshold for action potential generation. Centered-triangular waveforms exploit these dynamics by providing higher current during the initial phase of the pulse when the membrane is most responsive to rapid charging, followed by a symmetric decrease in current as the membrane approaches threshold.

The centered-triangular waveform is defined by its symmetric ramp-up and ramp-down phases, with the peak current occurring at the midpoint of the pulse duration. This symmetry ensures charge balancing, which is critical for preventing electrode corrosion and tissue damage in chronic stimulation applications. The mathematical definition of the centered-triangular waveform can be expressed as I(t) = Ipeak × (1 - |2t/PW - 1|) for 0 ≤ t ≤ PW, where Ipeak is the peak current amplitude and PW is the pulse-width. This waveform shape has been demonstrated experimentively to achieve equivalent neural activation with approximately 12% less energy compared to the most energy-efficient rectangular waveform configuration.

The energy savings from centered-triangular waveforms arise primarily from reduced average voltage requirements during stimulation. While the peak current may be higher than that required for a rectangular waveform of equivalent pulse-width, the time-integrated current (charge) and, more importantly, the time-integrated power (energy) are reduced due to the more efficient matching of current delivery to membrane charging dynamics. Experimental measurements show that the mean voltage during stimulation is significantly lower for triangular waveforms compared to rectangular waveforms, which directly translates to reduced energy consumption given the relationship E = ∫V(t)I(t)dt.

The implementation of centered-triangular waveforms in an implantable pulse generator requires specialized waveform generation circuitry capable of producing precise current profiles with high temporal resolution. Digital-to-analog converters (DACs) with sufficient bit depth and sampling rate can generate the required current profiles, while feedback control loops ensure accurate current delivery despite variations in load impedance. The waveform generation system must also incorporate charge balancing mechanisms to ensure that the net DC component over each stimulation cycle is zero, preventing electrode polarization and associated safety hazards.

A third critical component of the invention is pulse-width optimization based on the electrophysiological characteristics of the targeted neural population. The relationship between pulse-width and energy consumption follows a non-monotonic, U-shaped curve due to the fundamental strength-duration relationship governing neural excitation. Shorter pulse-widths require exponentially higher current amplitudes to achieve activation (following the Lapicque equation), resulting in high instantaneous power requirements. Longer pulse-widths require lower current amitudes but consume energy over extended durations, leading to increased total energy consumption. Between these extremes lies an optimal pulse-width that minimizes total energy consumption.

The location of this energy-optimal pulse-width depends critically on axon diameter, with larger diameter axons exhibiting optimal energy efficiency at shorter pulse-widths compared to smaller diameter axons. Computational modeling using the MRG mammalian myelinated axon model demonstrates that for large diameter fibers (10–15 micrometers, typical of peripheral motor nerves and spinal cord stimulation targets), the optimal pulse-width ranges from approximately 200–300 microseconds. For smaller diameter fibers (2–5 micrometers, typical of deep brain stimulation targets), the optimal pulse-width extends to 500–700 microseconds or longer.

This axon diameter dependence arises from differences in membrane time constants and spatial extent of current spread. Larger diameter axons have lower axial resistance and larger membrane surface areas, resulting in faster charging dynamics and greater sensitivity to brief, high-amplitude stimuli. Smaller diameter axons have higher axial resistance and smaller membrane surface areas, requiring longer duration stimuli to achieve sufficient depolarization. The invention incorporates this knowledge into pulse-width selection algorithms that recommend appropriate pulse-width ranges for different clinical applications based on typical axon diameters encountered in those applications.

For deep brain stimulation applications targeting small diameter axons (2–5 micrometers), the invention recommends pulse-widths in the range of 400–800 microseconds when combined with adjustable compliance voltage and centered-triangular waveforms. For spinal cord stimulation and peripheral nerve stimulation applications targeting larger diameter axons (10–15 micrometers), the invention recommends pulse-widths in the range of 150–300 microseconds. These recommendations represent significant departures from conventional clinical practice, which typically employs pulse-widths of 60–90 microseconds regardless of target characteristics.

The integration of all three energy-saving strategies—adjustable compliance voltage, centered-triangular waveforms, and axon diameter-appropriate pulse-widths—produces multiplicative energy savings that far exceed the benefits achievable through any single approach alone. Experimental data demonstrates that the combination of these strategies can achieve energy reductions of up to 90% compared to traditional fixed-compliance, rectangular waveform stimulation with conventional pulse-widths. This dramatic improvement in energy efficiency translates directly into extended battery life, enabling smaller device form factors, reduced surgical intervention frequency, and expanded therapeutic applications.

Safety considerations are paramount in the implementation of these energy-efficient stimulation strategies. The invention incorporates multiple safety mechanisms to ensure that optimized stimulation parameters remain within established safety guidelines for charge injection and charge density. Charge density limits vary depending on electrode material, with platinum/iridium electrodes typically limited to 50 microcoulombs per square centimeter, while alternative materials such as iridium oxide or titanium nitride may permit higher charge density limits. The invention includes charge monitoring circuits that continuously calculate injected charge and charge density, automatically adjusting stimulation parameters if safety thresholds are approached.

The invention also addresses potential concerns regarding increased charge injection with energy-efficient parameters. While centered-triangular waveforms and longer pulse-widths may result in higher total charge injection compared to conventional short rectangular pulses, the reduced voltage requirements and improved energy efficiency more than compensate for this increase in terms of overall safety and battery life. Moreover, the use of charge-balanced waveforms and appropriate electrode materials ensures that electrochemical safety limits are maintained.

The technical implementation of the invention encompasses specialized circuit architectures designed to minimize additional complexity while maximizing energy savings. The adjustable compliance voltage circuit can be implemented with minimal additional components beyond those already present in conventional current-regulated stimulators. The waveform generation system leverages existing digital signal processing capabilities found in modern implantable devices, requiring only software modifications to implement non-rectangular waveforms. The pulse-width optimization algorithms can be incorporated into existing programming interfaces, providing clinicians with energy-optimized parameter recommendations while maintaining compatibility with established clinical workflows.

Specific applications of the invention span the full spectrum of neuromodulation therapies. For deep brain stimulation, the combination of adjustable compliance voltage, centered-triangular waveforms, and extended pulse-widths (400–800 microseconds) can achieve predicted energy savings of approximately 63% compared to conventional parameters. For spinal cord stimulation, the invention enables energy savings of 70–85% through the use of 200–300 microsecond pulse-widths combined with optimized waveforms and compliance voltage. Peripheral nerve stimulation applications benefit similarly, with experimental data demonstrating 12% energy savings from waveform optimization alone and additional savings from compliance voltage adjustment and pulse-width optimization.

The invention also facilitates the development of next-generation neuromodulation therapies that require more sophisticated stimulation patterns or higher stimulation intensities than currently feasible with existing power-constrained devices. Extended battery life enables longer trial periods for new therapeutic applications, reduces the economic burden of device replacement surgeries, and improves patient quality of life through reduced charging requirements for rechargeable systems. The comprehensive energy optimization framework provided by the invention represents a significant advancement in implantable medical device technology, addressing a long-standing limitation in neuromodulation therapy while maintaining the highest standards of safety and reliability.
