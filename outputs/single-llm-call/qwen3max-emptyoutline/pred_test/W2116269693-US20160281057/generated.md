# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of reproductive medicine and assisted reproductive technologies, particularly to methods for improving sperm motility in human males suffering from asthenozoospermia or other forms of male infertility characterized by reduced sperm motility. More specifically, the invention provides a method for enhancing the forward motility of human spermatozoa—both experimentally damaged and cryopreserved—by exposing the sperm to molecular hydrogen (H₂) under controlled gaseous or liquid-phase conditions. The invention further encompasses compositions and devices configured for the administration of H₂ to sperm suspensions, as well as their use in in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) procedures. The technical domain intersects with biochemistry, redox biology, mitochondrial physiology, and clinical embryology, wherein the invention leverages the unique physicochemical properties of molecular hydrogen as a selective antioxidant to mitigate oxidative stress–induced dysfunction in sperm cells without altering fundamental cellular energetics such as swimming velocity or inducing hyperactivation. This approach represents a novel therapeutic strategy for rescuing suboptimal or non-motile sperm populations that would otherwise be unsuitable for successful fertilization in assisted reproductive settings.

## BACKGROUND ART

Male infertility constitutes a significant proportion of reproductive disorders worldwide, with asthenozoospermia—defined as a reduction in the percentage of progressively motile spermatozoa below the threshold established by the World Health Organization (WHO)—being one of the most common phenotypic manifestations. Sperm motility is a critical determinant of fertilization competence, as it enables the sperm to navigate the female reproductive tract, penetrate the cumulus oophorus, and ultimately fuse with the oocyte. The etiology of asthenozoospermia is multifactorial, involving genetic abnormalities (e.g., mutations in DNMT3B or MTHFR), systemic diseases such as polycystic kidney disease, mitochondrial DNA haplogroup variations, and environmental or lifestyle factors that exacerbate oxidative stress. Spermatozoa are uniquely vulnerable to oxidative damage due to their high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids in the plasma membrane, minimal cytoplasmic volume limiting the presence of antioxidant enzymes, and limited capacity for DNA repair. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly the hydroxyl radical (•OH), are generated endogenously primarily through mitochondrial electron transport chain leakage and can induce lipid peroxidation, axonemal structural damage, and depletion of intracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP)—all of which impair motility. Paradoxically, low physiological levels of ROS, such as superoxide and nitric oxide, serve as essential signaling molecules in processes like capacitation and the acrosome reaction, underscoring the delicate redox balance required for normal sperm function.

In response to these challenges, various therapeutic approaches have been explored to improve sperm quality, including oral supplementation with antioxidants such as zinc, coenzyme Q10, L-carnitine, and myoinositol, or direct in vitro treatment with compounds like xanthine derivatives. While some of these interventions have shown modest benefits in clinical or experimental settings, they often lack specificity, exhibit variable efficacy across patient populations, or fail to address the root cause of oxidative damage at the subcellular level. Moreover, many conventional antioxidants are unable to penetrate cellular membranes efficiently or may disrupt beneficial redox signaling due to their non-selective reactivity. Consequently, there remains a pressing need for a safe, targeted, and rapidly acting agent capable of restoring sperm motility without compromising other functional parameters or introducing cytotoxic side effects.

Molecular hydrogen (H₂) has emerged in recent years as a novel therapeutic gas with unique biochemical properties. As the smallest and lightest molecule in the universe, H₂ diffuses rapidly across biological membranes, including those of mitochondria and the nucleus, enabling it to access intracellular compartments where oxidative damage occurs. Critically, H₂ selectively scavenges only the most cytotoxic ROS—specifically the hydroxyl radical (•OH) and peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻)—while leaving physiologically relevant ROS such as superoxide (O₂•⁻), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and nitric oxide (NO•) unaffected. This selectivity preserves essential redox signaling pathways while neutralizing the primary mediators of oxidative injury. Numerous preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the protective effects of H₂ in diverse pathological contexts, including ischemia-reperfusion injury in the brain, heart, liver, and retina; nephrotoxicity; radiation-induced lung damage; and neurodegenerative diseases. Importantly, H₂ exhibits an excellent safety profile, with no known toxicity even at high concentrations, and is naturally eliminated via respiration.

Despite these promising attributes, the application of H₂ to male reproductive medicine has remained largely unexplored. Prior art does not disclose or suggest the use of H₂ to enhance sperm motility, particularly in clinically relevant scenarios such as the handling of aged or cryopreserved sperm samples used in IVF and ICSI. Existing methods for sperm preparation—such as density gradient centrifugation, swim-up techniques, or cryopreservation protocols—do not incorporate redox-modulating agents capable of reversing oxidative damage post-collection. Furthermore, no known technique utilizes H₂ gas or H₂-saturated media to rescue motility in sperm that have become immotile due to prolonged storage or freeze-thaw stress. The absence of such a method represents a critical gap in assisted reproductive technology, especially given that the decision to proceed with ICSI often hinges on the availability of even a single motile sperm, and the injection of completely immotile sperm is associated with reduced fertilization and pregnancy rates. Thus, the development of a reliable, rapid, and non-invasive method to restore motility in compromised sperm populations would constitute a significant advancement in the field of reproductive medicine.

### PRIOR ART DOCUMENTS

Several prior art documents have addressed aspects of sperm motility enhancement and antioxidant therapy in male infertility, but none disclose the specific use of molecular hydrogen as described in the present invention. For instance, U.S. Patent No. 6,258,791 B1 describes the use of platelet-activating factor (PAF) to improve sperm motility, reporting an increase from 17.95% to 25.1% in treated samples. Similarly, scientific literature has documented the stimulatory effects of exogenous pyruvate on sperm motility and hyperactivation through enhanced glycolysis and ATP production (see, e.g., Aitken et al., Biol Reprod, 2004). Other studies have evaluated the impact of myoinositol and xanthine derivatives on sperm kinetics, demonstrating moderate improvements in progressive motility. However, these approaches either lack mechanistic specificity, require metabolic substrates that may alter sperm physiology beyond motility, or fail to address mitochondrial dysfunction directly.

Additionally, patents and publications related to hydrogen therapy—such as Japanese Patent JP2007-505872A and U.S. Patent Application US2010/0015255A1—focus on the administration of H₂ for systemic conditions like inflammation, neurodegeneration, or organ ischemia, but do not contemplate its application to gametes or reproductive cells. The seminal work by Ohsawa et al. (Nat Med, 2007) established H₂ as a selective antioxidant capable of reducing •OH in cell cultures and animal models, yet this foundational discovery has not been extended to human spermatozoa in any prior art. Cryopreservation protocols, as described in standard embryology manuals (e.g., Practice Committee of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine, Fertil Steril, 2013), emphasize cooling rates and cryoprotectant composition but offer no post-thaw intervention to reverse motility loss due to oxidative stress. Likewise, WHO guidelines for semen analysis (2010) provide diagnostic criteria for asthenozoospermia but no therapeutic recommendations involving gaseous or dissolved H₂.

Collectively, the prior art fails to recognize that the unique diffusion properties and selective reactivity of H₂ render it ideally suited for rescuing sperm motility impaired by oxidative damage, particularly in time-sensitive clinical settings such as IVF and ICSI. The absence of any teaching, suggestion, or motivation in the existing literature to apply H₂ to human sperm suspensions—whether fresh, aged, or frozen-thawed—underscores the novelty and non-obviousness of the present invention.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### Object to be Solved by the Invention

The primary object of the present invention is to provide a safe, effective, and rapid method for improving the forward motility of human spermatozoa that have been compromised by oxidative stress, aging, or cryopreservation-induced damage. Conventional approaches to managing male infertility, particularly asthenozoospermia, are often inadequate due to the irreversible nature of oxidative damage to sperm structures such as the axoneme and mitochondria. Current antioxidant therapies lack the ability to penetrate cellular membranes efficiently or selectively neutralize the most harmful reactive oxygen species without disrupting essential redox signaling. Furthermore, in assisted reproductive technologies, there is no reliable method to restore motility in sperm samples that have become immotile during storage or after thawing, thereby limiting the pool of usable sperm for fertilization and potentially reducing success rates in IVF and ICSI procedures. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by introducing molecular hydrogen—a biocompatible, membrane-permeable, and selectively reactive gas—as a novel therapeutic agent capable of rescuing motility in otherwise non-viable sperm populations without altering their intrinsic swimming dynamics or inducing unintended physiological changes.

### Means to Solve the Object

The invention achieves the aforementioned object through a method comprising the step of exposing a human sperm suspension to molecular hydrogen (H₂) under controlled conditions sufficient to enhance the forward motility of the spermatozoa contained therein. In one embodiment, the sperm suspension is contacted with a gas mixture containing H₂, carbon dioxide (CO₂), oxygen (O₂), and optionally nitrogen (N₂), wherein the H₂ concentration ranges from 10% to 95% by volume, preferably 50%, and the exposure duration is between 5 and 120 minutes, more preferably 30 to 60 minutes. The gas mixture is delivered into a sealed exposure chamber housing the sperm suspension, ensuring saturation of the liquid phase with dissolved H₂. In an alternative embodiment, the sperm suspension is washed or resuspended in a culture medium that has been pre-saturated with H₂, wherein the H₂-saturated medium constitutes 50% to 100% of the final wash solution, with optimal results observed at 50% and 75% H₂ saturation. The method is applicable to both experimentally damaged sperm (e.g., aged for more than 5 days at room temperature) and clinically relevant frozen-thawed sperm samples obtained from patients undergoing fertility treatment. The invention further includes the use of such H₂-treated sperm in IVF or ICSI procedures, as well as kits comprising H₂-generating devices, pre-saturated media, or gas delivery systems configured for sperm motility enhancement.

### Effect of the Invention

The present invention provides several significant advantages over existing methods. First, it demonstrates a statistically significant and reproducible increase in forward motility—up to approximately 4.7-fold in severely damaged sperm samples—following H₂ treatment, without concomitant increases in swimming velocity or induction of hyperactivation, thereby preserving the natural motility pattern required for successful fertilization. Second, the effect is sustained for at least 2.5 hours post-treatment, even after removal of H₂ from the environment, which is clinically sufficient for the duration of standard IVF or ICSI procedures. Third, repeated H₂ treatment can reactivate motility in sperm that have undergone secondary decline, offering flexibility in clinical scheduling. Fourth, the method is particularly effective in sperm suspensions with viability greater than 20%, and even elicits sluggish motility in a subset of low-viability samples, expanding the utility of marginal sperm specimens. Fifth, H₂ treatment enhances mitochondrial membrane potential, suggesting improved ATP production as a mechanistic basis for motility restoration, without interfering with glycolytic pathways. Sixth, the approach is non-toxic, non-invasive, and compatible with existing embryology laboratory workflows, requiring no modification to standard culture media or equipment beyond the introduction of H₂ delivery. Finally, the invention offers a practical solution for cryopreserved sperm, where H₂ treatment significantly improves motility in asthenozoospermic samples (forward motility <50%), thereby increasing the number of viable sperm available for microinjection and potentially improving fertilization outcomes. These combined effects establish molecular hydrogen as a novel, safe, and efficacious agent for the management of male infertility in assisted reproductive settings.

## MODE OF CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The present invention is carried out by exposing human sperm suspensions to molecular hydrogen (H₂) in either gaseous or dissolved form, under conditions that promote the selective scavenging of cytotoxic reactive oxygen species while preserving essential cellular functions. The method is designed for use in clinical embryology laboratories and can be integrated into standard protocols for sperm preparation prior to IVF or ICSI. The following detailed description outlines the materials, procedures, and parameters necessary to implement the invention effectively.

Human sperm suspensions used in the practice of the invention may be obtained from donors or patients undergoing fertility evaluation and treatment. Semen samples are collected following a standardized ejaculatory abstinence period of 3 to 5 days, in accordance with WHO guidelines. The samples are initially assessed for baseline parameters including sperm concentration, motility, morphology, and viability. For experimental or clinical application, the raw semen is processed to remove seminal plasma, which contains variable levels of antioxidants and enzymes that may confound the assessment of H₂ effects. This is typically achieved by washing the sample in a cleavage-stage embryo culture medium (e.g., SAGE Cleavage Medium, CooperSurgical) supplemented with 10% plasma protein fraction (PPF) to maintain osmotic stability and prevent premature capacitation. Further purification may be performed using a two-layer Percoll® density gradient centrifugation at 600 × g for 15 minutes, followed by a final wash at 400 × g for 5 minutes to concentrate the motile sperm fraction. The resulting sperm pellet is resuspended in the same culture medium with 10% PPF to yield a standardized suspension suitable for treatment.

In one mode of carrying out the invention, the sperm suspension is subjected to H₂ treatment in a gaseous environment. A volume of 50 to 200 μL of the prepared sperm suspension is placed in a sterile culture dish or microcentrifuge tube, which is then positioned inside a sealed exposure chamber with a total internal volume of approximately 1 to 10 liters, preferably 5 liters. The chamber is connected to a gas delivery system capable of supplying a precisely mixed gas blend. The preferred gas mixture consists of 50% H₂, 20% O₂, 5% CO₂, and 25% N₂, although variations in H₂ concentration (ranging from 10% to 95%) and balance gases may be employed depending on the desired effect. The gas is introduced at a flow rate of 0.5 to 2 liters per minute, with 1 L/min being optimal, to ensure complete displacement of ambient air and saturation of the chamber atmosphere within 5 to 10 minutes. The sperm suspension is exposed to this H₂-rich atmosphere for a duration of 5 to 120 minutes, with 30 to 60 minutes being the most effective window. During this time, H₂ rapidly diffuses across the air-liquid interface and into the sperm suspension, achieving equilibrium concentrations in the aqueous phase. Control treatments may be performed using a nitrogen-rich gas mixture (e.g., 75% N₂, 20% O₂, 5% CO₂) to account for any mechanical or pressure-related effects of gas exposure.

In an alternative and clinically more practical mode, the sperm suspension is treated with a liquid medium pre-saturated with H₂. This is accomplished by bubbling pure H₂ gas through the culture medium (e.g., cleavage medium with 10% PPF) for at least 30 minutes under sterile conditions, or by using commercially available H₂-saturated solutions. The H₂-saturated medium is then mixed with standard culture medium equilibrated with 5% CO₂ to achieve final H₂ concentrations of 50%, 75%, or 100% saturation. The sperm suspension is gently washed by resuspension in this H₂-containing medium and incubated for 30 minutes at 37°C in a standard CO₂ incubator. This method avoids the need for specialized gas chambers and is readily adaptable to routine embryology workflows. Notably, experimental data indicate that 50% and 75% H₂ saturation yield superior motility improvements compared to 100% saturation, possibly due to the maintenance of adequate oxygen tension necessary for mitochondrial respiration.

The invention is particularly effective when applied to sperm that have suffered oxidative damage. Such damage may be induced experimentally by storing sperm suspensions at room temperature for more than 5 days, which accelerates ROS accumulation and motility loss. Alternatively, and more relevantly, the method is applied to frozen-thawed sperm samples, which inherently suffer from cryo-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Cryopreservation is performed using standard slow-freezing protocols: equal volumes of sperm suspension and TEST-yolk buffer (Irvine Scientific) are mixed, aliquoted into cryovials, exposed to nitrogen vapor for 5 minutes, and stored in liquid nitrogen. Thawing is conducted by immersion in a 37°C water bath for 5 minutes. The resulting thawed suspension is then subjected to H₂ treatment as described above.

Following treatment, sperm motility is assessed using standard manual or computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) techniques. A 5–10 μL aliquot of the suspension is placed in a Makler or Leja counting chamber, and at least three fields are evaluated under phase-contrast microscopy to determine the percentages of forward motile, non-forward motile, and immotile sperm. For velocity measurements, time-lapse video recordings of 10 seconds duration are captured using a digital microscope, and sperm trajectories are analyzed using ImageJ software with the CASA plugin to calculate curvilinear velocity (VCL), straight-line velocity (VSL), and average path velocity (VAP). Viability is assessed by dual staining with propidium iodide (PI, 10 μM) and Hoechst 33342 (10 μM): PI-negative/Hoechst-positive cells are considered viable. Mitochondrial function is evaluated by co-staining with MitoTracker Green (MTG, 2 μM), which labels mitochondria regardless of membrane potential, and tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM, 2 μM), whose fluorescence intensity correlates with mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm). Confocal microscopy is used to acquire images, and TMRM fluorescence is quantified using image analysis software to assess mitochondrial health.

The invention is validated through rigorous statistical analysis. Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation, and significance is determined using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, or paired t-tests for before-and-after treatment analyses within the same sample. A p-value of less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant. Experimental results consistently demonstrate that H₂ treatment significantly improves forward motility in both aged and frozen-thawed sperm, with maximal effects observed after 30 minutes of exposure. The effect is dependent on baseline sperm viability, with optimal responses in samples containing >20% viable sperm, though partial activation is also observed in low-viability samples. Importantly, H₂ treatment does not alter sperm swimming speed, confirming that it enhances motility without inducing hyperactivation or disrupting normal flagellar beating patterns. The motility improvement is sustained for at least 2.5 hours post-treatment, and a second H₂ exposure can re-activate motility after 24 hours, indicating the potential for repeated clinical use.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Effect of H₂ Gas Treatment on Experimentally Damaged Sperm Motility**

Thirty-five human sperm suspensions from patients undergoing IVF were prepared as described and stored at room temperature for more than 5 days to induce oxidative damage. Baseline forward motility averaged 3.6% (90% CI: 0.6–6.7%). Each suspension was divided into three aliquots: untreated control, H₂-treated (50% H₂, 20% O₂, 5% CO₂, 25% N₂), and N₂-treated (75% N₂, 20% O₂, 5% CO₂). After 40 minutes of gas exposure in a 5-L chamber at 1 L/min flow, motility was reassessed. H₂ treatment increased forward motility to 16.8% (90% CI: 13.8–19.9%; p < 0.001), whereas N₂ treatment yielded only 7.7% (90% CI: 4.6–10.8%), confirming that the effect is specific to H₂ and not due to mechanical agitation. pH remained stable at 7.3 ± 0.1 across groups.

**Example 2: Time-Dependence of H₂ Treatment**

Twelve sperm suspensions with moderate initial motility were exposed to H₂ gas for 5, 30, or 60 minutes. Only 30- and 60-minute treatments significantly improved motility (p < 0.001), establishing 30 minutes as the minimum effective duration.

**Example 3: Influence of Sperm Viability on H₂ Response**

Thirty sperm suspensions were stratified by viability (>20% vs. <20% PI-negative). In the high-viability group (n=17), H₂ treatment increased forward motility from 3.0% to 11.0% (p < 0.001), with 16 of 17 samples responding positively. In the low-viability group (n=13), no significant motility was observed, but 3 samples exhibited sluggish pendular movement post-H₂, indicating residual functional capacity.

**Example 4: Sperm Velocity Analysis**

Ten high-viability suspensions were analyzed by CASA before and after H₂ treatment. No significant differences were found in VCL, VSL, or VAP, demonstrating that H₂ enhances motility without altering swimming speed.

**Example 5: Retention of Motility Post-Treatment**

Twelve suspensions were treated with H₂ for 30 minutes, then maintained in room air at 25°C. Motility remained significantly elevated at 2.5 hours (p < 0.001). After 24 hours, motility declined but was restored to 67% of initial post-treatment levels upon a second H₂ exposure.

**Example 6: Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Enhancement**

Six sperm suspensions were stained with TMRM post-treatment. H₂-treated samples showed significantly higher TMRM fluorescence intensity (p < 0.001) compared to untreated or N₂-treated controls, indicating improved mitochondrial function.

**Example 7: Application to Frozen-Thawed Sperm**

Twenty-one frozen-thawed sperm suspensions were divided into normozoospermic (≥50% motility, n=6) and asthenozoospermic (<50%, n=15) groups. Asthenozoospermic samples treated with 50% H₂-saturated wash medium showed significant motility improvement (p < 0.05), with 13 of 15 samples responding. Surprisingly, 100% H₂ saturation was ineffective, suggesting an optimal H₂:O₂ ratio is required.

These examples collectively demonstrate the robustness, specificity, and clinical relevance of the invention across diverse sperm conditions.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The present invention possesses significant industrial applicability in the field of assisted reproductive technology (ART) and clinical embryology. It provides a standardized, scalable, and cost-effective method for enhancing sperm motility that can be readily integrated into existing IVF and ICSI laboratory protocols without requiring major infrastructure modifications. Fertility clinics worldwide routinely encounter cases of male factor infertility, particularly asthenozoospermia, where the availability of motile sperm is the limiting factor for successful fertilization. By enabling the rescue of motility in aged, damaged, or cryopreserved sperm samples, the invention expands the pool of usable sperm for microinjection, thereby increasing the likelihood of successful fertilization and embryo development. This is especially valuable in cases of severe oligoasthenozoospermia or cryptozoospermia, where only a few sperm are recoverable. The method’s compatibility with standard culture media and equipment—requiring only the addition of H₂ gas cylinders, saturators, or pre-saturated solutions—ensures easy adoption by embryology laboratories of varying resource levels. Furthermore, the invention supports the growing trend toward personalized and precision reproductive medicine by offering a targeted intervention based on sperm redox status. Beyond clinical IVF, the technology may be applied in sperm banking, research settings studying sperm physiology, and veterinary reproduction. Given the excellent safety profile of H₂ and the absence of regulatory barriers for its use in medical gases, commercialization pathways are straightforward, with potential for kit-based products including H₂-generating tablets, saturated media vials, or integrated gas delivery modules for incubators. Thus, the invention meets all criteria for industrial applicability, offering tangible benefits to patients, clinicians, and the broader reproductive healthcare industry.

## EXPLANATION OF LETTERS OR NUMERALS

In the accompanying figures and descriptions referenced herein, the following letters and numerals are used with the indicated meanings:

- **H₂**: Molecular hydrogen, the active agent used in the invention for sperm motility enhancement.
- **•OH**: Hydroxyl radical, a cytotoxic reactive oxygen species selectively scavenged by H₂.
- **ROS**: Reactive oxygen species, a collective term for oxygen-derived free radicals and non-radical oxidants.
- **ATP**: Adenosine triphosphate, the primary energy currency of the cell, synthesized by mitochondria and essential for sperm motility.
- **IVF**: In vitro fertilization, an assisted reproductive technique involving the union of sperm and oocyte outside the body.
- **ICSI**: Intracytoplasmic sperm injection, a specialized form of IVF wherein a single sperm is injected directly into an oocyte.
- **WHO**: World Health Organization, the international body that establishes diagnostic criteria for semen parameters.
- **PPF**: Plasma protein fraction, a supplement added to culture media to stabilize sperm and prevent premature capacitation.
- **CASA**: Computer-assisted sperm analysis, a digital imaging system used to quantify sperm motility parameters.
- **PI**: Propidium iodide, a fluorescent dye that stains dead or membrane-compromised cells.
- **TMRM**: Tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester, a potentiometric dye used to assess mitochondrial membrane potential.
- **MTG**: MitoTracker Green, a mitochondrial dye independent of membrane potential, used for structural labeling.
- **ΔΨm**: Mitochondrial membrane potential, an indicator of mitochondrial health and ATP-producing capacity.
- **VCL, VSL, VAP**: Curvilinear velocity, straight-line velocity, and average path velocity, respectively—standard CASA-derived metrics of sperm swimming speed.
- **n**: Sample size, denoting the number of sperm suspensions or patients in a given experimental group.
- **p**: Probability value used in statistical hypothesis testing; p < 0.05 indicates statistical significance.
- **CI**: Confidence interval, a range estimate of the true population parameter with a specified level of confidence (e.g., 90% or 95%).
- **SD**: Standard deviation, a measure of data dispersion around the mean.
- **ANOVA**: Analysis of variance, a statistical method for comparing means across multiple groups.
- **DIC**: Differential interference contrast, a microscopy technique for visualizing unstained live cells.
