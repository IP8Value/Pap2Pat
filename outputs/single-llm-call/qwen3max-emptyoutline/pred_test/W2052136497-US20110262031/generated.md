# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Image-based modeling and rendering have emerged as powerful techniques for reconstructing three-dimensional representations of real-world objects from a set of two-dimensional images. Among these methods, the visual hull approach—rooted in the classical “Shape-from-Silhouette” paradigm—has gained significant attention due to its computational efficiency, relative simplicity, and ability to produce visually plausible results without requiring complex surface reconstruction or dense correspondence matching. The core principle of the visual hull method is that each silhouette extracted from a calibrated reference image defines a viewing cone in three-dimensional space, and the intersection of all such cones yields a convex approximation of the object’s shape, known as the visual hull. This technique has been widely applied in domains such as virtual reality, digital heritage preservation, telepresence, and interactive visualization, where rapid generation of 3D models from minimal input is highly desirable.

Despite its advantages, the standard visual hull methodology suffers from inherent limitations that restrict its fidelity in representing certain geometric features of real objects. Two particularly problematic cases are the reconstruction of the bottom surface of an object and the accurate modeling of concave regions. These limitations arise not from algorithmic shortcomings per se, but from fundamental constraints imposed by the silhouette-based nature of the method. Specifically, because silhouettes only encode the outer boundary of an object as seen from a given viewpoint, any internal or occluded geometry—such as the underside of a teapot or the interior of a cup—remains invisible to the silhouette extraction process. Consequently, the visual hull, being the intersection of outward-facing cones, inevitably produces a convex envelope that fails to capture such non-convex or occluded surfaces. In practice, this manifests as geometric distortions: flat bottoms may appear as conical protrusions, and concave cavities may be filled in with spurious convex surfaces, leading to both topological inaccuracies and severe texture misalignments in the rendered output.

The problem of bottom reconstruction is exacerbated by practical constraints in image acquisition. To generate a complete visual hull, reference images must be captured from multiple viewpoints distributed around the object, ideally forming a full 360-degree coverage. However, capturing the bottom of the object typically requires either physically rotating the object or placing a camera beneath it—both of which introduce significant complications. If the object is rotated, the resulting bottom-view image exists in a different coordinate frame than the original set of images, necessitating a complex and error-prone alignment process to register the new view into the common reference system used for visual hull computation. Alternatively, if a fixed camera is placed below the object, the setup becomes mechanically cumbersome, especially for delicate or large artifacts, and may still fail to provide seamless integration with the upper views due to lighting inconsistencies, parallax errors, or calibration drift. Moreover, even when bottom images are successfully acquired, they often suffer from poor visibility due to reflections, shadows, or limited field-of-view, further degrading reconstruction quality.

Similarly, concave surface reconstruction presents a more profound theoretical challenge. By definition, a concavity is a region of the object that is not visible from external viewpoints; thus, no silhouette derived from an external camera can reveal its true shape. Traditional visual hull methods, which rely exclusively on external silhouettes, are therefore fundamentally incapable of distinguishing between a solid convex form and a hollow concave one—they will always reconstruct the former. While some prior approaches have attempted to mitigate this issue through the incorporation of additional cues—such as shading, stereo disparity, or user-provided annotations—these solutions often compromise the simplicity and efficiency that make visual hull methods attractive in the first place. Furthermore, they typically require specialized hardware, extensive manual intervention, or assumptions about surface reflectance that do not hold for general objects.

Several research efforts have explored the use of reflective surfaces, such as planar mirrors, to extend the effective field of view in image-based modeling. For instance, certain systems employ a single camera in conjunction with two mirrors arranged at an angle to synthesize multiple virtual viewpoints from a single physical capture, thereby enabling stereo reconstruction without camera calibration. Other approaches utilize a mirror-and-camera rig to simultaneously capture five distinct views of an object within a single image frame, leveraging the symmetry of reflection to infer 3D structure. While these methods demonstrate the utility of mirrors in expanding viewpoint diversity, they remain constrained by the same silhouette-based limitations: they cannot directly observe the bottom of an object unless the mirror is positioned underneath, and they offer no mechanism for resolving concavities that are not visible even in reflected views. Moreover, these systems often impose strict geometric constraints on the placement of the object and mirrors, limiting their flexibility and practicality in real-world settings.

In light of these challenges, there exists a clear and unmet need for an improved visual hull framework that can faithfully reconstruct both the bottom surfaces and concave regions of objects using a simple, efficient, and easily deployable image acquisition setup. Such a system should preserve the core advantages of traditional visual hull methods—namely, low computational cost, minimal hardware requirements, and compatibility with standard image-based rendering pipelines—while overcoming their most critical geometric deficiencies. Ideally, it should enable the simultaneous capture of top, bottom, and internal views within a unified reference frame, eliminating the need for post-hoc alignment or complex calibration procedures. Furthermore, it should incorporate a principled mechanism for integrating negative space information (i.e., knowledge about what is *not* part of the object) to approximate concave geometries without resorting to expensive volumetric or photometric computations. The present invention addresses precisely these needs through a novel combination of a dual-element optical platform and an enhanced visual hull algorithm that leverages virtual cameras and silhouette subtraction to achieve high-fidelity, photo-realistic reconstructions of previously inaccessible object features.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides an improved method and system for image-based visual hull reconstruction that overcomes the longstanding limitations of conventional approaches in accurately modeling the bottom surfaces and concave regions of three-dimensional objects. This is achieved through the synergistic integration of a novel image acquisition platform and an enhanced visual hull rendering algorithm, both of which are designed to operate within the existing framework of boundary-based visual hull methods while significantly extending their geometric and textural fidelity.

The image acquisition platform comprises a planar glass plate and a planar mirror, arranged in a vertically stacked configuration such that the mirror lies directly beneath the glass. The target object is placed on the upper surface of the glass plate, which is sufficiently transparent to allow direct imaging of the object’s top and side surfaces from above, while simultaneously permitting the mirror to reflect the object’s bottom surface. A single camera, positioned above the glass, captures a single composite image that contains both the direct view of the object and its mirror-reflected bottom view. This configuration enables the simultaneous acquisition of top and bottom visual information in a single exposure, ensuring that all data are inherently aligned within the same spatial and temporal reference frame. Crucially, the use of a planar mirror introduces a well-defined geometric relationship between the real and reflected views, which can be exploited to derive virtual camera parameters for the bottom view without requiring additional calibration or mechanical repositioning of the object or camera.

Building upon this acquisition platform, the invention further introduces an enhanced visual hull algorithm that extends the standard Image-Based Visual Hull (IBVH) framework to incorporate both bottom and concave surface information. For bottom reconstruction, the algorithm employs the concept of a “virtual camera,” which is defined as the mirror-symmetric counterpart of the real camera with respect to the mirror plane. Using the known symmetry of the mirror setup, the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of this virtual camera are analytically derived from those of the real camera, allowing the reflected bottom image to be treated as if it were captured by a physical camera located beneath the object. This virtual image is then seamlessly integrated into the visual hull computation as an additional reference view, enabling the reconstruction of the object’s bottom geometry with the same rigor and efficiency as the upper surfaces.

To address the challenge of concave surface reconstruction, the invention introduces a novel “concave silhouette cone” technique. When the object contains a concave region—such as the interior of a cup—a supplementary image is captured from a viewpoint directly above the concavity. From this image, the silhouette of the concave opening is extracted, and a virtual camera is positioned inside the concave volume, oriented to look outward. A virtual silhouette image is then synthesized from this internal viewpoint, effectively encoding the negative space of the concavity. During rendering, this virtual silhouette is used to generate a “negative” silhouette cone that is subtracted from the standard visual hull intersection. This subtraction operation carves out the concave region from the otherwise convex hull, yielding a more accurate approximation of the true object geometry. Critically, this subtraction is performed entirely in 2D image space during the epipolar line intersection step, preserving the computational efficiency of the original IBVH method.

The combined effect of these innovations is a visual hull system that produces photo-realistic renderings of objects from arbitrary viewpoints, with faithful reproduction of both bottom and concave features in both geometry and texture. The method requires no special hardware beyond a standard camera, a piece of planar glass, and a planar mirror, making it highly accessible and cost-effective. All reference images—real and virtual—are registered in a common coordinate system by design, eliminating the need for complex alignment procedures. Furthermore, the algorithm maintains the view-dependent, boundary-based nature of IBVH, ensuring that reconstruction and rendering remain computationally efficient and scalable to complex objects. Experimental results demonstrate significant improvements over conventional visual hull methods, with accurate rendering of bottom textures (e.g., labels or engravings on the underside of artifacts) and correct geometric representation of concave interiors (e.g., the inner walls of vessels), thereby enabling high-quality applications in digital archaeology, museum exhibition, product visualization, and other domains where geometric completeness and visual realism are paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to an improved system and method for image-based visual hull reconstruction that specifically addresses the persistent challenges of accurately modeling the bottom surfaces and concave regions of three-dimensional objects. The solution encompasses both a novel physical setup for image acquisition and an enhanced computational algorithm that together enable high-fidelity, photo-realistic rendering without sacrificing the efficiency and simplicity of traditional visual hull approaches. The following detailed description sets forth the structure, operation, and implementation of the invention with sufficient particularity to enable any person skilled in the art to make and use it.

The image acquisition platform forms the foundational component of the invention. It consists of three primary elements: a planar mirror, a planar glass plate, and a digital camera. The mirror is a standard flat reflective surface, typically made of glass with a silvered backing, and is dimensioned according to the size of the target objects to be reconstructed—commonly ranging from 300 mm × 300 mm to 1000 mm × 1000 mm, though larger or smaller variants may be employed as needed. The glass plate is a transparent, optically flat substrate, preferably made of low-iron glass to minimize color distortion, and is selected to be as thin as structurally feasible—typically between 3 mm and 10 mm in thickness—to reduce optical aberrations such as refraction and double reflection. The glass plate is positioned horizontally and supported above the mirror by a rigid frame or stand, maintaining a consistent vertical separation that is sufficient to accommodate the height of the target object while ensuring that the entire bottom surface remains visible in the mirror reflection. The distance between the glass and the mirror is not critical to the geometric calibration, as will be explained below, which affords considerable flexibility in object placement.

The target object is placed directly on the upper surface of the glass plate. A digital camera, equipped with a standard lens and capable of capturing high-resolution color images, is mounted on a tripod or robotic arm above the glass plate, oriented to look downward at the object. The camera is positioned such that its field of view encompasses both the object itself and its reflection in the mirror below. In a single photographic exposure, the camera thus records a composite image containing two distinct views: a direct (real) view of the object’s top and lateral surfaces, and a reflected (virtual) view of the object’s bottom surface. This simultaneous capture ensures perfect temporal and spatial coherence between the two views, eliminating motion blur, lighting inconsistencies, and registration errors that would arise from sequential imaging.

To facilitate camera calibration, the mirror surface is marked with a known calibration pattern, such as a grid of concentric colored circles or a checkerboard, which remains visible in the captured image through the transparent glass. Standard perspective-n-point (PnP) calibration techniques, such as the P4P algorithm, are then applied to compute the intrinsic parameters (focal length, principal point, skew, and lens distortion coefficients) and extrinsic parameters (rotation matrix and translation vector) of the camera relative to a world coordinate system. Critically, the world coordinate system is defined such that the X-Z plane coincides with the mirror surface, and the Y-axis is perpendicular to it, pointing upward. This choice simplifies subsequent geometric transformations, as the mirror plane becomes the plane of symmetry for virtual camera generation.

Once the reference images are acquired and calibrated, the enhanced visual hull algorithm proceeds in two main phases: preprocessing and rendering. During preprocessing, each composite reference image is segmented into foreground and background using standard image segmentation techniques (e.g., chroma keying, thresholding, or machine learning-based segmentation). This yields a binary silhouette mask for the entire scene. The mask is then partitioned into two components: a top silhouette corresponding to the direct view of the object, and a bottom silhouette corresponding to the reflected view. The top silhouette, along with the calibrated camera parameters, is used directly in the visual hull computation. The bottom silhouette, however, requires transformation to account for the mirror reflection.

This transformation is achieved through the introduction of a virtual camera. For each real camera position **C** with extrinsic parameters (**R**, **T**), a corresponding virtual camera **C'** is defined as the mirror image of **C** with respect to the mirror plane (i.e., the X-Z plane). Mathematically, if the real camera has translation vector **T** = [T_x, T_y, T_z]^T, then the virtual camera has translation vector **T'** = [T_x, -T_y, T_z]^T. Similarly, if the real camera’s orientation is described by rotation angles (α, β, γ) about the X, Y, and Z axes, respectively, then the virtual camera’s orientation is given by (-α, β, -γ), reflecting the parity inversion induced by the mirror. The intrinsic parameters of the virtual camera are identical to those of the real camera, as the optical properties are unchanged by reflection. With these parameters, the bottom silhouette is treated as if it were captured by the virtual camera **C'**, and is thus fully compatible with the standard visual hull pipeline.

For bottom rendering, an additional refinement step is employed to ensure geometric accuracy. Even with the virtual camera, the visual hull may still exhibit minor inaccuracies near the bottom plane due to quantization or silhouette noise. To correct this, the algorithm estimates the true height of the object’s bottom surface. This is done by identifying a set of points along the boundary between the object and its reflection in the reference images—typically 10 to 20 points distributed around the perimeter. For each such point, its 3D position on the preliminary visual hull is computed via ray-silhouette intersection. The average Y-coordinate of these points is then taken as an estimate of the bottom plane height, denoted Y_bottom. During rendering from a novel viewpoint, if the viewing ray for a pixel intersects the visual hull below Y_bottom, the intersection point is projected onto the plane Y = Y_bottom along the viewing ray direction. This “push-back” operation ensures that the bottom surface is rendered as a flat plane (or as close to flat as the data permit), eliminating spurious conical distortions.

The concave surface reconstruction module operates in parallel with the bottom rendering pipeline. When the target object contains a concave region—such as a bowl, cup, or cavity—a supplementary reference image is captured from a viewpoint directly above the concavity, ensuring that the opening of the concave region is fully visible. This image is segmented to extract the silhouette of the concave opening. A virtual camera **C_v** is then positioned inside the concave volume, at a location estimated from the geometry of the opening (e.g., at the centroid of the opening, offset inward along the surface normal). The orientation of **C_v** is set to look outward through the opening, and its focal length is adjusted so that the entire concave silhouette fits within its field of view. A virtual silhouette image **I'_c** is synthesized by projecting the concave silhouette curves onto the image plane of **C_v**. This virtual image encodes the negative space of the concavity and is used solely for geometric reconstruction, not texture synthesis.

During rendering, for each pixel in the novel view, the standard IBVH ray-intersection procedure is performed to obtain an initial 3D segment set **B** representing the intersection of the viewing ray with the standard visual hull. Simultaneously, the viewing ray is projected onto the virtual concave image **I'_c**, and its intersection with the virtual concave silhouette yields a 2D interval set, which is back-projected to obtain a 3D segment set **A** representing the intersection with the concave negative space. The final segment set is computed as **B \ A** (set difference), effectively removing the concave region from the convex hull. The nearest remaining intersection point is then used to sample texture from the real reference images, weighted by view proximity, to produce the final pixel color.

The entire system preserves the view-dependent, image-space efficiency of IBVH. All operations—silhouette binning, epipolar line intersection, virtual camera projection, and concave subtraction—are performed in 2D, avoiding costly 3D voxelization or mesh generation during rendering. When a static mesh is required, the implicit visual hull can be converted to an explicit triangle mesh using standard surface extraction algorithms such as Marching Cubes.

Experimental validation confirms that the invention produces significantly more accurate and visually realistic results than conventional visual hull methods. Objects with labeled bottoms, such as ceramic vases or electronic devices, show correct rendering of bottom textures without distortion. Concave objects like cups exhibit proper interior geometry and texture, with no filling-in of the cavity. The method is robust across a range of materials, including translucent objects like jade, where internal refraction might otherwise confuse silhouette extraction. The platform is simple to assemble, requiring only off-the-shelf components, and the algorithm integrates smoothly into existing IBVH implementations with minimal code modification.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, end-to-end solution for high-fidelity visual hull reconstruction that overcomes the two most critical limitations of prior art. By combining a clever optical setup with mathematically rigorous virtual camera modeling and negative silhouette subtraction, it achieves unprecedented accuracy in bottom and concave surface rendering while maintaining the speed, simplicity, and accessibility that have made visual hull methods popular in the first place.
