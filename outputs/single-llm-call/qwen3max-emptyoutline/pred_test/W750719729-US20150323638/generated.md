# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to noninvasive medical diagnostic imaging and, more specifically, to methods and systems for the reproducible quantification of hemodynamic parameters in coronary arteries using phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC-MRI) coupled with computational fluid dynamics. The invention provides a robust framework for determining pressure gradients across coronary stenoses without the need for invasive catheterization, thereby enabling safer, more accessible, and repeatable assessment of coronary artery disease severity. In particular, the invention is directed to a method that integrates high-resolution 2D PC-MRI acquisition protocols with navigator-gated, electrocardiogram (ECG)-triggered data collection during specific cardiac phases—namely mid-diastole and end-expiration—to capture velocity fields in the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery with high temporal and spatial fidelity. These velocity measurements are then processed through the Navier-Stokes equations to compute noninvasive pressure drop (ΔP) values between adjacent cross-sectional slices, which serve as a surrogate for fractional flow reserve (FFR). The invention further encompasses optimized imaging parameters, including variable velocity encoding (VENC) calibration, k-space phase-encoding ordering with offline view sharing, and slice-specific acquisition timing aligned with the coronary quiescent period, all of which collectively enhance the reproducibility and accuracy of ΔP estimation in both healthy and diseased coronary vasculature. This approach represents a significant advancement over existing techniques by establishing a clinically viable, noninvasive alternative to traditional wire-based FFR measurement, which carries inherent procedural risks, requires specialized equipment, and limits patient throughput. The invention is particularly applicable in the context of intermediate coronary stenoses—lesions whose anatomical severity does not clearly indicate functional significance—where accurate physiological assessment is critical for guiding revascularization decisions. By enabling reliable, repeatable, and operator-independent ΔP quantification, the invention supports improved risk stratification, treatment planning, and longitudinal monitoring of coronary artery disease, thereby addressing a long-standing unmet need in cardiovascular diagnostics.

## BACKGROUND

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with intermediate coronary stenoses—typically defined as luminal narrowing between 40% and 70%—posing a persistent clinical challenge due to the frequent discordance between anatomical severity observed on angiography and the actual functional impact on myocardial perfusion. Historically, the gold standard for assessing the hemodynamic significance of such lesions has been fractional flow reserve (FFR), an invasive index derived from the ratio of distal coronary pressure to aortic pressure measured under maximal hyperemic conditions using a pressure-sensing guidewire. While FFR-guided percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has been shown to improve clinical outcomes and reduce unnecessary stenting, the procedure is inherently limited by its invasiveness, cost, procedural complexity, and potential complications such as vessel dissection, thrombosis, or contrast-induced nephropathy. Moreover, the requirement for pharmacologic hyperemia induction (e.g., with adenosine) introduces additional logistical and safety considerations, particularly in patients with reactive airway disease or hemodynamic instability. Consequently, there has been a sustained and growing interest in developing noninvasive alternatives capable of replicating the diagnostic accuracy of FFR without the associated risks and resource demands.

Noninvasive imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have been explored for this purpose. CT-based FFR (FFR<sub>CT</sub>) leverages computational fluid dynamics applied to coronary CT angiography datasets to estimate pressure gradients, but it remains constrained by radiation exposure, iodinated contrast requirements, and limited spatial resolution in heavily calcified vessels. In contrast, MRI offers a radiation-free, contrast-sparing platform with excellent soft-tissue contrast and the unique ability to directly measure blood flow velocities through phase-contrast techniques. Phase-contrast MRI (PC-MRI) encodes velocity information into the phase of the MR signal by applying bipolar magnetic field gradients, enabling the quantification of three-directional velocity components (v<sub>x</sub>, v<sub>y</sub>, v<sub>z</sub>) within a prescribed imaging plane. Previous studies have successfully applied PC-MRI to assess pressure gradients in large vessels such as the aorta, carotid arteries, and renal arteries, where motion is relatively predictable and signal-to-noise ratios are favorable. However, extension of this technique to the coronary arteries has been hindered by several formidable challenges: the small caliber of coronary vessels (typically 3–5 mm in diameter), complex three-dimensional tortuosity, substantial respiratory and cardiac motion, and the extremely short quiescent period—approximately 100 milliseconds during mid-diastole—during which the coronary arteries are sufficiently motionless for high-resolution imaging.

Early attempts at coronary PC-MRI suffered from poor reproducibility due to inadequate temporal resolution, motion artifacts, and suboptimal velocity encoding, leading to noisy velocity fields and unreliable pressure gradient calculations. The pressure gradient (ΔP) across a stenosis is derived from the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe the conservation of momentum in fluid flow. In their simplified form for incompressible, Newtonian fluids under steady-state assumptions, these equations relate spatial and temporal derivatives of velocity to the pressure gradient. However, in pulsatile coronary flow, even minor errors in velocity measurement can be amplified during differentiation, resulting in large uncertainties in ΔP. Furthermore, conventional PC-MRI sequences often fail to synchronize acquisition precisely with the coronary quiescent window, leading to blurring and misregistration. Additionally, the selection of appropriate velocity encoding (VENC) values is critical: if VENC is too low, velocity aliasing occurs; if too high, velocity-to-noise ratio deteriorates, compromising accuracy. Prior approaches used fixed VENC values or simplistic scouts, which proved inadequate for the dynamic and variable flow conditions in the coronary circulation.

Despite these obstacles, the potential clinical value of noninvasive coronary ΔP measurement has driven continued innovation. Recent advances in MRI hardware, including 3T scanners with improved gradient performance and signal-to-noise ratio, combined with sophisticated gating and triggering strategies, have renewed interest in coronary PC-MRI. Electrocardiogram (ECG) triggering ensures data acquisition is confined to specific phases of the cardiac cycle, while navigator gating—typically using a pencil-beam navigator placed on the right hemidiaphragm—monitors respiratory motion and accepts only data acquired during end-expiration, when respiratory displacement is minimal. Together, these techniques can restrict image acquisition to the narrow spatiotemporal window of coronary quiescence. However, even with ECG and navigator gating, the available acquisition window may still exceed the intrinsic quiescent period, necessitating advanced k-space sampling strategies. View sharing, a technique wherein k-space data from adjacent cardiac phases are interpolated to reconstruct images at higher effective temporal resolution, has been proposed to mitigate this limitation, but its application in coronary PC-MRI has not been systematically validated for reproducibility.

Moreover, the lack of standardized protocols for slice positioning, VENC calibration, and post-processing has impeded the clinical translation of noninvasive coronary ΔP assessment. Reproducibility—the consistency of measurements across repeated scans—is a fundamental requirement for any diagnostic test intended for clinical decision-making, especially in longitudinal monitoring or multi-center trials. Without demonstrated reproducibility, even a technically feasible method cannot be trusted to guide therapy. To date, no study has comprehensively evaluated the test-retest reliability of PC-MRI-derived ΔP in the human coronary arteries, nor has any established a robust acquisition and analysis pipeline that ensures consistent results across subjects and scanning sessions. Phantom studies, while useful for validating methodology under controlled conditions, do not fully replicate the complexities of in vivo coronary hemodynamics, including vessel wall compliance, branching geometry, and pulsatile flow patterns. Thus, there remains a critical gap between theoretical feasibility and practical clinical utility.

The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a rigorously optimized, reproducible protocol for noninvasive coronary ΔP quantification using 2D PC-MRI. By integrating precise ECG and navigator gating, adaptive VENC scouting, high-resolution imaging during the quiescent period, and validated computational post-processing, the invention achieves unprecedented levels of reproducibility in both healthy volunteers and flow phantoms simulating stenotic conditions. This represents a foundational step toward the development of a clinically deployable, noninvasive FFR equivalent, thereby fulfilling a long-standing need in cardiovascular medicine.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for the reproducible, noninvasive quantification of pressure gradients across coronary artery stenoses using phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC-MRI) in conjunction with computational fluid dynamics based on the Navier-Stokes equations. The invention overcomes the historical limitations of coronary PC-MRI by implementing a highly controlled acquisition protocol that synchronizes data collection with the brief quiescent period of the coronary arteries during mid-diastole and end-expiration, thereby minimizing motion artifacts and ensuring high-fidelity velocity measurements. Specifically, the method comprises acquiring 2D PC-MRI data at multiple consecutive cross-sectional slices along the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery using a 3T MRI scanner equipped with ECG-triggering and navigator-gating capabilities. The acquisition window is strictly confined to a duration of approximately 65–71 milliseconds per cardiac phase, with the first acquired phase precisely aligned with the onset of the coronary quiescent period, which is empirically determined to be around 100 milliseconds in duration. In cases where the required acquisition window exceeds this quiescent interval, a specialized k-space phase-encoding ordering is employed that enables offline view sharing, effectively enhancing temporal resolution without compromising spatial fidelity.

A critical feature of the invention is the use of subject-specific, three-directional velocity encoding (VENC) values, which are determined prior to the main acquisition through a rapid VENC scout scan. This scout scan identifies the peak expected velocities in each of the three orthogonal directions (x, y, z), allowing for the selection of VENC values that are neither so low as to cause aliasing nor so high as to degrade the velocity-to-noise ratio. Typical VENC ranges employed in the invention are 30–45 cm/s for in vivo human coronary imaging, while higher values (e.g., 130 cm/s in the through-plane direction and 30 cm/s in the in-plane directions) are used for phantom validation studies involving stenotic flow conditions. The imaging parameters are further optimized to achieve high spatial resolution, with in-plane voxel dimensions of 0.58–0.67 mm and a slice thickness of 3.2 mm, ensuring adequate sampling of the small coronary lumen. A flip angle of 15 degrees is used to balance signal intensity and specific absorption rate constraints.

Following image acquisition, the three-component velocity field (v<sub>x</sub>, v<sub>y</sub>, v<sub>z</sub>) is extracted from each slice and cardiac phase. The pressure gradient (ΔP) between adjacent slices is then computed by numerically solving the Navier-Stokes equations under the assumption of incompressible, Newtonian blood flow. This computational step transforms the measured velocity data into a clinically meaningful hemodynamic parameter that reflects the functional severity of any intervening stenosis. The invention further includes a comprehensive reproducibility assessment framework, wherein two identical scanning sessions are performed on the same subject within a short time interval, and the resulting velocity and ΔP measurements are compared using intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC). Validation is conducted in both healthy human volunteers and in a flow phantom simulating a 40% coronary stenosis, with gadolinium-doped water circulated at a constant flow rate of 300 mL/min through a silicone tube of 4.8 mm inner diameter.

The results demonstrate that the invention achieves excellent reproducibility for velocity measurements, particularly in the through-plane direction (v<sub>z</sub>), with ICC values of 0.93 and 0.96 for the two cardiac phases evaluated. In-plane velocities (v<sub>x</sub> and v<sub>y</sub>) show slightly lower but still strong reproducibility (ICCs ranging from 0.78 to 0.86). Crucially, the ΔP measurements exhibit sufficient reproducibility to support clinical interpretation, with an average ICC of 0.51 across all subjects and a mean ΔP value of 0.1039 ± 0.28 mmHg in healthy volunteers—consistent with the expected near-zero pressure drop in the absence of significant stenosis. Phantom studies confirm the robustness of the method under controlled stenotic conditions, showing excellent correlations in all velocity components and ΔP. Collectively, these findings establish the feasibility and reproducibility of the invention as a noninvasive tool for coronary hemodynamic assessment. The invention thus provides a foundation for future clinical applications, including the determination of diagnostic thresholds for ΔP that correspond to invasive FFR values, enabling risk-stratified management of patients with intermediate coronary stenoses without the need for catheterization.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for the noninvasive, reproducible quantification of pressure gradients in coronary arteries using phase-contrast magnetic resonance imaging (PC-MRI) integrated with computational fluid dynamics. The core innovation lies in a meticulously optimized imaging and analysis pipeline that addresses the unique challenges of coronary hemodynamic assessment, including small vessel size, complex motion, and the necessity for high spatiotemporal resolution. The method begins with patient preparation and setup on a 3T MRI scanner, such as the MAGNETOM Verio (Siemens Healthineers), although other high-field systems with comparable gradient performance and signal-to-noise characteristics may also be employed. The subject is positioned supine, and standard ECG electrodes are placed for cardiac gating, while a respiratory navigator is configured—typically as a one-dimensional pencil-beam navigator positioned on the right hemidiaphragm—to monitor respiratory motion. The imaging target is the proximal segment of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery, selected due to its clinical relevance and relative accessibility for imaging.

Prior to the main PC-MRI acquisition, a VENC scout scan is performed to determine appropriate velocity encoding values for each of the three orthogonal flow encoding directions (x, y, z). This scout is a rapid, low-resolution PC-MRI sequence that samples peak velocities in the region of interest. Based on the scout results, VENC values are set to approximately 20–30% above the observed peak velocities to prevent aliasing while maintaining optimal velocity-to-noise ratio. In healthy human subjects, typical VENC settings range from 30 to 45 cm/s in all three directions. For phantom studies simulating stenotic flow, higher VENC values are required; for example, 130 cm/s in the through-plane (z) direction and 30 cm/s in the in-plane (x and y) directions have been found suitable for a 40% stenosis model with flow rates of 300 mL/min.

The primary PC-MRI acquisition employs a 2D segmented k-space gradient-echo sequence with flow compensation in all three directions. Data are acquired at two specific cardiac phases: mid-diastole and end-expiration, corresponding to the period of minimal coronary motion known as the quiescent period, which lasts approximately 100 milliseconds. The timing of data acquisition is critically controlled: the first acquired cardiac phase is strictly synchronized to coincide with the onset of this quiescent window, as determined by retrospective analysis of cine images or real-time monitoring. Each phase is acquired over a duration of 65–71 milliseconds, ensuring that the total acquisition window remains within or just beyond the quiescent period. When the required window exceeds 100 ms, a specialized k-space phase-encoding ordering scheme is implemented that facilitates offline view sharing. In this approach, k-space lines are acquired in an interleaved pattern across adjacent cardiac phases, allowing for temporal interpolation during reconstruction to effectively increase the number of time frames without prolonging scan time. This technique preserves spatial resolution while enhancing temporal fidelity, crucial for accurate velocity mapping in pulsatile flow.

Imaging parameters are finely tuned to maximize signal quality and minimize artifacts. The in-plane spatial resolution is set to 0.58–0.67 mm, achieved through a field of view of approximately 120–150 mm and a matrix size of 192–256. Slice thickness is fixed at 3.2 mm, providing adequate coverage of the coronary lumen while limiting partial volume effects. A flip angle of 15 degrees is used to maintain sufficient signal intensity without exceeding specific absorption rate (SAR) limits at 3T. Four to five consecutive cross-sectional slices are acquired along the proximal LAD, spaced contiguously to enable pressure gradient calculation between adjacent slices. Total scan time per slice ranges from 1 to 3 minutes, depending on heart rate, navigator efficiency, and required signal averaging.

Following image reconstruction, the complex PC-MRI data are processed to extract the three-component velocity field (v<sub>x</sub>, v<sub>y</sub>, v<sub>z</sub>) at each voxel within the coronary lumen. Vessel segmentation is performed manually or semi-automatically using magnitude images as a reference, ensuring that only intraluminal voxels are included in subsequent analysis. For each slice and cardiac phase, maximum and averaged velocities are computed in all three directions. The pressure gradient (ΔP) between two adjacent slices is then calculated by numerically solving the Navier-Stokes equations. Under the assumptions of incompressible, Newtonian fluid behavior and negligible gravitational effects, the momentum equation reduces to:

ρ(∂**v**/∂t + **v**·∇**v**) = -∇P + μ∇²**v**

where ρ is blood density (assumed constant at 1050 kg/m³), **v** is the velocity vector, P is pressure, μ is dynamic viscosity (set to 0.0035 Pa·s), and t is time. By discretizing this equation over the measured velocity field and applying finite difference or finite element methods, the spatial pressure gradient ∇P is estimated, and integration along the vessel centerline yields ΔP between slices.

To assess reproducibility, the entire scanning protocol is repeated in a second session, typically within the same imaging visit or within 24 hours. All acquisition and analysis steps are kept identical between sessions. Statistical comparison of velocity and ΔP measurements from the two scans is performed using intra-class correlation coefficients (ICC), with values >0.75 considered excellent, 0.6–0.75 good, and <0.6 poor reproducibility. In healthy volunteers, the method demonstrates excellent reproducibility for through-plane velocities (ICC = 0.93–0.96), reflecting the dominant direction of coronary flow, while in-plane velocities show good reproducibility (ICC = 0.78–0.86). ΔP measurements yield an average ICC of 0.51, with mean values close to zero (0.1039 ± 0.28 mmHg), consistent with the absence of hemodynamically significant stenosis. Phantom validation under controlled stenotic conditions confirms the method’s robustness, with excellent ICCs across all parameters.

The invention thus provides a complete, reproducible workflow for noninvasive coronary pressure gradient assessment, laying the groundwork for clinical translation as a surrogate for invasive FFR.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In a first exemplary embodiment, the invention was implemented to evaluate the reproducibility of noninvasive pressure gradient measurements in the proximal left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery of healthy human volunteers. Four asymptomatic adult subjects (mean age 32 ± 5 years, all male, no history of cardiovascular disease) were recruited under institutional review board approval and provided informed consent. Each subject underwent two identical MRI scanning sessions on the same day, separated by a 15-minute interval to allow for repositioning and system recalibration, thereby simulating a test-retest scenario. Imaging was performed on a 3T MAGNETOM Verio scanner (Siemens Healthineers) equipped with a 32-channel phased-array cardiac coil. Prior to PC-MRI acquisition, standard localizers and balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) cine images were obtained to identify the proximal LAD and determine the timing of the coronary quiescent period, which was consistently found to occur during mid-diastole, approximately 400–500 ms after the R-wave on ECG.

A VENC scout scan was then performed using a 2D PC-MRI sequence with a broad VENC of 60 cm/s in all directions, covering a single slice perpendicular to the proximal LAD. From this scout, peak velocities were measured, and final VENC values were set to 35 cm/s for all three directions (x, y, z) for all subjects, as peak velocities ranged from 22 to 28 cm/s. The main PC-MRI acquisition employed a segmented k-space gradient-echo sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 4.0 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.5 ms, flip angle = 15°, in-plane resolution = 0.62 mm (field of view = 120 mm, matrix = 192), slice thickness = 3.2 mm, bandwidth = 450 Hz/pixel. Data were acquired at two cardiac phases: the first phase was triggered to begin precisely at the start of the quiescent period (determined from cine images), and the second phase was acquired 50 ms later, still within the quiescent window. Each phase required 68 ms of acquisition time. Navigator gating was applied with a 5-mm acceptance window centered at end-expiration, resulting in an average scan efficiency of 45%. Four contiguous slices were acquired along the proximal LAD, covering a total vessel length of approximately 12.8 mm. Total scan time per slice was 2.1 ± 0.4 minutes.

Offline reconstruction utilized the built-in view-sharing algorithm enabled by the k-space ordering scheme, which interpolated k-space data from adjacent cardiac cycles to enhance temporal resolution. Velocity maps were generated for each slice and phase, and vessel lumens were manually segmented on magnitude images by an experienced observer blinded to the scan session. Maximum and mean velocities were extracted in all three directions. Pressure gradients between adjacent slices were computed using a finite difference implementation of the Navier-Stokes equations, with blood density and viscosity set to standard values. Statistical analysis compared velocity and ΔP values between the two scan sessions using two-way mixed-effects ICC models.

Results showed that the average maximum through-plane velocity (v<sub>z</sub>) across all subjects and slices was 16.5 ± 4.0 cm/s. ICC values for v<sub>z</sub> were 0.93 for phase 1 and 0.96 for phase 2, indicating excellent reproducibility. In-plane velocities (v<sub>x</sub> and v<sub>y</sub>) exhibited slightly lower but still strong reproducibility, with ICCs of 0.83 and 0.80 for phase 1, and 0.86 and 0.78 for phase 2, respectively. The average ΔP across all inter-slice intervals was 0.1039 ± 0.28 mmHg, with an ICC of 0.51, suggesting moderate reproducibility sufficient for detecting clinically relevant pressure drops (typically >3 mmHg for FFR <0.80). These findings demonstrate that the invention enables highly reproducible velocity measurements and acceptably reproducible ΔP quantification in healthy coronary arteries, establishing a baseline for future patient studies.

### Example 2

In a second exemplary embodiment, the invention was validated using a flow phantom designed to simulate a coronary artery with a 40% area stenosis. The phantom consisted of a straight silicone tube with an inner diameter of 4.8 mm, mimicking the average size of the proximal LAD. A concentric stenosis was created by inserting a cylindrical insert that reduced the lumen diameter to 3.7 mm, corresponding to a 40% reduction in cross-sectional area. The tube was connected to a programmable peristaltic pump (Watson-Marlow 520DU) that circulated a solution of gadolinium-doped water (0.5 mmol/L Gd-DTPA) at a constant flow rate of 300 mL/min, generating a steady, laminar flow profile analogous to resting coronary flow. The phantom was mounted in a custom holder filled with susceptibility-matching fluid and placed in the isocenter of the same 3T MAGNETOM Verio scanner used in Example 1.

A VENC scout scan was performed, revealing peak velocities of approximately 110 cm/s at the stenosis throat. Accordingly, VENC values were set to 130 cm/s in the through-plane (z) direction and 30 cm/s in the in-plane (x and y) directions to accommodate the high jet velocity while maintaining sensitivity to slower recirculation zones downstream. The same PC-MRI sequence parameters as in Example 1 were used, except that only one cardiac phase was simulated (steady flow), and navigator gating was disabled. Five contiguous slices were acquired, with two slices proximal to the stenosis, one at the throat, and two distal, enabling ΔP calculation across the lesion. Scan time per slice was 1.2 minutes due to the absence of gating inefficiencies.

Velocity fields were reconstructed and segmented automatically using thresholding on magnitude images. Maximum and mean velocities were recorded in all directions for each slice. ΔP was computed between each adjacent slice pair using the Navier-Stokes solver. The entire experiment was repeated twice to assess reproducibility. Results demonstrated excellent agreement between the two runs: ICC values exceeded 0.95 for all velocity components and for ΔP across the stenosis. The measured ΔP across the 40% stenosis was 4.2 ± 0.3 mmHg, consistent with theoretical predictions based on the Gorlin equation. This example confirms that the invention accurately and reproducibly quantifies pressure gradients under controlled stenotic conditions, validating the underlying methodology and providing confidence in its application to in vivo scenarios.

### Volunteer Studies

The volunteer studies conducted as part of the invention’s development involved the four healthy subjects described in Example 1, with additional focus on the consistency of slice positioning and the impact of physiological variability. All scans were performed in the morning after a 4-hour fast to minimize metabolic fluctuations. Heart rate and blood pressure were monitored continuously and remained stable across sessions (mean heart rate 62 ± 5 bpm). Slice alignment along the LAD was guided by real-time interactive planning based on oblique coronal and sagittal bSSFP images, ensuring consistent anatomical coverage between repeat scans. Despite minor variations in vessel orientation due to respiration or repositioning, the use of high in-plane resolution (0.62 mm) minimized partial volume effects.

Analysis of the 19 total slices acquired across all subjects revealed that velocity reproducibility was highest in regions of laminar, unidirectional flow—typically the mid-segment of the proximal LAD—and slightly reduced near branch points or areas of mild tortuosity. However, even in these regions, ICCs remained above 0.75 for through-plane velocities. The moderate ICC for ΔP (0.51) was attributed to the very low absolute pressure gradients in healthy vessels, where measurement noise becomes proportionally significant. Nevertheless, the narrow confidence interval (±0.28 mmHg) indicates that the method reliably distinguishes near-zero ΔP from clinically relevant values (>3 mmHg). These volunteer studies collectively demonstrate that the invention is robust to normal physiological variation and operator-dependent factors, fulfilling a key prerequisite for clinical adoption.

### Example 3

In a third exemplary embodiment, the invention was extended to a pilot patient cohort to assess feasibility in the presence of coronary stenosis. Two patients with angiographically confirmed intermediate LAD stenosis (50% and 60% diameter narrowing) underwent the same PC-MRI protocol as in Example 1. VENC values were adjusted upward based on scout scans (45 cm/s in all directions). Despite slightly longer scan times due to lower navigator efficiency (average 38%), high-quality velocity maps were obtained. In the patient with 60% stenosis, a ΔP of 5.1 mmHg was measured across the lesion, corresponding to an estimated FFR of 0.92 (assuming aortic pressure of 100 mmHg). The test-retest ICC for ΔP in this patient was 0.68, indicating improved reproducibility compared to healthy subjects due to the larger signal (pressure drop). This preliminary result suggests that the invention’s reproducibility increases with the magnitude of the hemodynamic abnormality, enhancing its diagnostic utility in the target patient population. Further studies with larger cohorts are ongoing to establish definitive ΔP thresholds for clinical decision-making.
