# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of computer vision and artificial intelligence, specifically to systems and methods for video anomaly detection. More particularly, the invention relates to an unsupervised, zero-shot cross-domain video anomaly detection framework that operates without requiring any training data from the target domain in which anomalies are to be detected. The invention further encompasses a novel architecture comprising a future-frame prediction generator, a Normalcy Classifier module, and an Untrained Convolutional Neural Network-based Anomaly Synthesis module, all integrated to enable robust generalization across diverse visual domains without domain adaptation or fine-tuning. The technical scope includes real-time surveillance applications, intelligent monitoring systems, and automated security infrastructure where labeled anomaly data is unavailable, impractical to collect, or subject to privacy and intellectual property constraints. The invention also introduces the strategic utilization of task-irrelevant video datasets—originally curated for unrelated computer vision tasks such as action recognition or gesture classification—as auxiliary sources of normal behavioral patterns and pseudo-anomalous content to enhance model generalizability. This approach fundamentally redefines the paradigm of unsupervised anomaly detection by shifting from absolute modeling of normality to relative discrimination between normal and abnormal visual semantics, thereby enabling deployment in completely unseen environments with no prior calibration or data collection.

## BACKGROUND

Video anomaly detection (VAD) has long been a critical component in automated surveillance and security systems, tasked with identifying unusual or potentially hazardous activities in video streams without explicit supervision for every possible anomaly type. Traditional approaches to VAD have largely fallen into two categories: supervised and unsupervised methods. Supervised VAD techniques require extensive annotated datasets containing both normal and abnormal events, which are inherently difficult to obtain due to the rarity, unpredictability, and ethical sensitivity of anomalous occurrences such as violence, theft, or accidents. Consequently, the research community has gravitated toward unsupervised VAD methods, which operate under the assumption that only normal event videos are available during training. These models learn the statistical or structural characteristics of normal behavior and flag deviations during inference as anomalies. Early unsupervised VAD systems relied on handcrafted features—such as optical flow, trajectory clustering, or spatiotemporal histograms—to model regular patterns, but these were limited in expressiveness and scalability.

With the advent of deep learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and generative models have significantly advanced unsupervised VAD performance. A prominent strategy involves training a generator network—often within a generative adversarial network (GAN) or autoencoder framework—to reconstruct or predict future video frames based solely on sequences of normal activity. The underlying hypothesis is that the model will accurately reconstruct normal frames but fail on anomalous ones, with reconstruction error serving as an anomaly score. Some of these methods incorporate memory modules to store prototypical normal patterns, enhancing the model’s capacity to generalize across diverse normal scenarios. However, a fundamental limitation of these approaches is their strong dependence on the source domain distribution; when deployed in a new environment (e.g., a different camera angle, lighting condition, or scene layout), performance degrades significantly due to domain shift.

To address this, recent works have introduced cross-domain VAD (xVAD) frameworks, which aim to detect anomalies in a target domain using training data from a different source domain. While promising, existing xVAD methods still impose practical constraints that hinder real-world applicability. For instance, some require access to unlabeled video data from the target domain during training or adaptation phases, which may be unavailable due to privacy policies, data ownership restrictions, or operational limitations. Others rely on pre-trained object detectors—such as YOLOv3 or CenterNet—to extract foreground objects as strong priors for anomaly localization. These detectors introduce significant computational overhead, increase model complexity, and assume the availability of large-scale object detection datasets that may not align with the target domain’s visual semantics. Moreover, such reliance on external models compromises the end-to-end trainability and self-sufficiency of the VAD system.

A more severe limitation is the assumption that the types of anomalies in the target domain resemble those implicitly learned or synthesized in the source domain. Since unsupervised models only observe normal data, they cannot anticipate novel anomaly categories absent from the source context. This leads to poor generalization when the target domain exhibits entirely different forms of abnormal behavior. Furthermore, the exclusive focus on modeling "normalcy" in isolation—without explicit contrastive learning against abnormal patterns—results in feature representations that are overly specific to the source domain and lack discriminative power in unfamiliar settings.

Compounding these issues is the scarcity of large-scale, diverse VAD training datasets. Standard benchmarks like UCSD Ped1/Ped2, Avenue, or ShanghaiTech contain fewer than 100 training videos, severely limiting the richness of normal behavioral patterns that can be learned. This data paucity exacerbates overfitting and reduces robustness. In contrast, other computer vision domains—such as action recognition (e.g., UCF101, HMDB51) or gesture classification (e.g., Jester)—offer orders of magnitude more video data, yet these have remained untapped in VAD due to perceived task irrelevance. Prior art has not established a principled mechanism to leverage such task-irrelevant (TI) datasets, which, despite differing in semantic labels, often depict coherent, uninterrupted human activities that closely resemble the "normal" sequences sought in VAD.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a truly zero-shot cross-domain VAD system—one that requires no target domain data, no pre-trained object detectors, no domain adaptation, and no assumptions about anomaly types—while simultaneously achieving high accuracy, computational efficiency, and broad generalizability. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel learning paradigm centered on *relative normalcy*: instead of modeling normal events in absolute terms, the system learns to distinguish normal from abnormal by explicitly contrasting them during training, using synthetically generated pseudo-anomalies derived from both VAD-relevant and task-irrelevant video sources. This approach, combined with a training-free object localization mechanism based on randomly initialized CNNs, enables unprecedented out-of-the-box performance across diverse surveillance scenarios.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for zero-shot cross-domain video anomaly detection (zxVAD) that overcomes the limitations of prior art by eliminating the need for any target domain training data, pre-trained object detectors, or domain adaptation procedures. The core innovation lies in a novel architectural framework that integrates three key components: a future-frame prediction generator with a memory module, a Normalcy Classifier (NC) module, and an Untrained Convolutional Neural Network-based Anomaly Synthesis (O) module. Unlike conventional unsupervised VAD systems that model only normal event distributions, zxVAD employs a *relative learning strategy* wherein the generator is trained to differentiate normal video frames from synthetically generated pseudo-abnormal frames. This is achieved through four complementary loss functions within the NC module that jointly optimize the model to recognize both the global coherence of normal scenes and the localized disruption caused by foreign objects in anomalous frames.

The O module generates pseudo-anomalies in a computationally efficient, training-free manner by leveraging the surprising ability of randomly initialized CNNs to localize foreground objects in video frames. Given an input frame—whether from a VAD dataset or a task-irrelevant (TI) dataset such as UCF101 or HMDB—the O module processes it through an untrained CNN (e.g., ResNet152) and applies channel-wise summation to produce an attention map. A binary mask is then derived by thresholding this map, effectively isolating foreground regions without any learned parameters. This mask is used to extract an object patch, which is pasted onto a normal VAD training frame at a random location and scale to create a pseudo-abnormal frame. Critically, this process works equally well on TI data, enabling the synthesis of diverse anomalies containing objects never seen in the source VAD domain.

The NC module is trained using four novel loss functions: (1) a Normalcy Loss that classifies predicted future frames as normal and pseudo-abnormal frames as abnormal; (2) a Relative Normalcy Loss that enforces a probabilistic ordering wherein normal frames are deemed *more normal* than pseudo-abnormal ones; (3) an Attention Affirmation Loss that ensures the classifier’s decision is grounded in spatially accurate regions—whole-scene attention for normal frames and object-localized attention for abnormal frames; and (4) a Relative Attention Affirmation Loss, implemented via ArcFace metric learning, that enhances intra-class compactness among normal and augmented-normal frames while maximizing inter-class separation from pseudo-abnormal frames.

A further distinguishing feature of zxVAD is its strategic incorporation of task-irrelevant (TI) video datasets. These datasets, originally designed for non-VAD tasks like action recognition, provide abundant examples of continuous, coherent human activities that serve as effective proxies for "normal" behavior in surveillance contexts. The invention demonstrates that zxVAD can be trained using only TI data—without any VAD-specific training videos—and still achieve state-of-the-art performance on standard VAD benchmarks. This capability is enabled by the relative learning framework, which does not require domain-specific anomaly definitions but instead learns general principles of visual coherence versus disruption.

The zxVAD system is trained end-to-end using a combination of reconstruction losses (mean squared error, SSIM, gradient loss), adversarial losses from a PatchGAN discriminator, memory sparsity regularization, and the four NC losses. During inference, the system operates in a fully unsupervised, zero-shot manner: given a test video from an unseen target domain, it predicts future frames using the generator, computes reconstruction errors, and outputs normalized anomaly scores without any adaptation or fine-tuning. Experimental results confirm that zxVAD outperforms existing xVAD methods—including those requiring target domain data or object detectors—in both same-dataset and cross-dataset evaluations, while simultaneously reducing model size, parameter count, energy consumption, and computational complexity. The invention thus provides a practical, scalable, and highly generalizable solution for real-world video surveillance applications where data access is restricted and anomaly types are unknown.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### System Overview

The zxVAD system is an end-to-end trainable deep learning framework designed for unsupervised video anomaly detection in cross-domain settings without any access to target domain training data. The system architecture comprises three primary modules: a Generator (G), a Discriminator (D), and a Normalcy Classifier (N), along with an auxiliary Anomaly Synthesis (O) module that operates without trainable parameters. The Generator G is implemented as a U-Net-style encoder-decoder network augmented with a memory module at its bottleneck layer, following established practices in future-frame prediction for VAD. It takes a sequence of T consecutive video frames as input and predicts the (T+1)-th future frame. The memory module stores a set of prototype vectors representing recurring normal spatiotemporal patterns, enabling the generator to reconstruct diverse normal behaviors by attending to relevant memory slots. The Discriminator D is a PatchGAN that distinguishes between the generator’s predicted frames and ground-truth future frames, providing adversarial feedback to improve realism.

The core innovation resides in the Normalcy Classifier N and the Anomaly Synthesis O modules, which together implement the relative normalcy learning strategy. The O module receives video frames from either VAD-relevant training data or task-irrelevant (TI) datasets (e.g., UCF101, HMDB51, Jester). For each input frame x ∈ ℝ^(C×H×W), it passes x through a randomly initialized CNN R(·)—such as ResNet152, DenseNet161, or AlexNet—without updating R’s weights during training. The feature tensor G ∈ ℝ^(d×h×w) output by R’s final convolutional layer is processed via Sum of Channel-wise Differences Activation (SCDA) to produce an attention map A ∈ ℝ^(h×w). A binary mask M is obtained by thresholding A at a fixed value ς = 0.1, where M(i,j) = 1 indicates foreground object presence. This mask is resized to the original frame dimensions and applied to x to isolate the foreground object, yielding M ⊙ x. The object patch is then pasted onto a randomly selected normal VAD training frame v_t at a random location r_z and random scale (r_x × r_y), producing a pseudo-abnormal frame ṽ. This synthesis process requires no training, object annotations, or domain-specific tuning, making it universally applicable across datasets.

The Normalcy Classifier N is a lightweight CNN (e.g., PatchGAN architecture) trained to distinguish between the generator’s predicted normal frame v and the pseudo-abnormal frame ṽ. Its training is governed by four loss functions. First, the Normalcy Loss L_N applies binary cross-entropy to enforce correct classification: N(v) → 1 (normal), N(ṽ) → 0 (abnormal). Second, the Relative Normalcy Loss L_RN maximizes the probability that v is *more normal* than ṽ, formulated as -log σ(N(v) - N(ṽ)), where σ is the sigmoid function. Third, the Attention Affirmation Loss L_AA ensures spatial fidelity: for v, the attention map A(v) (derived via SCDA on N’s features) should be uniformly distributed across the entire frame (enforced by minimizing ||A(v) - 1||²); for ṽ, A(ṽ) should align with the ground-truth anomaly mask M (minimizing ||A(ṽ) - M||²). Fourth, the Relative Attention Affirmation Loss L_RAA uses ArcFace metric learning to embed attention maps into a hyperspherical space where normal and augmented-normal frames (via color jitter, affine transforms) form tight clusters separated by a margin m from pseudo-abnormal frames.

The full training objective combines generator reconstruction losses (L_rec = α_MSE·MSE + α_SSIM·(1 - SSIM) + α_GD·L_GD), adversarial loss (L_D), memory sparsity loss (L_MEM = entropy of memory addressing weights), and NC losses (L_N = α_n·L_N + α_rn·L_RN + α_aa·L_AA + α_raa·L_RAA). All components are optimized jointly via Adam optimizer with domain-specific learning rates. At inference, zxVAD processes each test video frame-by-frame: it predicts future frames using G, computes per-frame Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) between prediction and ground truth, normalizes PSNR scores to [0,1] per video, and outputs the complement as the anomaly score. No target domain data, adaptation, or post-processing is required. The system’s reliance on relative discrimination—rather than absolute normality modeling—and its use of diverse pseudo-anomalies from TI data enable robust zero-shot generalization across unseen domains, as validated on benchmarks including ShanghaiTech, UCFCrime, Ped1/Ped2, and Avenue.
