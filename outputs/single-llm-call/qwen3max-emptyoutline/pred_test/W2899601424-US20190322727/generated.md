# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of immunoassay-based detection of antimicrobial resistance determinants, specifically to methods and reagents for the direct detection of mobile colistin resistance (MCR) proteins, particularly MCR-1 and MCR-2, in biological and environmental samples. More particularly, the invention provides novel recombinant protein standards, high-affinity polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies that specifically recognize MCR-1 and/or MCR-2, and a highly sensitive and specific sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) configured for the detection of these resistance-conferring enzymes in complex matrices such as bacterial cultures, food products, and clinical specimens. The invention further encompasses kits and diagnostic platforms incorporating these reagents for rapid, reliable, and cost-effective identification of colistin-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, thereby enabling timely surveillance, containment, and mitigation of the spread of plasmid-mediated colistin resistance in human and animal populations.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Colistin, also known as polymyxin E, is a cationic polypeptide antibiotic that has historically been used as a last-resort therapeutic agent against multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacterial infections. Its mechanism of action involves binding to the anionic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) components of the outer bacterial membrane, particularly the lipid A moiety, through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged phosphate groups. This binding displaces divalent cations such as Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ that stabilize the LPS layer, leading to disruption of membrane integrity, increased permeability, and ultimately bacterial cell lysis and death. Due to its nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity, colistin was largely abandoned for systemic human use in the 1970s and 1980s in favor of safer alternatives. However, the global rise of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) and other extensively drug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens has necessitated the reintroduction of colistin into clinical practice as a critical last-line defense.

For many years, resistance to colistin was believed to be exclusively chromosomal in origin, arising from mutations in regulatory genes such as *pmrA*, *pmrB*, *phoP*, and *phoQ*, which control the expression of enzymes that modify lipid A by adding positively charged groups such as phosphoethanolamine (PEA) or 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose (L-Ara4N). These modifications reduce the net negative charge of the bacterial surface, thereby diminishing the electrostatic attraction between colistin and its target, resulting in reduced binding and resistance. While such chromosomal mutations can lead to clonal outbreaks in healthcare settings, they are not readily transferable between bacterial species, limiting their epidemiological impact.

This paradigm shifted dramatically in November 2015 with the discovery of the *mcr-1* gene, a plasmid-borne determinant of colistin resistance first identified in *Escherichia coli* isolates from food animals and humans in China. The *mcr-1* gene encodes a phosphoethanolamine transferase enzyme—designated MCR-1—that catalyzes the addition of PEA to the 4′-phosphate group of lipid A, effectively neutralizing the negative charge and conferring resistance to colistin. Critically, because *mcr-1* resides on a conjugative plasmid, it can be horizontally transferred across diverse bacterial species, including clinically relevant pathogens such as *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Salmonella enterica*, and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. Since its initial report, *mcr-1* has been detected in over 40 countries across five continents, in isolates from humans, livestock, poultry, retail meat, and environmental sources, signaling a global public health emergency.

Subsequently, additional homologous genes—*mcr-2* through *mcr-10*—have been identified, each encoding variant MCR enzymes with varying degrees of sequence identity to MCR-1 (ranging from 32% to 83% at the amino acid level) but sharing a conserved functional architecture: an N-terminal transmembrane domain anchoring the protein to the inner membrane and a C-terminal soluble catalytic domain responsible for PEA transfer. Among these, MCR-1 and MCR-2 are the most prevalent and well-characterized, with MCR-2 exhibiting even higher prevalence than MCR-1 in some porcine and bovine *E. coli* isolates. The co-occurrence of *mcr* genes with carbapenemase genes on the same plasmids has given rise to pan-drug-resistant strains that are untreatable by any currently available antibiotics, posing a dire threat to modern medicine.

In response to this crisis, accurate and rapid detection of MCR-producing bacteria is essential for infection control, antimicrobial stewardship, and public health surveillance. Current diagnostic approaches fall into two broad categories: phenotypic and genotypic. Phenotypic methods, such as broth microdilution (BMD) for minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination, are considered the gold standard but suffer from significant limitations. Colistin’s polycationic nature causes it to adhere to plastic surfaces and laboratory equipment, leading to inconsistent drug concentrations and unreliable MIC results. Moreover, these methods require 16–24 hours of culture, delaying clinical decision-making.

Genotypic methods, including conventional PCR, real-time PCR, and whole-genome sequencing (WGS), offer faster turnaround times and do not require live culture. However, they detect only the presence of *mcr* nucleic acid sequences, not the functional protein product. This distinction is critical because the mere presence of an *mcr* gene does not guarantee expression of a functional enzyme; promoter mutations, transcriptional silencing, or translational defects can render the gene nonfunctional. Consequently, PCR-based assays may yield false-positive results when detecting non-expressing or cryptic *mcr* sequences. Conversely, false negatives can occur due to primer mismatches caused by genetic variability in emerging *mcr* variants or inhibition by substances in complex samples like stool or food. WGS, while comprehensive, is prohibitively expensive, technically demanding, and inaccessible in most routine diagnostic laboratories, especially in resource-limited settings.

There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a diagnostic method that directly detects the MCR protein—the actual effector of colistin resistance—thereby providing unambiguous evidence of functional resistance. Immunoassays, which rely on antigen-antibody interactions, represent an ideal solution due to their high specificity, sensitivity, speed, and adaptability to high-throughput formats. However, prior to the present invention, no validated antibodies against MCR-1 or MCR-2 were commercially available, and the development of such reagents was hindered by the hydrophobic, membrane-associated nature of full-length MCR proteins, which are difficult to express and purify in soluble form.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention overcomes the limitations of existing diagnostic methods by providing the first comprehensive set of reagents and assays for the direct, protein-level detection of MCR-1 and MCR-2. Specifically, the invention discloses the successful production of recombinant MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins, including both full-length and catalytic domain variants, which serve as critical standards and immunogens. Using these recombinant proteins, the inventors have generated high-affinity polyclonal antibodies (pAbs) and a panel of nine monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with defined specificities for MCR-1 and/or MCR-2. These antibodies exhibit exceptional binding characteristics and enable the development of a robust sandwich ELISA capable of detecting MCR proteins at sub-nanogram per milliliter concentrations in buffer and at bacterial cell densities as low as 0.4 × 10⁶ colony-forming units (cfu) per milliliter in culture.

The invention further demonstrates that the sandwich ELISA maintains high sensitivity and specificity in complex food matrices, including ground beef, chicken, and pork, even when spiked with as few as 10 cfu of MCR-1-producing bacteria per 25 grams of meat. This performance is achieved without extensive sample purification, highlighting the assay’s tolerance to interfering substances commonly found in biological and environmental samples. The ELISA configuration utilizes a polyclonal antibody as the capture reagent and a selected monoclonal antibody (MCR-1-7) as the detection reagent, leveraging the polyclonal antibody’s ability to bind multiple epitopes for enhanced capture efficiency and the monoclonal antibody’s high affinity and cross-reactivity with both MCR-1 and MCR-2 for broad detection capability.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a method for detecting MCR-1 and/or MCR-2 in a sample, comprising: (a) contacting the sample with a capture antibody that specifically binds to MCR-1 or MCR-2 under conditions permitting formation of an antibody-antigen complex; (b) contacting the complex with a detection antibody that specifically binds to a different epitope on MCR-1 or MCR-2; and (c) detecting the presence of the detection antibody, wherein detection indicates the presence of MCR-1 or MCR-2 in the sample. The capture antibody may be a polyclonal antibody raised against the catalytic domain of MCR-1, and the detection antibody may be a monoclonal antibody selected from the group consisting of MCR-1-1 through MCR-1-9, preferably MCR-1-7 for broad reactivity or MCR-1-9 for MCR-1-specific detection.

In another embodiment, the invention provides a kit for detecting MCR-1 and/or MCR-2, comprising: (i) a solid support coated with a capture antibody that specifically binds to MCR-1 or MCR-2; (ii) a detection antibody that specifically binds to a different epitope on MCR-1 or MCR-2, optionally conjugated to a detectable label; and (iii) instructions for use. The kit may further include positive and negative controls, such as purified recombinant cMCR-1 or cMCR-2 protein and MCR-negative bacterial lysate.

The invention also encompasses the recombinant proteins themselves, including full-length MCR-1 (residues 1–541), full-length MCR-2 (residues 1–538), catalytic domain of MCR-1 (cMCR-1, residues 214–541), and catalytic domain of MCR-2 (cMCR-2, residues 212–538), each optionally comprising an N-terminal or C-terminal affinity tag (e.g., His₆-tag) for purification. These proteins are useful not only as immunogens and assay standards but also as tools for structural and functional studies of MCR enzymes.

By directly detecting the functional MCR protein, the methods and reagents of the present invention provide a more accurate and reliable indicator of colistin resistance than nucleic acid-based methods. They enable rapid screening of clinical, veterinary, food, and environmental samples, facilitating early intervention to prevent the spread of mobile colistin resistance. The simplicity, affordability, and robustness of the ELISA format make it suitable for deployment in routine diagnostic laboratories worldwide, including those in developing countries where the burden of antimicrobial resistance is often highest.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Materials and Methods

The present invention is based on the development and characterization of recombinant MCR proteins, high-affinity antibodies against these proteins, and a sandwich ELISA platform for their detection. The following sections describe the construction, expression, and purification of recombinant MCR-1 and MCR-2 proteins; the generation and validation of polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies; and the optimization and performance evaluation of the ELISA in various sample types.

**Construction, Expression, and Purification of Recombinant MCR Proteins**  
The genes encoding full-length MCR-1 (GenBank accession AKF16168.1, residues 1–541) and its catalytic domain (cMCR-1, residues 214–541) were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using plasmid pBSKSII-KanR-mcr1 as a template. Similarly, genes for full-length MCR-2 (GenBank NG_051171.1, residues 1–538) and its catalytic domain (cMCR-2, residues 212–538) were synthesized and amplified. All four constructs were cloned into the pET His6 TEV LIC cloning vector (Addgene #29653), which adds an N-terminal His₆-tag for purification. The resulting plasmids were transformed into *E. coli* BL21(DE3) cells for protein expression. Cultures were grown in LB medium supplemented with 50 µg/mL kanamycin at 37°C until an OD₆₀₀ of 0.6 was reached, at which point expression was induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and continued at 16°C for 20 hours to promote proper folding and solubility.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation and lysed by sonication in buffer A (300 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, 20 mM imidazole, pH 8.0, 10% glycerol) containing protease inhibitors and DNase. For full-length MCR proteins, which contain five transmembrane domains and are highly insoluble, 1% Triton X-100 was included in the lysis buffer to solubilize membrane fractions. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 1 hour, and the supernatant was filtered and loaded onto a 5-mL HisTrap FF column. Bound proteins were eluted with buffer A containing 500 mM imidazole. The catalytic domain proteins (cMCR-1 and cMCR-2), being soluble, were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superdex 200 column to remove aggregates and contaminants. For cMCR-1, the His₆-tag was removed by incubation with tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease prior to SEC to eliminate potential interference with antibody binding. Protein concentrations were determined by UV absorbance at 280 nm using calculated extinction coefficients. The integrity of full-length MCR-1 was confirmed by mass spectrometry, which covered 60.6% of the protein sequence, validating the production of intact recombinant protein despite challenges in solubilizing the hydrophobic N-terminus.

**Antibody Production**  
Polyclonal antibodies were generated by immunizing two New Zealand White rabbits with 300 µg of purified, tag-free cMCR-1 emulsified in adjuvant at three-week intervals for four injections. Serum titers were monitored by direct ELISA, and high-titer bleeds were pooled and purified by Protein A affinity chromatography. Monoclonal antibodies were produced by immunizing female Balb/cJ mice with 100 µL of cMCR-1 (50 µg/mL in Sigma Adjuvant System) via intraperitoneal injection at two-week intervals. After three immunizations, mice with strong serum titers were boosted with a fourth dose of antigen in PBS without adjuvant. Three days later, splenocytes were fused with SP2/0 myeloma cells using polyethylene glycol. Hybridoma supernatants were screened by direct ELISA for binding to cMCR-1, and positive clones were isolated by limiting dilution. Nine stable hybridoma lines (MCR-1-1 through MCR-1-9) were expanded, and antibodies were purified from culture supernatants using Protein G affinity chromatography. Isotyping revealed that eight mAbs were IgG1, kappa, and one (MCR-1-7) was IgG2b, kappa.

**ELISA Development and Validation**  
Direct ELISAs were performed by coating microtiter plates with cMCR-1, blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk in TBST, incubating with primary antibodies, and detecting with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies followed by chemiluminescent substrate. Sandwich ELISAs were configured by first coating plates with rabbit polyclonal anti-MCR-1 IgG (1 µg/mL in PBS), then adding samples containing MCR protein, followed by detection with biotinylated or HRP-conjugated mAb MCR-1-7 (100 ng/mL). Luminescence was measured in counts per second (cps). The limit of detection (LOD) was defined as the lowest analyte concentration yielding a signal greater than background plus three standard deviations. Precision was assessed by calculating intra-assay (% CV from triplicates within a run) and inter-assay (% CV from means across three independent runs) variability. Specificity was evaluated by testing against MCR-negative bacterial lysates and unrelated proteins.

**Sample Testing**  
To simulate real-world conditions, ground beef, chicken, and pork were purchased from retail sources and inoculated with 10 cfu of MCR-1-positive (*E. coli* AR-Bank #346 or #349) or MCR-negative (*E. coli* ATCC25922) strains per 25 g of meat. Samples were enriched in 75 mL of tryptic soy broth containing 2 µg/mL colistin for 16 hours at 37°C to selectively promote the growth of resistant bacteria. Post-enrichment, bacterial cells were lysed with B-PER reagent, and lysates were analyzed directly by sandwich ELISA without further purification. Recovery rates and matrix effects were calculated by comparing ELISA signals from meat samples to those from pure culture controls.

### Results and Discussion

The successful execution of the above methods yielded a suite of reagents and data that collectively demonstrate the feasibility and utility of immunoassay-based detection of MCR proteins. The key findings are summarized below.

**Production of Recombinant MCR Standards**  
Full-length MCR-1 and MCR-2, as well as their catalytic domains (cMCR-1 and cMCR-2), were successfully expressed in *E. coli* and purified to near homogeneity. SDS-PAGE and Western blot analyses confirmed that the catalytic domains migrated at the expected molecular weight of ~38.4 kDa, while full-length proteins appeared smaller than the predicted 61 kDa, likely due to anomalous migration caused by their hydrophobic transmembrane regions. Mass spectrometry coverage of 60.6% for full-length MCR-1 verified that the recombinant protein was intact, despite the inability to detect the N-terminal 100 amino acids—a common issue with hydrophobic membrane proteins. These purified proteins served as essential tools for immunization, antibody screening, and assay calibration.

**Generation and Characterization of Antibodies**  
Both polyclonal and monoclonal antibodies exhibited high specificity and affinity for MCR-1. The rabbit polyclonal IgG recognized both recombinant cMCR-1 and full-length MCR-1 in Western blots and showed no cross-reactivity with MCR-negative bacterial lysates. Among the nine monoclonal antibodies, six (MCR-1-1, -2, -3, -5, -7, -8) cross-reacted with both MCR-1 and MCR-2, while three (MCR-1-4, -6, -9) were specific to MCR-1 only. This differential reactivity reflects the ~87% amino acid identity in the catalytic domains of MCR-1 and MCR-2, with cross-reactive mAbs targeting conserved epitopes and MCR-1-specific mAbs recognizing divergent regions. Notably, mAb MCR-1-7 demonstrated the highest binding affinity in direct ELISA, making it the optimal choice for detection in the sandwich format. The availability of both broad-spectrum and type-specific antibodies provides flexibility for different applications: surveillance programs can use cross-reactive mAbs to detect all MCR variants, while source-tracking studies can employ MCR-1-specific mAbs to differentiate between resistance mechanisms.

**Performance of the Sandwich ELISA**  
The sandwich ELISA, configured with polyclonal anti-MCR-1 as the capture antibody and mAb MCR-1-7 as the detection antibody, exhibited excellent analytical performance. In phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the assay detected cMCR-1 with a limit of detection (LOD) of 0.01 ng/mL and a linear range of 0.01–4 ng/mL (R² = 0.98), and cMCR-2 with an LOD of 0.1 ng/mL and a linear range of 0.1–6 ng/mL (R² = 0.99). The slightly lower sensitivity for cMCR-2 is attributable to the fact that both antibodies were raised against MCR-1, resulting in suboptimal recognition of MCR-2 epitopes. Precision was outstanding, with intra-assay % CV < 5% and inter-assay % CV < 15%, meeting or exceeding FDA CBER guidelines for bioanalytical assays. Specificity was confirmed by the absence of signal in MCR-negative bacterial lysates even at high cell densities (8 × 10⁸ cfu/mL).

**Detection in Complex Matrices**  
The true innovation of the present invention lies in the assay’s robustness in complex food matrices. When ground beef, chicken, and pork were spiked with 10 cfu of MCR-1-positive bacteria per 25 g and subjected to selective enrichment, the ELISA unambiguously detected MCR-1 in all positive samples, with luminescence counts significantly above the LOD (907 cps). Although matrix effects reduced the signal intensity to 7–13% of that observed in pure culture (due to interference from fats, proteins, and other meat components), the assay remained sufficiently sensitive to detect contamination at levels far below what is typically found in retail meat. Crucially, no false positives were observed in samples inoculated with MCR-negative strains or buffer alone, demonstrating high specificity even in challenging backgrounds. These results validate the ELISA as a practical tool for food safety monitoring, where rapid, on-site screening can prevent contaminated products from entering the supply chain.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a complete solution for the direct detection of functional MCR proteins, addressing a critical gap in the global fight against antimicrobial resistance. By moving beyond nucleic acid detection to target the actual resistance enzyme, the methods and reagents disclosed herein offer superior accuracy, reliability, and applicability across diverse sample types. The modular design of the ELISA—using well-characterized antibodies and standardized protocols—ensures reproducibility and scalability, paving the way for widespread adoption in clinical, veterinary, and public health laboratories worldwide.
