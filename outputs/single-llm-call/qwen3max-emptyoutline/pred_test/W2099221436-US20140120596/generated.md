# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of lignocellulosic biomass conversion and, more specifically, to an improved pretreatment method for enhancing the enzymatic digestibility of lignocellulosic feedstocks such as agricultural and forestry residues. The invention provides a novel alkaline twin-screw extrusion (ATSE) process that integrates mechanical disruption, chemical delignification, and controlled thermal conditioning in a continuous, energy-efficient, and scalable manner. This process is particularly suited for the conversion of corn stover and other non-wood lignocellulosic materials into fermentable sugars for the production of biofuels, biochemicals, and value-added bioproducts. The invention further encompasses the design and configuration of a specialized twin-screw extruder comprising alternating transport and reversed screw elements that generate high shear, compression, and frictional forces, thereby enabling effective fibrillation and structural modification of biomass at relatively low temperatures without the need for external heating. The technical domain of this invention intersects with chemical engineering, bioprocessing, renewable energy technologies, and sustainable industrial biotechnology, with direct applicability to integrated biorefineries and pulp-and-paper mill retrofits seeking to valorize lignocellulosic waste streams.

The invention addresses long-standing challenges in the pretreatment of recalcitrant lignocellulosic biomass, including inefficient lignin removal, excessive carbohydrate degradation, high energy consumption, chemical recovery difficulties, and scalability limitations inherent in conventional pretreatment methods such as dilute acid hydrolysis, steam explosion, ammonia fiber expansion (AFEX), and organosolv processes. By leveraging the synergistic effects of mechanical size reduction and alkaline chemistry within a single-unit operation, the ATSE process achieves high delignification and sugar yields while minimizing xylan loss and operational complexity. Moreover, the invention is designed to be compatible with existing industrial infrastructure, particularly alkali-based chemical recovery systems used in kraft or soda pulping mills, thereby reducing capital expenditure and facilitating commercial deployment. The technical scope also includes the recovery of high-quality lignin from the spent liquor—a byproduct with significant potential in the production of polymeric materials such as phenol-formaldehyde resins and concrete additives—thus contributing to the economic viability and circularity of the overall biorefinery concept. The invention is therefore positioned at the forefront of sustainable biomass utilization technologies aimed at displacing fossil-derived resources and advancing the principles of a circular bioeconomy.

## BACKGROUND

The global transition toward sustainable energy and chemical production has intensified research efforts into the utilization of lignocellulosic biomass as a renewable feedstock. Lignocellulosic materials, including agricultural residues such as corn stover, rice straw, wheat straw, and bagasse, as well as forestry wastes like sawdust, represent abundant, low-cost, and non-food sources of carbohydrates that can be converted into liquid fuels, platform chemicals, and biomaterials. However, the natural structural complexity of lignocellulose—comprising cellulose microfibrils embedded in a matrix of hemicellulose and encased by a lignin sheath—imparts significant recalcitrance to enzymatic and chemical degradation. This recalcitrance arises from multiple interrelated factors, including the crystallinity of cellulose, the degree of polymerization of polysaccharides, the presence of acetyl groups on hemicellulose, the physical barrier function of lignin, and the limited accessible surface area of the biomass. Among these, lignin plays a particularly detrimental role in enzymatic saccharification by sterically blocking enzyme access to cellulose and promoting non-productive binding of cellulases, thereby reducing hydrolysis efficiency and sugar yields.

To overcome this recalcitrance, pretreatment is an essential step in any lignocellulosic biorefinery process. A wide array of pretreatment technologies has been developed, broadly categorized as physical, chemical, physicochemical, and biological methods. Physical methods such as milling reduce particle size but are energy-intensive and impractical for large-scale applications. Chemical methods include acid hydrolysis (e.g., dilute sulfuric acid), which effectively solubilizes hemicellulose but generates fermentation inhibitors like furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) and requires corrosion-resistant equipment. Alkaline pretreatments using sodium hydroxide, lime, or ammonia disrupt lignin-carbohydrate linkages and solubilize lignin, but often require long residence times, high chemical loadings, and complex recovery systems. Organosolv processes use organic solvents to extract high-purity lignin but suffer from high solvent costs and flammability risks. Physicochemical methods such as steam explosion, ammonia fiber explosion (AFEX), and hot water treatment combine heat and pressure to disrupt biomass structure; however, they typically operate at elevated temperatures (130–220°C), necessitating high-pressure reactors and substantial energy input, and may still yield suboptimal sugar recoveries or require extensive detoxification steps.

Extrusion-based pretreatment has emerged as a promising alternative due to its continuous operation, mechanical simplicity, and potential for integration with downstream processes. Early studies employed single-screw or twin-screw extruders, sometimes in combination with chemicals or heat, to pretreat biomass such as corn stover, switchgrass, and wood. For example, Karunanithy and Muthukumarappan used a single-screw extruder with varying temperatures and screw speeds but achieved only moderate sugar yields and required fine particle size reduction prior to extrusion. Kadam et al. developed a two-stage twin-screw extrusion process with NaOH addition, reporting high sugar yields but operating at temperatures exceeding 114°C, thus requiring external heating and increasing operational complexity. Other extrusion methods either omitted chemical additives—resulting in insufficient delignification—or used acids, which introduced corrosion and inhibitor formation issues. Furthermore, most prior extrusion studies were conducted at laboratory scale, raising questions about scalability, reproducibility, and economic feasibility in industrial settings.

A critical limitation of many existing pretreatment technologies is the trade-off between delignification efficiency and carbohydrate preservation. Excessive alkali or acid loading, prolonged reaction times, or high temperatures often lead to significant degradation of hemicellulose, particularly xylan, which is more labile than cellulose under alkaline conditions. This not only reduces total sugar yield but also increases the burden on wastewater treatment due to dissolved organic matter. Additionally, the requirement for severe size reduction (e.g., grinding biomass to 1–2 mm particles) prior to pretreatment adds substantial preprocessing costs and energy consumption. Another challenge lies in the recovery and reuse of chemicals and byproducts. In alkaline processes, the spent liquor contains dissolved lignin and inorganic salts, and efficient recovery is essential for process economics and environmental sustainability. However, non-wood biomass such as corn stover contains high levels of silica, which reacts with sodium hydroxide to form sodium silicate, potentially fouling evaporators and interfering with causticizing in conventional recovery systems.

Despite these advances, there remains a need for a pretreatment method that simultaneously achieves high enzymatic digestibility, high carbohydrate recovery, minimal inhibitor formation, low energy consumption, compatibility with coarse feedstock, and seamless integration with existing industrial infrastructure. The present invention addresses these unmet needs through the development of an alkaline twin-screw extrusion (ATSE) process that uniquely combines mechanical fibrillation, in-situ alkaline delignification, and controlled post-extrusion heat preservation—all conducted at near-ambient pressure and relatively low temperature (approximately 99°C) without external heating. The process leverages the heat generated by friction and shear during extrusion, enabling efficient chemical penetration and reaction while preserving glucan and minimizing xylan loss. Moreover, the ATSE system operates at a high solid-to-liquid ratio (1:2 w/w), reducing water usage and simplifying downstream processing. The invention thus represents a significant advancement over prior art by offering a scalable, cost-effective, and environmentally sound solution for lignocellulosic biomass deconstruction.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides an improved method for the pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass to enhance its enzymatic digestibility and facilitate the efficient recovery of fermentable sugars, particularly glucose and xylose, while concurrently enabling the valorization of lignin as a co-product. The method, designated as Alkaline Twin-Screw Extrusion (ATSE), comprises the continuous feeding of lignocellulosic biomass into a co-rotating twin-screw extruder equipped with a specific arrangement of transport screw elements (TSE) and reversed screw elements (RSE). Within the extruder, the biomass is subjected to intense mechanical forces—including compression, shear, and friction—generated by the interaction between the TSE and RSE, resulting in physical disruption, fibrillation, and size reduction of the biomass fibers. Concurrently, an aqueous alkaline solution, preferably sodium hydroxide, is introduced into the extruder at a controlled dosage relative to the dry weight of the biomass, typically in the range of 0.04 to 0.1 grams of NaOH per gram of oven-dried biomass. The mechanical energy imparted during extrusion elevates the temperature of the biomass-alkali mixture to approximately 99°C without the need for external heating, thereby facilitating the chemical delignification process in situ.

Following extrusion, the pretreated biomass slurry is subjected to a heat preservation step at ambient pressure and substantially the same temperature for a duration ranging from 0 to 10 hours, although optimal results are achieved with a preservation time of 1 hour. This post-extrusion conditioning enhances lignin solubilization and further improves substrate accessibility without causing significant carbohydrate degradation. The preserved slurry is then washed to neutrality to remove residual alkali and dissolved components, yielding a solid fraction enriched in cellulose and hemicellulose and a liquid fraction containing solubilized lignin, hemicellulose-derived sugars, and inorganic salts. The solid fraction exhibits markedly improved enzymatic digestibility, with glucan and xylan conversions exceeding 80% under standard enzymatic hydrolysis conditions, corresponding to total sugar yields of up to 80.2% based on the theoretical carbohydrate content of the original biomass. Notably, the process achieves a delignification efficiency of up to 83%, with a preferred operating condition of 71% lignin removal at a NaOH loading of 0.06 g/g biomass and 1-hour heat preservation, while preserving over 90% of the original glucan and minimizing xylan loss to less than 22%.

The invention further encompasses the recovery of lignin from the spent liquor via spray-drying or other concentration techniques, yielding a lignin product with a weight-average molecular weight (Mw) of approximately 22,780 Da, a number-average molecular weight (Mn) of 16,430 Da, and a polydispersity index (PDI) of 1.386, indicating a relatively narrow molecular weight distribution suitable for downstream applications in polymer synthesis. The ATSE process is designed to operate at a high solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:2 (w/w), reducing water consumption and facilitating integration with existing chemical recovery systems, such as those employed in modern pulp mills. Importantly, the method requires only minimal size reduction of the feedstock—cutting biomass into 2–5 cm pieces suffices—thereby eliminating the need for energy-intensive grinding operations. The entire process is continuous, scalable to pilot and industrial capacities (demonstrated at 200 kg/h of dry biomass), and compatible with non-wood feedstocks despite their high silica content, though alternative alkalis such as sodium carbonate may be employed to mitigate silicate-related issues in chemical recovery.

In summary, the ATSE method of the present invention overcomes key limitations of prior pretreatment technologies by integrating mechanical and chemical deconstruction in a single, energy-efficient unit operation that operates at low temperature, high solids loading, and minimal preprocessing, while delivering high sugar yields, effective delignification, and a valuable lignin co-product. The invention is particularly advantageous for deployment in integrated biorefineries or retrofitted pulp mills, where it can leverage established infrastructure for chemical and energy recovery, thereby reducing capital costs and accelerating commercial adoption.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a novel and highly effective method for pretreating lignocellulosic biomass to overcome its natural recalcitrance and render it amenable to efficient enzymatic hydrolysis for the production of fermentable sugars. The core of the invention lies in the Alkaline Twin-Screw Extrusion (ATSE) process, which synergistically combines mechanical disruption, in-situ alkaline delignification, and controlled thermal conditioning in a continuous, scalable, and energy-efficient manner. The following detailed description sets forth the essential components, operational parameters, and mechanistic principles of the ATSE process, as well as its advantages over conventional pretreatment methods.

The lignocellulosic biomass suitable for use in the present invention includes, but is not limited to, agricultural residues such as corn stover, rice straw, wheat straw, sugarcane bagasse, and barley straw; forestry residues such as sawdust, wood chips, and bark; and dedicated energy crops such as miscanthus, switchgrass, and reed canary grass. The biomass is preferably dried and cut into pieces ranging from 2 to 5 centimeters in length, which represents a significant advantage over prior art methods that require fine grinding to particle sizes below 2 millimeters. This coarse feedstock preparation minimizes preprocessing energy and cost while maintaining high pretreatment efficiency, as the mechanical action of the extruder itself achieves sufficient size reduction and fibrillation.

The central apparatus of the ATSE process is a co-rotating twin-screw extruder, which is specifically engineered to generate high shear, compression, and frictional forces necessary for effective biomass deconstruction. The extruder barrel houses two intermeshing, co-rotating screws, each composed of a series of modular screw elements. Critically, the screw configuration includes alternating segments of transport screw elements (TSE) and reversed screw elements (RSE). The TSE are standard forward-pitch elements that convey the biomass along the extruder barrel. The RSE, in contrast, have a reverse pitch direction, creating zones of backpressure and accumulation where the biomass is compressed and subjected to intense mechanical stress. As the biomass is forced through the narrow gaps between the TSE and RSE, it undergoes repeated cycles of compression, shearing, and decompression, leading to fiber separation, fibrillation, and a substantial increase in specific surface area. This mechanical action not only physically breaks down the biomass structure but also generates significant frictional heat, raising the internal temperature of the biomass to approximately 99°C without any external heating source. This self-heating characteristic is a key innovation of the present invention, as it eliminates the need for energy-intensive external heaters and enables the process to operate at a relatively low temperature compared to steam explosion or AFEX.

During the extrusion process, an aqueous alkaline solution is introduced into the extruder barrel at a precisely controlled point downstream of the initial compression zone. The alkali is preferably sodium hydroxide (NaOH), although other alkaline agents such as potassium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide, or sodium carbonate may be used depending on feedstock composition and downstream recovery considerations. The alkali dosage is a critical parameter and is typically maintained in the range of 0.04 to 0.1 grams of NaOH per gram of oven-dried biomass. Dosages below 0.04 g/g are insufficient for effective delignification, while dosages above 0.1 g/g lead to excessive xylan degradation and reduced total sugar yield. The alkali solution is mixed with the biomass at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:2 (w/w), which is significantly higher than the ratios used in many conventional alkaline pretreatments (which often exceed 1:10). This high solids loading reduces water consumption, lowers wastewater treatment burden, and enhances the concentration of dissolved lignin in the spent liquor, facilitating its recovery.

Upon exiting the extruder, the pretreated biomass slurry is immediately transferred to a sealed container or continuous insulated conveyor for a heat preservation step. This step is conducted at ambient pressure and at the temperature attained during extrusion (approximately 99°C), with no additional heating or cooling. The preservation time can range from 0 to 10 hours, but experimental results demonstrate that a duration of 1 hour is optimal, providing substantial improvements in delignification and enzymatic digestibility without incurring significant carbohydrate losses or operational delays. Longer preservation times beyond 3 hours yield diminishing returns, as the rate of lignin removal plateaus and the risk of xylan degradation increases slightly. After the preservation period, the slurry is washed thoroughly with water until the effluent reaches neutrality (pH ~7), typically using a 300-mesh nylon cloth or similar filtration medium. The washed solid fraction is then stored at 4°C for subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis or further processing.

The enzymatic hydrolysis of the pretreated solid is performed under standard conditions using a cocktail of cellulase and β-glucosidase enzymes. A typical protocol involves a 2% (w/v) solids loading in a citrate buffer (pH 4.8) at 50°C with shaking at 100 rpm for 48 hours. The enzyme loading is approximately 20 FPU of cellulase and 5 IU of β-glucosidase per gram of substrate. Under these conditions, the ATSE-pretreated biomass achieves glucan conversions of up to 83% and xylan conversions of up to 89%, corresponding to a total sugar yield of 78–80% based on the theoretical carbohydrate content of the raw biomass. These results are markedly superior to those obtained with untreated biomass, which yields only 17.5% glucan and 5.1% xylan conversion under identical hydrolysis conditions.

The liquid fraction, or spent liquor, contains solubilized lignin, degraded hemicellulose sugars, and inorganic salts. The lignin can be recovered by concentration and drying, for example using a spray dryer, yielding a powder with a relatively high molecular weight and low polydispersity, making it suitable for applications in adhesives, resins, and composite materials. The inorganic fraction, particularly in the case of NaOH-based pretreatment, can be recovered using established chemical recovery systems analogous to those in the kraft pulping process, including evaporation, combustion in a recovery boiler, and causticizing. Although silica present in non-wood biomass may form sodium silicate and pose challenges in recovery, the use of sodium carbonate as an alternative alkali can mitigate this issue by retaining silica in the solid fraction and eliminating the need for causticizing.

The ATSE process has been successfully demonstrated at pilot scale with a throughput of 200 kg of oven-dried corn stover per hour, achieving consistent results across multiple runs. The energy consumption of the extrusion step is approximately 240 kWh per ton of dry biomass, which is competitive with or lower than many conventional pretreatment methods when accounting for the elimination of external heating and fine grinding. The screw speed, tested in the range of 100–325 rpm, has minimal impact on sugar yield, allowing operational flexibility. Overall, the ATSE process represents a robust, scalable, and economically viable solution for lignocellulosic biomass pretreatment, with clear advantages in terms of sugar yield, lignin quality, energy efficiency, and process integration.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the Alkaline Twin-Screw Extrusion (ATSE) process was applied to corn stover collected from Qingdao, Shandong Province, China, to evaluate the effects of NaOH dosage and heat preservation time on biomass composition and enzymatic digestibility. The corn stover was initially cut into 2–5 cm pieces and characterized as containing 36.2% glucan, 22.1% xylan, and 18.5% acid-insoluble lignin on a dry weight basis. The pretreatment was conducted using a pilot-scale co-rotating twin-screw extruder (Tianzheng Co., China) with a capacity of 200 kg of oven-dried corn stover per hour. The extruder was configured with four transport screw elements (TSE) and four reversed screw elements (RSE) per screw, arranged to maximize compression and shear. The biomass was fed continuously into the extruder, and an aqueous NaOH solution was injected at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:2 (w/w). The NaOH dosage was varied at 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, and 0.1 g per gram of oven-dried biomass. Following extrusion, the slurry was sealed in plastic bags and preserved at ambient temperature (maintaining the extrusion temperature of approximately 99°C) for 0, 1, 2, 3, or 10 hours. After preservation, the samples were washed to neutrality and analyzed for compositional changes and enzymatic hydrolysis performance.

Compositional analysis revealed that glucan was well preserved across all conditions, with recoveries consistently above 96%, attributed to the semi-crystalline nature of cellulose. In contrast, xylan recovery decreased with increasing NaOH dosage, ranging from 78.3% at 0.06 g/g NaOH to 60.7% at 0.1 g/g NaOH after 1 hour of preservation, indicating greater susceptibility of hemicellulose to alkaline degradation. Lignin removal was highly dependent on NaOH dosage, with reductions of 14% at 0.04 g/g NaOH increasing to 83% at 0.1 g/g NaOH after 10 hours of preservation. Notably, even without heat preservation (0 hours), lignin reductions of 50–75% were achieved at NaOH dosages of 0.06–0.1 g/g, demonstrating the effectiveness of the extrusion-induced structural modifications in facilitating rapid delignification.

Enzymatic hydrolysis was performed at 2% solids loading with Celluclast 1.5 L and Novozyme 188 for 48 hours. Untreated corn stover yielded only 17.5% glucan and 5.1% xylan conversion. After ATSE pretreatment, glucan conversion increased to 42% at 0.04 g/g NaOH and 1 hour preservation, and further to 83% at 0.06 g/g NaOH. Xylan conversion followed a similar trend, reaching 89% at 0.06 g/g NaOH. However, at 0.1 g/g NaOH and preservation times over 1 hour, xylan conversion slightly decreased, likely due to the degradation of easily digestible xylan fractions during pretreatment. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) confirmed extensive fiber separation and surface roughening after pretreatment, with increased porosity at higher NaOH dosages. Mass balance calculations showed a maximum total sugar yield of 80.2% at 0.06 g/g NaOH and 10 hours preservation, but a nearly equivalent yield of 77.6% was achieved with only 1 hour preservation, suggesting that the latter condition offers the best compromise between yield and process efficiency. Under these optimal conditions (0.06 g/g NaOH, 1 hour preservation), the process achieved 71% lignin removal and conserved 90% of the original polysaccharides.

### Example 2

This example demonstrates the scalability and reproducibility of the ATSE process at pilot scale and evaluates the quality of lignin recovered from the spent liquor. Corn stover pretreated under the optimal conditions identified in Example 1—0.06 g NaOH per g oven-dried biomass, 1 hour heat preservation—was processed continuously at a feed rate of 200 kg/h of dry biomass. The extrusion duration was approximately 30 seconds, and the energy consumption was measured at 240 kWh per ton of dry biomass. The pretreated slurry was separated into solid and liquid fractions by filtration through a 300-mesh nylon cloth. The solid fraction, representing 66% of the original biomass mass, was used for enzymatic hydrolysis, yielding 467 g of total sugars per kg of original biomass, equivalent to 78% of the theoretical carbohydrate potential.

The liquid fraction, or spent liquor, was concentrated using a spray dryer (FH-1500, Shanghai Gaoji, China) without any purification steps. The recovered lignin was analyzed for molecular weight distribution using gel permeation chromatography. The weight-average molecular weight (Mw) was determined to be 22,780 Da, the number-average molecular weight (Mn) was 16,430 Da, and the polydispersity index (PDI) was 1.386. These values indicate a relatively high molecular weight and narrow distribution, which are desirable characteristics for lignin intended for use in high-value applications such as phenol-formaldehyde resins or concrete additives. The low PDI suggests uniform depolymerization during pretreatment, likely due to the controlled alkaline conditions and short residence time in the extruder. The lignin recovery yield was approximately 65% of the original lignin content, with the remainder presumably degraded or retained in the solid fraction. The spent liquor also contained dissolved xylan fragments and inorganic salts, primarily sodium hydroxide and sodium silicate (due to the high silica content of corn stover). Although silica poses challenges for chemical recovery, the high concentration of the spent liquor (achieved through the high solids loading of the ATSE process) facilitates efficient evaporation and potential integration with existing recovery systems in pulp mills. This example confirms that the ATSE process not only produces a highly digestible solid substrate but also generates a valuable lignin co-product with consistent quality at pilot scale.

### Example 3

This example compares the ATSE process with other extrusion-based and conventional pretreatment methods reported in the literature to highlight its distinguishing advantages. A comparative analysis was conducted based on key performance indicators including sugar yield, pretreatment temperature, feedstock size requirements, chemical usage, and scalability. Conventional extrusion pretreatments without chemical addition, such as those reported by Karunanithy and Muthukumarappan, achieved total sugar yields of only 50–60% and required biomass to be ground to 1–2 mm particles. Extrusion methods employing NaOH, such as that of Kadam et al., reported higher sugar yields (82–88%) but operated at temperatures of 114–220°C, necessitating external heating and increasing equipment and energy costs. Acid-based extrusion methods suffered from equipment corrosion and inhibitor formation.

In contrast, the ATSE process achieved a total sugar yield of 78% at a temperature of 99°C without external heating, using only minimally sized feedstock (2–5 cm pieces). The process required no severe size reduction, eliminating a major energy-intensive preprocessing step. Moreover, the ATSE process was demonstrated at pilot scale (200 kg/h), whereas most comparative methods were limited to laboratory-scale batch operations. When compared to leading non-extrusion pretreatments such as dilute acid, steam explosion, and AFEX, the ATSE process operates at lower temperature and pressure, avoids the formation of fermentation inhibitors, and enables lignin recovery—advantages not shared by acidic or non-catalyzed methods. Although some conventional methods report slightly higher sugar yields (85–95%), these results are often derived from different feedstocks, analytical methods, and hydrolysis protocols, making direct comparisons difficult. The ATSE process compensates for its marginally lower sugar yield with significant operational and economic benefits, including compatibility with existing pulp mill infrastructure, high solids loading, and low energy consumption. Additionally, the option to substitute NaOH with sodium carbonate could address silica-related recovery issues in non-wood feedstocks, further enhancing the process’s versatility. This example underscores the ATSE process as a balanced, practical, and industrially relevant solution for lignocellulosic biomass pretreatment.
