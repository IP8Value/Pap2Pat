# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to the field of microfluidic cell culture systems, and more specifically to pumpless organ-on-chip devices that utilize evaporation-driven fluid flow mediated by hydrophilic threads to enable continuous perfusion of cellular monolayers under controlled shear stress conditions. The invention provides a novel, simplified, and robust platform for culturing cells in dynamic microenvironments without the need for external pumps, tubing, or power sources, thereby significantly lowering the technical barrier to entry for laboratories lacking specialized microfluidics infrastructure. The disclosed devices are particularly suited for applications in drug screening, disease modeling, and fundamental studies of cellular mechanobiology, where precise control over fluid shear stress is essential to recapitulate physiologically relevant tissue phenotypes.

### Description of Related Art

Organ-on-chip technology represents a transformative advancement in in vitro cell culture, offering three-dimensional, dynamic microenvironments that more accurately mimic the structural and functional complexity of human tissues compared to conventional static culture methods. Central to the physiological relevance of these models is the application of fluid shear stress (FSS), a mechanical cue that profoundly influences cellular behavior, including proliferation, differentiation, migration, and barrier formation. For instance, endothelial and epithelial cells—key components of vascular and organ barriers—are exquisitely sensitive to FSS, which modulates gene expression, cytoskeletal organization, and intercellular junction integrity. Consequently, the ability to impose controlled and tunable shear stress within microfluidic channels is critical for generating predictive and reproducible organotypic models.

Despite these advantages, widespread adoption of organ-on-chip platforms has been hindered by practical limitations associated with current perfusion methodologies. Most existing systems rely on external peristaltic or syringe pumps connected to the microfluidic device via tubing to drive medium flow. While effective in generating precise flow rates, this configuration introduces several significant drawbacks. First, the integration of tubing and external pumps increases system complexity, requiring specialized training and equipment that may not be available in standard biological or pharmaceutical laboratories. Second, the fluidic connections are prone to bubble formation and leakage, both of which disrupt flow continuity and compromise experimental reproducibility. Third, the use of open tubing systems presents biosafety concerns when handling hazardous biological agents, such as pathogens or viral vectors, as they increase the risk of aerosol generation and environmental contamination. Finally, the capital cost of high-precision microfluidic pumps represents a substantial financial barrier, particularly for academic or small-scale research settings.

In response to these challenges, alternative pumpless perfusion strategies have been explored. One approach employs gravity-driven flow, either through hydrostatic pressure differentials or by placing the device on a rocking platform to induce bidirectional flow. While eliminating the need for pumps, these methods suffer from inherent limitations. Hydrostatic pressure-based systems offer limited flexibility in flow modulation and are ill-suited for rapid medium exchange, such as during drug administration. Rocking platforms generate complex, non-uniform flow patterns that result in spatially heterogeneous shear stress distributions, complicating data interpretation and reducing physiological fidelity. Another strategy involves modifying siphon-based infusion sets, but these too lack fine control over flow dynamics and are often incompatible with long-term culture requirements.

Capillary action has also been investigated as a passive driving force in microfluidics. Hydrophilic materials, such as paper or porous polymers, can wick fluids through capillary channels, enabling autonomous flow in diagnostic assays. However, these systems typically operate in open configurations and are not designed for the sustained, sterile, and precisely controlled perfusion required for organ-on-chip applications. Moreover, prior art has not successfully integrated capillary-driven flow into closed, cell-compatible microfluidic architectures capable of maintaining stable shear stress over days-long culture periods.

Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for a simple, reliable, and cost-effective perfusion system that eliminates external pumps and tubing while providing tunable, continuous, and physiologically relevant fluid flow for organ-on-chip models. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel mechanism wherein controlled evaporation from a hydrophilic thread inserted into the outlet of a microfluidic device generates a steady driving force for medium perfusion. This approach combines the simplicity of passive flow with the precision of active systems, enabling broad accessibility and enhanced experimental robustness.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a pumpless organ-on-chip device for the continuous perfusion of cellular monolayers under controlled fluid shear stress, obviating the need for external pumps, tubing, or electrical power sources. The device comprises a microfluidic channel formed between a polymeric substrate and a glass coverslip, an inlet reservoir for receiving cell culture medium, and an outlet port configured to receive a hydrophilic thread. The hydrophilic thread, composed of superhydrophilic fibers such as Spunlace rayon-polyester blends, is inserted into the outlet port such that it contacts the culture medium within the microfluidic channel but does not contact the bottom surface of the channel, thereby preventing complete drainage of the medium. When placed in a standard cell culture incubator—typically maintained at 37°C, 5% CO₂, and high humidity (e.g., 90%)—the culture medium is wicked through the thread via capillary action and subsequently evaporates from the exposed end of the thread. This continuous evaporation establishes a negative pressure gradient that drives a steady, unidirectional flow of fresh medium from the inlet reservoir through the microfluidic channel and out through the thread.

A key innovation of the invention lies in the tunability of the flow rate, which is achieved by adjusting the physical dimensions of the hydrophilic thread. Specifically, the diameter and length of the thread directly influence the wicking capacity and evaporation rate, respectively, thereby enabling precise control over the resulting fluid shear stress experienced by the cultured cells. Experimental characterization has demonstrated that flow rates ranging from approximately 0.15 µL min⁻¹ to 6 µL min⁻¹ can be reliably achieved by varying the thread diameter from 0.5 mm to 2 mm and the length from 1 mm to 28 mm. This range corresponds to physiologically relevant shear stress values (e.g., 0.002 to 0.15 dyn cm⁻²), suitable for modeling a variety of epithelial tissues, including intestinal and pulmonary barriers.

The invention further encompasses methods for culturing cells within the pumpless device, including seeding, differentiation, and phenotypic analysis. Human intestinal epithelial Caco-2 cells cultured under a shear stress of 0.02 dyn cm⁻² using the thread-driven system exhibited hallmark features of a mature, differentiated monolayer, including dense F-actin networks, high expression of tight junction protein ZO-1, and abundant active mitochondria—phenotypes indistinguishable from those obtained using conventional pump-driven systems. Similarly, human alveolar epithelial A549 cells responded to shear stress in a dose-dependent manner, with vimentin expression—a marker of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)—increasing significantly above a threshold of approximately 0.03 dyn cm⁻², consistent with known mechanobiological responses.

Additionally, the invention includes a multiplexed device design featuring multiple parallel microchannels, each connected to a common inlet reservoir but outfitted with threads of differing lengths or diameters. This configuration enables the simultaneous application of distinct shear stress levels across a single chip, facilitating high-throughput screening of cellular responses to mechanical cues under otherwise identical biochemical conditions. The entire device can be housed within a standard sterile Petri dish, minimizing contamination risk and enhancing biosafety.

In summary, the invention offers a simple, versatile, and equipment-free platform for organ-on-chip applications that maintains the physiological relevance of dynamic culture while dramatically improving accessibility, reliability, and ease of use. By eliminating the primary technical and financial barriers associated with traditional perfusion systems, the disclosed technology is poised to accelerate the adoption of organ-on-chip models in diverse research and industrial settings.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Examples

The present invention is now described in greater detail through specific examples that illustrate the design, fabrication, operation, and biological validation of the pumpless organ-on-chip devices. These examples are provided for illustrative purposes and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention, which encompasses all variations and modifications falling within the spirit and claims of the disclosure.

**Example 1: Fabrication and Characterization of Thread-Driven Flow**

Microfluidic devices were fabricated using standard soft lithography techniques. A silicon wafer was coated with SU-8 50 photoresist to a thickness of 150 µm via spin coating at 2000 rpm. The photoresist was patterned using a mask writer to define channel geometries, followed by development and thermal hardening to produce a master mold. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) prepolymer (10:1 base-to-curing agent ratio) was cast onto the mold, cured at 65°C for 2 hours, and then peeled off to form a PDMS slab containing microfluidic channels. Inlet and outlet ports were created by punching holes of 8 mm and 1.5 mm diameter, respectively, into the PDMS slab. The 1.5 mm outlet hole was specifically dimensioned to accommodate a hydrophilic thread while ensuring that the thread, when inserted, would not contact the bottom glass surface, thus preventing complete channel drainage. The PDMS slab was then plasma-bonded to a No. 1 glass coverslip to seal the channels.

Superhydrophilic Spunlace threads, composed of 70% rayon and 30% polyester, were used as the evaporation-driven flow actuators. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the threads consist of twisted fibers with smooth surfaces and average diameters of approximately 15 µm, forming a porous network of capillary channels. To enhance hydrophilicity and remove surface impurities, threads were optionally treated with oxygen plasma or mercerization prior to use.

To characterize capillary wicking, threads of four diameters (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0 mm) were vertically immersed in a dye solution, and the wicking height was measured over time. Results confirmed that wicking rate increases with thread diameter, demonstrating dimensional tunability of capillary transport.

Flow rates within the microfluidic channels were quantified by tracking the motion of fluorescent polystyrene nanoparticles (200 nm diameter) using time-lapse microscopy. In a constant-width channel (1 mm wide, 150 µm high), insertion of a thread into the outlet generated a steady flow over 24 hours, with velocity remaining constant throughout the observation period. By varying thread length and diameter, flow rates were tuned from 0.15 µL min⁻¹ (0.5 mm diameter, 1 mm length) to 6 µL min⁻¹ (2.0 mm diameter, 28 mm length). Shorter threads remained orthogonal to the device outlet, while longer threads tended to bend under their own weight when saturated, altering the evaporation geometry. Threads remained functional for up to five days under standard culture conditions before potential clogging due to salt or biomolecule accumulation necessitated replacement.

**Example 2: Pumpless Culture of Caco-2 Intestinal Epithelial Cells**

Human colorectal adenocarcinoma Caco-2 cells were cultured in the pumpless device to model the intestinal epithelial barrier. Prior to cell seeding, channels were sterilized with 70% ethanol, rinsed with PBS, and coated with Matrigel to promote cell adhesion. Cells were introduced via the outlet to minimize loss in the large inlet reservoir and allowed to adhere for one hour. Fresh medium was then added to the inlet reservoir, and a hydrophilic thread (diameter 0.5 mm, length 1 mm) was inserted into the outlet to initiate perfusion at a flow rate of 0.15 µL min⁻¹, corresponding to a shear stress of 0.02 dyn cm⁻².

After five days of culture, confluent monolayers exhibited morphological and molecular characteristics of a fully differentiated intestinal epithelium. Confocal microscopy revealed a dense cortical F-actin network, indicative of structural integrity. Immunostaining showed strong expression of ZO-1 at cell-cell junctions, confirming tight barrier formation. Mitochondrial activity, assessed using MitoTracker Deep Red FM, was high, reflecting metabolic maturity. These phenotypes were comparable to those observed in Caco-2 monolayers cultured under identical shear stress using conventional peristaltic pumps, validating the physiological relevance of the pumpless system.

**Example 3: Shear Stress-Dependent EMT in A549 Lung Epithelial Cells**

To demonstrate the utility of the platform in studying mechanotransduction, human lung adenocarcinoma A549 cells, including a vimentin-RFP reporter line (A549 VIM RFP), were cultured under varying shear stress conditions. In a Hele-Shaw channel—designed to generate a linear gradient of shear stress along its length—cells were exposed to stresses ranging from 0.008 to 0.135 dyn cm⁻². After five days, vimentin expression decreased significantly from the high-shear (Section 1) to low-shear (Section 5) regions (p = 0.02), with a clear threshold for EMT induction observed at approximately 0.03 dyn cm⁻². Cells in high-shear regions adopted spindle-like morphologies and formed less dense monolayers, hallmarks of mesenchymal transition.

In a separate experiment, A549 VIM RFP cells were cultured in a constant-width channel under uniform high shear stress (0.15 dyn cm⁻²). Throughout the channel, cells exhibited high vimentin expression, loss of cytokeratin (an epithelial marker), and characteristic spindle-shaped morphology, confirming a complete EMT phenotype. These results underscore the system’s ability to resolve subtle, shear-dependent phenotypic changes.

**Example 4: Multiplexed Shear Stress Screening Platform**

A multiplexed device was fabricated with five parallel microchannels, each connected to a common inlet reservoir but outfitted with threads of different lengths to achieve distinct flow rates. Nanoparticle tracking confirmed that each channel sustained a unique, stable flow velocity, enabling simultaneous exposure of identical cell populations to a spectrum of shear stresses. This design is particularly advantageous for dose-response studies of mechanical stimuli or for optimizing culture conditions for new cell types without requiring multiple independent experiments.

In all examples, the entire device was placed within a sterile 92 × 16 mm Petri dish and incubated at 37°C, 5% CO₂, and 90% humidity. The absence of external tubing minimized contamination risk and simplified handling, making the system accessible to non-specialist users. The invention thus provides a robust, scalable, and user-friendly platform for advanced in vitro tissue modeling.
