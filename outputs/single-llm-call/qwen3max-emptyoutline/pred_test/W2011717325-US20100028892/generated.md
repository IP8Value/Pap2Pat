# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Proteolytic enzymes, or proteases, constitute a fundamental class of biological catalysts that mediate the hydrolysis of peptide bonds within proteins. This irreversible post-translational modification serves as a critical regulatory mechanism across a vast spectrum of physiological and pathological processes, including but not limited to cell division, programmed cell death (apoptosis), cellular differentiation, innate immune responses, host-pathogen interactions, and intracellular protein trafficking and degradation. Given their central role in maintaining cellular homeostasis and their frequent dysregulation in disease states, proteases have emerged as highly attractive targets for therapeutic intervention. Indeed, the development of small-molecule modulators—both inhibitors and activators—of protease activity holds significant promise for the treatment of a wide array of human disorders, such as cancer, neurodegenerative conditions like Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases, ischemic injuries resulting from stroke or myocardial infarction, chronic inflammatory syndromes, and infectious diseases caused by pathogens that either encode their own proteases or hijack host proteolytic systems.

Despite this clear therapeutic potential, the discovery and optimization of selective and potent protease modulators present formidable scientific and technical challenges. A primary obstacle arises from the structural conservation of catalytic domains among members of large protease families. For instance, caspases—a family of cysteine-dependent aspartate-specific proteases—are characterized by highly similar active site architectures, which makes it exceedingly difficult to develop chemical compounds that selectively inhibit one caspase without affecting others. This lack of selectivity can lead to off-target effects and undesirable toxicities, thereby limiting the clinical utility of such agents. Consequently, there is a pressing need for alternative screening strategies that move beyond direct active-site inhibition and instead target upstream regulatory components of protease activation pathways. Such an approach could yield modulators that act allosterically or through protein-protein interaction interfaces, potentially offering greater specificity and fewer side effects.

Another major limitation of conventional protease drug discovery efforts lies in the reliance on in vitro biochemical assays that utilize purified recombinant protease catalytic domains. While these assays are often amenable to high-throughput screening (HTS) due to their simplicity and robustness, they suffer from several critical drawbacks. First, they are inherently incapable of identifying activators of protease activity, as the assay format typically measures only the reduction of enzymatic signal upon compound addition. Second, many full-length proteases are notoriously difficult to express and purify in a stable, active conformation, particularly those that require multi-subunit assembly or membrane association for function. Third, the use of isolated catalytic domains precludes the discovery of allosteric modulators that bind outside the active site, as these regions may be absent or improperly folded in truncated constructs. Fourth, and perhaps most importantly, single-component in vitro assays completely fail to recapitulate the complex cellular context in which proteases are naturally activated. Many proteases, especially initiator caspases, are synthesized as inactive zymogens that require specific multi-protein complexes for their activation—a process that cannot be faithfully modeled with purified components alone. As a result, compounds identified in simplified in vitro screens may lack efficacy in more physiologically relevant cellular environments.

To address these limitations, there has been a growing interest in developing cell-based assays for protease activity. Such systems offer the advantage of preserving the native conformational state of the protease, maintaining necessary co-factors and subcellular localization, and enabling the interrogation of entire signaling pathways rather than isolated enzymatic steps. However, implementing cell-based HTS for intracellular proteases presents its own set of challenges, particularly in mammalian cells where background noise, cytotoxicity, and complex regulatory networks can confound results. The yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* has long served as a powerful model organism for studying eukaryotic cell biology due to its genetic tractability, rapid growth, and well-characterized molecular toolkit. Importantly, yeast lacks endogenous caspases and other mammalian-specific protease families, providing a "clean" background for the heterologous expression of human proteolytic systems without interference from cross-reactive endogenous activities.

Prior art has demonstrated the feasibility of expressing individual mammalian caspases in yeast and observing phenotypic consequences such as growth inhibition or cell death. Additionally, cleavable transcription factor reporter systems have been described in which a membrane-tethered transcription factor is released upon protease-mediated cleavage, leading to the activation of a downstream reporter gene. However, these earlier approaches have been largely limited to single-protease expression or simple two-component systems and have not been systematically optimized or validated for HTS applications. Critically, no prior art has successfully reconstituted complex, multi-component mammalian protease activation pathways—such as the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) for caspase-8 or the inflammasome for caspase-1—in yeast in a manner that is both physiologically relevant and amenable to high-throughput chemical or genetic screening. Furthermore, existing methods have not provided a comprehensive platform capable of interrogating the entire repertoire of human caspases or extending to other classes of intracellular proteases like autophagins.

Thus, there remains a significant unmet need in the field for a versatile, robust, and scalable cell-based platform that enables the reconstitution of complex mammalian protease activation networks in a simple eukaryotic host. Such a system would facilitate the discovery of novel modulators—both chemical and genetic—that act at various points within protease signaling cascades, from direct enzyme inhibition to upstream pathway regulation. The present invention addresses this need by providing a comprehensive yeast-based assay system that overcomes the limitations of prior art through careful engineering of expression levels, substrate specificity, and pathway complexity, thereby enabling high-fidelity modeling of human protease biology in a format suitable for both high-throughput compound screening and functional cDNA library screening.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive and versatile yeast-based platform for assaying the activity of mammalian intracellular proteases and their upstream activators, enabling both high-throughput chemical library screening and functional cDNA library screening. At the core of this platform is a cleavable reporter gene system in which a membrane-tethered chimeric transcription factor is specifically cleaved by a protease of interest, releasing the transcription factor into the cytosol where it translocates to the nucleus and activates reporter genes containing cognate operator sequences in their promoters. This system has been meticulously engineered to accommodate the substrate specificities of diverse protease families, including all ten human caspases and the autophagin ATG4B, through the incorporation of protease-specific cleavage sequences between the membrane anchor and the transcription factor domain.

A key innovation of the present invention lies in the systematic optimization of expression levels for both proteases and their upstream activators through the strategic use of promoters of varying strengths, plasmid copy number control elements, and epitope tagging for detection and quantification. This careful titration of expression levels is critical for achieving protease activation without inducing cytotoxicity or growth suppression, which would otherwise interfere with reporter gene readouts. The invention further encompasses the successful reconstitution of complex multi-component mammalian protease activation pathways in yeast, including two-component systems (e.g., pro-caspase plus its specific adapter protein) and three-component systems that faithfully recapitulate physiologically relevant signaling complexes such as the death-inducing signaling complex (Fas/FADD/caspase-8) and the inflammasome (NLRP1/ASC/caspase-1).

The platform's adaptability is demonstrated through its successful application to both caspase and autophagin families, indicating its general utility for diverse classes of intracellular cysteine proteases. The invention provides specific vectors and plasmid constructs encoding cleavable transcription factors with various protease-specific linkers, expression plasmids for proteases and their activators under controlled promoter systems, and engineered yeast strains containing integrated reporter genes with variable numbers of transcription factor binding sites to optimize signal-to-noise ratios. These components work in concert to create assay systems that exhibit excellent performance characteristics in high-throughput formats, including Z' factors greater than 0.6 in 384-well plate configurations, making them suitable for large-scale compound screening campaigns.

Moreover, the invention includes comprehensive methodologies for both forward chemical screening and reverse genetic screening. In chemical screening applications, the yeast-based assays enable the identification of compounds that modulate protease activity either directly or through effects on upstream pathway components, with built-in counter-screening capabilities to eliminate false positives arising from non-specific effects on reporter gene expression or β-galactosidase activity. In genetic screening applications, the system facilitates the functional cloning of novel proteases, adapter proteins, and pathway regulators through cDNA library screening, with the hierarchical nature of multi-component systems allowing for straightforward deconvolution of hit mechanisms through systematic omission of pathway components.

The present invention thus represents a significant advancement over prior art by providing the first comprehensive, HTS-compatible platform for modeling complex mammalian protease activation networks in yeast. This technology enables the discovery of novel therapeutic agents and biological regulators that would be inaccessible through traditional in vitro biochemical approaches, thereby opening new avenues for drug discovery and basic research into protease biology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Caspases

Caspases represent a conserved family of intracellular cysteine proteases that play pivotal roles in the execution of programmed cell death (apoptosis) and the regulation of inflammatory responses. The human genome encodes at least ten distinct caspase genes, which can be broadly categorized into two functional groups: initiator caspases and effector caspases. Initiator caspases, including caspases-2, -8, -9, and -10, possess long N-terminal prodomains containing protein-protein interaction motifs such as death effector domains (DEDs) or caspase recruitment domains (CARDs). These prodomains facilitate the assembly of large multi-protein activation complexes in response to specific cellular signals. Effector caspases, comprising caspases-3, -6, and -7, have shorter prodomains and are primarily responsible for the proteolytic cleavage of numerous cellular substrates that ultimately lead to the characteristic morphological and biochemical changes associated with apoptosis. Additionally, caspases-1, -4, and -5 are classified as inflammatory caspases due to their roles in processing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β and interleukin-18.

All caspases are synthesized as inactive zymogen precursors that require proteolytic processing for activation. The canonical activation mechanism involves dimerization-induced conformational changes followed by intermolecular autocleavage at specific aspartate residues, resulting in the formation of active heterotetrameric enzymes composed of two large (∼20 kDa) and two small (∼10 kDa) subunits. This activation process is tightly regulated and typically occurs within specialized multi-protein complexes that bring multiple zymogen molecules into close proximity, facilitating their mutual activation. For example, caspase-8 is activated within the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC), which forms upon engagement of death receptors such as Fas (CD95) by their cognate ligands. Similarly, caspase-9 is activated within the apoptosome, a complex assembled by the adapter protein Apaf-1 in response to mitochondrial cytochrome c release. Inflammatory caspases like caspase-1 are activated within inflammasomes, large platforms formed by NOD-like receptor (NLR) proteins in response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns or danger signals.

The substrate specificity of caspases is remarkably precise, with all members recognizing and cleaving peptide bonds immediately following aspartic acid residues (hence their classification as aspartate-specific proteases). However, each caspase exhibits distinct preferences for amino acid residues at positions P4-P1 relative to the cleavage site (where P1 is the aspartate residue). This substrate specificity profile determines the functional roles of different caspases within cellular signaling networks and provides the molecular basis for designing specific substrates and inhibitors. For instance, caspase-3 and caspase-7 preferentially cleave after the tetrapeptide sequence DEVD, while caspase-8 and caspase-10 recognize LETD sequences, and caspase-1 cleaves WEHD motifs. Understanding these specificity profiles is essential for developing selective assay systems and therapeutic agents targeting specific caspase family members.

### Substrate Specificity of Caspases

The substrate specificity of caspases is governed by the architecture of their substrate-binding pockets, which accommodate four amino acid residues N-terminal to the scissile aspartate bond (designated P4-P1 positions). While the P1 position is invariably occupied by aspartic acid due to the catalytic mechanism requiring recognition of this acidic residue, the preferences for residues at P4, P3, and P2 positions vary significantly among different caspase family members, thereby conferring functional specificity to each enzyme. Extensive biochemical characterization using synthetic peptide libraries and natural protein substrates has established consensus cleavage motifs for each human caspase.

Caspase-1, along with its inflammatory counterparts caspase-4 and caspase-5, demonstrates a strong preference for tryptophan (W) at the P4 position, histidine (H) at P3, and glycine (G) at P2, resulting in the optimal cleavage sequence WEHD. This specificity profile enables these inflammatory caspases to selectively process pro-inflammatory cytokines while avoiding inappropriate activation of apoptotic pathways. Caspase-2 exhibits a preference for aspartic acid (D) at P4, glutamic acid (E) at P3, and histidine (H) at P2, yielding the consensus sequence DEHD, although it also demonstrates significant activity toward DEVD sequences. The effector caspases-3 and -7 share nearly identical substrate specificities, with a strong preference for aspartic acid at P4, glutamic acid at P3, and valine (V) at P2, forming the well-characterized DEVD motif. Caspase-6 shows a distinct preference for threonine (T) at P4, resulting in the TEVD consensus sequence.

Among the initiator caspases, caspase-8 and caspase-10 both recognize leucine (L) at P4, glutamic acid at P3, and threonine at P2, generating the LETD consensus motif. Caspase-9 prefers leucine at P4, glutamic acid at P3, and histidine at P2, corresponding to the LEHD sequence. These distinct specificity profiles reflect the hierarchical organization of caspase activation cascades, where initiator caspases activate downstream effector caspases through cleavage at their specific recognition sequences, while avoiding cross-activation of parallel pathways. The precise knowledge of these specificity profiles has been instrumental in the development of the present invention's cleavable reporter system, enabling the design of substrate linkers that confer high selectivity for individual caspase family members or specific functional groups.

### Mechanisms of Activating Upstream Initiator Caspases

The activation of upstream initiator caspases represents a critical regulatory node in both apoptotic and inflammatory signaling pathways, involving the assembly of large multi-protein complexes that facilitate zymogen dimerization and subsequent autocatalytic processing. Unlike effector caspases, which can be activated through cleavage by upstream initiators, initiator caspases require specific protein-protein interaction platforms for their activation due to their low intrinsic catalytic activity in the monomeric state. The activation mechanisms are mediated through specialized protein interaction domains present in the N-terminal prodomains of initiator caspases and their corresponding adapter proteins.

Caspase-8 and caspase-10, which contain tandem death effector domains (DEDs) in their prodomains, are activated within the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC). This complex assembles upon ligand-induced trimerization of death receptors belonging to the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor superfamily, such as Fas (CD95), TRAIL-R1 (DR4), and TRAIL-R2 (DR5). The intracellular death domains (DDs) of these activated receptors recruit the bipartite adapter protein FADD (Fas-associated death domain protein), which contains both a DD for receptor binding and a DED for caspase recruitment. Through DED-DED interactions, FADD brings multiple procaspase-8 or procaspase-10 molecules into close proximity, enabling their dimerization and subsequent intermolecular autocleavage to generate active enzymes.

Caspase-9 activation occurs within the apoptosome, a wheel-like heptameric complex formed by the adapter protein Apaf-1 (apoptotic protease-activating factor 1) in response to mitochondrial cytochrome c release during intrinsic apoptosis. Apaf-1 contains a CARD domain that interacts with the CARD domain present in procaspase-9's prodomain. Cytochrome c binding to Apaf-1, in the presence of dATP or ATP, triggers a conformational change that exposes the CARD domain and promotes Apaf-1 oligomerization into the apoptosome platform. This oligomerization recruits multiple procaspase-9 molecules through CARD-CARD interactions, facilitating their dimerization and activation.

Inflammatory caspase-1 activation takes place within inflammasomes, which are assembled by NOD-like receptor (NLR) proteins in response to various danger signals or pathogen-associated molecular patterns. Different NLR family members form distinct inflammasome complexes. For example, NLRP1 and NLRP3 inflammasomes require the bipartite adapter protein ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD), which contains both a pyrin domain (PYD) and a CARD. The PYD of ASC interacts with the PYD present in NLRP1 or NLRP3, while the CARD of ASC interacts with the CARD domain of procaspase-1, thereby bridging the sensor protein to the effector protease. This assembly brings multiple procaspase-1 molecules together, enabling their activation through proximity-induced dimerization and autocleavage.

Caspase-2 activation involves the PIDDosome complex, which forms in response to DNA damage. This complex consists of the p53-induced protein with a death domain (PIDD), the adapter protein RAIDD (RIP-associated ICH-1/CED-3 homologous protein with a death domain), and procaspase-2. RAIDD contains both a CARD domain for caspase-2 recruitment and a death domain for PIDD binding, thereby serving as the molecular bridge that brings procaspase-2 molecules into proximity for activation.

These distinct activation mechanisms highlight the importance of protein-protein interaction domains and multi-protein complex assembly in regulating initiator caspase activity. The present invention successfully recapitulates these complex activation mechanisms in yeast through careful engineering of expression levels and protein interaction domains, enabling the faithful modeling of mammalian caspase activation pathways in a simple eukaryotic system.

### Phenotypes of Animal Caspases Expressed in Yeast

The heterologous expression of mammalian caspases in the yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* produces distinct phenotypic outcomes that depend on the specific caspase expressed, its expression level, and the presence or absence of upstream activators. Yeast serves as an ideal host for these studies due to its lack of endogenous caspase homologs and other mammalian-specific protease families, providing a clean genetic background free from cross-reactive proteolytic activities that could confound experimental results. However, the expression of active caspases in yeast can lead to growth inhibition or cell death, phenomena that must be carefully managed to enable reporter gene-based assays.

When expressed at moderate to high levels from strong constitutive promoters such as ADH or TEF, several caspases—including caspases-1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 9—undergo spontaneous activation in yeast without requiring their canonical upstream activators. This spontaneous activation likely occurs through concentration-dependent dimerization and subsequent autocatalytic processing, mimicking the proximity-induced activation that normally occurs within mammalian multi-protein complexes. While this property enables the creation of simple one-component assay systems for these caspases, the levels of expression must be carefully titrated to avoid excessive cytotoxicity that would prevent yeast growth and interfere with reporter gene readouts.

In contrast, caspases-8 and -10 exhibit more stringent activation requirements even when overexpressed in yeast. These initiator caspases typically require co-expression of their specific adapter protein FADD to achieve significant activation, reflecting their dependence on DED-mediated protein interactions for proper activation in mammalian cells. When expressed alone at high levels, caspases-8 and -10 can still induce some degree of growth suppression, but this effect is dramatically enhanced when co-expressed with FADD. Therefore, successful assay development for these caspases requires careful balancing of expression levels, typically involving weak promoter-driven expression of the procaspase combined with strong promoter-driven expression of FADD.

The phenotypic consequences of caspase expression in yeast extend beyond simple growth inhibition. Some caspases induce morphological changes reminiscent of apoptotic cell death in mammalian cells, including chromatin condensation, DNA fragmentation, and reactive oxygen species production. These phenotypes provide additional readouts for caspase activity but are generally less quantitative and more difficult to implement in high-throughput formats compared to reporter gene systems. The present invention leverages the understanding of these phenotypic outcomes to optimize expression conditions that maintain sufficient caspase activity for reporter gene activation while minimizing cytotoxic effects that would compromise assay performance.

Interestingly, the expression of catalytically inactive caspase mutants (typically generated by substituting the active site cysteine with alanine or glycine) does not produce growth inhibitory phenotypes, confirming that the observed effects are specifically due to proteolytic activity rather than non-specific protein aggregation or other artifacts. This observation provides an important negative control for validating the specificity of caspase-dependent phenotypes in yeast and underscores the importance of including catalytically inactive controls in all experimental designs.

## EXAMPLE 1

### Development and Testing of a Cleavable Reporter Gene System for Assaying Protease Activity in Living Yeast

The foundation of the present invention rests upon a sophisticated cleavable reporter gene system specifically engineered for monitoring protease activity in living yeast cells. This system employs a membrane-tethered chimeric transcription factor that becomes liberated upon specific proteolytic cleavage, enabling nuclear translocation and activation of reporter genes containing cognate operator sequences in their promoters. The design of this reporter construct draws inspiration from previous conceptual frameworks but represents a significant advancement through systematic optimization for high-throughput applications and broad protease family coverage.

The core architecture of the cleavable reporter consists of three principal domains arranged in an N-to-C terminal configuration: (1) a type I transmembrane anchor derived from the extracellular and transmembrane domains of human Fas (CD95) receptor (amino acids M1-L224), designated as Fas-d to indicate the absence of the cytoplasmic death domain; (2) a protease-specific cleavage linker sequence positioned immediately C-terminal to the transmembrane domain; and (3) a chimeric transcription factor comprising the DNA-binding domain of bacterial LexA protein fused to the transcriptional activation domain of yeast B42 protein. The Fas transmembrane domain serves as an effective membrane anchor that sequesters the transcription factor in the cytoplasmic compartment, preventing nuclear access and reporter gene activation in the absence of protease activity. Upon specific cleavage at the engineered linker sequence, the transcription factor domain is released from membrane tethering and can diffuse into the nucleus to activate transcription.

The cleavage linker sequences were meticulously designed based on established substrate specificity profiles for each human caspase family member. For caspase-1 and related inflammatory caspases, the WEHD tetrapeptide sequence was incorporated, flanked by glycine residues to provide flexibility and accessibility (GWEHDG). Similarly, DEHD and DEVD sequences were engineered for caspase-2 and effector caspases-3/7, respectively, while TEVD, LETD, and LEHD sequences were designed for caspases-6, 8/10, and 9. Critical control constructs were generated by substituting the essential P1 aspartate residue with glycine (e.g., WEHG instead of WEHD), rendering the linker resistant to caspase-mediated cleavage while maintaining similar structural properties.

Reporter gene activation was monitored using two complementary selection markers integrated into the yeast genome: LEU2 and lacZ. The LEU2 gene, encoding β-isopropylmalate dehydrogenase essential for leucine biosynthesis, was placed under the control of promoters containing varying numbers (1-8) of LexA operator sequences (lexAop). This arrangement enables growth selection on leucine-deficient media only when the cleaved LexA-B42 transcription factor is present and functional. The lacZ gene, encoding β-galactosidase, was similarly controlled by lexAop-containing promoters and provided a colorimetric readout through X-gal substrate conversion to blue product, enabling visual confirmation of reporter activation.

Extensive empirical optimization was conducted to determine the ideal configuration for each caspase-reporter combination. Variables systematically evaluated included the number of cleavage sites within the linker (single versus multiple tandem repeats), the strength of promoters driving reporter expression (TEF versus ADH), plasmid copy number (high-copy 2µ versus low-copy CEN/ARS origins), and the number of lexA operators in reporter gene promoters. Surprisingly, single cleavage sites proved superior to multiple tandem repeats, likely due to reduced steric hindrance and improved accessibility for protease recognition. The optimal number of lexA operators varied depending on caspase expression levels and activity, with lower numbers (2-4 operators) generally providing better signal-to-noise ratios for highly active caspases, while higher numbers (6-8 operators) were required for less active or weakly expressed variants.

Validation experiments demonstrated exquisite specificity of the cleavable reporter system. Yeast expressing caspase-1, -4, or -5 activated only the WEHD-containing reporter, while caspases-3 and -7 specifically activated the DEVD reporter. Caspase-2 showed activity toward both DEHD and DEVD reporters, consistent with its known substrate flexibility. Importantly, non-cleavable control reporters (with P1 aspartate substituted by glycine) remained inactive regardless of caspase expression, and catalytically inactive caspase mutants failed to activate any reporter constructs. These rigorous controls confirmed that reporter activation resulted specifically from protease-mediated cleavage at the engineered recognition sequences rather than non-specific proteolysis or transcriptional artifacts.

### A Genetic System for Monitoring Caspase 1 Activity in Yeast Cells.

The development of a dedicated genetic system for monitoring caspase-1 activity in yeast represents a critical component of the present invention's comprehensive platform. Caspase-1, as a key mediator of inflammatory responses through its processing of pro-interleukin-1β and pro-interleukin-18, presents unique challenges and opportunities for assay development. Unlike apoptotic caspases that primarily function in cell death pathways, caspase-1 operates within the context of inflammasome complexes and exhibits distinct regulatory mechanisms that must be faithfully recapitulated in the yeast system.

The caspase-1-specific reporter construct, designated Fas-d-S1-TA, incorporates the WEHD tetrapeptide sequence optimized for caspase-1 recognition, flanked by glycine residues to ensure proper spacing and accessibility (GWEHDG). This construct was expressed from high-copy plasmids under the control of the strong constitutive TEF promoter to ensure sufficient substrate availability without inducing toxicity. The corresponding control construct, Fas-d-G1-TA, contains the non-cleavable WEHG sequence (GWEHGG) and serves as an essential negative control for all experiments.

For caspase-1 expression, full-length human procaspase-1 cDNA was cloned into yeast expression vectors with C-terminal FLAG epitope tags for detection and quantification. Initial attempts using strong promoters (ADH, TEF) resulted in spontaneous activation and significant growth inhibition, necessitating careful titration of expression levels. Through systematic evaluation of weaker promoters (CYC1, ΔGPD1) and low-copy plasmid backbones (CEN/ARS origin), optimal conditions were established that permitted detectable caspase-1 activity without compromising yeast viability. The resulting expression system enabled robust activation of the WEHD-containing reporter while maintaining minimal background activation of the non-cleavable control.

The genetic system was further refined through optimization of reporter gene configurations. Yeast strain EGY48, containing six lexA operators upstream of the integrated LEU2 gene (6op-LEU2), was transformed with additional plasmids carrying lacZ reporters with varying numbers of lexA operators (2op-lacZ, 4op-lacZ, 6op-lacZ, 8op-lacZ). Comparative analysis revealed that 2op-lacZ provided the best balance between sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio for caspase-1 activity measurements, likely due to the relatively high basal activity of caspase-1 when overexpressed in yeast.

This dedicated caspase-1 monitoring system demonstrated exceptional performance characteristics, enabling both qualitative assessment through colony color development on X-gal plates and quantitative measurement through β-galactosidase activity assays in liquid culture. The system's specificity was rigorously validated through multiple control experiments, including the use of catalytically inactive caspase-1 mutants (C285A), non-cleavable reporter constructs, and cross-testing against other caspase family members. No cross-reactivity was observed with caspases-2 through -10, confirming the high selectivity of the WEHD recognition sequence for inflammatory caspases.

The caspase-1 monitoring system proved particularly valuable for functional cDNA library screening applications. When used to screen human cDNA expression libraries, the system successfully identified multiple clones encoding caspase-1 and caspase-4, demonstrating its ability to detect endogenous protease activity from library-derived expression. This capability extends the utility of the system beyond simple activity monitoring to include the discovery of novel inflammatory caspase regulators and activators.

### Substrate Specificities of the Caspases Expressed in Yeast.

The comprehensive characterization of substrate specificities for all ten human caspases expressed in yeast constitutes a major achievement of the present invention, providing unprecedented insights into the functional relationships and potential cross-reactivities among caspase family members within a controlled cellular environment. This systematic analysis was made possible through the parallel development of multiple cleavable reporter constructs, each incorporating the optimal tetrapeptide recognition sequence for a specific caspase or caspase subgroup.

The experimental design involved co-expressing each of the ten human caspases with all available cleavable reporter constructs in the engineered yeast strain EGY48, which contains integrated LEU2 and episomal lacZ reporter genes under lexA operator control. Caspase expression levels were carefully calibrated using appropriate promoter strengths and plasmid copy numbers to ensure detectable activity without excessive cytotoxicity. The resulting activation patterns revealed both expected specificities and unexpected cross-reactivities that provide valuable information for assay design and interpretation.

As anticipated, the inflammatory caspases (caspases-1, -4, -5) exclusively activated the WEHD-containing reporter, demonstrating perfect concordance with their established substrate preferences. Similarly, the effector caspases-3 and -7 showed strong preference for the DEVD reporter, with minimal activity toward other substrates. Caspase-2 exhibited broader specificity, activating both DEHD and DEVD reporters, consistent with its known flexibility at the P2 position where histidine can be accommodated by caspases-3 and -7. Caspase-6 demonstrated good specificity for its TEVD substrate, with little cross-reactivity observed with other reporters.

The initiator caspases showed more complex patterns. Caspase-8 and caspase-10 both activated the LETD reporter as expected, but also demonstrated low-level activity toward the LEHD substrate designed for caspase-9. Caspase-9 primarily activated its LEHD reporter but showed some cross-reactivity with LETD and DEVD sequences. These observations highlight the importance of using appropriate negative controls and confirmatory experiments when interpreting results from caspase activity assays.

Notably, the downstream effector caspases (caspases-3, -6, -7) did not activate reporters containing sequences optimized for upstream initiator caspases (LETD, LEHD), confirming the unidirectional nature of caspase activation cascades. Conversely, some initiator caspases demonstrated limited ability to cleave effector caspase substrates, suggesting potential bypass mechanisms or alternative activation pathways that warrant further investigation.

The substrate specificity analysis also revealed important practical considerations for assay design. For instance, the DEHD reporter designed for caspase-2 showed nearly identical activation patterns to the DEVD reporter, suggesting that either sequence could be used effectively for detecting caspase-2 activity. Similarly, the LETD reporter proved suitable for detecting both caspase-8 and caspase-10 activity, simplifying assay development for these closely related initiator caspases.

These comprehensive specificity data provide a valuable reference framework for selecting appropriate reporter constructs for specific experimental applications and for interpreting results from compound or genetic screening campaigns. The ability to distinguish between different caspase activities within the same cellular context represents a significant advantage over traditional in vitro assays that often struggle with selectivity issues due to the high structural similarity of caspase active sites.

### Development of Two-component Systems Demonstrating Function of Caspase Activators in Yeast

The successful development of two-component systems for assaying caspase activity in yeast represents a critical advancement beyond simple one-component overexpression systems, enabling the faithful recapitulation of physiological activation mechanisms that depend on specific protein-protein interactions. These two-component systems consist of the inactive procaspase zymogen expressed at low levels combined with its cognate adapter protein expressed at high levels, mimicking the natural activation paradigm where adapter proteins nucleate the assembly of activation complexes.

Five distinct two-component systems were developed and validated, each representing a major caspase activation pathway: (1) procaspase-1 plus ASC for inflammatory caspase activation; (2) procaspase-2 plus RAIDD for DNA damage-induced caspase activation; (3) procaspase-9 plus a gain-of-function mutant of Apaf-1 for intrinsic apoptotic pathway activation; (4) procaspase-8 plus FADD for extrinsic death receptor pathway activation; and (5) procaspase-10 plus FADD for alternative death receptor signaling. Each system was carefully engineered to maintain the procaspase in an inactive state in the absence of its specific adapter protein while enabling robust activation upon adapter co-expression.

The key to successful two-component system development lay in the precise titration of expression levels for both components. Procaspases were expressed from low-copy plasmids (CEN/ARS origin) under the control of weak to moderate strength promoters (CYC1, ΔGPD1), ensuring that spontaneous activation through concentration-dependent dimerization was minimized. Adapter proteins were expressed from high-copy plasmids (2µ origin) using strong promoters (TEF, ADH) to provide sufficient molecular scaffolding for procaspase recruitment and activation. This differential expression strategy effectively recreated the natural stoichiometry of activation complexes while preventing non-specific activation.

Each two-component system demonstrated strict dependence on both components for reporter gene activation. Expression of procaspase alone or adapter protein alone failed to produce any detectable reporter activity, confirming that activation required the specific protein-protein interaction between the two components. Furthermore, pairing procaspases with non-cognate adapter proteins (e.g., Apaf-1* with procaspase-8 or ASC with procaspase-9) also failed to activate reporters, demonstrating the specificity of the protein interaction domains.

The two-component systems proved particularly valuable for studying hierarchical caspase activation cascades. For example, active caspase-9 (expressed at high levels) was shown to efficiently activate procaspase-3 or procaspase-7 (expressed at low levels) in two-component configurations, faithfully recapitulating the natural downstream activation events in the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. These hierarchical systems enabled the dissection of complex signaling relationships and provided platforms for identifying modulators that act at specific points within caspase activation cascades.

The successful implementation of these two-component systems in yeast demonstrates the remarkable plasticity of this simple eukaryotic host for reconstituting complex mammalian signaling pathways. By preserving the essential protein interaction domains and carefully controlling expression levels, the present invention achieves faithful modeling of physiological caspase activation mechanisms in a genetically tractable system amenable to high-throughput screening applications.

### Schematic Representation of a Two-component System for Caspase1 Activators in Yeast Cells.

The schematic representation of the two-component system for caspase-1 activation in yeast provides a clear visual framework for understanding the molecular architecture and functional relationships within this engineered signaling pathway. The system comprises three essential genetic elements working in concert: (1) the procaspase-1 expression construct; (2) the ASC adapter protein expression construct; and (3) the WEHD-containing cleavable reporter construct.

The procaspase-1 expression construct consists of full-length human procaspase-1 cDNA fused to a C-terminal FLAG epitope tag for detection and purification purposes. This construct is cloned into a low-copy yeast plasmid (CEN/ARS origin) under the control of a weak constitutive promoter such as CYC1 or an attenuated version of a stronger promoter (e.g., ΔTEF1, ΔTEF3). The low expression level ensures that procaspase-1 remains predominantly in its inactive monomeric state, preventing spontaneous activation through concentration-dependent dimerization.

The ASC adapter protein expression construct contains the full-length human ASC cDNA, which encodes a bipartite protein containing both a pyrin domain (PYD) and a caspase recruitment domain (CARD). This construct is cloned into a high-copy yeast plasmid (2µ origin) under the control of a strong constitutive promoter such as TEF or ADH. The high expression level of ASC provides sufficient molecular scaffolding to nucleate the formation of activation complexes when procaspase-1 is present.

The cleavable reporter construct, designated Fas-d-S1-TA, consists of the Fas transmembrane domain (amino acids M1-L224) fused to the WEHD cleavage sequence (GWEHDG) and the LexA-B42 chimeric transcription factor. This construct is expressed from a moderate-strength promoter (TEF) on a high-copy plasmid to ensure adequate substrate availability without inducing toxicity.

The functional operation of this two-component system proceeds through a series of well-defined molecular events. In the absence of ASC expression, procaspase-1 remains monomeric and inactive, unable to cleave the WEHD-containing reporter substrate. Upon ASC expression, the PYD and CARD domains of ASC mediate homotypic interactions that lead to the formation of large oligomeric complexes. The CARD domain of ASC then recruits procaspase-1 through CARD-CARD interactions, bringing multiple procaspase-1 molecules into close proximity. This proximity-induced dimerization triggers conformational changes that expose the catalytic site and enable intermolecular autocleavage, generating active caspase-1 heterotetramers.

The active caspase-1 enzymes then recognize and cleave the WEHD sequence within the Fas-d-S1-TA reporter construct, releasing the LexA-B42 transcription factor from membrane tethering. The liberated transcription factor translocates to the nucleus, binds to lexA operator sequences in the promoters of LEU2 and lacZ reporter genes, and activates their transcription. This results in leucine prototrophy (enabling growth on leucine-deficient media) and β-galactosidase production (yielding blue colonies on X-gal plates).

The schematic representation emphasizes the critical importance of differential expression levels, with low procaspase-1 expression preventing spontaneous activation while high ASC expression providing sufficient scaffolding for complex assembly. It also highlights the specificity of protein-protein interactions, as demonstrated by the failure of non-cognate adapter proteins to activate procaspase-1. This visual framework serves as a valuable guide for understanding the molecular logic of the two-component system and for designing similar systems for other caspase family members.

### Development of a Plural-component System—Mammalian Protease Activating Pathways Reconstituted in Yeast

The successful development of plural-component systems represents the pinnacle achievement of the present invention, demonstrating the remarkable capacity to reconstitute complex mammalian protease activation pathways in the simple eukaryotic host *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*. These plural-component systems faithfully recapitulate the hierarchical organization and molecular interactions of native signaling complexes, enabling the study of protease regulation within physiologically relevant contexts that extend far beyond simple enzyme-substrate relationships.

Two major plural-component systems were developed and validated: (1) the death-inducing signaling complex (DISC) for caspase-8 activation, comprising Fas death receptor, FADD adapter protein, and procaspase-8; and (2) the inflammasome complex for caspase-1 activation, comprising NLRP1 or NLRP3 sensor proteins, ASC adapter protein, and procaspase-1. Each system required careful engineering of expression levels, protein interaction domains, and activation mechanisms to achieve faithful functional reconstitution in yeast.

The DISC reconstitution involved three distinct strategies to achieve optimal activation dynamics. In the first approach, FADD was expressed at low levels from a weak promoter such that insufficient adapter protein was available for procaspase-8 activation in the absence of receptor engagement. High-level expression of Fas death receptor then provided the necessary oligomerization platform to concentrate FADD molecules and enable efficient procaspase-8 recruitment and activation. In the second approach, procaspase-8 was expressed at low levels while FADD was expressed at high levels, with death receptor expression providing additional enhancement of activation efficiency. Both strategies successfully recapitulated the natural requirement for receptor-mediated complex assembly while maintaining tight control over basal activity levels.

The inflammasome reconstitution employed gain-of-function mutants of NLRP1 and NLRP3 that lacked their auto-inhibitory leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domains, rendering them constitutively active without requiring pathogen-derived ligands for activation. These truncated NLR proteins were expressed under strong inducible (GAL1) or constitutive (TEF) promoters, while procaspase-1 and ASC were expressed from weak constitutive promoters at levels insufficient for spontaneous activation. Upon NLR expression, the PYD domains mediated homotypic interactions leading to large oligomeric complex formation, which then recruited ASC through PYD-PYD interactions and subsequently procaspase-1 through CARD-CARD interactions, resulting in caspase-1 activation.

Both plural-component systems demonstrated strict dependence on all required components for reporter gene activation. Omission of any single component—whether receptor, adapter, or procaspase—completely abolished reporter activity, confirming the faithful reconstitution of native activation mechanisms. Furthermore, the systems exhibited appropriate specificity, with Fas failing to activate caspase-1 pathways and NLR proteins failing to activate caspase-8 pathways, demonstrating the preservation of pathway fidelity in the heterologous yeast environment.

The successful implementation of these plural-component systems represents a significant technological breakthrough, as it demonstrates for the first time the ability to reconstitute complex mammalian signaling platforms in yeast with sufficient fidelity to support high-throughput screening applications. This achievement opens new possibilities for studying the molecular mechanisms of protease activation, identifying novel pathway components, and discovering therapeutic modulators that act at various points within these complex signaling networks.

### Schematic Representation of Plural-component Systems for Caspase-8 Activation in Yeast.

The schematic representation of plural-component systems for caspase-8 activation in yeast provides a comprehensive visual framework for understanding the molecular architecture and functional dynamics of reconstituted death-inducing signaling complexes (DISCs). Two distinct configurations were developed and optimized, each representing different aspects of natural DISC assembly and activation mechanisms.

The first configuration, designated as the "low-FADD complementation" system, consists of four essential genetic elements: (1) Fas death receptor expressed at high levels from a strong constitutive promoter (TEF) on a high-copy plasmid; (2) FADD adapter protein expressed at low levels from a weak promoter (ΔADH1) on a high-copy plasmid; (3) procaspase-8 expressed at low levels from a weak promoter (CYC1) on a low-copy plasmid; and (4) the LETD-containing cleavable reporter construct (Fas-d-S8-TA) expressed from a moderate-strength promoter (TEF) on a high-copy plasmid.

In this configuration, the low-level expression of FADD ensures that insufficient adapter protein is available for spontaneous procaspase-8 activation in the absence of receptor engagement. However, high-level expression of Fas death receptor leads to its spontaneous oligomerization through death domain interactions, creating a concentrated platform for FADD recruitment. The death domains of Fas interact with the death domain of FADD, bringing multiple FADD molecules into close proximity. The death effector domains (DEDs) of FADD then recruit procaspase-8 through DED-DED interactions, enabling proximity-induced dimerization and activation of the caspase zymogen.

The second configuration, designated as the "high-FADD baseline" system, reverses the expression strategy: procaspase-8 is expressed at low levels while FADD is expressed at high levels from a strong promoter (TEF) on a high-copy plasmid. In this arrangement, the high concentration of FADD provides sufficient molecular scaffolding for some degree of spontaneous procaspase-8 activation, establishing a baseline activity level. Expression of Fas death receptor then provides additional enhancement by concentrating FADD molecules through receptor oligomerization, leading to increased activation efficiency.

Both configurations incorporate the LETD-containing cleavable reporter construct, which serves as the ultimate readout for caspase-8 activity. Upon activation, caspase-8 recognizes and cleaves the LETD sequence within the reporter construct, releasing the LexA-B42 transcription factor for nuclear translocation and reporter gene activation. The schematic emphasizes the critical role of differential expression levels in maintaining tight control over activation dynamics, with weak promoters and low-copy plasmids preventing excessive basal activity while strong promoters and high-copy plasmids providing sufficient components for robust activation upon complex assembly.

The visual representation also highlights the modular nature of the system, showing how individual components can be systematically omitted or substituted to probe specific aspects of DISC function. For example, substitution of Fas with other death receptors like DR4 or DR5 enables the study of alternative DISC compositions, while replacement of FADD with dominant-negative mutants allows investigation of specific protein interaction requirements. This modularity makes the plural-component system an exceptionally powerful tool for both basic research and drug discovery applications.

### Testing of Yeast-based, Plural-component Systems for Activating Caspases-8 or -10.

The rigorous testing and validation of yeast-based plural-component systems for activating caspases-8 and -10 represents a critical milestone in demonstrating the physiological relevance and robustness of the present invention's platform technology. These validation experiments encompassed multiple dimensions of system performance, including component dependence, pathway specificity, quantitative response characteristics, and suitability for high-throughput screening applications.

Component dependence was systematically evaluated by constructing yeast strains containing all possible combinations of the three essential DISC components: Fas death receptor, FADD adapter protein, and procaspase-8 or procaspase-10. Reporter gene activation was measured through both LEU2-dependent growth selection and lacZ-dependent colorimetric assays. Results demonstrated absolute requirement for all three components, with no detectable reporter activity observed in any two-component combination or single-component control. This strict dependence confirmed the faithful reconstitution of natural DISC assembly requirements in the yeast system.

Pathway specificity was assessed through extensive cross-testing experiments involving non-cognate components. For example, Fas failed to activate caspase-1 pathways even when co-expressed with ASC and procaspase-1, while NLRP1ΔLRR failed to activate caspase-8 pathways when combined with FADD and procaspase-8. Additionally, substitution of FADD with non-homologous adapter proteins or expression of catalytically inactive caspase mutants completely abolished reporter activity, confirming the specificity of both protein-protein interactions and proteolytic activity.

Quantitative response characteristics were evaluated through dose-response experiments involving titration of individual components. For instance, increasing Fas expression levels in the low-FADD complementation system produced graded increases in reporter activity, demonstrating the system's sensitivity to receptor concentration. Similarly, titration of FADD expression in the high-FADD baseline system revealed a threshold effect, with reporter activity increasing sharply above a critical FADD concentration. These quantitative relationships mirror those observed in mammalian systems and validate the physiological relevance of the reconstituted pathways.

The plural-component systems were further validated through functional cDNA library screening experiments designed to identify novel DISC components. Screens of human cDNA libraries successfully recovered known death receptors (DR4, DR5) and adapter proteins (FADD), demonstrating the system's ability to detect authentic pathway components. Additionally, novel hits including caspase-2 and c-FLIP were identified and characterized, revealing unexpected cross-talk between different caspase activation pathways. The hierarchical nature of the plural-component system enabled straightforward deconvolution of hit mechanisms through systematic omission of pathway components, providing clear evidence of direct versus indirect effects.

Finally, the systems were adapted to 384-well plate formats for high-throughput screening applications. Optimization of culture conditions, cell densities, and incubation times yielded robust assay performance with Z' factors exceeding 0.6, well above the threshold considered suitable for HTS. Pilot screens of bioactive compound libraries produced acceptable hit rates and identified both known caspase inhibitors and novel modulators, confirming the systems' utility for drug discovery applications. These comprehensive validation experiments collectively demonstrate that the yeast-based plural-component systems for caspases-8 and -10 represent faithful, robust, and versatile platforms for studying death receptor signaling pathways and discovering novel therapeutic agents.

## Experimental Designs & Methods Used

### Vectors Encoding Caspase Cleavable Transcription Factors:

The development of specialized vectors encoding caspase-cleavable transcription factors represents a foundational element of the present invention's platform technology, enabling the precise monitoring of protease activity through reporter gene activation. These vectors were meticulously engineered to incorporate protease-specific cleavage sequences between a membrane-tethering domain and a chimeric transcription factor, creating substrates that undergo specific proteolytic processing upon caspase activation. The core architecture of these vectors follows a modular design principle that facilitates easy customization for different protease specificities while maintaining consistent expression characteristics.

The membrane-tethering domain was derived from the extracellular and transmembrane regions of human Fas (CD95) receptor, specifically amino acids M1-L224, which provides a stable type I transmembrane anchor without the cytoplasmic death domain that could interfere with yeast physiology. This Fas-d fragment was fused in-frame to various tetrapeptide cleavage sequences optimized for specific caspase family members, each flanked by glycine residues to ensure proper spacing and accessibility. The cleavage sequences included GWEHDG for caspase-1 and related inflammatory caspases, GDEHDG and GDEVDG for caspase-2 and effector caspases-3/7 respectively, GTEVDG for caspase-6, GLETDG for caspases-8/10, and GLEHDG for caspase-9. Critical control vectors were constructed by substituting the essential P1 aspartate residue with glycine, generating non-cleavable versions such as GWEHGG, GDEHGG, GDEVGG, GTEVGG, GLETGG, and GLEHGG.

The C-terminal portion of these constructs encodes a chimeric transcription factor consisting of the DNA-binding domain of bacterial LexA protein fused to the transcriptional activation domain of yeast B42 protein. This LexA-B42 fusion provides strong transcriptional activation capability while maintaining specificity for LexA operator sequences, enabling tight control over reporter gene expression. The entire Fas-d-cleavage site-LexA-B42 cassette was cloned into yeast expression vectors under the control of the strong constitutive TEF promoter, ensuring consistent and robust expression across different experimental conditions.

For autophagin-specific applications, a specialized vector was constructed by replacing the tetrapeptide cleavage sequence with the full-length LC3B protein coding sequence. This LC3B-containing reporter, designated Fas-d-LC3B-TA, serves as a physiological substrate for ATG4 family proteases, which specifically cleave LC3B at its C-terminus to expose a glycine residue required for subsequent conjugation reactions in the autophagy pathway. This adaptation demonstrates the versatility of the vector platform for accommodating different classes of intracellular proteases beyond the caspase family.

All vectors were constructed using standard recombinant DNA techniques, including PCR amplification, restriction enzyme digestion, and ligation cloning. The resulting constructs were verified by DNA sequencing to ensure correct reading frames and absence of mutations. These specialized vectors provide the essential substrate components for the present invention's comprehensive protease assay platform, enabling specific and sensitive detection of protease activity across diverse experimental contexts.

### Plasmids for Expressing Caspases and Their Upstream Activators:

The successful implementation of the present invention's protease assay platform required the development of a comprehensive suite of specialized plasmids for expressing caspases and their upstream activators under precisely controlled conditions. These expression plasmids were engineered to provide a range of expression levels through strategic combinations of promoter strengths, plasmid copy number control elements, and selectable markers, enabling fine-tuned regulation of protein expression to achieve optimal assay performance while minimizing cytotoxic effects.

The foundation of this plasmid collection consists of modified pRS series vectors, which provide well-characterized yeast replication origins and selectable markers. High-copy plasmids (2µ origin) containing URA3, HIS3, or TRP1 markers were used for strong expression of adapter proteins and reporter constructs, while low-copy plasmids (CEN/ARS origin) with the same markers enabled weak expression of procaspases to prevent spontaneous activation. Additionally, pESC series vectors (Stratagene/Agilent) were employed for inducible expression applications requiring galactose-responsive regulation.

Promoter selection represented a critical aspect of expression control, with a hierarchy of constitutive promoters providing graduated expression levels. The relative strength ranking established through empirical testing was: ΔCYC4 < ΔCYC2 < CYC1 < ΔGPD2 < ΔGPD1 < ΔADH1 < ADH < ΔTEF3 < ΔTEF2 < ΔTEF1 < TEF. These promoters were systematically combined with different plasmid backbones to achieve the precise expression levels required for each experimental configuration. For example, procaspase-1 expression for two-component systems utilized ΔTEF1 or ΔTEF3 promoters on CEN/ARS plasmids, while ASC adapter protein expression employed TEF promoters on 2µ plasmids.

Epitope tagging was incorporated throughout the plasmid collection to facilitate protein detection and quantification. N-terminal or C-terminal HA (hemagglutinin) and FLAG tags were added to caspases and adapter proteins as appropriate, enabling verification of expression levels through immunoblotting without interfering with protein function. The positioning of tags was carefully considered to avoid disruption of critical protein interaction domains, with C-terminal tags generally preferred for caspases to preserve N-terminal prodomain integrity.

Complex plasmids containing multiple expression cassettes were constructed to reduce the number of plasmids required per yeast strain and minimize potential incompatibilities between different selectable markers. For example, dual-expression plasmids were created containing both Fas-d-S8-TA reporter and procaspase-8 expression cassettes on a single CEN/ARS backbone with HIS3 selection. Similarly, lacZ reporter and FADD expression cassettes were combined on 2µ plasmids with URA3 selection. These complex constructs required careful placement of transcription termination elements between expression cassettes to prevent transcriptional interference and ensure independent regulation of each gene.

The resulting plasmid collection provides a comprehensive toolkit for configuring diverse experimental setups, from simple one-component caspase expression systems to complex three-component pathway reconstitutions. Each plasmid was rigorously validated through functional testing to confirm appropriate expression levels and biological activity, ensuring reliable performance across different experimental contexts and screening applications.

### Complex Plasmids Constructions.

The construction of complex plasmids containing multiple expression cassettes represents a sophisticated engineering achievement that significantly enhances the practical utility and experimental flexibility of the present invention's platform technology. These multifunctional plasmids were designed to consolidate multiple genetic elements onto single vectors, thereby reducing the total number of plasmids required per yeast strain and minimizing potential complications arising from plasmid incompatibility or uneven segregation during cell division.

The design principles underlying complex plasmid construction emphasized modularity, independence, and compatibility. Each expression cassette within a complex plasmid was engineered as a self-contained unit comprising a promoter, coding sequence, and transcription termination element, ensuring that individual genes could be expressed independently without transcriptional interference. Promoters of appropriate strength were selected for each cassette based on the required expression level, with weaker promoters typically used for procaspases and stronger promoters for adapter proteins or reporter constructs.

One exemplary complex plasmid, designated p413-TEF-Fas-d-S8-TA/CYC1-Caspase8-HA, combines two essential elements for caspase-8 activation studies on a single low-copy (CEN/ARS) backbone with HIS3 selection. The first cassette expresses the LETD-containing cleavable reporter Fas-d-S8-TA under the control of the strong TEF promoter, ensuring abundant substrate availability. The second cassette expresses C-terminally HA-tagged procaspase-8 from the weak CYC1 promoter, maintaining the caspase zymogen at levels insufficient for spontaneous activation. A transcription termination element separates the two cassettes to prevent read-through transcription and ensure independent regulation.

Another representative complex plasmid, p426-2op-lacZ/ΔADH1-FADD, integrates reporter gene and adapter protein expression on a high-copy (2µ) backbone with URA3 selection. This construct contains the lacZ reporter gene under the control of two LexA operator sequences, providing a sensitive colorimetric readout for caspase activity. The second cassette expresses FADD adapter protein from the attenuated ΔADH1 promoter, delivering sufficient adapter protein for DISC assembly while preventing excessive basal activity. Again, appropriate transcription termination elements ensure independent regulation of each cassette.

The construction methodology for these complex plasmids involved sequential cloning steps using compatible restriction sites and careful verification of reading frames and orientations. PCR-assisted mutagenesis was employed to introduce specific restriction sites or modify promoter sequences as needed. All final constructs were verified by restriction mapping and DNA sequencing to ensure correct assembly and absence of mutations.

These complex plasmids offer several practical advantages over simpler single-cassette vectors. First, they reduce the metabolic burden on yeast cells by decreasing the total number of plasmids that must be maintained. Second, they ensure coordinated inheritance of functionally related genetic elements, eliminating concerns about plasmid loss or uneven segregation during cell division. Third, they simplify strain construction by reducing the number of transformation steps required to generate complete assay systems. Finally, they enable more sophisticated experimental designs by allowing precise control over the relative expression levels of different components within the same genetic context.

The successful development and validation of these complex plasmids demonstrate the advanced molecular engineering capabilities underlying the present invention's platform technology. They represent a critical enabling technology that facilitates the implementation of complex multi-component assay systems in high-throughput screening formats while maintaining experimental rigor and reproducibility.

### Reporter Gene Assays.

The implementation of robust reporter gene assays constitutes a cornerstone of the present invention's platform technology, providing sensitive, specific, and quantitative readouts for protease activity in living yeast cells. These assays leverage the cleavable transcription factor system to convert proteolytic events into measurable transcriptional outputs through two complementary reporter genes: LEU2 for growth selection and lacZ for colorimetric detection.

The LEU2 reporter system enables positive selection for protease activity through leucine prototrophy. Yeast strain EGY48 contains the LEU2 gene under the control of promoters with varying numbers (1-8) of LexA operator sequences integrated into the genome. In the absence of protease activity, the membrane-tethered LexA-B42 transcription factor remains sequestered in the cytoplasm, unable to activate LEU2 transcription. Consequently, transformed yeast cannot grow on leucine-deficient media. Upon specific protease-mediated cleavage of the reporter construct, the liberated LexA-B42 transcription factor translocates to the nucleus, binds LexA operators, and activates LEU2 transcription, enabling growth on selective media. This growth-based selection provides a powerful enrichment mechanism for identifying active proteases or pathway activators in genetic screening applications.

The lacZ reporter system provides a quantitative colorimetric readout through β-galactosidase activity. Episomal plasmids containing lacZ under LexA operator control are introduced into the engineered yeast strains, enabling detection of reporter activation through X-gal substrate conversion to blue product. The intensity of blue color development correlates with the level of protease activity, providing a semi-quantitative measure of pathway activation. Multiple lacZ reporter variants containing different numbers of LexA operators (2op, 4op, 6op, 8op) were developed to optimize sensitivity and dynamic range for different experimental contexts.

Assay conditions were carefully optimized to maximize signal-to-noise ratios while maintaining yeast viability. Transformants were initially grown on complete synthetic dropout (SD) medium containing glucose and supplemented leucine to ensure plasmid maintenance and general health. For assay induction, cells were transferred to selection plates containing galactose and raffinose as carbon sources (to avoid glucose repression of GAL1 promoter-driven constructs when used), BU salts for optimal growth, and X-gal for colorimetric detection. Incubation at 30°C for 4-6 days allowed sufficient time for reporter gene expression and color development while minimizing background activation.

For liquid culture assays, similar principles applied but with quantitative measurement of β-galactosidase activity through spectrophotometric detection of X-gal cleavage products at 620 nm. Culture conditions were optimized for cell density, incubation time, and substrate concentration to achieve linear response ranges suitable for quantitative comparisons. These liquid assays formed the basis for high-throughput screening adaptations in microtiter plate formats.

Critical validation controls were incorporated throughout the reporter gene assay system. Non-cleavable reporter constructs (with P1 aspartate substituted by glycine) served as essential negative controls, confirming that reporter activation resulted specifically from protease-mediated cleavage rather than non-specific proteolysis or transcriptional artifacts. Catalytically inactive caspase mutants (with active site cysteine substituted by alanine or glycine) provided additional validation of protease dependence. Cross-testing against non-cognate caspases confirmed the specificity of cleavage sequences for their intended targets.

The dual-reporter system combining LEU2 selection with lacZ detection proved particularly powerful for genetic screening applications, enabling two-step validation of positive hits. Primary selection on leucine-deficient media enriched for true positives, while secondary confirmation through X-gal color development eliminated false positives arising from non-specific LEU2 activation. This two-tiered validation approach significantly improved the reliability and efficiency of functional cDNA library screening campaigns.

### HTS Assays.

The adaptation of the yeast-based protease assay platform to high-throughput screening (HTS) formats represents a critical technological achievement that enables large-scale compound library screening for protease modulators. This adaptation required systematic optimization of multiple experimental parameters to achieve robust assay performance in microtiter plate formats while maintaining physiological relevance and specificity.

The HTS assay format was implemented in 384-well microtiter plates, with β-galactosidase activity serving as the primary readout through colorimetric detection of X-gal cleavage products at 620 nm. Key optimization parameters included initial cell seeding density, culture duration, X-gal substrate concentration, and carbon source composition. Empirical testing revealed that optimal conditions varied slightly between different assay systems but generally converged on ∼2×10⁵ cells per well starting density, 2-3 day culture duration, 400 µg/mL X-gal concentration, and raffinose-containing media (to avoid glucose repression while maintaining good growth characteristics).

Assay performance was rigorously evaluated using statistical parameters appropriate for HTS applications, particularly the Z' factor, which measures the separation between positive and negative control signals relative to their variability. Both the Fas/FADD/caspase-8 DISC assay and the NLRP1ΔLRR/ASC/caspase-1 inflammasome assay achieved Z' factors exceeding 0.6, well above the 0.5 threshold generally considered suitable for HTS. This robust performance was maintained across multiple independent experiments and different plate batches, demonstrating assay reproducibility and reliability.

Positive and negative controls were carefully defined for each assay system. For the DISC assay, maximum signal (assay max) was obtained from cells expressing all three components (Fas, FADD, caspase-8) with X-gal substrate, while minimum signal (assay min) was established either by omitting X-gal substrate or by adding the broad-spectrum caspase inhibitor zVAD-fmk. Similar control definitions applied to the inflammasome assay, with NLRP1ΔLRR expression providing maximum signal and zVAD-fmk treatment establishing minimum signal.

Pilot screening campaigns validated the HTS assay performance using the LOPAC (Library of Pharmacologically Active Compounds) collection, a 1280-compound library enriched for bioactive molecules. Screening at 10 µM final compound concentration (1% DMSO) produced acceptable hit rates of approximately 0.5% for both assay systems, consistent with expectations for biologically relevant compound collections. Hit compounds were subjected to rigorous post-HTS deconvolution using specialized yeast strains designed to eliminate false positives and characterize mechanism of action.

False positive elimination strategies included testing compounds against yeast strains constitutively expressing the LexA-B42 transcription factor (to identify β-galactosidase inhibitors) and against alternative caspase assay systems (to assess selectivity). True positive compounds were further characterized through dose-response analysis and orthogonal validation using in vitro enzymatic assays with fluorogenic peptide substrates. This comprehensive validation pipeline ensured that identified hits represented genuine protease modulators with defined mechanisms of action.

The successful implementation of HTS-compatible assay formats demonstrates the scalability and robustness of the present invention's platform technology. The ability to perform large-scale compound screening while maintaining physiological relevance through complex multi-component pathway reconstitution represents a significant advance over traditional in vitro biochemical approaches. This capability opens new avenues for drug discovery targeting protease activation pathways rather than just catalytic activity, potentially yielding more selective and therapeutically relevant modulators.

### Yeast-based Counter-screen Assays.

The development of comprehensive yeast-based counter-screen assays represents a critical quality control component of the present invention's platform technology, enabling the efficient identification and elimination of false positive compounds that might otherwise confound high-throughput screening results. These counter-screen assays were specifically designed to detect compounds that interfere with reporter gene readouts through non-specific mechanisms rather than genuine modulation of protease activity.

The primary counter-screen assay targets compounds that directly inhibit β-galactosidase enzyme activity or interfere with lacZ gene expression. This assay employs yeast strain EGY48 transformed with plasmid p424-GAL1-TA, which constitutively expresses the LexA-B42 transcription factor under the control of the GAL1 promoter. These cells exhibit high basal levels of β-galactosidase activity regardless of protease status, providing a direct readout for compounds that affect the reporter system itself. Compounds showing inhibition in this counter-screen are flagged as potential false positives arising from non-specific effects on the detection system rather than genuine protease modulation.

A secondary counter-screen assay evaluates compound selectivity across different caspase pathways. For example, hits identified in the Fas/FADD/caspase-8 DISC assay are tested against the Asc/caspase-1 two-component system to determine whether they exhibit cross-reactivity with inflammatory caspase pathways. This selectivity profiling helps distinguish compounds that target specific pathway components (such as FADD or caspase-8) from those that act more broadly on caspase family members or common downstream effectors. The availability of assays covering all ten human caspases enables comprehensive selectivity profiling across the entire caspase family.

Additional counter-screen assays were developed to detect compounds that affect general yeast physiology or viability. These include assays measuring growth rates in the absence of protease expression and assays evaluating membrane integrity through vital dye exclusion. Compounds showing general toxicity or growth inhibition are flagged for careful interpretation, as their effects on protease assays might reflect non-specific cellular stress rather than specific pathway modulation.

The counter-screen assay workflow was integrated into the overall HTS pipeline through automated liquid handling systems and standardized data analysis protocols. Primary screening hits were automatically transferred to counter-screen assay plates using robotic systems, ensuring consistent compound concentrations and minimizing handling errors. Data analysis algorithms compared primary assay results with counter-screen results to automatically flag potential false positives and prioritize true positives for further validation.

This comprehensive counter-screening strategy significantly improved the reliability and efficiency of the HTS process by reducing the number of compounds requiring manual validation and follow-up studies. The ability to rapidly distinguish genuine protease modulators from assay artifacts represents a major advantage of the present invention's platform technology over simpler assay systems that lack built-in quality control mechanisms. This capability is particularly valuable in large-scale screening campaigns where the cost and time required for manual validation of false positives can be prohibitive.

### Cloning Systems for Caspase Activators.

The development of specialized cloning systems for caspase activators represents a crucial enabling technology that facilitates functional cDNA library screening for novel pathway components and regulators. These cloning systems were engineered to accommodate directional insertion of cDNA fragments downstream of strong or moderate strength promoters, enabling efficient expression of library-derived proteins in yeast while maintaining reading frame integrity and proper orientation.

The foundation of these cloning systems consists of modified p424 series vectors containing TRP1 selectable markers and either GAL1 (inducible) or ADH (constitutive) promoters for cDNA expression. The GAL1 promoter versions (p424-GAL1 and p424-GAL1-HA) were modified to include 5' XhoI and 3' NotI restriction sites immediately downstream of the promoter, enabling directional cloning of cDNA fragments generated with compatible termini. The HA-tagged version includes an HA epitope sequence between the promoter and the XhoI site, allowing detection of expressed proteins through anti-HA immunoblotting.

Similarly, the ADH promoter versions (p424-ADH and p424-ADH-HA) were modified with 5' XhoI and 3' SfiI sites downstream of the promoter, providing an alternative cloning strategy for constitutive expression applications. The choice between GAL1 and ADH promoters allowed flexibility in expression timing and level, with GAL1 providing strong inducible expression and ADH delivering moderate constitutive expression suitable for detecting proteins that might be toxic when overexpressed.

cDNA library construction employed directional cloning strategies to maintain proper reading frames and orientation. For GAL1 promoter libraries, first-strand cDNA synthesis was initiated with NotI-linked oligo(dT) or random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand synthesis and SalI adapter ligation. The resulting double-stranded cDNAs were digested with NotI and ligated into XhoI-NotI digested vector backbones. For ADH promoter libraries, SfiI-linked primers were used instead, with ligation into XhoI-SfiI digested vectors.

Multiple tissue-specific cDNA libraries were constructed using this system, including libraries from HEK293 renal epithelial cells, HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells, HeLa cervical carcinoma cells, human liver, and human placenta. Library complexity ranged from 1.3×10⁶ to 1.3×10⁷ independent transformants, ensuring comprehensive coverage of expressed genes in each tissue source. Quality control measures included verification of insert size distribution, assessment of cloning efficiency, and confirmation of library representation through PCR amplification of housekeeping genes.

The cloning systems proved exceptionally effective for functional screening applications, successfully identifying known caspase activators and novel pathway components across multiple screening campaigns. The directional cloning strategy maintained proper reading frames in the vast majority of clones, while the promoter choices enabled detection of both strong and weak activators. The availability of both inducible and constitutive expression options provided flexibility for optimizing screening conditions based on the specific assay system and expected hit characteristics.

These specialized cloning systems represent a critical enabling technology that complements the protease assay platform by providing the genetic tools necessary for comprehensive functional genomics applications. Their successful implementation demonstrates the integrated nature of the present invention's platform technology, combining sophisticated assay systems with powerful genetic tools to enable both chemical and genetic screening for protease pathway modulators.
