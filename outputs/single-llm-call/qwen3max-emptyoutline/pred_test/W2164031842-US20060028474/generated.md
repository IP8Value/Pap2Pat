# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of computer graphics and, more specifically, to systems and methods for rendering deformable surfaces using image-based representations that preserve material appearance under arbitrary geometric deformations and dynamic lighting conditions. The invention provides a novel framework for animating surface reflectance fields—hybrid representations that combine an approximate geometric impostor with high-fidelity, image-based reflectance data captured from real-world objects—by introducing a local parameterization of the impostor geometry that enables accurate evaluation of acquired reflectance images even after non-rigid, non-uniform deformations. This approach allows for the photorealistic animation of complex real-world materials—including those exhibiting specularities, translucency, self-shadowing, and anisotropic reflectance properties—under arbitrary illumination environments, including dynamically changing point light sources and environment maps, without requiring explicit knowledge of the object’s true surface normals or microstructure. The invention is particularly applicable to applications in virtual reality, film production, video games, e-commerce, and scientific visualization, where the ability to animate realistic, relightable digital assets derived from physical objects is of critical importance.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The quest for photorealistic rendering of three-dimensional objects has long been a central challenge in computer graphics. Traditional approaches rely on explicit geometric modeling combined with parametric Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Functions (BRDFs) to describe how light interacts with a surface. While these parametric models are computationally efficient and compatible with modern graphics hardware, they are fundamentally limited in their ability to capture the full complexity of real-world materials. Many natural and manufactured surfaces exhibit phenomena such as subsurface scattering, interreflections, self-shadowing, translucency, and complex anisotropic or spatially varying reflectance behaviors that cannot be accurately represented by simple analytical BRDF formulations. As a result, purely model-based approaches often fail to reproduce the nuanced visual appearance of real objects, especially under varying lighting conditions.

In response to these limitations, image-based modeling techniques have emerged as a powerful alternative. These methods capture the visual appearance of real objects directly from photographs, bypassing the need for explicit reflectance modeling. One prominent class of such techniques is known as 3D photography, which acquires both shape and appearance data through controlled imaging setups involving multiple cameras and lighting configurations. Among the most expressive of these representations is the surface reflectance field (SRF), which records, for each point on an object’s surface, its appearance under a wide range of incident lighting directions and viewing angles. In practice, an SRF is constructed from a large collection of high-dynamic-range (HDR) images captured from numerous viewpoints under varying directional illumination, effectively sampling the object’s full reflectance function in a discrete, data-driven manner.

To render an SRF, a geometric proxy—commonly referred to as an “impostor”—is used to provide a simplified 3D structure onto which the image-based reflectance data is mapped. This impostor is typically derived from the object’s silhouette information, such as a visual hull, and serves as a scaffold for interpolation and rendering. The combination of this approximate geometry with dense reflectance data constitutes a hybrid representation that retains the visual fidelity of image-based methods while enabling viewpoint flexibility and relighting capabilities not possible with static image collections. However, a major limitation of existing SRF implementations has been their static nature: once captured, the object cannot be meaningfully deformed or animated without severe degradation in visual quality.

Previous attempts to animate image-based representations have met with limited success. Surface light fields, a precursor to SRFs, allow for viewpoint changes but only under fixed illumination, making them unsuitable for dynamic lighting scenarios. Some researchers have proposed animating surface light fields by warping the underlying impostor geometry, but these methods either fail to properly handle diffuse components or assume restrictive BRDF properties, such as isotropy. Other techniques, such as feature-based light field morphing, require extensive user input and are not generalizable to arbitrary deformations. More recently, systems based on Bidirectional Texture Functions (BTFs) have demonstrated deformable relightable rendering, but they depend on highly accurate, tightly fitted geometry obtained via laser scanning, which limits their applicability to objects with complex or inaccessible shapes. Moreover, none of these approaches adequately address the problem of preserving the perceptual integrity of material appearance—particularly the angular and spatial characteristics of the BRDF—under non-uniform, skewed, or shearing deformations.

The core challenge in animating SRFs lies in the fact that deformation alters the relationship between surface points, viewing directions, and lighting directions. A naive application of the inverse warp to map query points back to the acquisition space does not preserve the local reflectance behavior because it fails to account for changes in surface orientation and local geometry. For instance, if a surface patch is stretched or sheared, the angles between incident light, viewing direction, and surface normal change, which in turn alters the observed reflectance. To maintain visual consistency, the shading system must compensate for these geometric changes by appropriately transforming the lighting and viewing vectors so that the original BRDF characteristics are preserved as closely as possible. This requires a sophisticated mapping strategy that respects the local differential properties of the deformation.

Furthermore, practical implementation poses significant computational hurdles. SRF datasets are extremely large, often exceeding hundreds of gigabytes, necessitating efficient data access and caching strategies. During deformation, the set of reflectance images required to shade a single point can vary widely across the surface, leading to poor cache coherence and excessive disk I/O. Additionally, interpolating reflectance values for arbitrary lighting and viewing directions—especially when those directions fall outside the original acquisition set—requires robust and flexible interpolation schemes that can handle irregular sampling and avoid artifacts such as aliasing or ghosting.

Thus, despite the promise of surface reflectance fields for capturing and rendering photorealistic objects, the lack of a general, robust, and efficient method for animating them under arbitrary deformations and dynamic lighting has remained a significant barrier to their widespread adoption in interactive and production environments. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel local parameterization of the impostor geometry, a rigid transformation-based look-up scheme that approximately preserves BRDF characteristics during deformation, and a deferred, cache-optimized shading architecture that enables practical rendering of animated SRFs.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for rendering deformable surface reflectance fields that preserve the visual appearance of real-world materials under arbitrary geometric deformations and dynamic lighting conditions. The invention overcomes the limitations of prior art by introducing a local parameterization of the impostor geometry that enables accurate evaluation of acquired reflectance data even after complex, non-rigid transformations. At the heart of the invention is a look-up function that maps shading queries in deformed object space back to the original acquisition space in a manner that approximately preserves the spatially varying BRDFs of the undeformed object.

Specifically, the invention begins by augmenting each point on the impostor geometry with a set of local parameters that encode its original position and orientation in the acquisition frame. These parameters include the original 3D position \( \mathbf{p}_0 \), a minimal representation of the original tangential frame (e.g., stored as a Rodrigues vector \( \mathbf{r}_0 \)), and two tangent vectors \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{v} \) that are updated dynamically according to the applied deformation. When the impostor geometry is warped by a differentiable function \( \Phi \), the tangent vectors \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{v} \) are transformed using directional derivatives of \( \Phi \), effectively capturing the local linear distortion induced by the warp.

During rendering, for each shaded point \( \mathbf{p}^* \) on the deformed surface, the system constructs a rigid transformation \( L_{\mathbf{p}^*} \) that aligns the warped local tangential frame with the original one stored in the parameterization. This transformation consists of a translation that maps \( \mathbf{p}^* \) back to \( \mathbf{p}_0 \), and a rotation that minimizes the angular discrepancy between the current and original tangent bases. The rotation is computed by orthogonalizing the deformed tangent vectors and solving for the optimal rotation that maps the resulting frame to the stored original frame. This rigid transformation is then applied to the incoming light direction \( \mathbf{l}^* \) and viewing direction \( \mathbf{v}^* \) to produce corresponding directions \( \mathbf{l} \) and \( \mathbf{v} \) in the acquisition space.

The transformed query \( (\mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \) is then used to evaluate the surface reflectance field via interpolation across the stored reflectance images. To handle arbitrary lighting, the invention employs a point light source approximation that avoids the need for expensive refiltering of environment maps. Instead of filtering the environment map per surface point, the system subsamples the environment into a dense set of virtual point lights, each colored by the corresponding environment value. Lighting is then performed by summing contributions from these point lights, with interpolation implicitly providing the necessary reconstruction filtering. This approach supports dynamic lighting and integrates seamlessly with the BRDF-preserving look-up scheme.

To address the computational burden of accessing massive SRF datasets, the invention implements a deferred shading architecture with reordered evaluation. Shading operations are decomposed into minimal tasks, each requiring a single SRF look-up. These tasks are collected, sorted to maximize cache coherence, and executed in batches, dramatically reducing disk I/O and improving performance. The system uses a small, efficient cache capable of holding only a few reflectance images at a time, made feasible by the optimized access pattern.

The geometric representation is implemented using an enhanced surfel model, where each surfel is defined not just by position and normal, but by a full local tangential coordinate system \( (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) \). This allows the surfels to be deformed into elliptical shapes that conform to the local warp, preventing holes and maintaining surface coverage during large deformations. Critically, the surfel attributes directly encode the local impostor parameterization, unifying geometry deformation with appearance preservation.

The invention is validated through experiments on complex real-world objects, including those with specular, diffuse, and refractive materials. Results demonstrate faithful preservation of material appearance under a wide range of deformations, including stretching, bending, and shearing, under both static and dynamic lighting. While the method does not model global illumination effects such as self-shadowing or subsurface scattering—which are inherently non-local and require exact geometry—it provides a robust approximation that is visually compelling and suitable for many practical applications.

In summary, the invention enables the first general-purpose animation of surface reflectance fields with arbitrary deformations and dynamic relighting, preserving the rich visual detail of real-world materials without requiring explicit BRDF models or high-fidelity geometry. It represents a significant advancement in image-based rendering and opens new possibilities for creating photorealistic, animatable digital assets from physical objects.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The present invention is directed to a system and method for rendering deformable surface reflectance fields that preserve the visual appearance of real-world materials under arbitrary geometric deformations and dynamic lighting. The core innovation lies in a local parameterization of the impostor geometry that enables accurate, appearance-preserving evaluation of acquired reflectance data during deformation. Below, we describe the preferred embodiment in detail, including the theoretical foundations, geometric representation, shading algorithm, and implementation optimizations.

A surface reflectance field (SRF) is a hybrid representation consisting of a large set of image-based reflectance data associated with an approximate geometric impostor. The reflectance data is acquired by capturing an object from multiple viewpoints under varying directional illumination, typically using a high-dynamic-range (HDR) imaging setup. For each viewpoint, a “reflectance image” is formed by stacking images taken under different lighting conditions. An SRF thus comprises hundreds of such reflectance images, each containing dozens of HDR exposures, resulting in a dataset that can exceed hundreds of gigabytes in size.

The impostor geometry, often a visual hull derived from object silhouettes, serves as a scaffold for rendering. It is not required to match the true object surface exactly but must sufficiently approximate its shape to enable plausible interpolation. Rendering an SRF involves two stages: first, for a given illumination environment, synthesizing an image of the object from each viewpoint by interpolating across the reflectance images; second, combining these synthesized images using the impostor geometry and an image-based rendering technique such as unstructured lumigraph rendering (ULR) to produce the final output.

To animate an SRF, the impostor geometry is deformed by a warp function \( \Phi: \mathbb{R}^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^3 \). The challenge is to evaluate the reflectance data in a way that preserves the material appearance of the original object. Naively applying \( \Phi^{-1} \) to map points back to acquisition space is insufficient because it does not account for changes in local surface orientation, which affect the BRDF response. The invention solves this by introducing a local parameterization at each point on the impostor.

Each point \( \mathbf{p}^* \) on the deformed impostor stores four key parameters: (1) its original position \( \mathbf{p}_0 \) in the acquisition frame; (2) a minimal representation of its original tangential frame, encoded as a Rodrigues vector \( \mathbf{r}_0 \); and (3–4) two tangent vectors \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{v} \) that define the current local coordinate system. The vectors \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \mathbf{v} \) are updated during deformation by applying the warp \( \Phi \) to the original tangent basis via directional derivatives:

\[
\mathbf{u} = \frac{\Phi(\mathbf{p}_0 + \epsilon \mathbf{u}_0) - \Phi(\mathbf{p}_0 - \epsilon \mathbf{u}_0)}{2\epsilon}, \quad
\mathbf{v} = \frac{\Phi(\mathbf{p}_0 + \epsilon \mathbf{v}_0) - \Phi(\mathbf{p}_0 - \epsilon \mathbf{v}_0)}{2\epsilon}
\]

for a small \( \epsilon > 0 \), where \( \mathbf{u}_0, \mathbf{v}_0 \) are derived from \( \mathbf{r}_0 \). This captures the local affine distortion induced by \( \Phi \).

During shading, for a query \( (\mathbf{p}^*, \mathbf{l}^*, \mathbf{v}^*) \)—comprising the deformed point, light direction, and view direction—the system constructs a rigid transformation \( L_{\mathbf{p}^*} \) that maps this query to acquisition space. The translation component is simply \( \mathbf{p}_0 - \mathbf{p}^* \). The rotational component is computed by first orthogonalizing \( \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \) to form a basis \( (\hat{\mathbf{u}}, \hat{\mathbf{v}}) \), then computing the rotation that aligns \( (\hat{\mathbf{u}}, \hat{\mathbf{v}}, \hat{\mathbf{n}}) \) with the original frame \( (\mathbf{u}_0, \mathbf{v}_0, \mathbf{n}_0) \), where \( \hat{\mathbf{n}} = \hat{\mathbf{u}} \times \hat{\mathbf{v}} \) and \( \mathbf{n}_0 \) is the original normal. This rotation is applied to \( \mathbf{l}^* \) and \( \mathbf{v}^* \) to yield \( \mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v} \).

The transformed query \( (\mathbf{p}_0, \mathbf{l}, \mathbf{v}) \) is then evaluated using the SRF. Interpolation is performed using ULR for both camera and light directions. Cameras are selected based on visibility and angular proximity, while light sources are interpolated without occlusion constraints to preserve translucent effects. For dynamic lighting, the invention uses a point light approximation: the environment map is subsampled into a dense set of virtual point lights, each contributing according to the BRDF-preserving look-up. This avoids per-pixel refiltering and supports real-time lighting changes.

To handle the massive data size, a deferred shading pipeline is employed. Shading operations are decomposed into minimal tasks, each requiring one SRF look-up. These tasks are sorted by data locality before execution, ensuring that reflectance images are loaded into a small cache (e.g., three images) in an optimal order. This reduces disk I/O by orders of magnitude.

Geometrically, the impostor is represented as a surfel cloud, where each surfel is defined by \( \mathbf{p}^*, \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \). During rasterization, surfels are rendered as elliptical splats whose shape adapts to the local deformation, preventing holes. The surfel attributes directly encode the local parameterization, unifying geometry and appearance.

This embodiment enables photorealistic animation of complex materials under arbitrary deformations and lighting, marking a significant advance in image-based rendering.

### Approximate BRDF Preservation

The concept of approximate BRDF preservation is central to the present invention and represents a fundamental departure from prior approaches to animating image-based representations. In traditional computer graphics, the Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF) is a mathematical function that describes how light is reflected at an opaque surface, defined as the ratio of reflected radiance to incident irradiance for given incoming and outgoing light directions. Real-world materials often exhibit complex, spatially varying BRDFs that cannot be captured by simple parametric models, which is why image-based methods like surface reflectance fields (SRFs) are employed—they sample the BRDF empirically across a wide range of lighting and viewing conditions.

However, when an object is deformed, the local geometry changes, altering the relationship between surface normals, light directions, and view directions. If the reflectance data is not adjusted accordingly, the material appearance will appear incorrect—for example, a specular highlight might shift unnaturally or disappear entirely. The goal of approximate BRDF preservation is to ensure that, after deformation, the observed reflectance at any point on the surface closely matches what would be expected if the original material were physically deformed in the same way.

The invention achieves this through a carefully designed look-up function that maps shading queries from deformed object space back to the original acquisition space in a manner that respects the local differential properties of the deformation. The key insight is that, while the true object geometry is unknown, the impostor geometry can be augmented with sufficient local information to approximate the necessary transformation.

The look-up function is derived under three primary conditions aimed at preserving the essential characteristics of the BRDF. First, the viewing ray in object space should intersect the impostor at a point that corresponds to the same material location as in the original acquisition. Since the true surface is unknown, this is approximated by ensuring that the origin of the viewing ray in acquisition space is the original impostor point \( \mathbf{p}_0 \). Second, the angles between the light direction, view direction, and surface normal must be preserved, as these angles determine the shape and intensity of reflectance lobes—critical for both isotropic and anisotropic materials. Third, for anisotropic materials, the azimuthal orientation of the light and view directions relative to the surface frame must be maintained, as this governs the directional dependence of the reflectance.

These conditions imply that the transformation applied to the light and view directions must be an isometry—that is, a rigid transformation comprising rotation and translation. Translation is handled by mapping the deformed point \( \mathbf{p}^* \) back to \( \mathbf{p}_0 \). Rotation is determined by aligning the local tangential frame of the deformed surface with that of the original. The local frame is defined by the tangent vectors \( \mathbf{u} \) and \( \math v \), which are updated during deformation to reflect the local warp. By orthogonalizing these vectors and computing the rotation that best aligns them with the stored original frame, the system ensures that the angular and orientational relationships required for BRDF preservation are maintained.

This approach is approximate because it relies on the impostor geometry rather than the true surface. Errors can arise in regions where the impostor deviates significantly from the object, leading to slight misalignments in the BRDF response. However, for reasonably accurate impostors—such as visual hulls—the approximation is visually compelling. Moreover, the method is robust to non-uniform deformations, including shearing and skewing, which are particularly challenging for other techniques.

Importantly, the invention does not attempt to model changes in the BRDF due to physical deformation of the material microstructure (e.g., stretching a fabric might alter its weave and thus its reflectance). Such effects would require detailed knowledge of the material's internal structure, which is not available in an SRF. Instead, the invention assumes that the original BRDF is to be preserved as-is, which is a reasonable assumption for many applications and aligns with user expectations in animation.

By enforcing these geometric constraints through a rigid transformation derived from local parameterization, the invention achieves a level of appearance preservation that was previously unattainable for deformable SRFs, enabling the photorealistic animation of complex real-world materials under arbitrary lighting.

## EFFECT OF THE INVENTION

The present invention has a profound and multifaceted effect on the field of computer graphics, particularly in the domains of image-based rendering, digital asset creation, and real-time animation. By enabling the first general-purpose method for animating surface reflectance fields with arbitrary deformations and dynamic relighting, the invention bridges a critical gap between the photorealistic fidelity of image-based representations and the flexibility of geometric animation. This breakthrough unlocks new possibilities for creating highly realistic, interactive digital content derived directly from physical objects, with significant implications across entertainment, design, and simulation.

One of the most immediate and tangible effects of the invention is the ability to animate complex real-world materials—such as fabrics, metals, plastics, and organic surfaces—with unprecedented visual accuracy. Prior to this invention, animating such materials required either labor-intensive manual modeling of parametric BRDFs, which often failed to capture subtle appearance details, or reliance on static image-based representations that could not be deformed. The invention eliminates this trade-off by allowing artists and developers to scan a real object once and then animate it freely, preserving the intricate interplay of light and material that makes the object visually distinctive. This is particularly valuable in film and game production, where the demand for realistic character clothing, props, and environments is high, and the cost of manual asset creation is prohibitive.

Moreover, the invention’s support for dynamic lighting—including moving point lights and environment maps—makes it suitable for interactive applications such as virtual reality and augmented reality. Users can now manipulate deformable, relightable objects in real time, with the material appearance responding correctly to changes in both shape and illumination. This enhances immersion and realism in virtual environments, enabling more natural interactions with digital content. For example, a virtual fashion designer could drape and stretch a scanned piece of fabric over a mannequin, observing how the material’s sheen and texture change under different lighting conditions, all without needing to understand the underlying physics of the material.

From a technical standpoint, the invention’s local parameterization and rigid transformation-based look-up scheme represent a novel and elegant solution to the problem of appearance preservation under deformation. Unlike previous methods that either assumed restrictive deformation types or required exact geometry, this approach works with approximate impostors and arbitrary warps, making it broadly applicable. The use of elliptical surfels for geometry representation further ensures that large deformations can be rendered without visual artifacts such as holes or cracks, which has been a persistent challenge in point-based rendering.

The deferred shading architecture with reordered evaluation also has a significant practical impact. By optimizing data access patterns and minimizing cache requirements, the invention makes it feasible to render animated SRFs on standard hardware, despite the enormous size of the datasets. This democratizes access to high-fidelity image-based rendering, moving it from the realm of offline, high-end production into more interactive and resource-constrained settings.

While the invention does not model global illumination effects such as self-shadowing or subsurface scattering—limitations inherent to the SRF representation itself—it provides a robust approximation that is visually convincing for a wide range of materials and deformations. Future extensions could integrate additional data, such as opacity hulls, to capture some of these effects, further enhancing realism.

In summary, the effect of the invention is transformative: it establishes a new paradigm for creating and animating photorealistic digital assets, combining the best of image-based and geometric approaches. It empowers creators to bring real-world objects into the digital domain with minimal effort and maximum fidelity, opening up new avenues for creativity, efficiency, and realism in computer graphics.
