# DESCRIPTION

## Example 1: Materials and Methods

The present invention provides a novel computational framework for the identification of minimally invasive surgical interventions in patients suffering from drug-resistant epilepsy, particularly in cases where the epileptogenic zone (EZ) is deemed inoperable due to its location within eloquent or functionally critical brain regions. The method integrates patient-specific structural brain connectivity data with advanced graph theoretical analysis and large-scale brain network simulations to derive optimized surgical targets—referred to herein as target zones (TZs)—that are distinct from the EZ but strategically positioned to suppress seizure propagation while preserving normal brain function. This approach is implemented through a closed-loop, iterative feedback system that alternates between modular decomposition of the brain connectome and in silico evaluation of candidate TZs using biophysically grounded neural mass models. The methodology is executed in a sequence of well-defined procedural steps, each of which is described in detail below.

The foundational step of the invention involves the reconstruction of a personalized structural brain network for each patient. This is achieved by processing high-resolution neuroimaging data, specifically diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI) and T1-weighted anatomical MRI scans, acquired using a 3 Tesla Siemens Magnetom Verio MR scanner. The dMRI data enables the mapping of white matter fiber tracts through tractography algorithms, while the T1-weighted images provide the cortical and subcortical parcellation necessary for node definition. The reconstruction pipeline employed in this invention utilizes the SCRIPTS (Structural Connectome Reconstruction and Integration for Patient-Specific Simulations) framework, which has been previously validated for clinical applications in epilepsy. Within this framework, the brain is partitioned into 84 distinct regions of interest: 68 cortical regions derived from the Desikan-Killiany atlas and 16 subcortical structures, including the thalamus, caudate, putamen, pallidum, hippocampus, amygdala, and cerebellar subdivisions. Each of these regions constitutes a node in the structural brain network. The edges between nodes are defined by the number of reconstructed streamlines connecting the corresponding regions, serving as a proxy for connection strength or weight. Additionally, the physical length of each fiber tract is computed to determine conduction delays, which are critical for accurately modeling the temporal dynamics of signal propagation in subsequent simulations. This patient-specific structural connectome forms the anatomical backbone upon which all subsequent computational analyses are performed.

Following structural network reconstruction, the invention proceeds to the derivation of candidate TZs through a constrained modularity analysis. Modularity, in the context of network science, refers to the degree to which a network can be subdivided into non-overlapping communities or modules such that connections within modules are denser than those between modules. The algorithm employed in this invention is based on Newman’s spectral optimization method, which seeks to maximize the modularity coefficient Q, defined as:

\[
Q = \frac{1}{2m} \sum_{i,j} \left[ A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m} \right] \delta(s_i, s_j)
\]

where \(A_{ij}\) is the weight of the connection between nodes i and j, \(k_i\) and \(k_j\) are the weighted degrees (sum of incident edge weights) of nodes i and j, m is the total edge weight in the network, and \(\delta(s_i, s_j)\) is 1 if nodes i and j belong to the same module and 0 otherwise. In practice, the algorithm computes the leading eigenvector of the modularity matrix B, whose elements are given by \(B_{ij} = A_{ij} - \frac{k_i k_j}{2m}\), and recursively partitions the network based on the signs of the eigenvector components. To enhance the resolution of the modular decomposition, a resolution parameter α is introduced, modifying the null model term to \(\alpha \frac{k_i k_j}{2m}\). By varying α from 0.5 to 1.5 in increments of 0.25, the invention generates a spectrum of modular configurations, ranging from coarse (few large modules at low α) to fine-grained (many small modules at high α). This parameter sweep is essential because it yields multiple candidate TZs, providing clinicians with a menu of surgical options that can be tailored to individual patient constraints.

Critically, the modularity analysis is augmented with a constraint mechanism to exclude inoperable zones from being selected as TZs. Inoperable zones are defined clinically as brain regions that, if resected or disconnected, would result in unacceptable neurological deficits—such as language impairment, motor dysfunction, or memory loss—and typically include the EZ itself in the worst-case scenario assumed by the invention. The constraint is implemented by ensuring that any inoperable node and its immediate neighbors (as defined by non-zero entries in the adjacency matrix) are assigned to the same module during the partitioning process. This is achieved by post-processing the initial eigenvector-based assignment: if an inoperable node and any of its neighbors are assigned to different modules, their group membership variables are harmonized to the majority value within the local neighborhood. This prevents the algorithm from identifying inoperable regions as inter-modular hubs—precisely the type of nodes that would otherwise be selected as TZs due to their high connectivity between modules. Once the constrained modular decomposition is complete, the EZ-containing module is further subdivided into sub-modules to isolate the EZ within a minimal functional unit. The TZs are then identified as the nodes (for resection) or edges (for disconnection) that serve as bridges between the EZ sub-module and all other sub-modules or modules. These bridging elements represent strategic chokepoints in the seizure propagation pathway and are thus prioritized as surgical targets.

The candidate TZs derived from modularity analysis are then subjected to rigorous in silico evaluation using large-scale brain network simulations implemented on The Virtual Brain (TVB) platform. Two distinct simulation paradigms are employed to assess effectiveness and safety, respectively. For effectiveness evaluation, the patient-specific network is modeled using the Epileptor—a six-dimensional phenomenological neural mass model capable of reproducing the full repertoire of seizure dynamics, including ictal onset, evolution, and termination. Each of the 84 brain regions is represented by an Epileptor, and the nodes are coupled through the structural connectivity matrix, with time delays proportional to fiber tract lengths. The excitability parameter \(x_0\) is set to -1.6 for nodes within the clinically defined EZ to induce spontaneous seizure activity, while all other nodes are assigned a value between -2.150 and -2.095, corresponding to a propagation-prone state that simulates the worst-case scenario of widespread seizure recruitment. Gaussian white noise is added to the fast subsystem to account for stochastic fluctuations and to generate interictal spikes as baseline activity. The simulation is run both before and after the virtual removal of candidate TZs (either by deleting nodes or setting edge weights to zero), and the resulting local field potentials (approximated by the sum \(x_1 + x_2\) of each Epileptor) are analyzed to quantify seizure propagation. The suppression ratio (SR) is computed as the percentage reduction in the number of seizure-recruited nodes post-intervention, providing a direct measure of therapeutic efficacy.

For safety evaluation, a separate simulation paradigm is employed that focuses on the integrity of resting-state (RS) functional networks. Rather than using the Epileptor, this assessment utilizes a generic two-dimensional oscillator model operating near a supercritical Andronov-Hopf bifurcation, which produces damped oscillations in response to transient perturbations. Eight canonical RS networks—default mode, visual, auditory-phonological, somato-motor, memory, ventral stream, dorsal attention, and working memory—are probed by applying a 2.5-second electrical pulse to specific stimulation sites known to elicit network-wide activation patterns resembling each RS network. The spatiotemporal response trajectories across all 84 nodes are recorded both before and after TZ removal. To quantify the impact of the intervention on network dynamics, a mode-level cognitive subtraction (MLCS) analysis is performed. Principal component analysis (PCA) is first applied to the pre-surgical response data to extract a low-dimensional subspace (typically the first three principal components) that captures the dominant modes of network activation. The post-surgical response data is then projected onto this same subspace, and the similarity between the reconstructed activation patterns is computed using a normalized overlap metric, yielding a similarity coefficient for each brain region. The mean similarity coefficient across all regions serves as the safety index: if this value falls below 0.75 for any RS network, the TZ is deemed unsafe due to excessive disruption of normal functional architecture. In such cases, the node within the TZ that causes the greatest degradation in similarity is identified as a critical node and reclassified as inoperable. This updated inoperability map is then fed back into the modularity analysis to generate a new set of candidate TZs, and the evaluation cycle repeats until a safe and effective solution is found.

This iterative feedback loop constitutes the core innovation of the invention, enabling the co-optimization of therapeutic efficacy and functional preservation. The entire workflow—from structural connectome reconstruction to final TZ selection—is automated within a custom MATLAB-based software environment, ensuring reproducibility and clinical scalability. By systematically exploring the space of possible surgical interventions outside the EZ, the invention provides a powerful decision-support tool for neurosurgeons treating complex epilepsy cases that are otherwise deemed inoperable.

## Example 2: Target Zone Derivation

The derivation of target zones (TZs) in accordance with the present invention is a sophisticated, multi-stage computational process that leverages the inherent modular architecture of the epileptic brain to identify optimal surgical targets located outside the epileptogenic zone (EZ). This process is predicated on the observation that the epileptic brain exhibits heightened segregation, characterized by increased local connectivity and reduced global integration, which manifests as a distinct modular structure when analyzed through graph theoretical frameworks. The invention exploits this pathological reorganization by strategically targeting the inter-modular connectors—nodes or edges that facilitate communication between the module containing the EZ and the rest of the brain network. The derivation procedure is designed to be both patient-specific and adaptable to clinical constraints, ensuring that the resulting TZs are not only theoretically optimal but also surgically feasible.

The initial phase of TZ derivation begins with the acquisition of a patient-specific structural connectome, as detailed in the preceding section. This connectome is represented as a weighted, directed graph comprising 84 nodes, each corresponding to a neuroanatomically defined brain region. The edge weights reflect the density of white matter fiber tracts between regions, while the edge lengths encode conduction delays. Concurrently, clinical data—including intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG), magnetoencephalography (MEG), and high-resolution MRI—are used to localize the EZ with the highest possible precision. Critically, for the purposes of this invention, the EZ is designated as inoperable, either because it overlaps with eloquent cortex or because its multifocal nature renders complete resection impractical. This assumption defines the worst-case scenario under which the invention operates, thereby ensuring that the derived TZs are applicable to the most challenging clinical cases.

With the structural connectome and EZ localization established, the invention proceeds to perform a constrained modularity analysis. The standard modularity maximization algorithm, as formulated by Newman, is modified to incorporate two key innovations: (1) a resolution parameter sweep to generate multiple modular decompositions, and (2) a constraint enforcement mechanism to exclude inoperable zones from inter-modular bridging roles. The resolution parameter α governs the scale of the detected modules; lower values of α favor larger, more inclusive modules, while higher values yield smaller, more numerous modules. By sweeping α from 0.5 to 1.5 in 0.25 increments, the invention produces a family of modular partitions, each offering a different perspective on the brain’s community structure. This multiplicity is crucial because it allows the identification of TZs at varying spatial scales, providing flexibility in surgical planning.

The constraint enforcement mechanism ensures that inoperable nodes—defined as the EZ and any other regions deemed critical for normal function—do not emerge as candidates for resection. This is achieved by post-processing the initial module assignments generated by the eigenvector algorithm. Specifically, for each inoperable node, the algorithm checks whether its immediate neighbors (i.e., nodes connected by non-zero edges) have been assigned to the same module. If discrepancies are found, the group membership variables of the inoperable node and its neighbors are adjusted to conform to the majority assignment within the local neighborhood. This effectively "locks" the inoperable region and its surroundings into a single module, preventing the algorithm from identifying it as a hub that connects disparate modules. As a result, the subsequent identification of TZs is biased toward operable regions that serve as alternative conduits for seizure propagation.

Once the constrained modular decomposition is complete, the invention isolates the module containing the EZ—referred to as the EZ module—and subjects it to a secondary partitioning step. This sub-modularization is necessary because, at certain resolution parameters, the EZ module may encompass a large number of brain regions, many of which are not directly involved in seizure generation. By recursively applying the modularity algorithm to the EZ module alone, the invention identifies a finer-grained structure in which the EZ is confined to a minimal sub-module. This refinement is critical for minimizing the extent of surgical intervention, as it ensures that only the most proximal propagation pathways are targeted.

The final step in TZ derivation is the identification of bridging elements between the EZ sub-module and the remainder of the network. For resection surgery, the invention selects target nodes—regions that have connections to multiple modules or sub-modules, thereby acting as integrators of inter-modular information flow. For disconnection surgery, the invention selects target edges—the specific fiber tracts that link the EZ sub-module to other modules. These bridging elements are identified algorithmically by scanning the adjacency matrix for non-zero entries that cross module boundaries. The resulting list of candidate TZs is then passed to the evaluation phase, where their therapeutic potential and safety profile are assessed through in silico simulations.

It is important to emphasize that the TZ derivation process is not static but rather embedded within an iterative feedback loop. If the initial set of TZs fails to meet safety criteria—as determined by the disruption of resting-state network dynamics—the most deleterious node within the TZ is reclassified as inoperable, and the modularity analysis is rerun with this updated constraint. This cycle of derivation, evaluation, and constraint updating continues until a set of TZs is found that simultaneously achieves high seizure suppression and minimal functional disruption. In the exemplary case of Patient IL described in the research paper, this process led to the reclassification of the left cerebellar cortex as inoperable after the initial TZs were found to severely impair the memory network. The subsequent re-analysis yielded a new set of TZs that maintained seizure suppression efficacy (89.86% SR for resection) while preserving all resting-state networks above the 0.75 similarity threshold.

The robustness of the TZ derivation process is further demonstrated by systematic analyses in which the EZ is artificially placed in every possible brain region. These simulations reveal that certain nodes and edges consistently emerge as high-frequency TZs across different EZ locations, indicating their role as universal hubs in the seizure propagation network. For instance, in Patient CV, the right postcentral gyrus (node 70) was the most frequently selected target node, while the connection between the left supramarginal and postcentral gyri was the most common target edge. Such findings not only validate the biological plausibility of the invention but also provide a reference library of high-yield targets that can inform clinical decision-making when EZ localization is uncertain.

In summary, the TZ derivation process of the present invention represents a paradigm shift in epilepsy surgery planning. By moving beyond the traditional focus on EZ resection and instead targeting the network infrastructure that enables seizure spread, the invention opens new therapeutic avenues for patients who would otherwise be denied surgical intervention. The integration of patient-specific anatomy, graph theory, and dynamic simulations ensures that the derived TZs are both scientifically grounded and clinically actionable.

## Example 3: Systematic Analysis According to an Epileptogenic Zone Location

The present invention further encompasses a systematic analytical framework for evaluating the robustness and generalizability of the target zone (TZ) derivation process across varying epileptogenic zone (EZ) locations. This framework is designed to address the inherent uncertainty in EZ localization that often complicates presurgical planning in epilepsy and to provide clinicians with a probabilistic map of high-yield surgical targets that remain effective across a range of plausible EZ scenarios. The systematic analysis is conducted through a series of in silico experiments in which the EZ is sequentially placed in each of the 84 brain regions of a patient-specific structural connectome, and the resulting TZs are cataloged to identify recurrent patterns of network vulnerability. This approach not only validates the biological plausibility of the invention but also generates actionable insights for clinical decision-making in cases of ambiguous or multifocal EZ localization.

The methodology for the systematic analysis begins with the selection of a representative patient structural connectome, reconstructed as described in the preceding sections. For each of the 84 brain regions, a virtual EZ is instantiated by setting the excitability parameter of the corresponding Epileptor to -1.6, while all other regions are assigned propagation-prone excitability values. The constrained modularity analysis is then executed with the virtual EZ designated as inoperable, yielding a set of candidate TZs for that specific EZ location. This process is repeated iteratively until all 84 possible EZ locations have been evaluated, resulting in a comprehensive atlas of TZs mapped onto the patient’s individual brain geometry. The cumulative frequency of each node and edge appearing as a TZ across all simulations is then computed, producing heat maps that highlight the brain regions and connections most consistently implicated in seizure propagation.

The results of this systematic analysis reveal several key insights. First, there is a strong positive correlation (r = 0.7842) between the initial frequency of a node being selected as a TZ and its node strength—the sum of its connection weights to all other nodes. This finding is consistent with the intuitive notion that highly connected hubs are more likely to serve as critical conduits for seizure spread. However, after the safety-constrained feedback loop is applied, this correlation weakens significantly (r = 0.3059), indicating that the most connected nodes are often also the most functionally critical and thus excluded from the final TZ set. This decoupling of structural prominence from surgical candidacy underscores the necessity of the safety evaluation step and highlights the invention’s ability to balance efficacy with functional preservation.

Second, the systematic analysis identifies a small set of brain regions that emerge as recurrent TZs across diverse EZ locations. In the exemplary case of Patient CV, the right postcentral gyrus (node 70) was the most frequently derived target node, appearing in over 60% of simulations, while the connection between the left supramarginal and postcentral gyri was the most common target edge. Anatomically, these regions correspond to the somatosensory cortex and its immediate association areas, which are known to have dense reciprocal connections with both frontal and parietal networks. Their recurrent identification as TZs suggests that they occupy a privileged position in the brain’s macroscale wiring diagram, serving as obligatory waypoints for the propagation of pathological activity from focal onset zones to distributed cortical territories. Similar patterns were observed across all seven patients analyzed, with primary and secondary sensory cortices, along with select prefrontal regions, consistently ranking among the top TZ candidates.

Third, the analysis reveals a striking consistency in the identity of critical nodes—the regions that, when removed, cause the greatest disruption to resting-state network dynamics. Across all seven patients, the superior frontal cortex (nodes 27 and 76) was the most frequently identified critical node, appearing in over 85% of cases where safety violations occurred. This region is known to be a major hub in the brain’s default mode and frontoparietal control networks, with extensive interhemispheric and transmodal connections. Its high node strength and central role in cognitive processing make it an effective target for seizure suppression but also render it unsuitable for resection due to the risk of working memory impairment and transient motor deficits, as corroborated by clinical literature. The recurrent identification of the superior frontal cortex as a critical node validates the biological fidelity of the invention’s safety evaluation paradigm and provides a concrete example of how the feedback loop successfully navigates the trade-off between therapeutic efficacy and functional preservation.

The clinical utility of the systematic analysis extends beyond validation. In cases where presurgical evaluation yields multiple competing hypotheses for EZ localization—for example, when iEEG recordings suggest bilateral temporal lobe involvement or when MEG source imaging points to a broad cortical region—the cumulative TZ atlas can serve as a decision-support tool. By overlaying the candidate EZ regions with the TZ frequency map, clinicians can identify surgical targets that are effective across all plausible scenarios, thereby increasing the likelihood of a successful outcome. Moreover, the analysis provides a quantitative basis for risk stratification: targets that appear frequently across EZ locations can be prioritized for minimally invasive interventions such as laser ablation or focused ultrasound, while those that are scenario-specific may warrant more extensive mapping prior to resection.

In conclusion, the systematic analysis according to EZ location is an integral component of the present invention, demonstrating its robustness across a wide range of pathological configurations and providing clinicians with a powerful tool for navigating the uncertainties of presurgical planning. By transforming the problem of EZ localization from a binary decision into a probabilistic landscape of network vulnerabilities, the invention paves the way for more personalized and effective epilepsy surgery.
