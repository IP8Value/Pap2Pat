# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of electrochemical sensing, particularly to ion-selective electrodes (ISEs) that incorporate metal–organic supercontainers (MOSCs) as novel ionophores for the selective and sensitive detection of molecular ions. More specifically, the invention provides a mixed-matrix membrane (MMM) comprising a MOSC-based ionophore embedded within a polymeric matrix, which is integrated into both conventional liquid-contact and solid-contact ISE architectures. The invention further encompasses methods for fabricating such sensors, their use in potentiometric measurements, and applications in environmental monitoring, including the detection of organic cationic pollutants such as methylene blue (MB⁺) in complex aqueous matrices like river water. The MOSC employed in the invention—designated herein as 1-Co—exhibits a unique nanostructured architecture featuring a central endo-cavity and six peripheral exo-cavities, enabling size-selective recognition of large molecular cations through geometric complementarity and non-covalent interactions such as cation–π effects. This structural design confers exceptional selectivity over small inorganic ions (e.g., K⁺, Ag⁺, NH₄⁺) while maintaining high sensitivity, rapid response kinetics, and long-term stability against leaching of membrane components. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the development of biomimetic, cavity-based ionophores for next-generation electrochemical sensors capable of discriminating molecular ions based on size and shape, rather than solely on charge or chemical functionality.

## BACKGROUND

Ion-selective electrodes (ISEs) have long served as indispensable tools in analytical chemistry, environmental science, clinical diagnostics, and industrial process control due to their simplicity, low cost, real-time response, and compatibility with miniaturization. Conventional ISEs operate on the principle of potentiometry, wherein the potential difference developed across a selective membrane interface correlates with the activity of a target ion in solution according to the Nernst equation. The heart of any ISE is the ion-selective membrane (ISM), typically composed of a plasticized polymer matrix—most commonly poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC)—doped with an ionophore (a molecule that selectively binds the target ion), lipophilic ionic sites (to ensure membrane electroneutrality and reduce interference), and a plasticizer (to impart flexibility and facilitate ion mobility). Historically, ionophores have been designed to recognize small inorganic ions (e.g., Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) or simple organic ions through coordination chemistry, hydrogen bonding, or electrostatic interactions. However, the selective detection of larger, structurally complex molecular ions—such as dyes, pharmaceuticals, neurotransmitters, or environmental contaminants—has remained a significant challenge due to the lack of ionophores with appropriately sized, preorganized binding cavities that can discriminate based on molecular dimensions rather than just functional group affinity.

Calixarenes, cyclodextrins, and crown ethers have been explored as macrocyclic ionophores for molecular recognition, but their binding pockets are often too small or insufficiently rigid to accommodate bulky organic cations with high selectivity. Moreover, many such receptors exhibit cross-reactivity with smaller ions that can access the same binding sites, leading to poor selectivity coefficients. In recent years, metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) and related supramolecular architectures have emerged as promising platforms for molecular sensing due to their tunable porosity and high surface area. However, their integration into polymeric membranes for ISEs has been hindered by issues such as poor dispersibility, particle aggregation, leaching, and incompatibility with standard membrane fabrication techniques. Furthermore, most MOFs function as passive adsorbents rather than active transducers in electrochemical systems, failing to generate a measurable phase-boundary potential change upon analyte binding.

The inventors have previously reported a new class of discrete, nanoscale, cage-like molecules termed metal–organic supercontainers (MOSCs), which are synthesized from the self-assembly of transition metal ions with functionalized calixarene ligands and bridging carboxylates. Unlike extended MOFs, MOSCs are soluble, monodisperse, and possess well-defined internal and external cavities. One such MOSC, designated 1-Co, is formed from Co(II), p-tert-butylsulfonyl-calix[4]arene, and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate, resulting in a roughly spherical structure with an outer diameter of 3.3 nm, an inner (endo) cavity of 1.7 nm diameter, and six smaller exo-cavities (~0.74 nm in diameter) arranged around the periphery. Single-crystal X-ray diffraction studies have confirmed that 1-Co can encapsulate up to seven equivalents of methylene blue (MB⁺), a planar, aromatic cationic dye approximately 1.6 nm in length and 0.7 nm in width, fitting precisely within the endo- and exo-cavities. This geometric complementarity, combined with favorable cation–π interactions between the electron-rich aromatic walls of the cavities and the positively charged MB⁺, results in strong and selective binding.

Despite these promising recognition properties, the application of MOSCs as ionophores in ISEs had not been demonstrated prior to the present invention. A critical challenge in ISE design is ensuring that the ionophore not only binds the target ion but also facilitates its transfer across the membrane–solution interface, thereby generating a measurable phase-boundary potential. Additionally, the ionophore must be immobilized within the membrane to prevent leaching, which would degrade sensor performance and contaminate the sample. Conventional approaches using unbound dyes or small-molecule ionophores often suffer from leaching, especially in aqueous environments. The present invention overcomes these limitations by embedding the MOSC directly into a PVC-based mixed-matrix membrane, where it acts as a multifunctional ionophore that simultaneously provides high-capacity binding, size-selective recognition, and stable immobilization of the target ion.

Furthermore, the invention extends the utility of MOSC-based ISEs to solid-contact configurations, which eliminate the need for an internal filling solution and enable miniaturization, integration with microelectronics, and improved mechanical robustness. Solid-contact ISEs require a conductive substrate that provides capacitive buffering and minimizes potential drift; the inventors have successfully integrated the MOSC-containing MMM onto a pyrolyzed SU-8-derived carbon layer, demonstrating compatibility with standard microfabrication processes. Prior art does not disclose or suggest the use of discrete, cavity-containing MOSCs as ionophores in either conventional or solid-contact ISEs, nor does it address the synergistic combination of endo- and exo-cavities for enhanced local concentration and selective potentiometric response to molecular ions. The present invention thus fills a critical gap in the field by providing a new paradigm for molecular ion sensing based on biomimetic, size-exclusion principles analogous to those found in enzyme active sites or biological ion channels.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides an ion-selective electrode (ISE) comprising a mixed-matrix membrane (MMM) that incorporates a metal–organic supercontainer (MOSC) as a size-selective ionophore for the potentiometric detection of molecular cations. The MOSC, specifically designated as 1-Co, is a discrete, nanoscale cage-like molecule formed from the coordination of cobalt(II) ions with p-tert-butylsulfonyl-calix[4]arene and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate linkers, resulting in a structure with one central endo-cavity (approximately 1.7 nm in diameter) and six peripheral exo-cavities (each approximately 0.74 nm in diameter). This unique architecture enables the selective binding of large molecular cations—such as methylene blue (MB⁺) and tetrabutylammonium (TBA⁺)—that geometrically match the cavity dimensions, while excluding smaller inorganic ions like K⁺, Ag⁺, and NH₄⁺. The MMM is fabricated by dissolving poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC) and a plasticizer (bis(2-ethylhexyl)sebacate, DOS) in tetrahydrofuran (THF), followed by the addition of a lipophilic ionic additive (potassium tetrakis(4-chlorophenyl)borate, KTpClPB), the MOSC (1-Co), and a redox-active indicator dye (methylene blue, MB⁺) that also serves as the target analyte. The resulting membrane solution is then applied to either a conventional ISE body (with an internal Ag/AgCl reference element and KCl filling solution) or a solid-contact substrate (such as pyrolyzed SU-8-derived carbon) to form a functional sensor.

The ISE of the invention exhibits a near-Nernstian potentiometric response to MB⁺ with a slope of 56.8 ± 2.5 mV per decade of concentration and a detection limit of approximately 300 nM. Similarly, it responds to TBA⁺ with a slope of 60.0 ± 1.5 mV/decade and a detection limit of about 1 μM. Critically, the response to smaller cations is negligible, confirming the size-selective nature of the MOSC ionophore. The sensor demonstrates rapid response times (less than 1 second for concentrations above 30 μM), excellent potential stability (drift < few mV/hour), and exceptional resistance to leaching—attributable to the strong encapsulation of MB⁺ within the MOSC cavities. The invention further includes methods for fabricating both conventional and solid-contact ISEs, as well as their application in real-world scenarios, such as the detection of MB⁺ in simulated wastewater samples derived from river water. Despite the presence of natural organic matter and reducing agents in such samples, the ISE maintains its near-Nernstian response, albeit with a slight shift in detection limit due to partial reduction of MB⁺ to its colorless leuco form, which is not recognized by the sensor. This feature enables the potential use of the ISE for potentiometric titration of redox-active species. The invention thus establishes MOSCs as a new class of architecturally defined, functionally robust ionophores that overcome the limitations of traditional receptors and enable selective, sensitive, and stable electrochemical sensing of molecular ions in complex environments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to an ion-selective electrode (ISE) system that utilizes a metal–organic supercontainer (MOSC) as a novel ionophore within a mixed-matrix membrane (MMM) to achieve size-selective potentiometric detection of molecular cations. The core innovation lies in the structural design of the MOSC, which provides multiple, well-defined binding cavities that act as molecular sieves, allowing only ions of compatible size and shape to be recognized and transduced into a measurable electrical signal. The following detailed description outlines the composition of the MMM, the fabrication of both conventional and solid-contact ISEs, the underlying electrochemical mechanisms, and the performance characteristics that demonstrate the advantages of the invention over prior art.

The mixed-matrix membrane is prepared by first dissolving high molecular weight poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC) and a plasticizer, specifically bis(2-ethylhexyl)sebacate (DOS), in a volatile organic solvent such as tetrahydrofuran (THF). The PVC serves as the structural backbone of the membrane, providing mechanical integrity and a hydrophobic environment that minimizes water uptake. The DOS plasticizer enhances the flexibility of the polymer matrix and facilitates ion mobility, which is essential for rapid sensor response. Once a homogeneous solution is obtained, a lipophilic ionic additive—potassium tetrakis(4-chlorophenyl)borate (KTpClPB)—is introduced. This additive functions as a source of ionic sites that ensure membrane electroneutrality during ion exchange and suppress the co-extraction of interfering anions, thereby improving selectivity. Subsequently, the MOSC, designated as 1-Co, is added to the solution. The 1-Co MOSC is synthesized according to established procedures involving the reaction of Co(II) salts with p-tert-butylsulfonyl-calix[4]arene and 1,4-benzenedicarboxylate under solvothermal conditions, yielding a discrete, octahedral cage with a total of seven binding domains: one central endo-cavity and six exo-cavities. Upon addition of 1-Co, the solution acquires a transparent light red color, indicative of the MOSC’s presence. Finally, methylene blue (MB⁺) is incorporated into the membrane formulation. While MB⁺ serves as the target analyte in sensing applications, its inclusion in the membrane during fabrication ensures that the MOSC cavities are pre-loaded, which stabilizes the membrane potential and prevents initial leaching. The solution turns dark blue upon MB⁺ addition, reflecting the formation of the MB⁺@1-Co inclusion complex.

For the fabrication of a conventional ISE, the final MMM solution is drawn into a disposable pipette tip via capillary action. An Ag/AgCl pellet is inserted into the narrow end of the tip, and the internal cavity is filled with a saturated KCl solution containing AgCl to establish a stable reference potential. The electrode is then conditioned by immersion in a dilute MB⁺ solution (e.g., 10 μM) overnight, allowing the membrane to equilibrate and the interfacial potential to stabilize. In contrast, the solid-contact ISE (SC-ISE) is fabricated on a silicon wafer coated with a 650 nm layer of SiO₂. A thin film of SU-8 photoresist is spin-coated onto the wafer and then pyrolyzed at 900°C under a reducing atmosphere (95% N₂/5% H₂) to convert it into a conductive, porous carbon layer. Polyethylene wells are adhered to this carbon surface to define the sensing area, and 40 μL of the MMM solution is drop-cast into each well. After drying, the SC-ISE is similarly conditioned in 10 μM MB⁺. The pyrolyzed SU-8-derived carbon provides a high-capacitance, redox-stable interface that minimizes potential drift and eliminates the need for an internal electrolyte, making the SC-ISE suitable for miniaturization and integration into lab-on-a-chip devices.

Electrochemical characterization of the ISEs is performed using a high-impedance potentiometer referenced to a standard Ag/AgCl electrode. Impedance spectroscopy reveals that the inclusion of 1-Co reduces the charge-transfer resistance at the membrane–solution interface by a factor of two compared to a reference membrane lacking the MOSC. This indicates that 1-Co actively promotes the transfer of MB⁺ across the interface, a prerequisite for potentiometric sensing. Potentiometric titrations show that the 1-Co MMM ISE exhibits a near-Nernstian response to MB⁺ with a slope of 56.8 ± 2.5 mV per decade, consistent with the theoretical Nernstian value of 59.2 mV for a monovalent cation at 25°C. The detection limit is approximately 300 nM, and the response time is less than 1 second for concentrations above 30 μM. Crucially, the sensor shows minimal response to K⁺, Ag⁺, and NH₄⁺, confirming that the MOSC’s cavities are too large to effectively bind these small ions. In contrast, tetrabutylammonium (TBA⁺), a spherical cation with a diameter of ~0.8 nm, elicits a near-Nernstian response (60.0 ± 1.5 mV/decade), demonstrating that the selectivity is governed by size rather than specific chemical functionality. The rapid response and low drift (< few mV/hour) attest to the stability and efficiency of the MOSC-mediated ion exchange process.

Leaching tests confirm that the MB⁺ is effectively immobilized within the MOSC cavities. When MMMs with and without 1-Co are stored in deionized water for over a week, only the control membrane (without MOSC) releases MB⁺ into the solution, as evidenced by the appearance of a blue color. The 1-Co-containing membrane retains its color, indicating that the MB⁺ remains bound. Furthermore, control electrodes lacking either the MOSC or the entire MMM show no significant response to MB⁺, proving that the observed potentiometric signal arises specifically from the MOSC–analyte interaction. The invention has also been validated in a simulated wastewater scenario using Fyris River water spiked with known concentrations of MB⁺. Despite the presence of natural organic matter and microbial activity—which partially reduces MB⁺ to its non-ionic leuco form—the ISE maintains a near-Nernstian response, though the detection limit shifts slightly due to the lower effective concentration of cationic MB⁺. This behavior suggests that the sensor could be used not only for direct detection but also for monitoring redox processes in environmental samples.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, selective, and versatile platform for the electrochemical sensing of molecular ions by leveraging the unique cavity architecture of MOSCs. The compatibility with both conventional and solid-contact formats, combined with exceptional stability and size-based selectivity, positions this technology for applications in environmental monitoring, biomedical diagnostics, and industrial process control where the discrimination of large organic cations from background inorganic ions is essential.

### EXAMPLE 1: PROOF-OF-CONCEPT STUDY WITH MB+ AND TBA+

A proof-of-concept study was conducted to validate the size-selective ionophoric capability of the 1-Co MOSC when incorporated into a mixed-matrix membrane (MMM) for use in ion-selective electrodes (ISEs). The target analytes selected for this study were methylene blue (MB⁺) and tetrabutylammonium (TBA⁺), both of which are organic cations with dimensions that closely match the endo- and exo-cavities of the 1-Co supercontainer. Methylene blue, a planar heterocyclic aromatic compound, measures approximately 1.6 nm in length and 0.7 nm in width, allowing it to align either longitudinally within the 1.7 nm endo-cavity or laterally within the 0.74 nm exo-cavities of the MOSC. Tetrabutylammonium, in contrast, is a roughly spherical cation with a diameter of about 0.8 nm, making it sterically compatible with the exo-cavities. These geometric considerations formed the basis for hypothesizing that both ions would be selectively recognized by the 1-Co MOSC, while smaller inorganic cations would be excluded.

Conventional ISEs were fabricated using the MMM formulation described herein, which included PVC, DOS, KTpClPB, 1-Co, and a small amount of MB⁺ as a membrane component. The electrodes were conditioned in 10 μM MB⁺ overnight and then subjected to potentiometric titration in aqueous solutions containing increasing concentrations of either MB⁺ or TBA⁺. The resulting calibration curves demonstrated near-Nernstian responses for both analytes: a slope of 56.8 ± 2.5 mV per decade was obtained for MB⁺, with a detection limit of approximately 300 nM, while TBA⁺ yielded a slope of 60.0 ± 1.5 mV per decade and a detection limit of about 1 μM. These results confirm that the 1-Co MOSC functions as an effective ionophore for both molecular ions, transducing their binding into a measurable phase-boundary potential change in accordance with the Nernst equation. The slight deviation from the ideal 59.2 mV/decade slope is attributed to minor non-idealities in membrane composition and interfacial kinetics, but remains well within the acceptable range for practical sensing applications.

Response time experiments further underscored the efficiency of the MOSC-mediated ion exchange. For concentrations above 30 μM, both MB⁺ and TBA⁺ induced a >90% potential change within 1 second, indicating rapid diffusion and binding kinetics at the membrane–solution interface. At lower concentrations (near the detection limit), the response time increased to 50–100 seconds, which is consistent with slower ion-exchange rates under diffusion-limited conditions. Importantly, the potential drift over time was minimal, remaining below a few millivolts per hour, which attests to the thermodynamic stability of the MB⁺@1-Co and TBA⁺@1-Co complexes within the membrane matrix. Control experiments with ISEs lacking the 1-Co MOSC showed no significant response to either MB⁺ or TBA⁺, confirming that the observed potentiometric signal is directly attributable to the MOSC’s ionophoric activity.

This proof-of-concept study thus establishes that the 1-Co MOSC can serve as a versatile ionophore for structurally distinct molecular cations, provided their dimensions are commensurate with the MOSC’s cavity architecture. The ability to detect both planar and spherical cations with high sensitivity and selectivity highlights the generality of the size-matching principle underlying the invention.

### EXAMPLE 2: LEECHING STUDY

To evaluate the long-term stability and resistance to leaching of the mixed-matrix membrane (MMM) incorporating the 1-Co MOSC, a dedicated leaching study was performed. Leaching of membrane components—particularly ionophores and indicator dyes—is a common failure mode in ISEs, leading to signal drift, reduced sensitivity, and sample contamination. In this study, two MMM formulations were prepared: one containing the full complement of components (PVC, DOS, KTpClPB, 1-Co, and MB⁺) and a control formulation identical in all respects except for the absence of 1-Co. Both membranes were cast as thin films and immersed in deionized (DI) water for a period exceeding one week. The aqueous phase was monitored visually and spectroscopically for the presence of leached MB⁺.

The results were strikingly clear: the DI water in contact with the control membrane (without 1-Co) rapidly developed a distinct blue color, indicating significant leaching of free MB⁺ from the membrane into the solution. In contrast, the water surrounding the 1-Co-containing membrane remained colorless throughout the entire duration of the experiment, demonstrating that the MB⁺ was effectively retained within the membrane. This retention is attributed to the strong encapsulation of MB⁺ within the endo- and exo-cavities of the 1-Co MOSC, which forms a stable host–guest complex with an apparent binding constant of 1.42 × 10⁴ M⁻¹. The high binding affinity and steric confinement prevent the MB⁺ from diffusing out of the membrane, even under prolonged exposure to aqueous media.

UV-visible spectroscopy corroborated these visual observations, showing a strong absorbance peak at 664 nm (characteristic of MB⁺) in the solution from the control membrane, but no detectable absorbance in the solution from the 1-Co-containing membrane. These findings confirm that the MOSC acts not only as an ionophore but also as a molecular anchor that immobilizes the target ion within the membrane matrix. This dual functionality is a critical advantage over conventional ionophores, which often require additional strategies (e.g., covalent tethering or bulky substituents) to mitigate leaching. The leaching study thus validates the robustness of the 1-Co MMM for long-term deployment in aqueous environments, a prerequisite for real-world sensing applications.

### EXAMPLE 3: SIMULATED WASTE WATER SAMPLE

To assess the practical utility of the 1-Co MOSC-based ISE in environmentally relevant conditions, a simulated wastewater experiment was conducted using water collected from the Fyris River (Fyrisån) in Uppsala, Sweden. Natural river water presents a complex matrix containing dissolved organic matter, inorganic ions, microorganisms, and variable oxygen levels, all of which can interfere with electrochemical sensing. In this test, known concentrations of methylene blue (MB⁺) were spiked into the river water, and the response of the ISE was compared to that obtained in pure deionized (DI) water.

The calibration curve generated in Fyris River water exhibited a near-Nernstian slope (comparable to that in DI water), confirming that the sensor retains its sensitivity in complex media. However, a slight upward shift in the detection limit was observed, which was traced to a reduction in the effective concentration of cationic MB⁺. UV-vis spectroscopy revealed that the absorbance of MB⁺ in river water was lower than in DI water at the same nominal concentration, indicating partial reduction of MB⁺ to its colorless, non-ionic leuco form. This reduction is attributed to the presence of microorganisms and reducing agents in the river water, which consume dissolved oxygen and create a mildly reducing environment. Since the ISE responds only to the cationic form of MB⁺, the lower active concentration results in a higher apparent detection limit.

Despite this matrix effect, the ISE maintained excellent linearity and reproducibility, demonstrating its resilience to real-world interferences. Moreover, this behavior opens up a novel application: the ISE could be used as a potentiometric probe for monitoring redox processes in environmental samples. By measuring the potential shift induced by the reduction of MB⁺, one could indirectly quantify the reducing capacity of the water, offering a new approach to assessing water quality. This example underscores the adaptability of the MOSC-based ISE platform to challenging analytical scenarios and highlights its potential for environmental monitoring beyond simple concentration measurements.

### EXAMPLE 4: MOLECULAR ION SENSING

The invention’s capability for molecular ion sensing was further demonstrated by evaluating the selectivity of the 1-Co MOSC-based ISE against a panel of cations spanning a wide range of sizes and chemical structures. In addition to the primary targets MB⁺ and TBA⁺, the sensor was exposed to small inorganic cations including K⁺ (ionic radius 0.275 nm), Ag⁺ (0.172 nm), and NH₄⁺ (0.143 nm). Potentiometric measurements revealed negligible responses to these ions: slopes of 10.4 ± 2.1 mV/p[K⁺], 5.0 ± 7.3 mV/p[Ag⁺], and −6.4 ± 1.4 mV/p[NH₄⁺] were recorded, all far below the Nernstian expectation and indicative of minimal interference. This stark contrast in response underscores the size-selective nature of the MOSC ionophore, which excludes ions that are too small to engage effectively with the cavity walls.

The selectivity mechanism is rooted in the architectural design of 1-Co. Unlike conventional calixarenes, which often feature functional groups on the lower rim for metal ion coordination, the calixarene units in 1-Co are fully engaged in coordinating Co(II) centers and are further bridged by benzenedicarboxylate linkers to form enclosed aromatic cavities. This configuration eliminates accessible binding sites for small metal ions while creating a hydrophobic, π-electron-rich environment ideal for cation–π interactions with large organic cations. The six exo-cavities act as auxiliary binding sites that concentrate the target ion near the membrane interface, enhancing both sensitivity and kinetics. This multi-cavity arrangement is a key innovation that distinguishes MOSCs from other macrocyclic receptors.

The successful discrimination of molecular ions from inorganic interferents confirms that the invention enables a new mode of electrochemical sensing based on molecular dimensions rather than chemical affinity alone. This capability is particularly valuable for detecting organic pollutants, pharmaceuticals, or biomarkers in samples with high ionic strength, where traditional ISEs often fail due to overwhelming background signals. The MOSC-based ISE thus represents a paradigm shift in ion-selective sensing, offering a biomimetic approach inspired by the size-exclusion principles of biological receptors.
