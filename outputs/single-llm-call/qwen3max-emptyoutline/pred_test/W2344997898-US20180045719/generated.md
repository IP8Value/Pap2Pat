# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of molecular biology and proteomics, and more specifically to methods and systems for the identification, characterization, and isolation of protein–protein interactions (PPIs) and protein–small molecule interactions under native cellular conditions. The invention provides a novel platform—referred to herein as “Virotrap”—that enables the capture and purification of endogenous or recombinant protein complexes without the need for cell lysis, thereby preserving the integrity of labile, transient, or weakly associated molecular assemblies that are often disrupted during conventional affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry (AP–MS). The Virotrap system leverages the natural biogenesis of virus-like particles (VLPs) derived from retroviral structural proteins, particularly the Gag polyprotein of human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1), to encapsulate bait-prey protein complexes within a protective proteinaceous shell during their assembly and secretion from mammalian cells. This physical encapsulation shields the trapped complexes from extracellular proteases, denaturants, and dissociative forces encountered during downstream purification steps. The invention further encompasses engineered chimeric constructs wherein a protein of interest (the “bait”) is fused to the Gag protein, enabling its incorporation into nascent VLPs and concomitant co-packaging of its interaction partners (the “prey”). Additionally, the invention includes adaptations of the Virotrap platform for the detection of interactions between small molecules and their cellular protein targets by employing bifunctional chemical probes that bridge a drug of interest to an engineered bait protein anchored within the VLP scaffold. The technical scope of the invention thus spans fundamental research in signal transduction, drug discovery, systems biology, and the functional annotation of the human interactome, offering a lysis-independent, physiologically relevant alternative to existing PPI mapping technologies such as yeast two-hybrid systems, classical AP–MS, proximity-dependent biotinylation (e.g., BioID, APEX), and mammalian two-hybrid assays.

## BACKGROUND

The comprehensive mapping of protein–protein interactions (PPIs) is a cornerstone of modern molecular and cellular biology, essential for understanding the architecture of signaling networks, the regulation of metabolic pathways, and the mechanisms underlying disease pathogenesis. Over the past two decades, numerous high-throughput methodologies have been developed to interrogate the human interactome, each with distinct advantages and limitations. Among these, affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry (AP–MS) has emerged as a widely adopted gold standard for the identification of co-complexed proteins. In a typical AP–MS workflow, a protein of interest (the “bait”) is epitope-tagged and expressed in cultured cells; following cell lysis under defined buffer conditions, the bait and its associated partners (the “prey”) are immunoprecipitated using an antibody against the tag, and the co-purified proteins are identified by mass spectrometry. Despite its utility, AP–MS is inherently susceptible to artifacts introduced during the cell lysis step. The disruption of cellular membranes and organelles leads to a dramatic alteration in the native subcellular environment, including changes in pH, ionic strength, macromolecular crowding, and compartmentalization. These perturbations can result in the dissociation of weak or transient interactions, the formation of non-physiological aggregates, or the spurious association of proteins that would not normally encounter one another in intact cells. Indeed, systematic studies have demonstrated that the choice of lysis buffer—varying in detergent type, salt concentration, and pH—can profoundly influence the composition of purified complexes, with no single condition capable of preserving all types of interactions. Consequently, researchers are often compelled to optimize lysis conditions empirically for each bait protein, a labor-intensive and impractical approach for large-scale interactome studies.

In response to these limitations, several “lysis-independent” strategies have been developed to probe PPIs in living cells. Proximity-dependent biotinylation techniques, such as BioID and APEX, fuse a promiscuous biotin ligase or peroxidase to the bait protein; upon addition of biotin or biotin-phenol, respectively, proximal proteins are covalently labeled and subsequently captured on streptavidin beads for identification. While these methods excel at mapping proximal neighborhoods and capturing transient interactions, they do not isolate intact protein complexes and may label proteins that are merely in close spatial proximity rather than direct binding partners. Mammalian two-hybrid systems, including MAPPIT (Mammalian Protein–Protein Interaction Trap), rely on the reconstitution of a signaling cascade (e.g., JAK–STAT) upon bait–prey interaction, providing a binary readout but limited information about complex stoichiometry or composition. Classical pull-down assays using immobilized small molecules suffer from similar drawbacks as AP–MS, requiring cell lysis and often yielding high background due to non-specific binding to the solid support.

An unmet need persists for a method that combines the ability to isolate intact, multi-protein complexes under truly native conditions with compatibility with downstream proteomic analysis. The present invention addresses this gap through a fundamentally different conceptual approach: rather than attempting to stabilize complexes after cell disruption, it physically sequesters them within a protective nanocontainer prior to any external manipulation. This strategy draws inspiration from the natural life cycle of enveloped viruses, which assemble and bud from host cells while encapsulating viral genomic RNA and associated proteins within a protein shell derived from the viral Gag polyprotein. By repurposing this self-assembling machinery, the Virotrap system co-opts the cell’s own secretory pathway to package bait–prey complexes into virus-like particles (VLPs) that are released into the culture medium. Because the encapsulation occurs intracellularly during VLP biogenesis, the complexes are shielded from the extracellular environment and remain undisturbed by subsequent purification procedures. This paradigm shift—from post-lysis stabilization to pre-lysis entrapment—offers significant advantages in preserving the authenticity of protein interactions, particularly those that are weak, dynamic, or dependent on specific post-translational modifications or subcellular localization.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method and system for the isolation and identification of protein complexes and protein–small molecule interactions under native, lysis-free conditions, termed “Virotrap.” The core principle of the invention involves the fusion of a protein of interest (the “bait”) to a viral structural protein, preferably the HIV-1 Gag polyprotein, which drives the formation and release of non-infectious virus-like particles (VLPs) from mammalian cells. During VLP assembly in the cytosol, the Gag-bait fusion protein incorporates into the nascent particle scaffold and simultaneously recruits its physiological interaction partners (the “prey”), thereby trapping the entire complex within the protective confines of the VLP. The VLPs are then harvested from the cell culture supernatant and purified using a streamlined, single-step affinity protocol enabled by the co-expression of a tagged envelope glycoprotein, such as vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein (VSV-G), on the VLP surface. This eliminates the need for ultracentrifugation and allows efficient recovery of VLPs from large volumes of conditioned medium. The encapsulated protein complexes are subsequently eluted under mild conditions and analyzed by immunoblotting or mass spectrometry to identify specific interactors.

In one embodiment, the invention is used to validate known binary PPIs or to discover novel interaction partners in an unbiased manner. The system has been demonstrated to detect both strong and weak interactions, including those with micromolar dissociation constants, and to capture dynamic complexes whose composition changes in response to cellular stimuli, such as cytokine activation of the NF-κB pathway. Comparative analyses with classical AP–MS reveal that Virotrap yields complementary interactome data, identifying both overlapping and unique prey proteins, thereby expanding the coverage of the cellular interactome.

In another embodiment, the invention is adapted for the identification of protein targets of small molecules. This is achieved by fusing a small molecule-binding protein, such as Escherichia coli dihydrofolate reductase (eDHFR), to Gag and treating the VLP-producing cells with a bivalent chemical probe consisting of the drug of interest linked via a polyethylene glycol spacer to methotrexate (MTX), a high-affinity ligand for eDHFR. The drug moiety of the probe engages its endogenous cellular targets, which are then co-recruited into the VLPs via the eDHFR–MTX interaction. Using this approach, the invention successfully identifies known drug targets, such as HMG-CoA reductase for simvastatin and Aurora kinase A for reversine, as well as novel off-target interactions.

The invention further provides a robust bioinformatic framework for distinguishing specific interactors from background contaminants. This involves the generation of a comprehensive “black list” of recurrent non-specific proteins identified across a large panel of negative control Virotrap experiments (e.g., 19 unrelated baits), which is used to filter candidate interactors from experimental datasets. Proteins that appear in the black list are excluded unless supported by orthogonal validation. The Virotrap platform is particularly well-suited for the study of cytosolic and membrane-associated protein complexes, though future engineering of alternative viral matrix proteins may extend its applicability to other subcellular compartments. Overall, the invention represents a powerful, complementary addition to the current arsenal of PPI mapping technologies, offering enhanced preservation of complex integrity, reduced experimental artifacts, and the unique capability to profile drug–protein interactions in a cellular context.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

As used herein, the term “Virotrap” refers to the integrated method and system described in the present invention for the lysis-free isolation of protein complexes by their encapsulation within virus-like particles (VLPs) during intracellular assembly and subsequent secretion from mammalian cells. The Virotrap platform comprises genetic constructs encoding chimeric bait proteins fused to a viral structural protein, protocols for VLP production and purification, and analytical workflows for the identification of co-encapsulated interaction partners.

The term “virus-like particle” or “VLP” denotes a non-infectious, self-assembling nanoparticle that mimics the structure of a native virus but lacks the viral genome and is therefore replication-incompetent. In the context of the present invention, VLPs are formed by the expression of the HIV-1 Gag polyprotein or a functional derivative thereof in mammalian cells, resulting in particles of approximately 100–150 nm in diameter that bud from the plasma membrane and are released into the extracellular medium.

A “bait” protein is a protein of interest that is fused to the viral structural protein (e.g., Gag) and serves as the anchor for the recruitment of interaction partners into the VLP. The bait may be a full-length protein, a functional domain, or a mutant variant designed to probe specific aspects of protein function or interaction.

A “prey” protein is any protein that interacts directly or indirectly with the bait protein and is consequently co-encapsulated within the VLP. Prey proteins may be endogenous cellular proteins or exogenously expressed tagged constructs used for validation purposes.

The term “Gag” refers to the group-specific antigen polyprotein of retroviruses, particularly HIV-1, which is sufficient for the assembly and budding of VLPs when expressed in isolation. The Gag protein contains multiple domains, including matrix (MA), capsid (CA), nucleocapsid (NC), and p6, which mediate membrane targeting, particle assembly, RNA packaging, and virion release, respectively. In the present invention, the full-length p55 Gag precursor or truncated variants retaining particle-forming capability may be employed.

“Vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein” or “VSV-G” is a viral envelope protein derived from vesicular stomatitis virus that is commonly used to pseudotype retroviral vectors and VLPs. When co-expressed with Gag, VSV-G is incorporated into the lipid envelope of the budding VLP, where it forms trimers that can be recognized by specific antibodies. In the Virotrap system, a fraction of the VSV-G is expressed with an epitope tag (e.g., FLAG, E-tag) to enable immunoaffinity purification of VLPs from culture supernatants.

A “bivalent small molecule probe” is a synthetic chemical compound comprising two pharmacophores connected by a linker. In the drug-targeting embodiment of the invention, one pharmacophore is a high-affinity ligand for an engineered bait protein (e.g., methotrexate for eDHFR), and the other is the small molecule of interest (e.g., a drug candidate). This design allows the small molecule to engage its endogenous cellular targets while being tethered to the VLP via the bait–ligand interaction.

The term “black list” refers to a curated database of proteins that are recurrently identified as non-specific background contaminants in Virotrap control experiments. This list is generated by performing Virotrap with a panel of unrelated bait proteins (e.g., 19 distinct baits) and compiling all proteins detected across these negative controls. Candidate interactors from experimental datasets are filtered against this black list to remove likely false positives.

“Lysis-free” describes a methodological approach that avoids the physical or chemical disruption of cells, thereby preserving the native intracellular environment and the integrity of labile macromolecular complexes throughout the experimental procedure. In the Virotrap system, protein complexes are isolated from the culture supernatant without any prior cell lysis step.

### EXAMPLES

#### Example 1

**Validation of the Virotrap Concept Using the HRAS–RAF1 Interaction**

To establish the foundational principle of the Virotrap system, the well-characterized binary interaction between the small GTPase HRAS and its effector kinase RAF1 was employed as a model pair. A chimeric construct was generated in which the coding sequence of HRAS was fused in-frame to the N-terminus of the HIV-1 p55 Gag protein under the control of the strong SRα promoter, resulting in the plasmid pMET7-GAG-HRAS. HEK293T cells were transfected with this GAG-HRAS bait construct along with an expression vector for untagged RAF1 as the prey. Following overnight expression, the culture supernatant was harvested and subjected to ultracentrifugation to pellet the secreted VLPs. Western blot analysis of the VLP pellet using anti-RAF1 antibodies confirmed the specific co-precipitation of RAF1 only in the presence of the GAG-HRAS bait, but not with a control GAG-EGFP fusion, thereby demonstrating that the HRAS–RAF1 complex was successfully packaged into the VLPs during their intracellular assembly.

To streamline the purification process and eliminate the need for time-consuming ultracentrifugation, a single-step immunoaffinity protocol was developed. This involved the co-expression of two forms of VSV-G: an untagged version and a FLAG-tagged version, alongside the GAG-bait and prey constructs. Both forms of VSV-G are incorporated into the VLP envelope as heterotrimers, presenting the FLAG epitope on the particle surface. The culture supernatant was then incubated with anti-FLAG antibody-conjugated magnetic beads, allowing rapid and efficient capture of VLPs. Elution under mild conditions followed by western blotting again confirmed the specific enrichment of RAF1 with the GAG-HRAS bait, validating the single-step protocol as a robust alternative to ultracentrifugation. Control experiments using irrelevant bait or prey proteins showed no detectable associations, confirming the specificity of the interaction. This example establishes that the Virotrap system can faithfully recapitulate known PPIs under native, lysis-free conditions and provides a scalable purification workflow suitable for high-throughput applications.

#### Example 2

**Systematic Evaluation of Binary Interactions and Comparison with Other PPI Technologies**

The Virotrap platform was next evaluated for its ability to detect a broader panel of binary PPIs. A set of reciprocal interactions was selected based on published evidence and cytosolic localization, including CDK2–CKS1B, LCP2–GRAP2, and S100A1–S100B. For each pair, both proteins were tested in bait and prey orientations using the single-step VLP purification protocol followed by western blot analysis. Specific interactions were readily detected for all three pairs in both orientations, with the exception of a minor non-specific association observed when LCP2 was used as prey with an irrelevant bait. Quantitative analysis of band intensities, normalized to bait levels in the VLPs, revealed strong enrichment for the specific interactions, confirming the robustness of the system.

To benchmark Virotrap against existing technologies, a side-by-side screen was performed using the human positive reference set (hsPRS-v1), which comprises 92 literature-curated PPI pairs, and a matched random reference set (hsRRS-v1) of 92 non-interacting pairs. All 184 bait and prey constructs were expressed in HEK293T cells in the appropriate combinations, and VLPs were purified and analyzed by western blotting. Virotrap detected 28 (30%) of the positive interactions in the hsPRS, while only 5 (5%) false positives were observed in the hsRRS, yielding a favorable signal-to-noise ratio. Notably, western blot analysis of cell lysates indicated that a significant fraction of baits (∼30%) and preys (∼40%) were not expressed at detectable levels, suggesting that the true detection rate of Virotrap is likely higher than reported. Comparison with data from other PPI methods, including yeast two-hybrid, co-IP, and MAPPIT, revealed substantial overlap as well as unique interactions detected only by Virotrap, underscoring its complementary nature.

The sensitivity of Virotrap for weak interactions was further assessed using the Toll-like receptor signaling pair MYD88 and MAL (TIRAP), which interact via their TIR domains with a dissociation constant (Kd) of approximately 8 μM. A series of MYC-tagged MAL mutants with progressively reduced binding affinities for MYD88 were tested as preys against a GAG-MYD88(TIR) bait. The amount of each MAL mutant detected in the VLPs correlated strongly with its binding affinity, mirroring the results obtained in a parallel MAPPIT assay. This demonstrates that Virotrap is capable of detecting and semi-quantitatively ranking weak, transient PPIs that are often lost in conventional AP–MS workflows.

#### Example 3

**Unbiased Discovery of Novel Protein Interactions and Dynamic Complexes**

The Virotrap system was scaled up for unbiased interactome profiling by adapting the VLP production and purification protocols for mass spectrometry (MS) compatibility. Large-scale cultures of HEK293T cells were transfected with Gag-bait constructs for six signaling proteins involved in innate immunity and apoptosis: FADD, A20 (TNFAIP3), IKBKG (NEMO), TANK, MYD88, and RNF41. VLPs were purified using the anti-FLAG bead protocol, eluted with FLAG peptide to preserve complex integrity, and processed for LC–MS/MS analysis.

To distinguish specific interactors from background contaminants, a stringent filtering strategy was applied. A “black list” of 174 highly recurrent proteins was compiled from 19 independent Virotrap experiments using unrelated baits; these included structural proteins (e.g., ACTB, EZR), serum proteins (e.g., albumin, A2M), and known Gag interactors (e.g., Cyclophilin A, ALIX). Any protein identified in the experimental datasets that also appeared in this black list was excluded from the final candidate list unless validated by orthogonal methods. The remaining high-confidence interactors—defined as proteins identified by at least two unique peptides in at least two biological replicates—included both known binding partners (e.g., CASP8 for FADD, RIPK1 for A20) and novel candidates.

The ability of Virotrap to capture stimulus-dependent interactions was demonstrated by activating the NF-κB pathway with tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα). Cells expressing a GAG-A20 bait were treated with TNFα during VLP production, and MS analysis revealed the specific enrichment of components of the activated TNF receptor signaling complex, including TRADD, RIPK1, TNFRSF1A, and TNF itself—proteins not detected in untreated controls. This confirms that Virotrap can profile dynamic, condition-specific interactomes.

Direct comparison with classical AP–MS using A20 and RNF41 as baits showed significant but incomplete overlap in the identified prey proteins, with each method yielding unique interactors. This complementarity highlights the value of Virotrap as an orthogonal approach for comprehensive interactome mapping.

#### Example 4

**Application of Virotrap for Small Molecule Target Identification**

The Virotrap platform was extended to identify cellular protein targets of small molecules using a chemical proteomics strategy. An eDHFR-Gag fusion protein was expressed in HEK293T cells as the bait. Bivalent probes were synthesized by linking methotrexate (MTX)—a high-affinity ligand for eDHFR—to small molecules of interest (simvastatin, tamoxifen, or reversine) via a polyethylene glycol (PEG) spacer. Upon addition to the culture medium, these probes entered the cells, where the MTX moiety bound to eDHFR-Gag, while the drug moiety engaged its endogenous protein targets. These drug-bound targets were then co-recruited into the assembling VLPs.

VLPs were purified and analyzed by MS. To account for background, a combined black list was used, incorporating proteins from the 19 standard controls plus four additional eDHFR bait experiments treated with DMSO vehicle. This analysis consistently enriched the known targets of simvastatin (HMGCR) and reversine (AURKA). Notably, HMGCR is an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-resident transmembrane protein, demonstrating that Virotrap can capture membrane-associated complexes. Novel interactions were also identified, including tamoxifen with HSD17B4 and reversine with NQO2. These hits were validated using an orthogonal MASPIT assay, which confirmed specific, dose-dependent interactions. This example illustrates the versatility of Virotrap for drug mechanism-of-action studies and off-target profiling in a native cellular context, overcoming key limitations of traditional pull-down approaches.
