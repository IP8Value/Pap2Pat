## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention generally relates to the field of artificial intelligence and natural language processing, and more specifically to systems and methods for improving the factual consistency of abstractive text summarization models. The invention provides a novel technique—referred to herein as Contrastive Parameter Ensembling (CaPE)—for reducing hallucinations in neural summarization systems by leveraging parameter-space ensembling between expert and anti-expert models trained on data subsets selected based on automated factual consistency metrics. The invention further pertains to computer-implemented methods for training, fine-tuning, and deploying such models within distributed computing environments, including cloud-based inference platforms and edge devices configured for real-time document summarization. The technical domain encompasses deep learning architectures, particularly transformer-based sequence-to-sequence models, and extends to the integration of automatic evaluation metrics—including entity overlap, dependency arc entailment, and question-answering-based factuality scores—into the model training and refinement pipeline. The invention addresses long-standing challenges in abstractive summarization, namely the generation of fluent yet factually inaccurate summaries due to noise in training data, and offers a computationally efficient solution that maintains high recall and fluency while significantly improving faithfulness to source documents.

## BACKGROUND

Abstractive text summarization, a core task in natural language processing, aims to generate concise, human-readable summaries that capture the essence of a source document without merely extracting verbatim sentences. Unlike extractive summarization, which selects and concatenates salient phrases from the input, abstractive systems paraphrase, generalize, and synthesize information, often producing novel phrasing that does not appear in the original text. This capability has been greatly enhanced by the advent of large-scale pre-trained language models such as BART, T5, and PEGASUS, which leverage self-supervised learning on massive corpora followed by task-specific fine-tuning on datasets like XSUM and CNN/DailyMail (CNN/DM). These models achieve high scores on standard evaluation metrics such as ROUGE, which measures n-gram overlap between generated and reference summaries, and BERTScore, which assesses semantic similarity using contextual embeddings.

However, despite their impressive lexical and semantic performance, state-of-the-art abstractive summarizers frequently suffer from a critical flaw: the generation of factually inconsistent content, commonly referred to as “hallucinations.” Hallucinations occur when a model introduces information that is unsupported by, contradicted by, or entirely absent from the source document. These errors range from simple extrinsic hallucinations—such as mentioning entities not present in the source—to complex intrinsic hallucinations involving incorrect predicate-argument structures, erroneous coreference links, or fabricated discourse relations. Empirical studies have demonstrated that hallucination rates are alarmingly high in models trained on widely used benchmarks; for instance, models trained on the XSUM dataset, which emphasizes extreme compression and abstraction, exhibit significantly higher factual error rates than those trained on the more extractive CNN/DM dataset. This discrepancy is strongly correlated with the quality of the training data itself, where reference summaries in XSUM often contain factual inaccuracies introduced during the original annotation process.

The prevalence of hallucinations poses a serious barrier to the deployment of abstractive summarization in high-stakes applications such as legal document analysis, medical report generation, news aggregation, and intelligence briefing, where factual fidelity is non-negotiable. Traditional approaches to mitigating this issue have largely fallen into two categories: data-centric and model-centric strategies. Data-centric methods involve filtering or cleaning the training corpus to remove samples with low factual consistency, typically identified using automated metrics such as entity precision or entailment scores. While effective at reducing hallucinations, these approaches suffer from a critical drawback: they drastically reduce the size and diversity of the training data, leading to significant degradation in other desirable summary qualities, including information recall (measured by entity recall against reference summaries), fluency, and overall content coverage. For example, training a BART model exclusively on the top 25% of XSUM samples filtered by entity precision results in an 8–18% drop in ROUGE scores and a 20% reduction in named entity recall, rendering the resulting summaries overly conservative and incomplete.

Model-centric approaches, in contrast, attempt to correct hallucinations either during or after generation without altering the training data. Post-processing techniques include re-ranking candidate summaries based on factual consistency scores, applying constrained decoding to restrict the output vocabulary to source-derived terms, or using auxiliary correction models to revise hallucinated spans. While these methods can improve factuality, they often introduce substantial computational overhead, require additional model storage, and may disrupt the coherence of the final output. Moreover, many such techniques are not easily integrated into standard autoregressive generation pipelines and can slow down inference, making them impractical for real-time applications. Another line of research explores modifying the training objective itself—such as through loss truncation or contrastive learning—but these methods are often complex to implement and lack generalizability across different model architectures and datasets.

A promising alternative lies in model ensembling, where multiple models are combined to produce a more robust output. Traditional ensembling operates at the output level, averaging logits or probabilities from separate models. However, this approach multiplies inference time and memory usage linearly with the number of ensemble members, which is prohibitive for large transformer models. Recent work in vision and language has explored parameter-space ensembling, where the weights of multiple models are averaged directly to create a single, unified model. Notably, the WiSE-FT (Weighted Subspace Ensembling for Fine-Tuning) method demonstrated that averaging the parameters of a pre-trained base model and a fine-tuned variant can yield improved robustness across distribution shifts. However, WiSE-FT only considers a single fine-tuned model and does not explicitly account for undesirable behaviors such as hallucination.

What is missing from the prior art is a principled, efficient, and effective method that simultaneously leverages both high-quality and low-quality training data to enhance factual consistency without sacrificing summary informativeness or fluency. There remains a need for a system that can identify and amplify the “factual signal” present in clean data while actively suppressing the “hallucinatory signal” embedded in noisy data, all within a single, deployable model that incurs minimal computational overhead. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel contrastive parameter ensembling framework that explicitly trains an “anti-expert” on hallucination-prone data and uses its parameter deviations to counteract those of an “expert” trained on factually consistent data, thereby refining the base model in a targeted and efficient manner.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Computer and Network Environment

The systems and methods described herein are implemented within a computer and network environment comprising one or more computing devices interconnected via communication networks. Each computing device may be a server, a desktop computer, a laptop, a tablet, a smartphone, or any other programmable electronic device capable of executing instructions and communicating over a network. In a preferred embodiment, the invention is deployed in a cloud computing environment, where summarization services are provided as a scalable, on-demand resource via application programming interfaces (APIs). The cloud infrastructure includes virtual machines, containerized microservices, and distributed storage systems managed by orchestration platforms such as Kubernetes.

The core computational components include at least one processor (e.g., a CPU or GPU), memory (RAM), and non-volatile storage (e.g., SSDs or hard drives). The processor executes program code implementing the Contrastive Parameter Ensembling (CaPE) method, which is stored in memory during execution and persisted in non-volatile storage when not in use. The software stack includes a deep learning framework such as PyTorch or TensorFlow, along with libraries for natural language processing (e.g., spaCy, Hugging Face Transformers) and numerical computation (e.g., NumPy, SciPy).

The network environment supports both training and inference phases. During training, large-scale datasets such as XSUM and CNN/DM are accessed from distributed file systems (e.g., HDFS, Amazon S3) or database clusters. Training jobs are distributed across multiple GPU-accelerated nodes using data parallelism, with gradients synchronized via protocols such as NCCL or Horovod. Automated factual consistency metrics—such as entity overlap and dependency arc entailment (DAE)—are computed in parallel across the training corpus to identify clean and noisy samples for expert and anti-expert training, respectively.

During inference, client applications submit source documents to the summarization service via HTTPS or gRPC. The service loads the pre-computed CaPE model parameters into memory and generates a summary using standard autoregressive decoding (e.g., beam search). Because CaPE produces a single set of model weights, inference latency is identical to that of the base model, with no additional computational overhead. The generated summary is returned to the client in JSON or plain text format. The system supports batch processing for high-throughput scenarios and real-time streaming for interactive applications.

Security and privacy are enforced through standard mechanisms including TLS encryption for data in transit, role-based access control for API endpoints, and data anonymization for sensitive inputs. Model updates and versioning are managed through continuous integration/continuous deployment (CI/CD) pipelines, ensuring that new CaPE variants can be tested, validated, and rolled out without service interruption.

### Example Workflows

The operation of the present invention is best understood through a series of example workflows that illustrate the end-to-end process of training, ensembling, and deploying a CaPE-enhanced summarization model. These workflows are designed to be modular, reproducible, and adaptable to different datasets, base models, and factual consistency metrics.

**Workflow 1: Base Model Initialization and Training**  
The process begins with the selection of a base abstractive summarization model, such as BART-large fine-tuned on the full XSUM or CNN/DM dataset. This base model, denoted as θ_B, is trained using standard maximum likelihood estimation to maximize the probability of reference summaries given their source documents. The training employs Adam optimization with a learning rate of 5e-5, batch size of 64, and early stopping based on validation ROUGE scores. The resulting model serves as the foundation for subsequent expert and anti-expert fine-tuning, ensuring that all derived models share a common initialization trajectory—a critical requirement for effective parameter-space ensembling.

**Workflow 2: Automated Factual Consistency Scoring and Data Selection**  
Next, the entire training dataset is scored using one or more automated factual consistency metrics. Two primary metrics are employed: (1) Entity Precision with respect to the source (E-P_src), defined as the percentage of named entity tokens in the reference summary that appear in the source document; and (2) Dependency Arc Entailment (DAE) error count, which quantifies the number of grammatical dependency arcs in the summary that are not entailed by the source. These metrics are computed using off-the-shelf tools: spaCy for named entity recognition and Stanza or spaCy for dependency parsing, coupled with a pre-trained entailment model (e.g., RoBERTa-large fine-tuned on MNLI) to evaluate arc-level entailment.

Based on these scores, two subsets are selected: a “clean” subset for expert training and a “noisy” subset for anti-expert training. For E-P_src, clean samples are those with E-P_src ≥ τ_clean (e.g., 95th percentile), and noisy samples are those with E-P_src ≤ τ_noisy (e.g., 5th percentile). For DAE, clean samples have DAE error count ≤ τ_clean, and noisy samples have DAE error count ≥ τ_noisy. Thresholds are chosen to balance subset size and extremity of factual quality, typically retaining 10–25% of the original data for each subset.

**Workflow 3: Expert and Anti-Expert Fine-Tuning**  
The base model θ_B is then fine-tuned separately on the clean and noisy subsets to produce an expert model θ_E and an anti-expert model θ_Ē, respectively. Each fine-tuning run lasts for one epoch using the same hyperparameters as the base model training. This short duration ensures that the models retain the base model’s general summarization capabilities (e.g., fluency, ROUGE performance) while adapting their parameters to emphasize or de-emphasize factual consistency. Critically, both θ_E and θ_Ē are initialized from θ_B—not from a raw pre-trained checkpoint—so that their parameter deviations reflect targeted adjustments rather than divergent learning paths.

**Workflow 4: Contrastive Parameter Ensembling**  
The final CaPE model θ_CaPE is constructed via a linear combination of the three parameter sets:  
θ_CaPE = θ_B + α(θ_E − θ_Ē)  
where α ∈ [0, 1] is a mixing coefficient that controls the strength of the contrastive adjustment. When α = 0, θ_CaPE = θ_B (no adjustment); when α = 1, the full difference between expert and anti-expert is applied. The value of α is selected based on validation performance: for each CaPE variant (e.g., CaPE_PP using E-P_src-based expert and anti-expert), α is chosen as the largest value such that ROUGE-1 and entity recall (E-R_ref) do not drop by more than 1% relative to the base model. This ensures that gains in factual consistency are not achieved at the expense of summary informativeness.

**Workflow 5: Evaluation and Deployment**  
The resulting CaPE model is evaluated on held-out test sets using a comprehensive suite of metrics: ROUGE-1/2/L, BERTScore (precision and recall w.r.t. source), E-P_src, E-R_ref, D_arc (percentage of entailed dependency arcs), D_sum (percentage of summaries with zero DAE errors), QAFactEval, and MNLI-based entailment. Human evaluations are conducted via pairwise comparisons, where annotators judge the factual consistency of CaPE vs. base model summaries on random samples. Once validated, the model is serialized and deployed to production inference servers, where it processes incoming documents with the same latency as the base model.

These workflows demonstrate that CaPE is not only effective but also practical: it requires only two additional fine-tuning runs on small data subsets, introduces no inference overhead, and integrates seamlessly into existing NLP pipelines. By contrastively leveraging both clean and noisy data, CaPE achieves a superior trade-off between factuality and informativeness, setting a new standard for reliable abstractive summarization.
