# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of structural design optimization, and more particularly to a method and system for performing fail-safe topology optimization of three-dimensional continuum structures under conditions of localized material loss or damage. The invention provides a computationally efficient framework for generating structural designs that maintain load-carrying capacity and structural integrity even when subjected to complete removal of material within a predefined damage zone of specified geometry and size, thereby satisfying stringent fail-safe design requirements commonly mandated in safety-critical applications such as aerospace, automotive, and civil infrastructure. The invention specifically addresses the challenge of incorporating fail-safe criteria—traditionally applied to discrete structural members—into the continuum-based topology optimization process, where structural members are not predefined but emerge as part of the optimization solution. By introducing a mathematically rigorous formulation based on random placement of damage zones within the design domain and a novel hierarchical damage population strategy, the invention enables the generation of highly redundant, robust structural topologies that can withstand localized catastrophic failures without global collapse. The method is implemented within a parallel computing environment to ensure practical feasibility for industrial-scale problems involving hundreds of thousands of finite elements.

## BACKGROUND

Topology optimization has evolved since its formal inception in the late 1980s into one of the most active and impactful research areas within engineering design optimization. Pioneered by Bendsøe and Kikuchi, the methodology enables the determination of optimal material distribution within a given design domain subject to performance objectives and constraints, typically resulting in highly efficient, often truss-like structural configurations. Over the decades, topology optimization has found widespread adoption across major industries, including significant applications in the development of modern commercial aircraft such as the Airbus A380, A350, and Boeing 787. Despite its success, a persistent limitation of conventional topology optimization lies in its inherent tendency to produce designs with minimal structural redundancy. This characteristic stems from the optimization’s drive toward maximum material efficiency, which often yields statically determinate configurations—exemplified by Michell trusses—that possess no alternative load paths upon failure of any single component. Such lack of redundancy renders these otherwise optimal designs critically vulnerable to localized damage or member failure, a deficiency that is unacceptable in safety-critical systems.

In response to this vulnerability, the concept of reliability-based design optimization (RBDO) emerged, aiming to account for uncertainties in loads, material properties, boundary conditions, and manufacturing tolerances. Within this broader context, reliability-based topology optimization (RBTO) has been explored since the mid-2000s, with numerous contributions addressing stochastic variations in input parameters. However, as noted by Jansen et al. (2014), the binary nature of structural failure—where a component either carries load or it does not—poses a fundamental challenge to statistical RBTO frameworks when attempting to enforce failsafe requirements. Fail-safe design, a cornerstone of modern aerospace engineering, mandates that a structure must retain sufficient integrity to support operational loads even after the complete failure of any single structural element or a defined portion of a monolithic component. This philosophy, codified in industry standards such as those articulated by Niu (1988, 1997), requires that an aircraft structure withstand 80–100% of limit loads following either the total loss of a discrete member in a redundant system or a partial material loss in a monolithic panel, where the size of the assumed damage is determined by inspection intervals and crack propagation models.

The integration of such deterministic fail-safe criteria into topology optimization remained elusive until recently. Jansen et al. (2014) introduced the first continuum-based approach by modeling local failure as the removal of a square-shaped void at every possible finite element location within a two-dimensional domain. Their formulation minimized the maximum compliance across all damage scenarios, effectively ensuring performance robustness against any single localized material loss. While conceptually sound, this method suffers from prohibitive computational cost: for a model with *N*<sub>E</sub> finite elements, it requires solving *N*<sub>E</sub> independent finite element analyses per optimization iteration. In practical industrial applications, where *N*<sub>E</sub> routinely exceeds several hundred thousand, this approach becomes intractable even on the largest high-performance computing (HPC) clusters. Moreover, Jansen et al. did not explicitly reconcile their damage model with the classical aerospace definition of fail-safe, which distinguishes between discrete member failure and partial monolithic failure, nor did they address how to define “a member” in a continuum where structural features emerge only through optimization.

This gap highlights two core challenges in fail-safe topology optimization. First, there is a conceptual dilemma: how to define a “member failure test” before structural members have been identified by the optimizer. Second, there is a computational barrier: how to achieve sufficient coverage of potential damage locations without resorting to an exhaustive, element-by-element analysis. Prior art offers no satisfactory resolution to these intertwined problems. Minimum and maximum length scale controls have been developed to impose manufacturability and robustness, but they do not inherently guarantee fail-safe behavior. Parallelization strategies exist for multi-model optimization, yet they remain ineffective if the number of models is astronomically large. Consequently, despite the clear engineering need and theoretical interest, fail-safe topology optimization has remained largely impractical for real-world three-dimensional design problems.

The present invention overcomes these limitations by establishing a mathematically rigorous foundation for fail-safe design in continuum topology optimization, grounded in the established aerospace fail-safe philosophy. It introduces a novel damage population series that systematically approximates the idealized condition of random damage placement with a finite, manageable set of damage instances. Through geometric analysis and numerical validation, the invention demonstrates that a base-level, gapless tiling of damage cubes (Level 1) already captures the essential redundancy requirements, while a strategically enriched partial Level 2 population offers significantly improved accuracy at modest additional cost. Critically, the invention reveals that when combined with a maximum length scale constraint limiting member cross-sections to half the damage size, the Level 2 population guarantees zero material survival in any representative member section—thereby strictly enforcing the discrete member failure criterion. Implemented within a message-passing interface (MPI) parallel framework, the method achieves near-perfect scalability, enabling the solution of industrial-scale 3D problems with hundreds of damage scenarios in feasible computational time. Thus, the invention bridges the gap between theoretical fail-safe requirements and practical computational design, enabling the automated generation of truly redundant, damage-tolerant structural topologies.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for performing fail-safe topology optimization of three-dimensional continuum structures, ensuring that the resulting design maintains structural integrity and load-carrying capacity even after the complete removal of material within a predefined damage zone of specified size and shape. The invention establishes a mathematically rigorous formulation wherein the structural domain is required to withstand the presence of a damage zone—modeled as a cube or sphere of edge length or diameter *d*—randomly located anywhere within the domain. This formulation inherently satisfies both classical fail-safe criteria: (1) complete failure of any discrete structural member, provided its cross-sectional dimension does not exceed *d*, and (2) partial failure of monolithic components, represented by a through-hole of size *d*. To render this infinite-dimensional problem computationally tractable, the invention introduces a hierarchical damage population series, beginning with a Level 1 population comprising a gapless tiling of non-overlapping damage cubes that completely cover the structural domain without gaps. Higher levels (e.g., Level 2) double the spatial density of damage placement in each coordinate direction, exponentially increasing population size but offering diminishing returns in modeling accuracy.

A key insight of the invention is the quantitative relationship between damage population size and the maximum material survival rate within a representative cube of size *d*, which serves as a measure of how closely the finite population approximates the ideal random damage condition (which implies zero survival). For the Level 1 population, the maximum volumetric survival rate is 87.5%, with a sectional survival rate of 75% in the most favorable alignment. The invention further introduces a partial Level 2 population (denoted PB<sub>2</sub>) that selectively adds damage cubes only at the interstitial “hideout” locations—specifically, the corners where four Level 1 cubes meet in 2D or eight meet in 3D—thereby doubling the Level 1 population rather than octupling it (as in a full Level 2 grid). This PB<sub>2</sub> population reduces the maximum sectional survival rate to 50%, offering a superior return on investment in terms of accuracy versus computational cost. Most significantly, the invention demonstrates that when a maximum length scale constraint is imposed during optimization such that no structural member exceeds *d*/2 in cross-sectional dimension, the full Level 2 population (PA<sub>2</sub>) guarantees zero material survival in any representative member section. This property ensures strict compliance with the discrete member failure test, as any member of size ≤ *d*/2 will be entirely removed by at least one damage instance in PA<sub>2</sub>.

The optimization problem is formulated as a minimax compliance problem, minimizing the worst-case total compliance (summed over all load cases) among all damage scenarios, subject to a global volume constraint. This is solved using a multiple-model optimization (MMO) framework, where each damage scenario corresponds to an independent finite element model with elements inside the damage zone removed. All models are analyzed in parallel using MPI, with perfect scalability: given sufficient computing nodes, the turnaround time equals that of a single undamaged analysis. Practical implementation includes automated exclusion of damage zones that would remove point loads, optional filtering of low-material-density damage zones to reduce population size, and enforcement of minimum and maximum length scales to ensure manufacturability and robustness. The invention is validated through multiple 2D and 3D examples, including replication of a benchmark cantilever plate problem from prior art. These demonstrate that the Level 1 population alone produces qualitatively equivalent fail-safe topologies to those obtained with orders-of-magnitude larger damage populations, while PB<sub>2</sub> provides enhanced robustness in cases with coarse damage grids relative to structural dimensions. For a 3D control arm with over 327,000 elements, the method generates a redundant design with additional ribs and load-path diversification near critical bearings, using only 45–73 damage scenarios compared to an estimated 200,000+ required by prior approaches. Thus, the invention enables practical, efficient, and theoretically grounded fail-safe topology optimization for real-world engineering applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for fail-safe topology optimization of three-dimensional continuum structures, designed to generate structural layouts that remain functional even after localized catastrophic material loss. The method is rooted in the established aerospace fail-safe design philosophy, which requires that a structure sustain operational loads following either the complete failure of any single discrete structural member or a defined partial failure in a monolithic component. To implement this requirement within the continuum topology optimization paradigm—where structural members are not predefined but emerge as part of the solution—the invention introduces a novel mathematical formulation, a hierarchical damage population strategy, and an efficient parallel computational scheme.

The core of the invention lies in the definition of damage as the complete removal of material within a geometric zone of prescribed size and shape, placed at arbitrary locations within the structural domain. While spherical damage is the most generic and directionally neutral representation, the invention primarily employs cubic damage zones for analytical tractability and computational convenience. A cube of edge length *d* is used to model the worst-case material loss scenario, with the understanding that it provides a conservative approximation of spherical damage of diameter *d*, as the cube fully contains the sphere when both are centered at the same point. The fail-safe requirement is then rigorously formulated as follows: the optimized structure must satisfy all performance constraints (e.g., stress limits, displacement bounds) for every possible configuration in which a damage cube of size *d* is removed from the domain, one instance at a time. This implies an infinite number of structural cases, as the damage cube can be centered at any point in the continuous domain Ω.

To make this problem numerically solvable, the invention introduces the concept of a damage population series. The base level, denoted PA<sub>1</sub>, consists of a gapless tiling of non-overlapping damage cubes that completely partition the design domain. Specifically, the domain is divided into a regular grid where each cell is a cube of size *d*, and a damage instance is defined for each cell, removing all material within that cell. This ensures that no finite element in the domain survives all damage scenarios; every element is removed in at least one case. The population size at Level 1 is thus proportional to the volume of the domain divided by *d*<sup>3</sup>. Higher levels are constructed by recursively subdividing each cube in the previous level into eight sub-cubes (in 3D), thereby doubling the spatial resolution in each coordinate direction. Level *L* thus has a population size that is 8<sup>*L*−1</sup> times that of Level 1. However, recognizing the exponential growth in computational cost, the invention also defines a partial damage population series, PB<sub>L</sub>, which for *L* ≥ 2 includes only the Level 1 population plus a selective subset of Level *L* damage cubes—specifically, those centered at the interstitial points where 2<sup>3</sup> = 8 Level (*L*−1) cubes meet. For PB<sub>2</sub>, this means adding one new damage cube at the center of every group of eight adjacent Level 1 cubes, effectively doubling the Level 1 population rather than octupling it.

The accuracy of a given damage population in representing the ideal random damage condition is quantified by the maximum material survival rate within a representative cube of size *d*. This rate is defined as the maximum fraction of material that remains in any cube of size *d* when overlaid onto the structural domain, considering all possible positions and orientations. Under the ideal random damage model, this rate must be zero, as any material point must be removed by some damage instance. For PA<sub>1</sub>, geometric analysis shows that the worst-case (i.e., maximum survival) occurs when the representative cube is positioned at the corner shared by eight adjacent damage cubes, resulting in a surviving L-shaped volume occupying 7/8 = 87.5% of the cube, with a minimum cross-sectional survival of 75% in the plane perpendicular to any axis. For PA<sub>2</sub>, the best hideout is reduced to a sofa-corner shape with 37/64 ≈ 57.8% volumetric survival and 43.75% sectional survival. The PB<sub>2</sub> population, by targeting these hideouts, achieves 62.5% volumetric and 50% sectional survival, offering nearly the same protection as PA<sub>2</sub> at one-fourth the population size. General formulae derived for Level *L* show that both volumetric and sectional survival rates decay geometrically with increasing *L*, confirming that the damage population series converges to the ideal random model as *L* → ∞.

A pivotal discovery of the invention is the interaction between damage population and maximum length scale constraints. When the topology optimization is augmented with a maximum length scale control that restricts the cross-sectional dimension of any structural member to at most *d*/2, the PA<sub>2</sub> population guarantees zero material survival in any representative member section. This is because a member of size *d*/2 cannot simultaneously avoid all damage cubes in the denser PA<sub>2</sub> grid; at least one damage instance will fully encompass it. This property allows the designer to strictly enforce the discrete member failure criterion: by setting the damage size *d* to twice the desired maximum member size, the Level 2 population ensures that every emergent member is tested for complete removal. This transforms the fail-safe requirement from a probabilistic notion into a deterministic guarantee, bridging the gap between continuum optimization and discrete member-based fail-safe standards.

The optimization problem is cast in the standard Solid Isotropic Material with Penalization (SIMP) framework, where the design variables are element-wise material densities *x*<sub>i</sub> ∈ [0,1], and the effective stiffness of each element is penalized as *K*<sub>i</sub> = *x*<sub>i</sub><sup>p</sup>*K*<sub>i</sub><sup>0</sup>, with *p* > 1 (typically 3–4). The objective is to minimize the maximum total compliance across all damage scenarios, where total compliance is the sum of compliances over all static load cases. Mathematically, this is expressed as min<sub>x</sub> max<sub>l=1,…,P</sub> *C*<sub>l</sub>(*x*), subject to a global volume constraint ∑<sub>i</sub> *v*<sub>i</sub>*x*<sub>i</sub> ≤ *V*<sub>U</sub>, and optionally, local stress or displacement constraints. The minimax problem is solved using the bound formulation of Olhoff, which introduces an auxiliary variable *C*<sub>max</sub> and enforces *C*<sub>l</sub> ≤ *C*<sub>max</sub> for all *l*, then minimizes *C*<sub>max</sub>. Sensitivity analysis for each damage scenario is performed using standard adjoint methods, with the caveat that elements within the damage zone contribute zero stiffness and thus zero sensitivity.

Computationally, the method leverages the independence of damage scenarios to achieve perfect parallel scalability. Each damage scenario corresponds to a separate finite element model, differing only in the set of active (non-damaged) elements. These models are distributed across multiple processors using the Message Passing Interface (MPI), with a master process coordinating the optimization iterations and aggregating compliance values and sensitivities. The implementation includes several practical enhancements: (1) automatic exclusion of damage zones that contain point loads, to preserve well-posed boundary conditions; (2) optional filtering of damage zones containing less than a threshold volume of material (e.g., 10%), to eliminate irrelevant scenarios; and (3) restriction of higher-level damage cubes to lie entirely within the structural domain, avoiding partial cubes at boundaries. The user specifies only the damage size *d* and the desired population level (e.g., PA<sub>1</sub> or PB<sub>2</sub>); the software automatically generates the damage instances and configures the parallel solve.

### EXAMPLES

The efficacy and practicality of the invention are demonstrated through three numerical examples: two two-dimensional benchmark problems and one three-dimensional industrial component. All examples employ the SIMP method with default OptiStruct settings: gradual penalty increase to final values of 3.0 (2D) or 4.0 (3D), minimum length scale equal to three times the average element size, and volume fraction constraints. The damage size *d* is chosen based on engineering judgment regarding plausible failure extents.

**Example 1: Rectangular Plate Under Shear Force.**  
A 100 × 50 rectangular plate, modeled with 20,000 quadratic elements, is fixed along the top edge and loaded with a horizontal force *P* = 1000 at the center of the bottom edge. A 20% volume fraction is enforced. A large square damage size of 25 × 25 is considered, yielding a PA<sub>1</sub> population of 8 damage zones and a PB<sub>2</sub> population of 13 (after excluding one zone that cuts the point load). The standard topology optimization yields a two-bar truss-like structure, as expected. In contrast, both PA<sub>1</sub> and PB<sub>2</sub> fail-safe optimizations produce a three-bar configuration with a central vertical member, mirroring the analytical three-bar truss result. The undamaged compliance increases by ~44% compared to the standard design, but the maximum damaged compliance (occurring when a diagonal bar is removed) is only ~120% higher than the undamaged state—significantly better than the 170% increase in the three-bar truss, indicating effective redundancy. Four damage zones are active (within 2% of max compliance) for PA<sub>1</sub>, increasing to five for PB<sub>2</sub>. When the damage grid is shifted by *d*/4, asymmetric topologies emerge, but symmetry enforcement restores the balanced three-bar layout, confirming robustness to grid placement. The results validate that PA<sub>1</sub> suffices for basic redundancy, while PB<sub>2</sub> refines load-path distribution.

**Example 2: Cantilever Plate Replication (Jansen et al. Benchmark).**  
This example replicates the cantilever plate studied by Jansen et al. (2014): a 120 × 40 domain, fixed on the left, loaded vertically at the bottom-right corner, with 50% volume fraction. Two damage sizes are tested: *d* = 10 and *d* = 22. For *d* = 10, PA<sub>1</sub> uses 108 damage zones versus Jansen’s 7,701; PB<sub>2</sub> uses 193. All methods produce nearly identical redundant truss networks with multiple parallel load paths. The maximum compliance under damage is comparable, confirming that PA<sub>1</sub> captures the essential fail-safe behavior at <2% of the computational cost. For *d* = 22, PA<sub>1</sub> (26 zones) and PB<sub>2</sub> (42 zones) again match Jansen’s topology (5,421 zones). However, PA<sub>1</sub> shows a vulnerability at the loading point, where a single member carries the load; PB<sub>2</sub> resolves this by splitting the member, demonstrating the value of PB<sub>2</sub> when damage zones are coarse relative to structural features. A mesh refinement study (43,200 elements) confirms mesh independence, with PB<sub>2</sub> maintaining 42 zones versus a quadrupled ~20,000+ for Jansen’s method.

**Example 3: 3D Control Arm.**  
An industrial control arm (450 × 550 × 110 mm) with 327,493 tetrahedral elements is optimized under two load cases (bending and torque combinations), with 30% volume fraction and a casting draw-direction constraint. A cubic damage size of 50 mm yields PA<sub>1</sub> = 45 zones and PB<sub>2</sub> = 73 zones. The standard design features slender ribs connecting front and rear bearings. The fail-safe design adds a prominent central rib and thickens connections near the rear bearings, creating redundant load paths. The undamaged compliance increases by ~19%, but the maximum damaged compliance (756.8 vs. 193.7) reveals critical vulnerabilities near the narrow rear pathways—highlighting regions where additional hardware (e.g., extra bearings) may be needed. Compared to an estimated >200,000 zones required by Jansen’s method, the invention reduces computational effort by over 99.9%, making the problem tractable on modest HPC resources.

Collectively, these examples confirm that the invention’s damage population strategy—particularly PA<sub>1</sub> and PB<sub>2</sub>—delivers fail-safe topologies equivalent to exhaustive methods at a fraction of the cost, while providing theoretical guarantees when combined with length scale control.
