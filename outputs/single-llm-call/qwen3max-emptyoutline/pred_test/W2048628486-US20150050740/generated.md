# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of molecular biology and genetic engineering, specifically to methods and compositions for enabling efficient genetic transformation of bacterial strains that are recalcitrant to conventional genetic manipulation due to the presence of multiple restriction-modification (R-M) systems. More particularly, the invention provides a novel pipeline—termed mimicking-of-DNA-methylation-patterns (MoDMP)—that overcomes the restriction barriers imposed by endogenous R-M systems in difficult-to-transform bacteria by replicating their native DNA methylation signatures in a specially engineered *Escherichia coli* host strain. This engineered host lacks all known endogenous R-M systems and orphan methyltransferases, thereby preventing unintended methylation or degradation of plasmid DNA during propagation. By expressing heterologous DNA methyltransferases (MTases) derived from the target bacterial species in this permissive *E. coli* background, the methylation profile of plasmid DNA is tailored to match that of the recipient organism, rendering it invisible to its restriction enzymes. The invention is broadly applicable across diverse bacterial taxa—including Gram-positive and Gram-negative species, chemoheterotrophs, and chemoautotrophs—and enables high-efficiency transformation, stable plasmid maintenance, and direct chromosomal mutagenesis using non-replicative integration vectors. The MoDMP pipeline thus constitutes a universal platform for genetic access to previously intractable microbial strains, with significant implications for basic research in microbial physiology, pathogenesis, and metabolism, as as industrial biotechnology applications involving non-model or industrially relevant bacterial hosts.

## BACKGROUND ART

Genetic manipulation of bacteria has long been a cornerstone of microbiological research, enabling functional characterization of genes, elucidation of metabolic pathways, and engineering of strains for biotechnological purposes. However, a substantial proportion of bacterial species—particularly those not traditionally used as laboratory models—remain refractory to standard genetic techniques. A primary obstacle to successful transformation in these organisms is the presence of restriction-modification (R-M) systems, which function as innate immune defenses against foreign DNA such as bacteriophages and plasmids. These systems typically consist of a restriction endonuclease (REase) that cleaves DNA at specific recognition sequences and a cognate DNA methyltransferase (MTase) that modifies the same sequence in the host genome, thereby protecting it from cleavage. When exogenous DNA lacking the appropriate methylation pattern enters the cell, it is recognized as foreign and degraded by the REase activity.

R-M systems are remarkably prevalent; genomic analyses indicate that approximately 95% of sequenced bacterial genomes encode at least one R-M system, and over one-third harbor four or more distinct REases. These systems are classified into four main types (I–IV) based on subunit composition, cofactor requirements, cleavage position relative to the recognition site, and sequence specificity. Types I, II, and III primarily target unmethylated DNA, whereas Type IV systems uniquely recognize and cleave DNA containing methylated bases with non-self patterns—such as those introduced by *E. coli*’s Dam (G^m6ATC) and Dcm (CC^m5WGG) methylases during standard plasmid propagation. Consequently, plasmids prepared in common laboratory *E. coli* strains often carry methylation marks that either fail to protect against the recipient’s Type I–III systems or actively trigger destruction by Type IV systems, leading to extremely low or undetectable transformation efficiencies.

Several strategies have been developed to circumvent R-M barriers. One approach involves using *E. coli* mutants deficient in Dam, Dcm, and EcoKI methylation (e.g., strains like SCS110, DB24, or HST04) to prepare “unmethylated” DNA, which can sometimes evade Type IV restriction. While effective in certain species—such as *Bacillus anthracis* or *Corynebacterium glutamicum*—this method fails when the target organism possesses active Type I–III systems that cleave unmethylated DNA. Alternatively, in vitro methylation of plasmids using purified MTases or co-expression of a single heterologous MTase in *E. coli* has been employed to protect against specific REases. However, these approaches are limited when multiple, diverse R-M systems are present, as is common in environmental or industrial isolates. For instance, *Nitrobacter hamburgensis* X14 harbors eleven predicted R-M systems, and *Bacillus cereus* ATCC 10987 encodes at least four characterized REases, making single-enzyme solutions inadequate.

Another strategy involves transient inhibition of restriction activity through heat shock or chemical treatment, or permanent deletion of R-M genes from the host chromosome. Yet gene knockouts are themselves dependent on efficient transformation—a circular problem when the strain is initially untransformable. Moreover, even when partial success is achieved (e.g., transformation of *B. cereus* ATCC 10987 with DNA from *B. subtilis*), efficiencies remain too low for routine genetic manipulation or markerless mutagenesis. The use of DNA mimic proteins like Ocr to inhibit Type I systems offers another avenue, but it does not address Type II, III, or IV restrictions.

Critically, prior art lacks a systematic, scalable method to simultaneously neutralize multiple, heterogeneous R-M systems by comprehensively recapitulating the native methylation landscape of the target organism. Existing *E. coli* hosts used for plasmid preparation either retain endogenous methylation (interfering with Type IV systems) or possess residual restriction activities (e.g., Mrr, McrA, McrBC) that degrade DNA bearing foreign methylation patterns, thereby limiting the expression of heterologous MTases. Thus, there remains an unmet need for a robust, generalizable platform that enables high-efficiency genetic transformation of bacteria with complex R-M defenses, particularly those of scientific or industrial interest that have remained genetically inaccessible.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly effective method—referred to herein as the mimicking-of-DNA-methylation-patterns (MoDMP) pipeline—for overcoming restriction-modification (R-M) barriers in bacterial strains that are otherwise resistant to genetic transformation. The core innovation lies in the creation of a specialized *Escherichia coli* host strain, designated EC135, which is genetically engineered to lack all six known endogenous R-M systems and orphan methyltransferases, including Dam, Dcm, HsdRMS (EcoKI), Mrr, McrA, and McrBC. This comprehensive deletion renders the host incapable of methylating propagated plasmid DNA in a manner that would trigger Type IV restriction in recipient bacteria, while also eliminating methylation-dependent restriction systems that would otherwise degrade chromosomal or plasmid DNA modified by heterologous methyltransferases (MTases).

Using this permissive EC135 strain as a chassis, the MoDMP pipeline involves the cloning and co-expression of multiple active MTase genes derived from the target difficult-to-transform bacterial species. These MTases are selected based on genomic annotation and validated for enzymatic activity through biochemical assays, including dot blot hybridization with modification-specific antibodies and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) detection of methylated nucleosides. The active MTase genes are assembled into synthetic operons within a shuttle vector compatible with both *E. coli* and yeast, leveraging the high-efficiency homologous recombination machinery of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* for rapid, scarless, multi-gene assembly. The resulting pMoDMP plasmids enable coordinated expression of all necessary MTases in EC135, thereby reprogramming the methylation profile of co-resident plasmids to mirror that of the native target organism.

Plasmid DNA prepared from EC135 cells harboring the appropriate pMoDMP construct exhibits methylation patterns that effectively camouflage it from the recipient’s R-M surveillance systems. As demonstrated herein, this approach achieves dramatic improvements in transformation efficiency—up to 10⁴- to 10⁷-fold increases—across phylogenetically and physiologically diverse bacterial species, including the Gram-negative chemoautotroph *Nitrobacter hamburgensis* X14 (for which no prior genetic transformation had been reported), the Gram-positive pathogen-related strain *Bacillus cereus* ATCC 10987, and the industrial guanosine producer *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens* TA208. Critically, the elevated transformation efficiencies enabled by MoDMP facilitate advanced genetic manipulations, such as direct chromosomal gene inactivation using non-replicative integration plasmids, as exemplified by the successful knockout of the *upp* gene in *B. amyloliquefaciens* TA208.

Furthermore, the invention includes detailed characterization of the sequence specificities of numerous previously uncharacterized MTases, revealing that many are multi-specific enzymes capable of modifying multiple recognition motifs. Comparative analysis between MoDMP-modified DNA and native genomic DNA shows that while the engineered system often installs a more complete methylation pattern than the native host (due to constitutive overexpression versus regulated endogenous expression), this enhanced coverage is advantageous for evading restriction. The MoDMP pipeline is modular, rapid (plasmid construction achievable in under one week), and universally adaptable to any bacterial species with a sequenced genome, thereby unlocking genetic access to a vast array of previously intractable microorganisms for research and biotechnological exploitation.

## SPECIFIC EMBODIMENTS

### Example 1

The first embodiment of the present invention demonstrates the construction and validation of the foundational *E. coli* host strain, EC135, which serves as the essential platform for the MoDMP pipeline. This strain was derived from the commonly used cloning strain TOP10 through a series of precise genetic modifications designed to eliminate all known sources of endogenous DNA methylation and methylation-sensitive restriction. Specifically, the *dam* and *dcm* genes, encoding the N6-adenine and C5-cytosine methyltransferases responsible for G^m6ATC and CC^m5WGG methylation, respectively, were deleted to prevent the introduction of methylation patterns that could be recognized as foreign by Type IV R-M systems in recipient bacteria. Additionally, the entire *hsdRMS* locus, which constitutes the Type I EcoKI R-M system, was removed to abolish both restriction and modification activities associated with this complex. Crucially, to enable the stable expression of heterologous MTases—which would otherwise introduce “foreign” methylation marks onto the *E. coli* chromosome and trigger cell death—the methylation-dependent restriction systems Mrr (which cleaves DNA containing hydroxymethylcytosine or glucosyl-hydroxymethylcytosine, but also responds to some methylated bases), McrA, and McrBC (which restrict DNA containing methylcytosine or hydroxymethylcytosine) were also inactivated by targeted gene deletions.

A critical consideration during the construction of EC135 was the known synthetic lethality between *dam* and *recA* mutations in *E. coli*. Since the TOP10 background carries a mutant *recA* allele (rendering it recombination-deficient and thus suitable for plasmid stability), direct deletion of *dam* in this strain would result in inviability. To circumvent this, a wild-type *recA* allele was first introduced into TOP10 via allelic exchange, restoring homologous recombination proficiency temporarily to allow for the subsequent clean deletion of *dam*. Following confirmation of the *dam* deletion, the strain’s recombination status was not further modified, as the presence of functional RecA does not interfere with the intended use of EC135 as a plasmid propagation host for MoDMP applications. The final EC135 strain thus carries deletions in *dam*, *dcm*, *hsdRMS*, *mrr*, *mcrA*, and *mcrBC*, and retains a functional *recA* gene.

Validation of EC135 confirmed the absence of Dam, Dcm, and EcoKI methylation activities through restriction enzyme sensitivity assays and dot blot analysis using antibodies specific for N6-methyladenine (m6A), N4-methylcytosine (m4C), and 5-methylcytosine (m5C). Genomic DNA isolated from EC135 showed no detectable signal for m6A or m5C, consistent with the loss of Dam and Dcm functions, and exhibited full sensitivity to restriction enzymes such as DpnII (which cuts unmethylated GATC sites) and MboI, confirming the absence of protective methylation. Furthermore, EC135 supported the stable maintenance of plasmids carrying foreign methylation patterns without growth inhibition, demonstrating the successful ablation of Mrr/Mcr-mediated restriction. This strain thus provides a neutral, permissive background wherein heterologous MTases can be expressed to install desired methylation marks on plasmid DNA without interference from the host’s own R-M machinery, forming the indispensable foundation for all subsequent MoDMP applications described herein.

### Example 2

The second embodiment details the identification, cloning, and functional characterization of putative DNA methyltransferases (MTases) from three representative difficult-to-transform bacterial strains: *Nitrobacter hamburgensis* X14, *Bacillus cereus* ATCC 10987, and *Bacillus amyloliquefaciens* TA208. Genomic sequences of these strains were interrogated using the REBASE database to identify all predicted MTase-encoding genes, resulting in the selection of 24 candidate genes encompassing Type I, II, III R-M system components and orphan MTases. Each gene was PCR-amplified from genomic DNA and cloned into the arabinose-inducible expression vector pBAD43, yielding a library of constructs for individual MTase expression in the EC135 host.

To assess enzymatic activity, total genomic DNA was extracted from EC135 strains harboring each pBAD43-MTase construct following induction with 0.2% arabinose. Southwestern dot blot assays were performed using highly specific antibodies against m6A, m4C, and m5C modifications. Thirteen of the 24 putative MTases produced clear, quantifiable signals above background, indicating successful transfer of methyl groups to DNA. Notably, several MTases exhibited weak signals in dot blots (e.g., BCE_0392 from *B. cereus*, Nham_0582, Nham_0803, and Nham_3225 from *N. hamburgensis*), prompting further validation via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). In this orthogonal assay, genomic DNA was enzymatically digested to deoxynucleosides and analyzed by HPLC-QTOF/MS for the presence of N6-methyl-2′-deoxyadenosine (m6dA). This approach confirmed m6A modification activity for BCE_0392, Nham_0582, and Nham_3225, resolving ambiguities from the less sensitive dot blot method. Nham_0803 remained inconclusive but was retained for downstream testing due to suggestive dot blot data.

One MTase, Nham_0569 from *N. hamburgensis*, caused significant growth retardation in EC135, reducing final biomass by approximately 40%. This toxicity was attributed to its modification of GATC sequences—the same motif targeted by *E. coli*’s native Dam methylase—which plays critical roles in DNA mismatch repair and replication initiation. Premature or ectopic methylation at these sites by Nham_0569 likely disrupted normal cell cycle regulation. Despite this, Nham_0569 was included in the MoDMP pipeline due to its confirmed activity and relevance to the target strain’s R-M defense.

Subsequent determination of sequence specificities involved challenging in vivo-methylated plasmid DNA (primarily pMK4) with panels of restriction enzymes whose recognition sites overlap potential MTase targets. For instance, BAMTA208_6525 from *B. amyloliquefaciens* conferred resistance to BamHI (GGATCC), BglII (AGATCT), and partial resistance to BclI (TGATCA), defining its specificity as RGATCY with partial activity on TGATCA. Similarly, BCE_0393 from *B. cereus* protected against at least twelve different REases, revealing a remarkably broad specificity encompassing GCWGC, GGCC, CCGG, GGNNCC, GCGCGC, GGWCC, and CCWGG (partial). This multi-specific nature underscores why single-MTase approaches fail in strains with complex R-M arsenals. Collectively, these characterizations enabled the rational selection of active, relevant MTases for inclusion in strain-specific MoDMP operons, ensuring comprehensive methylation coverage of plasmid DNA to evade all major restriction barriers in each target organism.

### Example 3

The third embodiment illustrates the assembly and application of multi-MTase expression constructs—designated pM.Bam, pM.Bce, and pM.Nham—for *B. amyloliquefaciens* TA208, *B. cereus* ATCC 10987, and *N. hamburgensis* X14, respectively. Leveraging the high-efficiency homologous recombination system of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, the active MTase genes identified in Example 2 were assembled into synthetic operons within the shuttle vector pWYE724. This vector was engineered to contain a CEN6 origin for stable maintenance in yeast and a TRP1 selectable marker, enabling selection of recombinant plasmids in *S. cerevisiae* strain DAY414. Each MTase coding sequence was amplified with 50-bp flanking homology arms matching adjacent fragments and the linearized pWYE724 backbone (digested at EcoRI and SalI sites). Co-transformation of these fragments into yeast yielded correctly assembled pMoDMP plasmids, which were subsequently rescued into *E. coli* TOP10 for verification by restriction digestion and sequencing.

The pMoDMP plasmids were then introduced into the EC135 host, and co-expression of multiple MTases was induced with arabinose. Dot blot analysis of genomic DNA from these strains confirmed the presence of multiple methylation types (m6A, m4C, m5C), demonstrating successful reprogramming of the host’s methylation profile. Plasmid DNA (including various shuttle vectors like pMK4, pAD123, and pDG148StuI) was prepared from EC135/pMoDMP cultures and used to transform the target bacterial strains via electroporation.

In *B. amyloliquefaciens* TA208, plasmids from standard *E. coli* TOP10 were completely non-transformable, while those from EC135 (lacking Dam/Dcm) yielded only low-efficiency transformants (~300 CFU/µg for pMK4), suggesting the presence of a Type IV system or incompatibility with Dam/Dcm methylation. In stark contrast, MoDMP-methylated pMK4 achieved 3×10⁶ CFU/µg—a 10⁴-fold increase—and enabled transformation of previously recalcitrant plasmids pAD123 and pDG148StuI at ~10⁵ CFU/µg. Similarly, in *B. cereus* ATCC 10987, MoDMP increased pMK4 transformation to 2×10⁷ CFU/µg and improved pMK3 efficiency by 10³-fold over controls. Notably, DNA from TOP10 and EC135 showed similar low efficiencies in this strain, indicating the absence of active Type IV restriction, and highlighting that the primary barrier was Type I–III systems targeting unmethylated sites.

These dramatically enhanced transformation efficiencies facilitated advanced genetic manipulations. Specifically, a non-replicative integration plasmid, pWYE748—carrying chloramphenicol resistance flanked by *upp* homology arms—was methylated via the pM.Bam/EC135 system and transformed into *B. amyloliquefaciens* TA208. Chloramphenicol-resistant colonies were obtained at sufficient frequency to allow isolation of a clean *upp* knockout strain (BS043), confirmed by PCR, sequencing, and phenotypic resistance to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU). This demonstrated, for the first time, the feasibility of direct chromosomal mutagenesis in this industrially important strain, opening avenues for markerless gene deletions and metabolic engineering.

### Example 4

The fourth embodiment presents the groundbreaking application of the MoDMP pipeline to achieve the first-ever genetic transformation of *Nitrobacter hamburgensis* X14, a Gram-negative chemoautotrophic bacterium central to the global nitrogen cycle but historically impervious to genetic manipulation due to its eleven predicted R-M systems. A GFP-expressing plasmid, pWYE561—comprising the *gfpmut3a* gene under control of the native *Nham_3450* promoter and carried on a broad-host-range pBBR1 replicon—was methylated in EC135 cells co-expressing the four active MTases from *N. hamburgensis* (Nham_0569, Nham_0582, Nham_0803, Nham_3225) via the pM.Nham construct.

Electroporation of this MoDMP-modified plasmid into *N. hamburgensis* X14, followed by extended recovery and antibiotic selection in DSMZ 756a medium, yielded a turbid culture after three weeks, indicative of transformant growth. Rigorous contamination checks via microscopy and plating on LB confirmed the culture’s purity. Plasmid rescue into *E. coli* TOP10, followed by restriction digestion and PCR amplification of *gfpmut3a*, verified the presence and integrity of pWYE561 in the *Nitrobacter* population. Most compellingly, confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed bright green fluorescence throughout the cytoplasm of a substantial fraction of cells, providing direct visual evidence of successful transformation and heterologous gene expression.

Flow cytometry quantified the proportion of fluorescent cells at 50.37%, confirming that over half the population stably maintained and expressed the plasmid. This high rate—despite the absence of a *Nitrobacter*-specific replicon and reliance on the broad-host-range pBBR1 origin—underscores the efficacy of MoDMP in neutralizing all major R-M barriers. Although the culture was multiclonal due to enrichment via serial subculture, the protocol established by Sayavedra-Soto et al. for *Nitrosomonas europaea* (involving filtration and transfer on nylon membranes) can be applied to isolate clonal derivatives.

This achievement represents a paradigm shift in nitrification research, enabling for the first time targeted genetic interrogation of *Nitrobacter* metabolism, stress responses, and ecological interactions. The success with *N. hamburgensis*—a slow-growing, fastidious autotroph with an exceptionally complex R-M repertoire—powerfully validates the universality and robustness of the MoDMP pipeline. It confirms that comprehensive methylation mimicry, rather than piecemeal R-M inactivation, is the key to unlocking genetic access to the vast majority of bacteria that have eluded manipulation due to defensive R-M systems. This embodiment thus exemplifies the transformative potential of the invention across the microbial world.
