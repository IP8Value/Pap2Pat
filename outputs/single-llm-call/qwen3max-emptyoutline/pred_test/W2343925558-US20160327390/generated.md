# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) and, more specifically, to high-performance vibratory gyroscopes for detecting angular rate about an axis perpendicular to the plane of vibration. The invention provides a novel substrate-decoupled bulk acoustic wave (SD-BAW) gyroscope that achieves exceptional environmental robustness, low bias instability, and high immunity to shock and vibration by integrating a stress isolation structure with a high-frequency axis-symmetric resonator. This architecture enables operation in the megahertz frequency range with quality factors exceeding 60,000 under moderate vacuum conditions, thereby significantly reducing sensitivity to external mechanical disturbances such as linear accelerations, random vibrations, and acoustic interference. The disclosed device is particularly suited for applications requiring precise inertial sensing in dynamic environments, including consumer electronics, automotive safety systems, industrial navigation, and personal dead-reckoning solutions where long-term stability, miniaturization, low power consumption, and manufacturability at scale are critical.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Inertial sensors based on microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) technology have become ubiquitous across a wide spectrum of modern applications, ranging from motion-controlled gaming interfaces and smartphone orientation detection to advanced driver assistance systems and autonomous vehicle navigation. Among these, MEMS gyroscopes—devices that measure angular velocity through the Coriolis effect—have seen rapid adoption due to their small form factor, compatibility with standard semiconductor fabrication processes, and decreasing cost per unit. However, despite significant advances in performance over the past two decades, conventional MEMS gyroscopes continue to face fundamental limitations when deployed in real-world environments characterized by mechanical noise, temperature fluctuations, and unpredictable shock events.

The most prevalent commercial implementation of MEMS gyroscopes today is the tuning-fork gyroscope (TFG), which operates on the principle of differential motion between two proof masses driven in anti-phase. While effective in rejecting common-mode linear accelerations under ideal symmetry, TFGs suffer from several inherent drawbacks that compromise their reliability in demanding applications. First, their operational resonance frequencies typically lie below 20 kHz, placing them squarely within the spectral band of common environmental vibrations (e.g., from machinery, vehicles, or human movement). This overlap renders TFGs highly susceptible to false signals induced by external mechanical stimuli, leading to degraded signal-to-noise ratios and long-term drift in output. Second, even minor asymmetries introduced during fabrication—such as mass imbalances, stiffness mismatches, or electrode misalignments—can result in significant quadrature errors and cross-axis coupling, necessitating complex post-fabrication trimming or closed-loop compensation schemes. Third, recent studies have demonstrated that consumer-grade TFGs can inadvertently act as microphones, converting low-frequency pressure waves into spurious angular rate signals—a vulnerability that raises serious privacy concerns in always-on mobile devices.

To mitigate acceleration sensitivity, some TFG designs incorporate redundant proof-mass architectures that exploit common-mode rejection. However, this approach invariably increases die area, complicates layout symmetry, and often requires additional calibration steps to compensate for process-induced imperfections, making it economically unfeasible for high-volume consumer markets. Moreover, such mechanisms do not address higher-order vibrational modes that may still fall within the disturbance spectrum, nor do they eliminate sensitivity to acoustic eavesdropping.

An alternative paradigm for high-performance rotation sensing involves the use of axis-symmetric resonators operating in degenerate vibrational modes. In such devices, two orthogonal standing-wave patterns—referred to as the drive and sense modes—are sustained within a single resonant structure. When the device rotates about its sensitive axis, the Coriolis force couples energy from the excited drive mode into the nominally idle sense mode, producing a measurable displacement proportional to the input angular rate. Because both modes share the same physical structure, they inherently exhibit matched thermal and mechanical responses, enabling superior bias stability compared to differential architectures like TFGs. Furthermore, when implemented using bulk acoustic wave (BAW) principles in high-aspect-ratio silicon structures, these gyroscopes can operate at frequencies in the megahertz range—well above the dominant components of environmental vibration spectra—thereby achieving intrinsic immunity to mechanical noise.

Despite these advantages, practical realization of high-performance BAW gyroscopes has been hindered by challenges related to energy dissipation and mode coupling. In particular, anchor loss—the leakage of vibrational energy from the resonator into the supporting substrate through mechanical supports—has emerged as a dominant source of damping in many MEMS resonators. In axis-symmetric devices fabricated on anisotropic substrates such as (100) single-crystal silicon (SCS), the directional dependence of elastic properties can cause the two degenerate modes to experience unequal anchor losses, even if geometrically identical. This phenomenon, known as anisodamping, introduces a velocity-proportional coupling term between the modes that manifests as an in-phase zero-rate output (ZRO) error indistinguishable from the true Coriolis signal. Unlike stiffness-induced quadrature errors—which appear 90° out of phase and can be readily nulled via electrostatic feedback—anisodamping errors cannot be easily separated from the desired response without resorting to complex modulation schemes such as mode reversal, which impose severe penalties in terms of bandwidth, power consumption, or system complexity.

Previous attempts to reduce anchor loss have included the integration of phononic crystal bandgaps, quarter-wavelength tethers, and material mismatch layers. While effective in certain contexts, these approaches often require intricate patterning, introduce additional fabrication steps, or compromise structural integrity. A simpler yet powerful strategy involves the use of stress isolation structures—compliant mechanical elements interposed between the resonator and its anchor points—that attenuate strain transmission at the interface. However, unless carefully designed to preserve axisymmetry, such decoupling mechanisms risk introducing new sources of imbalance or thermoelastic dissipation that could exacerbate mode coupling.

Additionally, even with perfect mode matching, the overall performance of a vibratory gyroscope remains contingent upon the quality factor (Q) of its resonant modes. High Q values enhance signal-to-noise ratio by increasing the amplitude of the Coriolis-induced response relative to thermal noise, but they also render the system more sensitive to parasitic couplings and environmental perturbations. Therefore, achieving both high Q and robust environmental insensitivity requires a holistic design approach that simultaneously addresses mechanical decoupling, symmetric loss engineering, and electronic interfacing.

Accordingly, there exists a compelling need for a MEMS gyroscope architecture that combines the high-frequency operation and intrinsic symmetry of BAW resonators with an effective substrate decoupling mechanism to suppress anisodamping, while maintaining compatibility with scalable manufacturing processes and enabling straightforward electronic compensation of residual non-idealities. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel substrate-decoupled BAW (SD-BAW) gyroscope that integrates a radially symmetric stress isolation system with a second-elliptical mode disk resonator, thereby achieving unprecedented levels of environmental robustness, bias stability, and manufacturability for high-end inertial sensing applications.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a substrate-decoupled bulk acoustic wave (SD-BAW) gyroscope configured for high-precision detection of angular rate about an axis normal to the plane of vibration. The core innovation lies in the integration of a mechanically compliant stress isolation structure between the active resonator and its substrate anchor, which dramatically reduces anchor loss and ensures symmetric energy dissipation for the two degenerate vibrational modes. This architectural feature enables the gyroscope to operate at high frequencies (typically in the megahertz range) with exceptionally high quality factors under moderate vacuum packaging conditions, while simultaneously minimizing environmentally induced offset drift and vibration sensitivity.

In one embodiment, the SD-BAW gyroscope comprises a circular or near-circular disk-shaped resonator fabricated from a single-crystal silicon layer of a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer, wherein the resonator is patterned to support second-elliptical (n=3) in-plane degenerate vibrational modes. These modes are selected for their favorable degeneracy properties on (100) SCS substrates and reduced sensitivity to crystalline misalignment. The resonator is connected to a central anchor point via a radially symmetric stress isolation structure composed of multiple curved or serpentine beams arranged to provide high compliance in the radial direction while maintaining structural rigidity in other degrees of freedom. This configuration effectively decouples the resonator from substrate-borne acoustic waves and minimizes strain energy density at the anchor interface, thereby suppressing anchor loss as the dominant damping mechanism.

Surrounding the resonator are a plurality of ultra-narrow capacitive electrodes formed with sub-300 nm gaps, enabling efficient electrostatic actuation, displacement sensing, and dynamic tuning of the resonant modes. Specifically, dedicated tuning electrodes aligned with the antinodes of each degenerate mode allow independent adjustment of their resonance frequencies via electrostatic spring softening, facilitating precise mode matching to eliminate frequency splitting caused by fabrication-induced anisoelasticity or anisoinertia. Additionally, quadrature cancellation electrodes positioned between the antinodes enable active suppression of stiffness coupling terms, thereby nulling quadrature error signals that would otherwise contribute to zero-rate output.

A key advantage of the disclosed architecture is its ability to maintain symmetric damping characteristics across both degenerate modes, even in the presence of anisotropic material properties and variable boundary conditions. By ensuring that any residual energy dissipation—whether from thermoelastic damping, surface losses, or internal friction within the stress isolation structure—is experienced equally by both modes, the invention eliminates the primary source of in-phase ZRO errors that plague conventional axis-symmetric gyroscopes. This symmetry is preserved over temperature variations and mechanical shocks, as demonstrated by stable and co-varying quality factors for both modes across a wide operational range.

The SD-BAW gyroscope is packaged at a moderate vacuum level (1–10 Torr) using wafer-level hermetic sealing techniques, which further enhances Q by mitigating squeeze-film damping without requiring ultra-high vacuum infrastructure. Through-silicon vias (TSVs) and redistribution layers provide electrical connectivity to external application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) that implement closed-loop drive and sense electronics, including automatic gain control, I/Q demodulation, and real-time quadrature feedback.

System-level characterization confirms that the SD-BAW gyroscope achieves an angle random walk (ARW) of less than 0.4°/√h and a bias instability (BI) below 11°/h in a fully integrated package, with potential for further improvement through optimized electronics. Critically, the device exhibits extraordinary immunity to environmental disturbances: under 14-gRMS random vibration (50 Hz–20 kHz), its vibration sensitivity is measured at only 0.012°/s/gRMS, and it withstands 40-g half-sine shocks with a peak-to-peak offset shift of merely 1.75°/s. These metrics represent a substantial advancement over state-of-the-art tuning-fork gyroscopes, whose performance degrades by orders of magnitude under similar conditions.

In summary, the invention offers a manufacturable, high-performance MEMS gyroscope that overcomes longstanding limitations in environmental robustness, bias stability, and scalability. By synergistically combining high-frequency BAW operation, symmetric stress decoupling, and intelligent electrode design, the SD-BAW platform establishes a new benchmark for inertial sensing in consumer, automotive, and industrial applications where reliability in real-world conditions is paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Mode-to-Mode Coupling in Vibratory Gyroscopes

Vibratory gyroscopes fundamentally rely on the transfer of energy between two orthogonal modes of vibration induced by the Coriolis effect when the device undergoes rotation about a sensitive axis. In an ideal axis-symmetric resonator, these two degenerate modes—commonly referred to as the drive mode and the sense mode—possess identical natural frequencies, damping coefficients, and effective masses, ensuring that any coupling between them arises solely from the Coriolis force proportional to the applied angular rate. However, in practical implementations, deviations from perfect symmetry due to material anisotropy, geometric imperfections, or asymmetric energy dissipation mechanisms introduce unwanted mode-to-mode coupling that manifests as erroneous output signals even in the absence of rotation. Understanding and mitigating these coupling effects is therefore central to achieving high-performance gyroscopic operation.

The dynamic behavior of a vibratory gyroscope can be mathematically described by a pair of coupled second-order differential equations representing the generalized coordinates of the two degenerate modes. Let \( q_1(t) \) and \( q_2(t) \) denote the modal displacements of the drive and sense modes, respectively. The equations of motion take the form:

\[
m_{11}\ddot{q}_1(t) + b_{11}\dot{q}_1(t) + b_{12}\dot{q}_2(t) + k_{11}q_1(t) + k_{12}q_2(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} F_{1,i} - 2\lambda m_{22} \Omega(t) \dot{q}_2(t)
\]

\[
m_{22}\ddot{q}_2(t) + b_{22}\dot{q}_2(t) + b_{21}\dot{q}_1(t) + k_{22}q_2(t) + k_{21}q_1(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{l} F_{2,j} + 2\lambda m_{11} \Omega(t) \dot{q}_1(t)
\]

Here, \( m_{11} \) and \( m_{22} \) represent the effective modal masses; \( k_{11}, k_{22} \) and \( k_{12}, k_{21} \) are the direct and cross-coupling stiffness terms, respectively; \( b_{11}, b_{22} \) and \( b_{12}, b_{21} \) denote the direct and cross-coupling damping coefficients; \( \Omega(t) \) is the time-varying angular rate about the sensitive axis; \( \lambda \) is the angular gain factor determined by the Bryan’s effect and dependent on the mode shape and geometry; and \( F_{1,i}, F_{2,j} \) are the electrostatic forces applied via external electrodes for actuation, sensing, and tuning.

In a perfectly symmetric device, \( m_{11} = m_{22} \), \( k_{11} = k_{22} \), \( b_{11} = b_{22} \), and all cross-coupling terms \( k_{12}, k_{21}, b_{12}, b_{21} \) vanish, resulting in degenerate resonance frequencies \( \omega_{01} = \omega_{02} = \sqrt{k_{11}/m_{11}} \). Under these conditions, the only coupling mechanism is the Coriolis term, which produces a sense-mode displacement \( q_{2,\text{Coriolis}}(t) \) that is amplitude-modulated by \( \Omega(t) \) and phase-shifted by 90° relative to the drive-mode oscillation.

However, real-world devices inevitably exhibit asymmetries. Fabrication tolerances may lead to slight ellipticity in an otherwise circular resonator, causing differences in effective stiffness (anisoelasticity) or mass distribution (anisoinertia). These imperfections result in a frequency split \( \Delta f = |\omega_{01} - \omega_{02}| / (2\pi) \), which degrades scale factor accuracy and increases susceptibility to noise. More critically, they generate non-zero stiffness coupling terms \( k_{12}, k_{21} \), which produce a quadrature error signal \( q_{2,\text{stiffness}}(t) \) in the sense mode even at zero rotation rate. This signal is given by:

\[
q_{2,\text{stiffness}}(t) = \frac{Q_2}{m_{22} \omega_{02}^2} k_{21} \overline{q}_1 \cos(\omega_{02} t)
\]

where \( \overline{q}_1 \) is the steady-state amplitude of the drive mode and \( Q_2 \) is the quality factor of the sense mode. Notably, this error is 90° out of phase with the Coriolis signal \( q_{2,\text{Coriolis}}(t) \propto \sin(\omega_{02} t) \), allowing it to be identified and canceled using synchronous demodulation techniques. Electrostatic quadrature cancellation electrodes, strategically placed between antinodal regions of the degenerate modes, apply corrective forces that nullify \( k_{21} \), thereby eliminating this source of ZRO.

A more insidious form of coupling arises from asymmetric damping, or anisodamping, captured by the cross-damping terms \( b_{12}, b_{21} \). Unlike stiffness coupling, damping coupling generates an error signal:

\[
q_{2,\text{damping}}(t) = -\frac{Q_2}{m_{22} \omega_{02}} b_{21} \overline{q}_1 \sin(\omega_{02} t)
\]

which is **in phase** with the Coriolis response. This makes it impossible to distinguish from the true rotation signal using standard demodulation, rendering it a dominant contributor to bias instability and long-term drift. Anisodamping primarily originates from unequal anchor losses—differences in how vibrational energy leaks from each mode into the substrate through the mechanical supports. In devices fabricated on (100) single-crystal silicon, the anisotropic elastic constants cause the strain energy density at the anchor interface to vary with the orientation of the mode’s antinodes, leading to direction-dependent dissipation.

Conventional approaches to mitigate anisodamping include mode reversal (alternating excitation between modes) or dual-mode simultaneous drive, but these impose significant overhead in terms of bandwidth, power, or circuit complexity. The present invention circumvents this problem entirely by introducing a radially symmetric stress isolation structure that decouples the resonator from the substrate, ensuring that both degenerate modes experience nearly identical damping environments. Finite element analysis confirms that the strain energy density at the anchor is attenuated by over four orders of magnitude compared to a directly anchored resonator, rendering anchor loss negligible relative to intrinsic mechanisms like thermoelastic damping. Crucially, because the isolation structure itself is axisymmetric, any residual dissipation within it affects both modes equally, preserving damping symmetry across temperature, shock, and vibration.

Thus, by addressing both stiffness and damping coupling through a combination of geometric symmetry, mechanical decoupling, and electrostatic compensation, the SD-BAW gyroscope achieves a level of mode orthogonality previously unattainable in high-frequency MEMS gyroscopes, enabling unprecedented bias stability and environmental robustness.
