# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of thermal management and phase-change heat transfer, and more particularly to an actively controllable boiling system that enables dynamic modulation of bubble nucleation, heat transfer coefficient (HTC), and critical heat flux (CHF) through the application of electric potentials in conjunction with charged surfactants. The invention provides a method and apparatus for real-time, reversible, and spatially resolved control over boiling behavior on engineered or plain surfaces immersed in a liquid medium containing ionic surfactants. This technology is applicable across a broad range of industrial domains, including but not limited to electronics cooling, power generation, chemical processing, microfluidics, and thermal energy storage systems. By leveraging electrostatic interactions between a conductive boiling surface and oppositely or similarly charged surfactant molecules dissolved in the working fluid, the wettability of the surface can be dynamically tuned, thereby directly influencing the onset, frequency, and spatial distribution of vapor bubble nucleation during boiling. The invention operates without inducing significant electrochemical reactions, electrolysis, or electrohydrodynamic (EHD) effects, relying instead on purely capacitive charging of the solid–liquid interface to modulate interfacial adsorption and desorption of surfactant species. This approach enables precise, sub-second temporal control and millimeter-scale spatial patterning of boiling activity, offering unprecedented adaptability in thermal systems that require responsive heat dissipation under varying operational loads.

## BACKGROUND ART

Boiling is a highly efficient mode of heat transfer widely employed in industrial applications due to its ability to remove large amounts of thermal energy at nearly constant temperature. Conventional pool boiling relies on passive surface engineering—such as microstructuring, porous coatings, or chemical functionalization—to enhance nucleation site density, delay the onset of film boiling, and increase the critical heat flux (CHF). However, these passive enhancements are static; once fabricated, their performance characteristics are fixed and cannot be adjusted in response to changing thermal demands or operating conditions. This limitation becomes particularly problematic in dynamic environments such as data centers, electric vehicles, or renewable energy systems, where heat loads fluctuate rapidly and unpredictably. Consequently, there has been longstanding interest in developing *active* methods to control boiling in real time.

Early attempts at active boiling control focused on external stimuli such as mechanical vibration, acoustic waves, or magnetic fields. While these techniques demonstrated some degree of modulation, they suffered from poor spatial resolution, high energy overhead, or incompatibility with compact systems. More recently, electric fields have been explored as a means to influence boiling behavior. Two primary mechanisms have emerged: electrohydrodynamic (EHD) boiling and electrowetting-based approaches. In EHD boiling, high-voltage electric fields (typically >100 V) induce body forces in the liquid that disrupt the vapor boundary layer, enhancing convection and delaying dryout. However, EHD requires substantial power input, risks dielectric breakdown, and lacks fine spatial control. Electrowetting, on the other hand, uses moderate voltages (typically <50 V) to reduce the contact angle of a liquid on a dielectric-coated electrode, thereby increasing wettability. While electrowetting has been successfully applied to droplet manipulation and lab-on-a-chip devices, its extension to boiling systems has been limited by the need for insulating dielectric layers that impede heat conduction and degrade under prolonged thermal cycling.

A separate line of research has investigated the role of surfactants in boiling. Surfactants are amphiphilic molecules that adsorb at interfaces and can alter surface tension, wettability, and nucleation dynamics. Anionic and cationic surfactants, in particular, carry net charges that enable interaction with electric fields. Prior studies have shown that adding surfactants to boiling liquids can either enhance or suppress heat transfer depending on concentration, molecular structure, and surface chemistry. However, these effects were considered passive and irreversible once the surfactant was introduced into the system. No prior art has demonstrated the *reversible*, *on-demand*, and *localized* control of boiling by combining low-voltage electric potentials with charged surfactants in a manner that exploits electrostatic adsorption/desorption at the boiling surface without triggering Faradaic reactions.

Notably, conventional wisdom holds that boiling control via electric fields must involve either high-voltage EHD effects or dielectric-based electrowetting. The present invention departs fundamentally from both paradigms. It operates at very low voltages (≤2 V), uses bare conductive surfaces (e.g., silver, gold, copper) without dielectric coatings, and achieves control not through bulk fluid motion or contact angle reduction via electrowetting, but through the voltage-mediated modulation of surfactant adsorption that *increases* hydrophobicity when surfactants adsorb—thereby *promoting* nucleation, which is the opposite of electrowetting behavior. This counterintuitive mechanism has not been previously recognized or exploited for boiling control. Moreover, existing literature does not disclose the use of square wave or programmable potential waveforms to cyclically switch boiling “on” and “off” with sub-second response times, nor does it describe spatially patterned electrode arrays for localized bubble activation. The absence of redox activity, minimal current draw (<60 μA cm⁻²), and compatibility with standard metals further distinguish the present approach from all known electro-thermal boiling technologies. Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for a low-power, reversible, spatially addressable, and material-compatible method to actively tune boiling performance in real time—a need that the present invention fulfills.

## SUMMARY OF EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a novel method and system for actively controlling boiling heat transfer by modulating the wettability of a conductive boiling surface through the electrostatic adsorption and desorption of charged surfactant molecules present in the working fluid. This is achieved by applying a small electric potential (typically between −0.1 V and −2.0 V versus a counter electrode) across the interface between the boiling surface and the liquid, wherein the polarity and magnitude of the potential determine whether charged surfactants are attracted to or repelled from the surface. When surfactants adsorb onto the surface, they form a hydrophobic monolayer that reduces wettability, lowers the energy barrier for vapor nucleation, and thereby enhances bubble formation and heat transfer. Conversely, when the potential is adjusted to repel surfactants, the surface becomes more wettable, suppressing nucleation and shifting heat dissipation toward single-phase convection. Critically, this process is fully reversible, rapid (response times under 600 ms), and operates in a purely capacitive regime with negligible Faradaic current, ensuring long-term stability and minimal energy consumption.

In one embodiment, the invention comprises a boiling apparatus including a conductive heating surface in thermal communication with a heat source, a reservoir containing a liquid medium with a low concentration (below the critical micelle concentration) of ionic surfactant, and a counter electrode immersed in the liquid. A voltage source is connected between the heating surface and the counter electrode, configured to apply a tunable DC or time-varying potential. By selecting surfactants with known charge (e.g., anionic sodium dodecyl sulfate or cationic dodecyl trimethylammonium bromide), the direction of the wettability response to a given potential polarity can be predetermined. For instance, applying a negative potential to a surface in a solution of anionic surfactant repels the surfactant, increasing wettability and suppressing boiling; the same negative potential in a cationic surfactant solution attracts the surfactant, decreasing wettability and promoting boiling. This dual-response capability enables flexible system design depending on desired operational modes.

In another embodiment, the boiling surface is segmented into multiple independently addressable conductive electrodes, each connected to a programmable voltage source. This configuration allows for spatial control of boiling, wherein specific regions of the surface can be selectively activated or deactivated to produce bubbles only in designated zones. Such spatial patterning is particularly valuable in flow boiling or multi-zone thermal management systems where localized heat fluxes require targeted cooling. The electrodes may be fabricated using standard microfabrication techniques on silicon, metal, or ceramic substrates, with integrated thin-film heaters providing uniform or zoned heating from the backside.

The invention further encompasses methods for optimizing heat transfer performance by dynamically adjusting the applied potential in response to real-time thermal feedback. For example, during periods of low heat load, a potential that promotes nucleation may be applied to maximize HTC; as the heat flux increases toward CHF limits, the potential may be switched to suppress nucleation and enhance CHF via improved wettability. This adaptive strategy enables operation across a wider range of heat fluxes than possible with static surfaces. Experimental validation demonstrates that the heat transfer coefficient can be modulated by up to 1,100% at a fixed superheat, and surface temperature can be varied by over 2°C at a fixed heat flux, solely through voltage control.

Importantly, the invention does not rely on electrohydrodynamic forces, electrolysis, or dielectric layers. Control additives such as nonionic surfactants or simple salts show no response to the applied potential, confirming that the effect is uniquely tied to the electrostatic interaction with *charged* surfactants. The system remains stable over extended operation (>10 hours) with no observable degradation, and surfactant concentrations as low as 0.2 mM are effective, though higher concentrations (up to the critical micelle concentration) yield stronger modulation. The approach is material-agnostic within the capacitive voltage window, having been demonstrated on silver, gold, and copper, and is compatible with elevated temperatures when thermally stable surfactants (e.g., fluorocarbon-based) are employed. Overall, the invention provides a robust, scalable, and energy-efficient platform for intelligent thermal management with applications spanning consumer electronics, power plants, aerospace systems, and biomedical devices.

## DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention will now be described in detail with reference to illustrative embodiments that demonstrate the core principles, operational modes, and practical implementations of the actively controllable boiling system. It should be understood that these embodiments are provided for explanatory purposes and do not limit the scope of the invention, which encompasses all variations falling within the spirit and claims appended hereto.

In a foundational embodiment, the boiling system consists of a planar conductive surface serving as both the heater and the working electrode, immersed in a pool of deionized water containing a low concentration (e.g., 2.6 mM) of an ionic surfactant such as sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), which carries a net negative charge. The surface is fabricated from a high-thermal-conductivity metal such as silver, which is roughened slightly (e.g., with 240-grit sandpaper) to provide baseline nucleation sites while remaining otherwise chemically and topographically homogeneous. A counter electrode, constructed from a corrosion-resistant, high-surface-area material such as titanium mesh, is positioned in the liquid at a defined distance from the boiling surface. A voltage source capable of precise, low-noise output in the range of −2.0 V to +0.5 V is connected between the boiling surface and the counter electrode, with the potential referenced as V<sub>cell</sub> = V<sub>surf</sub> − V<sub>counter</sub>. During operation, a constant heat flux (e.g., 60 W over a 2 × 2 cm area) is applied to the boiling surface via embedded cartridge heaters or resistive elements, maintaining the system in a nucleate boiling regime.

When V<sub>cell</sub> is set to a mildly negative value such as −0.1 V, the negatively charged SDS molecules experience a weak electrostatic attraction toward the surface due to image charge effects or residual positive surface charge, leading to partial adsorption of the surfactant tails onto the metal. This adsorbed layer presents a hydrophobic interface to the liquid, reducing the effective wettability and lowering the free energy barrier for vapor embryo formation. Consequently, bubble nucleation occurs readily, resulting in a high density of active nucleation sites and efficient heat removal via latent heat of vaporization. The heat transfer coefficient (HTC) under these conditions is relatively high, and the surface superheat (difference between surface temperature and saturation temperature) is correspondingly low for a given heat flux.

Upon stepping V<sub>cell</sub> to a more negative potential such as −2.0 V, the electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged surface and the anionic SDS headgroups dominates, causing rapid desorption of the surfactant molecules from the interface. The surface thus reverts to a more hydrophilic state, increasing the contact angle hysteresis and raising the nucleation energy barrier. Bubble formation is immediately suppressed, as observed in high-speed imaging, with nucleation ceasing within 600 milliseconds. Heat transfer shifts predominantly to single-phase convection, leading to a rise in surface temperature and a corresponding decrease in HTC. The thermal response—measured via embedded thermocouples and analyzed using a fin equation to account for axial conduction losses—shows that the surface temperature increases while HTC decreases, and these changes are out-of-phase and in-phase with the applied potential, respectively, confirming the causal link between interfacial wettability and boiling dynamics.

Reversing the potential back to −0.1 V reinitiates surfactant adsorption, restoring the hydrophobic coating and reactivating nucleation within 300 milliseconds. This hysteresis in response time (desorption slower than adsorption) is attributed to the energy landscape of nucleation itself, wherein the presence of pre-existing vapor pockets or surface heterogeneities facilitates faster re-initiation of boiling once the hydrophobic condition is restored. The entire cycle is repeatable indefinitely without degradation, as confirmed by continuous operation over 10 hours and across multiple experimental sessions spanning weeks.

To generalize the mechanism, the same apparatus is tested with a cationic surfactant such as dodecyl trimethylammonium bromide (DTAB). In this case, a negative potential (−2.0 V) *attracts* the positively charged DTAB molecules to the surface, enhancing adsorption and hydrophobicity, thereby *promoting* nucleation. Conversely, a less negative potential (−0.1 V) reduces adsorption, increasing wettability and suppressing boiling. Thus, the direction of the thermal response is inverted relative to the anionic case, demonstrating that the invention’s behavior is dictated by the sign of the surfactant charge and can be engineered accordingly. Control experiments with nonionic surfactants (e.g., MEGA-10) or simple electrolytes (e.g., NaBr) show no change in boiling behavior with applied potential, ruling out alternative mechanisms such as electroosmosis, Joule heating, or double-layer compression unrelated to surfactant charge.

In an advanced embodiment, the boiling surface is patterned into an array of eight electrically isolated gold electrodes, each 1 cm wide, fabricated on a silicon wafer using photolithography and sputter deposition. Each electrode is backed by an independent platinum thin-film heater, allowing uniform heat flux delivery across the entire surface. With DTAB in solution, individual electrodes are switched between −0.1 V (“off”) and −2.0 V (“on”) using solid-state relays controlled by a digital acquisition system. High-speed imaging reveals that bubbles form exclusively on electrodes held at −2.0 V, while adjacent electrodes at −0.1 V remain bubble-free, even under identical thermal loading. This spatial selectivity is achieved with sub-millimeter precision and sub-second switching, enabling dynamic reconfiguration of boiling zones. Such capability is invaluable in applications like chip-scale cooling, where hotspots can be tracked and cooled on demand, or in chemical reactors where localized boiling can enhance mixing or reaction rates.

The system’s performance is quantified through boiling curves (heat flux vs. superheat) obtained under quasi-static heating conditions. For SDS, the curve at −2.0 V is shifted rightward relative to −0.1 V, indicating higher superheat for the same heat flux due to suppressed nucleation. The vertical separation between curves at fixed superheat represents tunable HTC—up to 1,000% enhancement at 8.7°C superheat. Similarly, for DTAB, the −2.0 V curve shifts leftward, showing enhanced nucleation at low heat fluxes but reduced CHF due to premature dryout from excessive bubble crowding. Crucially, the intersection of the two curves defines an optimal switching point: below the intersection, the “on” state maximizes HTC; above it, the “off” state preserves CHF. This enables intelligent, load-adaptive control.

The invention’s efficacy depends on surfactant concentration, with stronger effects observed near but below the critical micelle concentration (CMC). At 0.2 mM DTAB, modulation is weak due to limited surface coverage; at 2.6 mM (below CMC of ~8 mM for DTAB), response is robust. Higher concentrations risk micelle formation, which can alter bulk properties and shift boiling curves independently of voltage. The counter electrode placement also influences performance: closer proximity reduces ohmic drop in the resistive liquid, ensuring full potential delivery to the interface. Contact angle measurements under boiling conditions confirm that advancing angles increase with adsorbed surfactant (e.g., DTAB at −2.0 V yields higher contact angle than at −0.1 V), directly linking wettability changes to nucleation control.

Materials compatibility is broad: silver, gold, and copper all exhibit the effect within their capacitive windows (verified by cyclic voltammetry to exclude redox peaks). Dielectric coatings are unnecessary and undesirable, as they impede heat flow. Thermal stability is ensured by selecting surfactants with decomposition temperatures well above operating conditions; for high-pressure steam systems near 374°C, fluorinated surfactants stable to 600°C may be employed. The electrical model is purely capacitive, with currents <60 μA cm⁻², confirming no Faradaic processes. Long-term tests show no performance drift, and periodic surfactant replenishment is feasible if needed, though not required in closed-loop systems with condensers.

In summary, the illustrative embodiments demonstrate a versatile, low-voltage, surfactant-mediated platform for active boiling control that is fast, reversible, spatially programmable, and compatible with industrial materials and operating conditions.

## EXAMPLES

The following examples provide detailed experimental demonstrations of the invention’s capabilities, validating the underlying mechanism and showcasing its performance across various configurations. All experiments were conducted under rigorously controlled conditions to isolate the effect of electric potential on boiling in the presence of charged surfactants.

**Example 1: Temporal On/Off Switching of Nucleation with SDS**  
A 2 × 2 cm silver foil surface, roughened with 240-grit sandpaper, was soldered to a copper heating block equipped with five cartridge heaters. The surface was immersed in 400 mL of deionized water containing 2.6 mM sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). A titanium mesh counter electrode was positioned 2 cm above the surface. A constant heat input of 60 W was applied, establishing steady nucleate boiling. The cell potential V<sub>cell</sub> was initially held at −0.1 V, resulting in vigorous bubble formation. At t = 0 s, V<sub>cell</sub> was stepped to −2.0 V. Within 600 ms, bubble nucleation ceased entirely, as recorded by a high-speed camera at 120 fps. The surface temperature, measured by a thermocouple 8 mm below the surface and corrected via fin analysis, rose from 108.7°C to 110.9°C, indicating reduced heat transfer efficiency. After 30 s, V<sub>cell</sub> was returned to −0.1 V, and nucleation resumed within 300 ms, with temperature dropping back to baseline. This cycle was repeated five times with identical response, confirming reversibility. Current measurements remained below 50 μA cm⁻² throughout, verifying capacitive behavior.

**Example 2: Square Wave Response with Oppositely Charged Surfactants**  
Using the same setup, square wave potentials oscillating between −0.1 V and −2.0 V with a 60-second period were applied to solutions of 2.6 mM SDS and 2.6 mM DTAB. For SDS, the surface temperature peaked during the −2.0 V phase (repelling surfactant) and troughed during −0.1 V (adsorbing surfactant), yielding an out-of-phase thermal response. HTC, calculated from heat flux and superheat, was in-phase with potential. For DTAB, the opposite occurred: temperature was lowest at −2.0 V (attracting surfactant) and highest at −0.1 V, confirming the charge-dependent mechanism. Control tests with 2.6 mM NaBr or MEGA-10 showed flat temperature and HTC profiles, irrespective of potential, eliminating ionic strength or nonionic adsorption as confounding factors.

**Example 3: Boiling Curve Tunability and Performance Metrics**  
Quasi-static boiling curves were generated by slowly ramping heater power from 0 to 50 W cm⁻² while maintaining fixed V<sub>cell</sub>. For SDS at −0.1 V, nucleation onset occurred at 4.2°C superheat; at −2.0 V, onset shifted to 6.8°C. At 8.7°C superheat, heat flux was 41 W cm⁻² (−0.1 V) versus 3.7 W cm⁻² (−2.0 V), representing a 1,007% HTC increase. Temperature modulation at fixed 17 W cm⁻² spanned 2.5°C (7.5°C to 10°C superheat). For DTAB, at 7°C superheat, heat flux rose from 1.6 W cm⁻² (−0.1 V) to 20 W cm⁻² (−2.0 V)—a 1,150% increase. CHF for DTAB decreased from 32 W cm⁻² (−0.1 V) to 26 W cm⁻² (−2.0 V), illustrating the trade-off between HTC and CHF that enables adaptive optimization.

**Example 4: Spatial Control with Patterned Electrodes**  
An 8-electrode gold array on silicon, each 1 cm wide and insulated by SiO<sub>2</sub>, was heated uniformly at 1.5 W cm⁻² with 2.6 mM DTAB. Electrodes 1, 3, 5, and 7 were held at −2.0 V (“on”); others at −0.1 V (“off”). High-speed video showed bubbles forming only on “on” electrodes, with sharp boundaries between active and inactive zones. Switching patterns every 2 seconds produced dynamic bubble maps, demonstrating real-time spatial reconfigurability. Identical results were obtained on copper-patterned surfaces, confirming material generality.

**Example 5: Concentration and Counter Electrode Effects**  
Square wave tests with 0.2 mM DTAB showed only a 15% HTC modulation versus 1,100% at 2.6 mM, consistent with Langmuir adsorption isotherms. Moving the counter electrode from 5 cm to 1 cm from the surface doubled the modulation depth due to reduced ohmic loss. Contact angle measurements on a copper block with a micro-orifice showed advancing angles of 78° (DTAB, −0.1 V) versus 92° (−2.0 V), directly correlating hydrophobicity with nucleation enhancement.

These examples collectively establish the invention as a robust, tunable, and scalable solution for active boiling control with transformative potential across thermal engineering disciplines.
