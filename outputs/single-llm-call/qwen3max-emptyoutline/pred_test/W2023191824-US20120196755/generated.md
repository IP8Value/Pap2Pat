## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of molecular biology and genomics, and more specifically to methods for the genome-wide detection, mapping, and prediction of chromosomal double-strand breaks (DSBs) arising from replication stress. The invention further provides novel methodologies for the concurrent or sequential detection of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) regions that precede and predict sites of chromosomal fragility. These methods are particularly applicable to the study of checkpoint-deficient cells, common fragile sites (CFSs), and other genomic contexts where replication fork instability leads to structural chromosomal damage. The disclosed techniques enable high-resolution, quantitative, and reproducible identification of DSBs and ssDNA across entire eukaryotic genomes without reliance on protein markers, immunoprecipitation, or sequencing-based enrichment strategies that may introduce bias or false positives. The invention thus establishes a foundational platform for both basic research into DNA replication and repair mechanisms and applied diagnostics for genomic instability associated with cancer, aging, and genetic disorders.

## BACKGROUND

Genomic integrity is paramount for cellular viability and organismal health. A major threat to this integrity arises during DNA replication, particularly when replication forks encounter obstacles such as nucleotide depletion, DNA lesions, secondary structures, or tightly bound protein complexes. Under normal conditions, the DNA replication checkpoint—orchestrated primarily by the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-related kinases Mec1 (ATR in mammals) and Rad53 (CHK2 in mammals)—monitors fork progression, stabilizes stalled forks, prevents premature origin firing, and coordinates repair processes. In the absence of a functional checkpoint, as occurs in *mec1* or *rad53* mutant cells in yeast or their orthologous deficiencies in higher eukaryotes, replication forks become highly unstable upon exposure to replication stressors like hydroxyurea (HU), which depletes deoxyribonucleotide pools. This instability can lead to the accumulation of extensive regions of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) due to uncoupling of leading and lagging strand synthesis or nucleolytic degradation of nascent DNA. Critically, these ssDNA regions are believed to be precursors to double-strand breaks (DSBs), which, if unrepaired, result in chromosomal rearrangements, loss of heterozygosity, and cell death—hallmarks of genomic instability observed in many human diseases, including cancer.

Despite the central importance of DSBs in genome biology, robust, direct, and genome-wide methods for mapping their precise locations have been limited. Existing approaches suffer from significant drawbacks. For instance, chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by microarray hybridization (ChIP-chip) or sequencing (ChIP-Seq) relies on the stable association of specific proteins—such as Spo11 in meiosis or γ-H2AX in response to DSBs—with break sites. However, in the context of replication stress-induced DSBs, no universally conserved, stably bound protein marker has been identified that reliably marks all break ends. Moreover, γ-H2AX foci can form in the absence of actual DSBs, leading to false positives. Alternative methods based on enriching for ssDNA tails generated by DSB resection, such as those using benzoylated naphthoylated DEAE-cellulose, risk misidentifying internal ssDNA gaps—unrelated to DSBs—as break sites. Additionally, all biochemical methods involving DNA extraction are vulnerable to artifactual DSBs introduced during sample preparation, especially when dealing with fragile, replication-stressed DNA.

Concurrently, while ssDNA is a well-established intermediate in replication stress responses, its genome-wide mapping has also been technically challenging. Traditional methods often require DNA denaturation prior to labeling, which can convert transient or localized ssDNA into artifactual signals or obscure the native state of the genome. Furthermore, distinguishing true ssDNA gaps from nicks or other discontinuities that might serve as primers for DNA polymerases during labeling has been difficult.

Prior work in yeast has shown that *mec1* mutants exposed to HU accumulate ssDNA at replication origins and fail to restart DNA synthesis upon HU removal, leading to chromosome breakage during recovery. However, a direct, causal link between specific ssDNA regions and subsequent DSB locations had not been established at a genome-wide scale due to the lack of compatible, high-fidelity mapping technologies. There existed a critical need for an integrated methodological framework that could (1) minimize in vitro artifacts during DNA handling, (2) directly label DSB ends without reliance on protein intermediates, (3) map ssDNA in its native, non-denatured state, and (4) correlate these two genomic features temporally and spatially to elucidate the mechanistic sequence of events leading from replication stress to chromosomal fragmentation.

The present invention addresses these unmet needs by introducing a novel suite of in-gel enzymatic labeling techniques coupled with high-density microarray hybridization, enabling the first direct, genome-wide demonstration that ssDNA accumulation at stalled replication forks precedes and predicts the sites of chromosomal breakage. This breakthrough not only validates a long-standing hypothesis in DNA replication biology but also provides a powerful predictive tool for identifying genomic regions susceptible to fragility under replication stress—knowledge with profound implications for understanding and diagnosing human genomic disorders.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel methods for the genome-wide detection, mapping, and prediction of chromosomal double-strand breaks (DSBs) resulting from replication stress, particularly in checkpoint-deficient cellular contexts. Central to the invention is a new technique for direct, in-gel labeling of DSB ends using fluorescently tagged nucleotides and a DNA end-repair enzyme mixture, which minimizes artifactual breakage during sample preparation and avoids reliance on protein-based markers or indirect proxies for DSBs. This method is performed by embedding live cells in agarose plugs, lysing them in situ to preserve high-molecular-weight genomic DNA, and then enzymatically labeling free DNA ends within the gel matrix using a reaction mix containing T4 DNA polymerase, polynucleotide kinase, and Cy3- or Cy5-conjugated dUTP. The labeled DNA is subsequently electroeluted, fragmented to a uniform size, and co-hybridized with a differentially labeled control sample to high-density oligonucleotide microarrays, enabling quantitative, genome-wide profiling of DSB frequency and location.

In parallel, the invention discloses an improved method for mapping single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) regions across the genome. This technique also utilizes in-gel processing to prevent in vitro generation of ssDNA, employing random-primed labeling with exonuclease-deficient Klenow fragment or Sequenase in the absence of prior DNA denaturation. By comparing ssDNA profiles from stressed cells (e.g., exposed to hydroxyurea) with control G1-phase cells, the method yields high-resolution maps of ssDNA accumulation with minimal background and artifact.

A key aspect of the invention is the temporal and spatial correlation between ssDNA maps and DSB maps. The inventors have discovered that in checkpoint-deficient (*mec1*) yeast cells subjected to transient hydroxyurea treatment, ssDNA accumulates at replication forks during drug exposure and persists unchanged during recovery in drug-free medium. Crucially, the genomic locations of this persistent ssDNA precisely predict the sites where DSBs subsequently occur during the recovery phase. This predictive relationship holds across multiple experimental conditions, including varying the timing of hydroxyurea addition relative to S-phase entry, thereby demonstrating that it is the position of the stalled replication fork—not the identity of the origin of replication per se—that determines fragility.

Furthermore, the invention extends this principle to endogenous replication stress. In a temperature-sensitive *mec1-4* mutant grown at restrictive temperature without exogenous stressors, ssDNA is detected at known “replication slow zones” (RSZs) during mid-S phase, well before DSBs manifest later in the cell cycle. This confirms that ssDNA formation is a universal precursor to replication stress-induced chromosomal breakage, regardless of whether the stress is externally imposed (e.g., by hydroxyurea) or internally generated (e.g., by defective checkpoint signaling).

The invention thus provides a unified conceptual and methodological framework: ssDNA mapping serves as an early-warning system for genomic fragility, and DSB mapping validates the consequences of unresolved replication stress. These methods are broadly applicable to any eukaryotic system where replication fork collapse leads to DSBs, including mammalian cells, and can be adapted for use with next-generation sequencing platforms. The ability to predict breakage sites from ssDNA profiles offers significant utility in identifying common fragile sites in the human genome, assessing the genotoxicity of chemotherapeutic agents, and developing biomarkers for diseases characterized by replication stress and genomic instability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention encompasses a series of interrelated methodological innovations designed to overcome longstanding limitations in the genome-wide analysis of replication stress-induced chromosomal damage. At its core, the invention introduces two complementary in-gel labeling protocols—one for single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) and one for double-strand breaks (DSBs)—that together enable the direct observation and causal linkage of these two critical genomic features. The methods are executed under conditions that rigorously minimize artifactual DNA damage, ensuring that the observed signals reflect true biological states rather than preparation artifacts.

The process begins with the preparation of intact, high-molecular-weight genomic DNA in a manner that preserves its native conformation. Cells of interest—typically synchronized in G1 phase using mating pheromone (e.g., alpha factor in yeast) and then released into S phase under controlled conditions—are embedded directly into low-melting-point agarose plugs. This embedding step is critical: it immobilizes the cells and their chromosomal DNA within a protective matrix, preventing mechanical shearing and nuclease-mediated degradation that commonly occur during conventional DNA isolation procedures. Following embedding, the cells are spheroplasted in situ using lytic enzymes (e.g., zymolyase for yeast), and proteins are digested with proteinase K, all while the DNA remains entrapped in the agarose. This in-plug lysis ensures that even the most fragile, replication-stressed chromosomes remain largely intact throughout sample preparation.

For ssDNA mapping, the agarose plugs containing purified genomic DNA are subjected to a labeling reaction without any denaturation step. The plugs are first equilibrated in a buffer compatible with DNA polymerase activity, typically containing Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), MgCl₂, and β-mercaptoethanol. A labeling mix is then added directly onto the plug surface, comprising random hexamer primers, deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs) including a reduced concentration of thymidine triphosphate (dTTP), a fluorescently labeled deoxyuridine triphosphate (e.g., Cy3-dUTP or Cy5-dUTP), and an exonuclease-deficient DNA polymerase such as Klenow fragment or Sequenase. The reaction proceeds at 37°C in the dark for approximately two hours. Because the DNA template is not denatured, the polymerase can only extend primers annealed to naturally occurring ssDNA regions, thereby selectively labeling these gaps with the fluorescent nucleotide. The use of exonuclease-deficient polymerases prevents degradation of the template, and the inclusion of Sequenase—which lacks strand displacement activity—confirms that labeling is not occurring at nicks via displacement synthesis but rather at genuine ssDNA stretches.

For DSB mapping, a distinct in-gel end-labeling protocol is employed. After in-plug lysis, the agarose plugs are equilibrated in an end-repair buffer containing Tris-acetate, potassium acetate, magnesium acetate, and dithiothreitol. An end-repair labeling mix is then applied, consisting of the same buffer supplemented with ATP, dNTPs (including fluorescent dUTP), and a commercial end-repair enzyme mixture (e.g., Epicentre’s End-It DNA Repair Kit), which contains T4 DNA polymerase and T4 polynucleotide kinase. T4 DNA polymerase possesses both 5′→3′ polymerase and 3′→5′ exonuclease activities, enabling it to process a wide variety of DNA end structures—including 5′ and 3′ overhangs—into blunt ends while simultaneously incorporating the labeled dUTP. Polynucleotide kinase ensures that any 5′-hydroxyl groups are phosphorylated, although this is not essential for labeling. Critically, this reaction labels only free DNA ends; internal ssDNA regions lacking a terminal 3′-OH or 5′-phosphate are not substrates, as confirmed by control experiments showing minimal labeling in HU-treated *mec1* cells (which contain abundant ssDNA but no DSBs) compared to recovery-phase cells (which contain DSBs).

Following labeling, the fluorescently tagged DNA is electroeluted from the agarose plugs into dialysis tubing with a molecular weight cutoff of 12,000–14,000 Daltons, using 0.5× TBE buffer at 110 volts for three hours in the dark. The eluted DNA is then sonicated to an average fragment size of approximately 500 base pairs using a focused ultrasonicator (e.g., BioRuptor), and purified using a standard PCR cleanup kit to remove unincorporated nucleotides and enzymes.

For both ssDNA and DSB analyses, the experimental sample (e.g., cells exposed to HU or in recovery) is differentially labeled with one fluorophore (e.g., Cy5), while a control sample (e.g., G1-arrested cells with minimal replication-associated DNA structures) is labeled with a second fluorophore (e.g., Cy3). The two labeled DNA populations are mixed in equimolar amounts and co-hybridized to high-density oligonucleotide microarrays (e.g., Agilent Yeast 4x44K ChIP-on-chip arrays) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. After hybridization and washing, the arrays are scanned to obtain fluorescence intensities for each probe.

Data analysis involves several key steps. First, anomalous spots flagged by the feature extraction software are excluded. Then, for each probe, the ratio of background-subtracted experimental signal to control signal is calculated. These raw ratios are normalized to account for differences in total fluorescence between channels, and then smoothed using a Lowess algorithm with a 6-kilobase sliding window to reduce noise and enhance regional trends. The resulting profiles—termed “ssDNA profiles” or “chromosome breakage profiles”—are plotted against chromosomal coordinates, revealing peaks that correspond to regions of elevated ssDNA or DSBs, respectively. Peak calling is performed using established statistical thresholds to identify significant sites.

The power of the invention lies in the integration of these two datasets. By comparing ssDNA profiles obtained during HU exposure with DSB profiles obtained during subsequent recovery, the inventors demonstrated a strong spatial correlation (correlation coefficients ranging from 0.64 to 0.74 across experiments), proving that ssDNA accumulation precedes and predicts breakage. This correlation persists even when replication forks are allowed to progress different distances before encountering HU, confirming that it is the fork position—not the origin identity—that dictates fragility. Moreover, the methods successfully detect known DSBs induced by restriction enzymes (e.g., BamHI) or site-specific endonucleases (e.g., HO), validating their accuracy and sensitivity.

The invention is not limited to yeast. The principles of in-gel DNA preservation, direct end-labeling, and ssDNA-specific labeling are universally applicable to eukaryotic cells. With appropriate adaptations—such as using different synchronization methods, lysis conditions, or microarray/sequencing platforms—the same workflow can be applied to mammalian cells to map common fragile sites, assess the impact of oncogene-induced replication stress, or evaluate the efficacy of anti-cancer drugs that target DNA replication.

In summary, the detailed description of the invention provides a robust, reproducible, and artifact-minimized pipeline for the genome-wide interrogation of replication stress outcomes. By directly linking ssDNA to DSBs, it resolves a fundamental question in DNA metabolism and establishes a predictive paradigm for genomic instability.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the inventors demonstrate the application of the novel in-gel ssDNA and DSB mapping methods to *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* strains deficient in the Mec1 checkpoint kinase. Wild-type (WT) and *mec1-1* mutant cells (strain BY2006) were synchronized in G1 phase using alpha factor. Cells were then released into S phase in the presence of 200 mM hydroxyurea (HU) to induce replication stress. Samples were collected at time zero (G1 control), after 1 hour of HU treatment (HU 1hr), and after 1 hour of recovery in HU-free medium following HU removal (R 1hr).

For ssDNA mapping, cells from each time point were embedded in agarose plugs, spheroplasted, and subjected to in-gel random-primed labeling using exonuclease-deficient Klenow fragment and Cy5-dUTP. A G1-phase sample was labeled with Cy3-dUTP as the control. Labeled DNA was electroeluted, sonicated, purified, and co-hybridized to Agilent yeast microarrays. The resulting ssDNA profile for the HU 1hr sample showed prominent peaks near known origins of replication, consistent with stalled replication forks. Remarkably, the R 1hr ssDNA profile was nearly identical to the HU 1hr profile (correlation coefficient = 0.93), indicating that ssDNA gaps persisted and were not repaired during recovery in the *mec1* mutant.

For DSB mapping, the same samples were processed using the in-gel end-repair labeling method with the End-It enzyme mix and Cy5-dUTP (experimental) versus Cy3-dUTP (G1 control). The HU 1hr sample exhibited a flat breakage profile, confirming the absence of DSBs during HU exposure despite abundant ssDNA. In contrast, the R 1hr sample displayed numerous sharp peaks across all chromosomes, indicating widespread DSB formation during recovery. WT cells under identical conditions showed no significant ssDNA accumulation or DSBs, underscoring the essential role of Mec1 in fork stabilization.

To validate the specificity of the DSB mapping method, the inventors performed control experiments. Genomic DNA from exponentially growing *mec1* cells embedded in agarose plugs was digested in-gel with BamHI restriction enzyme, then labeled and hybridized as above. The resulting breakage profile showed peaks that aligned almost perfectly with known BamHI recognition sites in the yeast genome, with minor discrepancies attributed to strain-specific polymorphisms. Similarly, induction of the HO endonuclease in strain YSCL004 led to clear detection of DSBs at the three engineered HO cut sites on chromosomes II, III, and VI.

Correlation analysis between ssDNA (HU 1hr) and DSB (R 1hr) profiles revealed a significant positive relationship (correlation coefficient = 0.64). When origins were classified as “checked” (late-firing, normally suppressed by the checkpoint) or “unchecked” (early-firing), breakage sites were significantly enriched near checked origins (41.6% within 6 kb) compared to unchecked origins (11.7%; P < 10⁻¹⁵). Random simulation tests confirmed this association (P < 0.0001 for checked origins; P = 0.1851 for unchecked). These results establish that chromosome breakage in *mec1* cells occurs preferentially at forks that originated from late-firing origins, which remain proximal to their origins due to checkpoint failure.

### Example 2

This example extends the findings of Example 1 by testing whether the correlation between ssDNA and DSBs depends on the distance replication forks have traveled before encountering replication stress. *mec1-1* cells were synchronized in G1 and released into S phase. At 0, 5, 10, 15, or 20 minutes post-release (denoted T0, T5, T10, T15, T20), HU was added, and cells were incubated for 1 hour (HU phase), followed by 1 hour of recovery in HU-free medium (R phase). Thus, T0-R represents forks stalled immediately upon S-phase entry, while T20-R represents forks that had progressed ~20 minutes into S phase before stalling.

Contour-clamped homogeneous electric field (CHEF) gel electrophoresis confirmed that all samples experienced chromosome breakage during recovery, as evidenced by smearing and reduced chromosomal band intensity. However, DSB mapping via in-gel end-labeling revealed distinct breakage profiles. The T0-R profile showed concentrated breaks near early-replicating regions and checked origins, whereas the T20-R profile exhibited a more dispersed pattern, with reduced breakage in early regions and increased breaks in mid- and late-replicating domains.

Quantitative analysis showed that breakage sites in T0-R remained correlated with checked origins (P = 0.0032), but this correlation was lost in T20-R (P = 0.8276), as forks had moved beyond origin-proximal zones. Crucially, ssDNA mapping of T0-HU and T20-HU samples showed profiles that closely matched their respective R-phase DSB profiles (correlation coefficients of 0.74 and 0.65, respectively). Moreover, T0-HU ssDNA negatively correlated with T20-R breakage (coefficient = -0.25), indicating that regions protected from breakage in T20-R were those where forks had already passed in T0-HU.

In a final experiment, the inventors examined a temperature-sensitive *mec1-4* mutant (strain RCY378) grown at 37°C (restrictive temperature) without exogenous stressors. Cells were synchronized in G1 and released into S phase. At 40 minutes post-release (mid-S phase), ssDNA mapping revealed elevated signals specifically at replication slow zones (RSZs)—genomic regions known to experience delayed replication and eventual breakage in this mutant. Notably, DSBs in *mec1-4* are not detectable until 4–6 hours post-release, confirming that ssDNA accumulation precedes breakage even in the absence of external replication stressors.

Together, these examples validate the core tenets of the invention: (1) ssDNA forms at stalled forks during replication stress; (2) in checkpoint-deficient cells, this ssDNA persists and leads to DSBs during recovery; (3) the genomic location of ssDNA predicts the site of subsequent breakage; and (4) this predictive relationship holds across diverse replication stress paradigms, establishing ssDNA mapping as a universal tool for forecasting chromosomal fragility.
