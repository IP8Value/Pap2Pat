# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to the field of artificial intelligence and machine learning, and more specifically to systems and methods for improving the efficiency and stability of off-policy reinforcement learning algorithms through enhanced experience replay mechanisms. The invention provides a novel framework for partitioning experience replay buffers into specialized data structures called Event Tables, which store sub-trajectories leading to significant events in the environment, and implements a stratified sampling algorithm that selectively over-samples these event-relevant experiences during training. This approach addresses fundamental limitations in conventional experience replay techniques by creating "fast lanes" for value function backups that accelerate convergence to optimal policies while maintaining theoretical guarantees of optimality. The invention is particularly applicable to complex reinforcement learning domains including but not limited to robotic control systems, autonomous vehicle navigation, game playing agents, industrial process optimization, and other applications where efficient learning from sparse or infrequent critical events is essential for practical deployment.

### 2. Description of Prior Art and Related Information

Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for training intelligent agents to solve complex sequential decision-making problems without explicit programming. Central to many recent breakthroughs in deep reinforcement learning is the technique of Experience Replay (ER), originally introduced by Lin (1992) and later popularized in deep RL contexts by Mnih et al. (2013). Experience Replay involves storing agent-environment interactions in a buffer structure, commonly referred to as an Experience Replay Buffer (ERB), from which training samples are drawn for updating the agent's policy and value functions. This approach decouples data collection from learning, enabling more stable and sample-efficient training compared to purely online methods.

However, conventional Experience Replay implementations typically employ uniform random sampling from the ERB, which becomes increasingly inefficient as the buffer grows large and contains predominantly routine or uninformative transitions. In domains where critical events occur infrequently—such as passing another vehicle in a racing simulator, achieving a goal state in sparse-reward environments, or encountering rare but important failure modes—the probability of sampling these crucial experiences becomes vanishingly small under uniform sampling. This limitation severely constrains the learning speed and ultimate performance of RL agents in practical applications.

To address this issue, Schaul et al. (2016) introduced Prioritized Experience Replay (PER), which skews the sampling distribution toward experiences with higher temporal difference (TD) errors, under the assumption that these represent states where the current value estimates are most inaccurate and thus most valuable for learning. While PER has demonstrated empirical improvements in certain domains, it suffers from several fundamental limitations. First, TD errors reflect discrepancies between current estimates and observed returns under the current behavior policy, not necessarily alignment with the optimal policy. Consequently, PER may prioritize states that are frequently visited by the current (suboptimal) policy but irrelevant to optimal behavior, while neglecting rare but crucial states on optimal trajectories. Second, in stochastic environments, PER can introduce bias by systematically undersampling low-TD-error transitions that may become important as the policy evolves. Third, the prioritization mechanism itself can be unstable and sensitive to hyperparameter choices, sometimes leading to degraded performance compared to uniform sampling.

Alternative approaches to experience prioritization have been explored in the literature. Some methods partition the ERB based on reward magnitude, storing high-reward and low-reward transitions in separate tables and applying stratified sampling (Narasimhan et al., 2015; Sharma et al., 2020). However, these approaches typically store only the immediate transition tuples rather than the contextual history leading to the reward event, missing opportunities for efficient credit assignment across extended sequences. Other works have investigated trajectory-based backup methods that propagate value updates backward from goal states along entire trajectories (Barto et al., 1995; Karimpanal & Bouffanais, 2018), but these can introduce problematic temporal correlations in mini-batches that destabilize learning (de Bruin et al., 2015, 2016).

Topological Experience Replay variants (Hong et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2019) attempt to prioritize backups along trajectories using graph embeddings originating from goal states, but these require discrete state representations or explicit goal specifications that limit their applicability to continuous or goal-free domains. Multi-table approaches have also been employed in specific applications such as simulated race car training (Wurman et al., 2022), where buffers are partitioned based on initial state conditions corresponding to different skill scenarios. However, these static partitions cannot adapt to dynamic event occurrences within episodes and fail to capture the rich temporal structure of experiences leading to significant events.

Related techniques from multi-task and lifelong learning attempt to balance data usage across different tasks or prevent catastrophic forgetting when learning sequential tasks (Isele & Cosgun, 2018; Rolnick et al., 2019; Yin & Pan, 2017). While these share some conceptual similarities with the present invention's ability to maintain multiple skills simultaneously, they are designed for explicitly multi-task settings rather than single-task domains with multiple relevant event types. Hindsight Experience Replay (Andrychowicz et al., 2017) augments the ERB by relabeling goals in multi-task settings, but this requires the ability to define alternative achievable goals for each trajectory, which is not always feasible.

Potential-based reward shaping (Ng et al., 1999; Grzes, 2017) provides another mechanism for incorporating domain knowledge by adding auxiliary rewards that guide exploration without changing the optimal policy. However, while shaping can accelerate exploration toward relevant regions, it does not directly address the sampling inefficiency problem in Experience Replay—shaped rewards still need to be bootstrapped back through potentially long sequences of routine transitions, which remains slow under uniform sampling.

The present invention addresses these limitations through a fundamentally different approach that combines the benefits of event-driven partitioning with trajectory-aware storage and theoretically-grounded sampling strategies. Unlike prior art that focuses on either immediate transitions or complete trajectories, the invention stores finite-length histories leading to specified events, creating what we term "Event Tables" that enable efficient credit assignment while avoiding temporal correlation issues. The accompanying Stratified Sampling from Event Tables (SSET) algorithm provides formal guarantees of improved sample complexity when events are properly aligned with optimal behavior, along with bias correction mechanisms that preserve the Bellman target even when event specifications are imperfect. This represents a significant advancement over existing experience replay techniques by providing both theoretical foundations and practical implementation strategies for dramatically accelerating off-policy reinforcement learning in complex domains.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel system and method for enhancing the efficiency and stability of off-policy reinforcement learning algorithms through the introduction of Event Tables and the Stratified Sampling from Event Tables (SSET) algorithm. The core innovation lies in partitioning the experience replay buffer into specialized data structures that store sub-trajectories leading to significant events in the environment, rather than treating all experiences uniformly or prioritizing based solely on immediate metrics like TD errors. Each Event Table corresponds to a specific event condition defined by domain experts or practitioners, capturing not just the event occurrence itself but also the finite-length history of transitions that preceded it. This design creates "fast lanes" for value function backups that chain together from initial states through intermediate waypoints to final goal states, dramatically accelerating the propagation of value information throughout the state space.

The SSET algorithm implements stratified sampling from these Event Tables according to fixed proportions, ensuring that experiences relevant to important events receive appropriate attention during training while maintaining sufficient coverage of the broader state space through a default buffer. The invention includes a theoretically-derived bias correction mechanism that preserves the Bellman target even when event specifications are imperfect or when sampling introduces distributional shifts, ensuring that the learned policy remains optimal despite the non-uniform sampling strategy. When event conditions are properly aligned with optimal behavior and history lengths are sufficiently long to connect events into coherent chains, the invention provides provable improvements in sample complexity compared to conventional uniform sampling or Prioritized Experience Replay.

The invention is complementary to existing prioritization techniques and can be combined with methods like TD-error-based PER within each Event Table to achieve synergistic benefits—focusing learning on high-value event trajectories while also prioritizing states along those trajectories that exhibit high Bellman error. Similarly, the invention can be integrated with potential-based reward shaping to simultaneously improve exploration guidance and backup efficiency. Empirical validation across diverse domains including MiniGrid environments, continuous control benchmarks (MuJoCo and Lunar Lander), and the highly-realistic Gran Turismo Sport racing simulator demonstrates consistent improvements in learning speed, policy stability, and resistance to catastrophic forgetting compared to baseline approaches.

A key advantage of the invention is its robustness to imperfect event specifications—when events are poorly chosen, performance typically degrades gracefully to match uniform sampling rather than catastrophically failing, making the approach practical for real-world deployment where perfect domain knowledge may be unavailable. The invention also naturally supports multi-skill acquisition and retention by treating each skill-relevant event as a separate table, preventing the interference and forgetting that commonly plague monolithic replay buffers when learning diverse behaviors. The system architecture is designed for efficient implementation using modern experience replay frameworks like Reverb, with minimal additional memory overhead beyond standard buffer structures.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS AND BEST MODE OF INVENTION

### Terminology

The present invention operates within the standard framework of reinforcement learning, specifically addressing the challenges of off-policy learning in episodic Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). An MDP is formally defined as a tuple M = ⟨S, A, R, P, γ, I, β⟩, where S represents the state space, A denotes the action space, R: S × A → Pr[ℝ] specifies the reward function that maps state-action pairs to probability distributions over real-valued rewards, P: S × A → Pr[S] defines the transition kernel that determines the probability distribution over next states given current state-action pairs, γ ∈ [0, 1) is the discount factor that balances immediate versus future rewards, I: Pr[S] represents the initial state distribution from which episodes begin, and β: S → {0, 1} serves as the episode termination function that indicates when an episode should end based on the current state.

At each time step t during interaction with the environment, the reinforcement learning agent employs its current behavior policy π_t: S → Pr[A] to select an action a_t, then observes the resulting reward r_t ∼ R(s_t, a_t) and next state s_{t+1} ∼ P(s_t, a_t). If the termination condition β(s_{t+1}) = 1 is satisfied or a predefined horizon T is reached, the episode concludes and the agent resets according to the initial state distribution I. The fundamental objective of reinforcement learning is to discover an optimal policy π* that maximizes the expected discounted return, which is quantified through the action-value function Q*(s, a) representing the expected cumulative reward obtained by taking action a in state s and thereafter following the optimal policy.

In the context of model-free off-policy reinforcement learning, the agent learns the optimal action-value function Q*(s, a) directly from collected experience without requiring explicit knowledge of the environment dynamics P or reward function R. This learning proceeds through incremental updates based on the temporal difference (TD) error, which measures the discrepancy between the current value estimate and the observed reward plus discounted future value. Specifically, for a current value function estimate Q_k(s, a), the TD error is computed as δ = r(s, a) + γV_k(s') - Q_k(s, a), where V_k(s') = max_{a'} Q_k(s', a') represents the estimated state value of the next state s'. The value function is then updated according to Q_{k+1}(s, a) = (1 - α)Q_k(s, a) + αδ, where α ∈ (0, 1] denotes the learning rate controlling the step size of updates.

In deep reinforcement learning settings, where the state space S is typically continuous and high-dimensional, both the value function and policy are represented using neural networks with learnable parameters θ. To enhance training stability, deep RL algorithms commonly employ fixed target networks for computing the next-state value V_k(s'), with these target networks being updated periodically rather than after every gradient step. Experience Replay (ER) serves as a critical component in these architectures, storing collected experience tuples ⟨s, a, r, s'⟩ in a buffer structure that enables multiple passes over the same data, thereby improving sample efficiency and breaking temporal correlations that can destabilize learning.

The present invention introduces the concept of an event specification (event spec), formally defined as ν = ⟨ω, τ⟩, comprising a Boolean event condition ω: S → {0, 1} and a history length parameter τ. An event is said to occur in state s when the event condition evaluates to true, i.e., ω(s) = 1. The event condition ω is typically specified by domain experts or practitioners based on their understanding of which states are particularly significant for the learning task at hand. Strong candidates for event conditions include terminal goal states that yield high rewards, bottleneck states that must be traversed to reach objectives, or rare but crucial states such as successfully passing another vehicle in a racing scenario. The history length τ determines how many preceding time steps should be stored in the corresponding Event Table when an event occurs, with the requirement that τ be sufficiently long to ensure connectivity between events and initial states, thereby forming coherent chains that facilitate efficient value propagation.

### MiniGrid Experiments

The efficacy of the present invention was rigorously validated through comprehensive experiments in the MiniGrid domain, a suite of grid-world environments specifically designed for testing reinforcement learning algorithms in controlled yet challenging settings. These experiments employed the Double Deep Q-Network (DDQN) algorithm as the base reinforcement learning method, utilizing dense neural network architectures with multiple hidden layers to represent the Q-functions and an ε-greedy behavior policy for data collection. Across all MiniGrid experiments, the invention demonstrated consistent improvements in both sample complexity and learning stability compared to established baselines including uniform experience replay (Uniform ER), reverse sweep update methods inspired by Episodic Backward Update (EBU), and Temporal Difference error-based Prioritized Experience Replay (PER).

In the foundational proof-of-concept experiment conducted in a three-room grid world environment, the agent's observation consisted of its three-dimensional grid position and orientation (x, y, θ), with available actions limited to turn-left, turn-right, and forward movements. The reward structure provided +1 upon reaching a designated green square goal location and -0.1 for all other transitions, creating a sparse reward scenario that challenges conventional learning approaches. The invention was implemented with two event conditions: one triggering at any gap state between adjacent rooms, and another activating upon reaching the green goal square, both configured with a history length of 200 time steps. Results demonstrated that the invention achieved superior performance in terms of both sample efficiency and learning stability, exhibiting significantly lower variance across multiple independent runs compared to competing methods. Notably, the inclusion of intermediate gap events proved crucial for establishing effective "waypoints" that facilitated efficient value propagation from the goal back to initial states, outperforming approaches that relied solely on goal-directed updates.

Comparative analysis against potential-based reward shaping (PBS) revealed complementary strengths between the two techniques. While PBS provides heuristic guidance to accelerate exploration toward relevant regions, it still requires conventional bootstrapping mechanisms to propagate value information back through sequences of transitions. The invention, by contrast, directly addresses this bootstrapping bottleneck by creating dedicated fast lanes for value updates through strategically placed event tables. When combined, PBS and the invention achieved synergistic improvements—shaping guided exploration toward beneficial regions while the invention ensured efficient credit assignment through those regions, resulting in substantially faster convergence than either technique alone.

Robustness testing with poorly designed event conditions confirmed the invention's graceful degradation properties. Even when event specifications were suboptimal—such as triggering near but not at actual bottleneck states, or activating only at initial positions—the invention typically performed comparably to uniform sampling rather than catastrophically failing. This resilience makes the approach particularly suitable for practical applications where perfect domain knowledge may be unavailable. Parameter sensitivity analysis regarding the default buffer sampling probability (η_0) indicated that well-designed event conditions were relatively insensitive to this hyperparameter, while poorly specified events benefited from higher η_0 values that maintained adequate coverage of the general state space.

More complex MiniGrid environments, including obstacle courses with randomized configurations of hazards, keys, doors, and goals, further validated the invention's capabilities. In these challenging scenarios requiring the acquisition of multiple distinct skills (avoiding lava, navigating gaps, opening doors with colored keys), uniform experience replay struggled to maintain balanced skill development, often overfitting to easier subtasks while neglecting more difficult ones. The invention, by contrast, successfully acquired and retained all required skills simultaneously through dedicated event tables for each obstacle category. This capability extends to mitigating catastrophic forgetting during extended training periods, as demonstrated in experiments where agents trained with the invention maintained robust performance when evaluated from perturbed initial conditions, while baseline methods exhibited significant performance degradation over time.

Empirical investigation of design choices regarding event placement and history lengths provided practical guidance for implementation. The results strongly supported the theoretical insight that multiple intermediate events acting as waypoints throughout the environment yield substantially better performance than relying solely on terminal goal events, even when equipped with long history lengths. This finding aligns with the theoretical analysis indicating that waypoint events create more effective fast lanes for value propagation in sparse reward settings. Additionally, experiments with conflict-averse gradient descent (CAGrad) demonstrated that the invention's multi-table structure naturally interfaces with advanced optimization techniques designed to handle conflicting learning objectives across different event categories, suggesting promising directions for further enhancement.

### Lunar Lander and Mujoco Experiments

The present invention was further validated through extensive experimentation on continuous control benchmark tasks from the OpenAI Gym suite, specifically targeting the LunarLanderContinuous-v3 environment and multiple domains from the MuJoCo physics simulation platform. These environments present distinct challenges compared to the discrete MiniGrid domains, featuring continuous state and action spaces, dense reward structures, and in the case of MuJoCo tasks, the absence of explicit goal states. The Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm served as the base reinforcement learning method for these experiments, representing a state-of-the-art approach for continuous control that balances exploration and exploitation through entropy regularization.

In the LunarLander environment, the invention was configured with two event conditions: one triggering when both landing legs made contact within the designated landing zone flags, and another activating when the lander's position approached the center of the flags, both employing a history length of 200 time steps. For the MuJoCo suite, which includes tasks such as HalfCheetah, Hopper, Walker2d, and Ant locomotion, event conditions were defined based on reward thresholds—specifically, events occurred when the agent received rewards exceeding manually selected thresholds that corresponded to meaningful performance milestones in each domain. Each of these reward-threshold events utilized history lengths of 200 time steps, with thresholds carefully calibrated based on the known reward bounds for each environment.

Results across all continuous control domains demonstrated consistent and substantial improvements in both sample efficiency and policy stability when employing the invention compared to uniform experience replay and various configurations of Prioritized Experience Replay (PER). The invention typically achieved comparable or superior final performance in approximately half the number of training epochs required by baseline methods, while exhibiting significantly reduced variance across independent runs. This enhanced stability is particularly valuable in continuous control settings where policy oscillations and catastrophic failures can severely impact practical deployment.

Notably, PER performance in these dense reward environments was generally disappointing, often matching or underperforming uniform sampling, with performance degrading further as the priority exponent increased. This counterintuitive result stems from the volatile nature of TD errors in environments with dense, continuous reward signals—small fluctuations in value estimates can lead to large swings in prioritization, causing the algorithm to chase transient errors rather than focusing on systematic policy improvement. The invention, by contrast, leverages domain-informed event specifications that remain stable across training iterations, providing consistent guidance for efficient learning regardless of the underlying reward density.

Ablation studies examining the sensitivity to history length parameters in the MuJoCo domains reinforced the robustness of the invention to reasonable variations in this hyperparameter. While sufficiently long history lengths generally yielded optimal performance, the invention maintained clear advantages over baseline methods even with suboptimal history length choices. This finding supports the practical recommendation to err on the side of longer history lengths when domain knowledge about optimal trajectory lengths is limited, as the additional contextual information rarely harms performance and often provides valuable credit assignment signals.

The success of the invention in these continuous control benchmarks demonstrates its versatility beyond discrete, goal-oriented environments. By adapting event specifications to reward thresholds rather than explicit goal states, the invention effectively identifies and prioritizes experiences corresponding to meaningful performance milestones, creating fast lanes for value propagation that accelerate learning across the continuous state-action space. This capability makes the invention particularly valuable for real-world applications where reward signals may be dense and continuous rather than sparse and discrete, yet certain performance thresholds remain critically important for efficient learning.

### Simulated Car Racing Experiments

The present invention was subjected to its most rigorous validation in the highly realistic Gran Turismo Sport racing simulator, a PlayStation-based platform that has previously served as a testbed for cutting-edge reinforcement learning research and recently demonstrated superhuman performance in competitive racing scenarios. These experiments focused on two distinct but complementary aspects of autonomous driving: learning complex overtaking maneuvers in opponent-rich environments and maintaining consistent on-track behavior during solo time trials. Both scenarios presented significant challenges that highlighted the invention's unique capabilities in handling real-world complexity, sparse critical events, and the need for robust multi-skill retention.

In the first experiment, conducted on a 1700-meter straightaway section of the Circuit de la Sarthe track using a Red Bull X2019 Competition race car, the invention was tasked with mastering the "slingshot" passing maneuver—a sophisticated racing technique that requires precise positioning behind an opponent vehicle to exploit aerodynamic slipstream effects, followed by timely acceleration to overtake and maintain the lead. The environment featured a built-in AI opponent with randomized starting positions and performance characteristics, creating a dynamic and challenging learning scenario. Two event conditions were defined: a "slipstream" event triggering when the agent's slipstream measurement exceeded a threshold of 0.7, and a "won" event activating upon successful completion of the passing maneuver and maintenance of the lead position at the end of the track segment. Both events employed history lengths of 10 and 15 seconds respectively, with sampling proportions set to 10% each.

Results demonstrated that the invention achieved significantly more consistent learning progress compared to uniform experience replay, with all five experimental replicas showing reliable convergence to successful passing policies. In contrast, uniform sampling exhibited high variance across runs, with some replicas failing to learn effective strategies within the allocated training budget. The invention's ability to consistently identify and prioritize experiences related to slipstream positioning and successful overtakes created efficient learning pathways that accelerated mastery of this complex maneuver. Even when the slipstream event threshold was increased to 0.9—requiring the agent to achieve extremely close proximity to the opponent vehicle early in training—the invention maintained superior performance characteristics, though with increased variance due to the greater exploration challenge.

The second experiment addressed the critical challenge of catastrophic forgetting in extended training scenarios, conducted on the full Lago Maggiore GP track using a Porsche 911 in a solo time trial setting. As training progressed and the agent learned to drive fast laps, off-course incidents became increasingly rare relative to the approximately 1200 time steps per lap, causing conventional experience replay methods to gradually forget the consequences of leaving the track. This manifested as oscillatory behavior in uniform sampling runs, where policies would alternate between consistent on-track performance and risky corner-cutting that resulted in frequent penalties.

To address this challenge, the invention implemented a specialized "re-establish" event that triggered when the agent returned to the track after an off-course excursion, with a history length of 7 seconds designed to capture the complete sub-trajectory of leaving and returning to the course. Despite allocating only 1% of the total buffer capacity to this event table, the invention successfully eliminated the oscillatory behavior observed in baseline methods, achieving consistent on-track performance across all experimental runs. Remarkably, even the worst-performing invention run maintained better on-course consistency than the best uniform sampling run, while achieving comparable lap times. This demonstrates the invention's exceptional capability to maintain multiple skills simultaneously—balancing the primary objective of fast lap times with the secondary but crucial skill of track adherence—through dedicated event tables that prevent the erosion of rarely exercised but important behaviors.

These racing simulator experiments collectively validate the invention's effectiveness in highly complex, realistic domains where traditional reinforcement learning approaches struggle with sparse critical events, multi-skill coordination, and long-term behavioral consistency. The invention's ability to create specialized learning pathways for distinct aspects of driving behavior—overtaking maneuvers, track adherence, and performance optimization—while maintaining theoretical guarantees of optimality represents a significant advancement toward practical deployment of reinforcement learning in safety-critical autonomous systems.

### Recommendations on How to Pick Helpful Events

The successful application of the present invention requires thoughtful specification of event conditions that effectively capture the most relevant aspects of the learning task. Drawing from extensive empirical validation across diverse domains, several practical guidelines emerge for selecting helpful events that maximize the invention's benefits while minimizing implementation complexity. For practitioners with limited domain expertise, a straightforward approach involves defining a "goal" event that triggers upon reaching terminal success states, which has proven effective across numerous discrete environments including the MiniGrid domains. In continuous or goal-free environments such as the MuJoCo suite, reward-threshold events provide a natural alternative—defining events that activate when the agent achieves rewards exceeding meaningful performance milestones. These approaches require minimal domain knowledge while still yielding substantial improvements over baseline methods.

Even when event specifications are imperfect, the invention demonstrates remarkable robustness, typically degrading gracefully to match uniform sampling performance rather than catastrophically failing. This property makes the approach particularly suitable for real-world applications where perfect domain knowledge may be unavailable or where the optimal policy's characteristics are not fully understood during initial implementation. Pathological cases that could potentially harm performance—such as allocating the majority of buffer capacity to completely incorrect event specifications—are easily avoidable in practice through reasonable constraints on event table sizes, such as limiting any single event table to no more than 30% of total buffer capacity.

From an implementation perspective, the invention's memory overhead is minimal compared to standard experience replay buffers. Event Tables require only unsigned integer indices pointing to data already stored in the main buffer, with the additional storage expanding linearly with the number of tables multiplied by their respective sizes. Modern experience replay frameworks such as Reverb already support this data structure efficiently, making integration straightforward for existing reinforcement learning pipelines.

For users seeking to maximize performance through careful event design, several principles emerge from theoretical analysis and empirical validation. Events should ideally correspond to states that are significantly more likely under the optimal policy than under the current behavior policy, as formalized in Definition 4 of the theoretical analysis. Terminal goal states, bottleneck states that must be traversed to reach objectives, and rare but crucial success states all represent strong candidates for event conditions. In complex environments requiring multiple skills, separate event tables for each skill category help prevent interference and forgetting, as demonstrated in the obstacle course and racing simulator experiments.

Looking forward, the question of automatically discovering helpful event conditions represents an intriguing direction for future research. While perfect event specification theoretically requires knowledge of the optimal policy's state distribution, practical approximations may be achievable through subgoal discovery methods or online learning of event weights. However, the current invention's reliance on practitioner-specified events should not be viewed as a limitation but rather as a feature that enables direct incorporation of domain knowledge into the learning process, complementing rather than replacing human expertise in complex decision-making domains.

### Summary

The present invention represents a significant advancement in experience replay methodology for off-policy reinforcement learning, addressing fundamental limitations of conventional approaches through the introduction of Event Tables and the Stratified Sampling from Event Tables (SSET) algorithm. By partitioning the experience replay buffer into specialized data structures that store sub-trajectories leading to significant events, the invention creates efficient "fast lanes" for value function backups that dramatically accelerate convergence to optimal policies while maintaining theoretical guarantees of optimality. The accompanying SSET algorithm implements stratified sampling from these Event Tables according to fixed proportions, ensuring appropriate attention to event-relevant experiences while preserving sufficient coverage of the broader state space through a default buffer.

Extensive empirical validation across diverse domains—including discrete MiniGrid environments, continuous control benchmarks from the MuJoCo suite, and the highly realistic Gran Turismo Sport racing simulator—demonstrates consistent improvements in sample efficiency, learning stability, and resistance to catastrophic forgetting compared to established baselines. The invention proves particularly effective in scenarios involving sparse critical events, multi-skill coordination, and long-term behavioral consistency, where traditional experience replay methods struggle with inefficient sampling and skill interference.

The invention's design incorporates several key innovations that distinguish it from prior art. Unlike methods that prioritize based solely on immediate metrics like TD errors, the invention leverages domain-informed event specifications that remain stable across training iterations and directly align with optimal behavior. Unlike trajectory-based approaches that risk temporal correlation issues, the invention stores finite-length histories while sampling individual steps independently, maintaining training stability. Unlike static partitioning schemes, the invention dynamically maps experiences to tables based on event occurrences within episodes, capturing rich temporal structure without requiring explicit goal specifications or discrete state representations.

Furthermore, the invention demonstrates remarkable robustness to imperfect event specifications, typically degrading gracefully to match uniform sampling performance rather than catastrophically failing. This property, combined with straightforward implementation requirements and minimal memory overhead, makes the approach highly practical for real-world deployment across diverse application domains. The invention also naturally complements existing techniques like TD-error-based PER and potential-based reward shaping, enabling synergistic combinations that leverage the strengths of multiple approaches simultaneously.

The theoretical foundation underlying the invention provides formal guarantees of improved sample complexity when events are properly aligned with optimal behavior and history lengths are sufficiently long to connect events into coherent chains. Even when these ideal conditions are not fully met, the invention's bias correction mechanism preserves the Bellman target, ensuring that the learned policy remains optimal despite non-uniform sampling strategies. This combination of theoretical rigor and practical effectiveness positions the invention as a significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning, with broad applicability to complex decision-making problems across robotics, autonomous systems, industrial optimization, and beyond.

### Definitions, Lemmas, Propositions Used Above

The theoretical foundation of the present invention rests upon a series of formal definitions, lemmas, and propositions that establish sufficient conditions for improved convergence speed and provide mechanisms for bias correction when employing Event Tables and the SSET algorithm. The analysis assumes a finite discrete episodic Markov Decision Process M = ⟨S, A, P, R, T, γ⟩, where S and A represent finite discrete state and action spaces, P denotes the state transition probability matrix, R specifies a bounded reward function with r(s, a) ∈ [0, 1] for all state-action pairs, T represents the fixed episode length, and γ ∈ (0, 1) serves as the discount factor. The agent follows a fixed stochastic behavior policy π_b that ensures every state-action pair can be visited with non-zero probability within the T-step horizon, while π* represents an optimal policy for the MDP.

Central to the theoretical analysis is Definition 1, which formalizes a trajectory Γ_π^{s_i,s_j} as a temporal sequence of transition tuples generated by following policy π from initial state s_i to terminal state s_j. Definition 2 introduces the concept of state density ρ_π,s_0,K: S → [0, 1], representing the probability distribution over states visited when following policy π from initial state s_0 over a finite time horizon K. Building upon this, Definition 3 defines state density disparity ∼ρ_π,s_0,K: S → [-1, 1] as the difference between state densities under the optimal policy π* and an arbitrary policy π, providing a measure of how closely a given policy aligns with optimal behavior across the state space.

Definition 4 formally specifies the conditions for valid event conditions ω: S → {0, 1}. For a given threshold µ ∈ (0, 1), an event condition is considered well-designed if it satisfies either of two criteria: (1) states where the optimal policy's state density exceeds the behavior policy's density by at least a factor of µ, or (2) terminal states visited by the optimal policy. This definition ensures that event conditions capture states that are either significantly more probable under optimal behavior or represent crucial endpoints of successful trajectories.

Definition 5 extends the concept of event conditions to event sections E_ωi, defined as sets of states from which the agent can easily reach event states within the specified history length. This concept is crucial for ensuring connectivity between events and initial states, enabling the formation of coherent fast lanes for value propagation. Definition 6 then formalizes Event Tables B_νi as multisets of transitions from trajectories of maximum length τ_i that all lead to states satisfying the corresponding event condition ω_i.

Lemma 1 quantifies the over-sampling effect achieved through the SSET algorithm, demonstrating that experiences in Event Tables are sampled with probability proportional to (1 - η)^{-m} where m represents a positive constant dependent on the event specification quality and history length. This mathematical characterization provides the foundation for understanding the sample complexity improvements enabled by the invention. Proposition 3 further refines this analysis by showing that m converges asymptotically to ln(τ_iµ) - ln ln(τ_iµ) + o(1) as µ → 0, highlighting the relationship between history length, event quality, and sampling efficiency.

Lemma 2 addresses the potential bias introduced by non-uniform sampling between Event Tables and the default buffer, providing a correction weight that preserves the Bellman target even when event specifications are imperfect. Specifically, the lemma shows that the bias can be corrected through weighted importance sampling, with correction weights computed based on the probability of transitions not being included in any Event Table. This theoretical guarantee ensures that the invention maintains optimality properties regardless of event specification quality.

The main theoretical result, Theorem 1, establishes that when events are correlated with optimal behavior and histories are sufficiently long, the sample complexity for achieving ε-optimal solutions is reduced compared to conventional uniform sampling. The theorem defines S_f as the set of states from which optimal trajectories are contained in the combined Event Buffer with probability greater than p ∈ (0, 1], and shows that under the specified conditions, the convergence bound improves by a factor related to the over-sampling coefficient m. Remark 1 extends this result to show that the speed-up compounds across multiple iterations of target Q-learning, benefiting even transitions not directly stored in Event Tables through the improved value estimates propagated through the fast lanes.

These theoretical results collectively provide a rigorous foundation for the invention's empirical success, demonstrating that the intuitive concept of creating fast lanes for value propagation through strategically placed Event Tables has formal mathematical justification in terms of improved sample complexity and preserved optimality guarantees.
