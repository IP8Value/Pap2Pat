# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of in vitro antibody engineering, particularly to methods and systems for the rapid discovery, selection, and optimization of high-affinity therapeutic antibodies or binding fragments thereof, with a specific focus on single-domain antibodies such as VHH domains or nanobodies. The invention provides a fully integrated, cell-free platform that combines the design and construction of highly diverse synthetic libraries of nanobodies with randomized complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), an optimized ribosome display-based selection process that maintains genotype-phenotype linkage while minimizing background noise and sequence shuffling, and a sophisticated computational framework for the comprehensive identification and ranking of functional binders from post-selection sequencing data. This platform, termed CeVICA (Cell-free VHH Integrated Construction and Analysis), enables the efficient generation of novel, high-performance therapeutic agents against a wide range of targets, including viral antigens such as the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein receptor-binding domain (RBD), without the need for animal immunization or cellular transformation steps. The invention further encompasses the specific nanobody sequences, variants, and multimeric constructs identified and engineered using this platform, which exhibit potent neutralizing activity, high binding affinity, and favorable biophysical properties suitable for therapeutic development.

## BACKGROUND

The development of therapeutic antibodies has revolutionized the treatment of numerous diseases, including cancers, autoimmune disorders, and infectious diseases. Traditional methods for generating monoclonal antibodies rely on the immunization of animals, followed by hybridoma technology to isolate antigen-specific B cells. While effective, these approaches are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and limited by the immune tolerance and repertoire of the host animal. Moreover, the resulting antibodies are typically of murine origin, which can elicit human anti-mouse antibody (HAMA) responses upon administration to patients, necessitating complex humanization procedures.

To overcome these limitations, in vitro display technologies have been developed, which allow for the selection of high-affinity binders from large, diverse libraries in a cell-free or cellular context. Among these, phage display, yeast display, and ribosome display are prominent examples. Phage and yeast display require the transformation of library DNA into bacterial or yeast cells, respectively, which imposes a practical upper limit on library size (typically 10^9 to 10^10 members) due to the inefficiency of cellular transformation. This limitation restricts the diversity of the antibody repertoire that can be explored and may preclude the isolation of rare, high-affinity binders.

Ribosome display, in contrast, is a completely cell-free technology that links the genotype (mRNA) and phenotype (protein) through the formation of stable ribosome-mRNA-protein complexes. In this system, the mRNA lacks a stop codon, causing the ribosome to stall at the 3' end of the transcript, thereby tethering the nascent polypeptide chain to the ribosome and its encoding mRNA. This allows for the direct selection of functional proteins from libraries of theoretically unlimited size (up to 10^14 members), as the library is generated and manipulated entirely in vitro. However, conventional ribosome display protocols have been plagued by several technical challenges, including the instability of the ribosome complexes, the occurrence of non-specific binding, and, critically, the phenomenon of "loop shuffling" or "CDR shuffling," where during the reverse transcription and PCR amplification steps, the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) from different library members can recombine, breaking the crucial genotype-phenotype linkage and leading to the loss of genuine high-affinity binders.

In parallel, the engineering of single-domain antibodies, such as camelid-derived VHH domains or nanobodies, has emerged as a powerful strategy for therapeutic development. Nanobodies possess several advantages over conventional antibodies, including their small size (~15 kDa), high stability, solubility, and the ability to access cryptic epitopes that are inaccessible to larger molecules. Synthetic nanobody libraries have been constructed to bypass the need for animal immunization, but these libraries often incorporate biases based on the analysis of natural nanobody sequences. For instance, previous designs have used amino acid profiles derived from natural repertoires to guide the randomization of CDR positions, under the assumption that these natural profiles represent an optimal or "fit" distribution for functional antibody binding. However, this assumption has not been rigorously tested, and such pre-biased libraries may inadvertently exclude potentially valuable, non-natural amino acid combinations that could confer superior binding properties.

Furthermore, the identification of true binders from the vast output of a selection experiment remains a significant bottleneck. High-throughput sequencing of post-selection libraries yields millions of sequences, but distinguishing genuine, enriched binders from background noise and sequencing errors is non-trivial. Traditional approaches often rely on simple frequency counts or fold-enrichment metrics, which can be misleading and fail to capture the full landscape of binder diversity. There is a critical need for more sophisticated computational methods that can effectively cluster and rank candidate sequences based on their CDR similarity, thereby providing a comprehensive view of the binder repertoire and enabling the rational selection of representatives for downstream characterization.

The present invention addresses these long-standing challenges in the field of in vitro antibody engineering by providing an integrated, cell-free platform that combines a novel, fully randomized nanobody library design with an optimized ribosome display protocol and a powerful CDR-directed clustering algorithm for binder identification. This platform overcomes the limitations of cellular transformation, minimizes the risk of CDR shuffling, and enables the efficient discovery of high-affinity, high-stability nanobodies with potent therapeutic activity, as demonstrated by the successful engineering of SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing agents.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive, integrated platform for the in vitro engineering of therapeutic antibodies, specifically VHH domains or nanobodies, termed CeVICA (Cell-free VHH Integrated Construction and Analysis). The platform comprises three core components: (1) a method for the design and construction of a highly diverse synthetic nanobody library with fully randomized complementarity-determining regions (CDRs); (2) an optimized ribosome display-based selection cycle with built-in mechanisms for background reduction and prevention of CDR shuffling; and (3) a computational approach for the global prediction and ranking of functional binders from post-selection sequencing data through CDR-directed clustering.

In one aspect, the invention provides a synthetic nanobody library wherein the CDRs are fully randomized using NNB codons, which encode all 20 canonical amino acids with a bias towards hydrophobic residues, and where the CDR boundaries and lengths are defined based on a comprehensive analysis of natural nanobody sequences. This library design maximizes sequence diversity without imposing pre-conceived biases based on natural amino acid profiles, and it has been demonstrated that such a fully random design does not incur a substantial binding fitness cost.

In another aspect, the invention provides an optimized ribosome display protocol that includes a pre-selection step to enrich for full-length, in-frame nanobody sequences, thereby reducing the background of unproductive library members. The protocol also employs a specialized PCR strategy during the recovery phase that minimizes the occurrence of loop or CDR shuffling, ensuring the integrity of the genotype-phenotype linkage throughout the selection process.

In a further aspect, the invention provides a computational method for analyzing the output of a selection experiment. This method involves clustering nanobody sequences based on the similarity of their CDR sequences, using a match score derived from a substitution matrix (e.g., BLOSUM62). Each cluster represents a unique binding family, and a representative sequence from each cluster can be selected for downstream characterization. This approach provides a comprehensive view of the binder landscape and dramatically reduces the number of candidates that need to be experimentally validated.

The invention also provides specific nanobody sequences that bind to the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which were identified and engineered using the CeVICA platform. These include monomeric nanobodies with high binding affinity and potent neutralizing activity, as well as dimeric and trimeric constructs that exhibit enhanced neutralization potency. The invention further provides methods for the affinity maturation of these nanobodies, which involve introducing random mutations across the full-length sequence and performing stringent selection to identify beneficial mutations that improve binding and/or neutralization.

In addition, the invention demonstrates that the nanobodies produced by the CeVICA platform possess excellent biophysical properties, including high thermal stability, efficient refolding after denaturation, and a predominantly monomeric state in solution. Notably, the presence of cysteine residues in the CDRs, which are more frequent in the CeVICA library than in natural nanobodies, does not necessarily lead to detrimental aggregation and can even contribute to enhanced functional activity through the formation of disulfide-linked dimers.

Overall, the CeVICA platform represents a powerful, generalizable solution for the rapid, reliable, and scalable generation of high-performance therapeutic antibodies, with significant advantages over existing in vitro and in vivo methods.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

### General Definitions

As used herein, the term “antibody” refers to an immunoglobulin molecule that specifically binds to a target antigen. An antibody can be a full-length antibody, comprising two heavy chains and two light chains, or an antibody fragment that retains the antigen-binding capability of the full-length antibody. Examples of antibody fragments include Fab, Fab', F(ab')2, Fv, and single-chain Fv (scFv) fragments. The term “single-domain antibody” or “nanobody” refers to an antibody fragment that consists of a single variable domain, such as a VHH domain from camelid heavy-chain-only antibodies or a human VH domain engineered to be soluble and stable in the absence of a light chain. The terms “VHH” and “nanobody” are used interchangeably herein.

The term “complementarity-determining region” or “CDR” refers to the hypervariable regions within the variable domain of an antibody that are primarily responsible for antigen recognition and binding. A VHH domain contains three CDRs: CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3. The regions between the CDRs are referred to as “framework regions” or “frames.” The term “randomized CDR” refers to a CDR in which the amino acid sequence at one or more positions is varied according to a defined scheme, such as the use of degenerate codons.

The term “ribosome display” refers to a cell-free in vitro selection technology that links the genotype (mRNA) and phenotype (protein) of a library member through the formation of a stable ternary complex comprising a ribosome, the mRNA from which it was translated, and the nascent polypeptide chain. This is achieved by using an mRNA template that lacks a stop codon, which causes the ribosome to stall at the 3' end of the transcript, thereby tethering the protein to the ribosome and its encoding mRNA.

The term “CeVICA” refers to the integrated platform of the present invention, which stands for “Cell-free VHH Integrated Construction and Analysis.” It encompasses the combined processes of library design, ribosome display selection, and computational binder identification as described herein.

The term “binder” or “binding agent” refers to a molecule, such as a nanobody, that specifically binds to a target antigen with a measurable affinity. The term “neutralizer” or “neutralizing agent” refers to a binder that is capable of inhibiting the biological activity of its target, such as preventing a virus from infecting a host cell.

The term “affinity maturation” refers to the process of improving the binding affinity of an antibody for its target antigen, typically through the introduction of random mutations into the antibody gene followed by selection for variants with enhanced binding properties.

### OVERVIEW

The CeVICA platform is a seamless, cell-free workflow for the engineering of high-affinity therapeutic nanobodies. The process begins with the design and construction of a linear DNA library encoding a vast repertoire of artificial nanobodies. Each nanobody in the library is designed with a conserved framework structure, derived from an analysis of natural nanobody sequences, and three fully randomized CDRs. The DNA library is constructed using a multi-stage PCR and ligation strategy that allows for the controlled, hierarchical randomization of the CDRs, with CDR3 being randomized last to preserve its highest diversity.

This linear DNA library serves as the input for an optimized ribosome display selection cycle. In the first step, the DNA is transcribed in vitro to produce mRNA, which is then translated in a cell-free protein synthesis system. Because the mRNA lacks a stop codon, the resulting ribosome-nascent chain complexes (RNCs) are stable, with the folded nanobody protein tethered to the ribosome and its encoding mRNA. These RNCs are then incubated with an immobilized target antigen. Complexes that bind to the target are retained, while non-binders are washed away. The mRNA from the bound complexes is then recovered and reverse-transcribed into cDNA, which is amplified by PCR to generate a new DNA library for the next round of selection. This cycle is repeated for several rounds to enrich for high-affinity binders.

A key innovation of the CeVICA selection protocol is a pre-selection step to enrich for full-length, in-frame nanobody sequences. Before the final CDR3 randomization, a library with only CDR1 and CDR2 randomized is subjected to a single round of selection against a C-terminal Myc tag. This step effectively removes a large fraction of out-of-frame and truncated sequences, resulting in a final library with a significantly higher proportion of functional, full-length nanobodies. Furthermore, the PCR conditions used for library recovery are carefully optimized to minimize the occurrence of CDR shuffling, a common problem in cell-free systems that can break the genotype-phenotype linkage.

After the final selection round, the enriched DNA library is subjected to high-throughput sequencing to obtain the full-length sequences of the nanobody genes. The raw sequencing data is then processed using a custom computational pipeline. The core of this pipeline is a CDR-directed clustering algorithm. Nanobody sequences are first parsed into their constituent CDRs and frames. Then, a pairwise match score is calculated for the CDRs of every pair of sequences, using a substitution matrix to account for the biochemical similarity of amino acids. Sequences with highly similar CDRs are grouped into clusters, with each cluster representing a unique binding family. This clustering approach transforms a list of tens of thousands of individual sequences into a manageable list of a few hundred clusters, each of which can be represented by a single consensus or most-frequent sequence for downstream characterization.

This integrated approach—combining a maximally diverse library, a robust and faithful selection process, and a smart computational analysis—enables the rapid and comprehensive discovery of high-performance therapeutic nanobodies, as exemplified by the successful engineering of potent SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing agents.

### Therapeutic Antibodies or Binding Fragments of an Antibody

The present invention provides novel therapeutic antibodies, specifically VHH domains or nanobodies, and their binding fragments, that have been engineered using the CeVICA platform. These nanobodies are characterized by their high binding affinity for a target antigen, their potent functional activity (e.g., virus neutralization), and their favorable biophysical properties, including high stability and solubility.

In one embodiment, the invention provides isolated nanobodies that specifically bind to the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. These nanobodies were identified from a CeVICA selection experiment against the SARS-CoV-2 RBD and have been shown to potently neutralize SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus infection in vitro. Exemplary sequences of these RBD-binding nanobodies include, but are not limited to, the sequences designated as SR1, SR2, SR4, SR6, SR8, SR12, and SR38, and their affinity-matured variants such as SR6c3, SR6v15, and SR12c3. The nucleotide and amino acid sequences of these nanobodies are provided in the accompanying sequence listing.

The invention also provides affinity-matured variants of these parent nanobodies. These variants contain one or more amino acid substitutions that have been identified through an error-prone PCR and selection process as being beneficial for enhancing binding affinity and/or neutralizing potency. For example, the variant SR6c3 contains a specific set of mutations that confer significantly improved neutralization activity compared to its parent, SR6. The variant SR6v15, which is a further affinity-matured version of SR6c3, exhibits a binding affinity (K_D) in the low nanomolar range (2.18 nM) and is one of the most potent neutralizers identified in this study.

In another embodiment, the invention provides multimeric constructs of these nanobodies. By linking two or three copies of a monomeric nanobody in tandem, the avidity of the binding agent can be dramatically increased, leading to a substantial enhancement in neutralization potency. For instance, the dimeric construct SR6v15.d was found to have an IC50 of 0.329 nM against SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus, which is more than 10-fold more potent than the monomeric SR6v15. Similarly, a trimeric construct can be generated for even greater avidity effects.

The nanobodies of the present invention can be used for a variety of therapeutic and diagnostic purposes. They can be formulated as pharmaceutical compositions for the prevention or treatment of SARS-CoV-2 infection and COVID-19 disease. They can also be used as research tools to study the structure and function of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, or as components of diagnostic assays to detect the presence of the virus or its variants. The high stability and refolding capability of these nanobodies make them particularly suitable for applications that require robustness, such as inhaled therapeutics or point-of-care diagnostics.

Furthermore, the CeVICA platform is not limited to the SARS-CoV-2 RBD. It is a generalizable technology that can be applied to engineer nanobodies against any target of interest, including other viral antigens, cancer cell surface markers, cytokines, and other disease-relevant proteins. The platform’s ability to rapidly generate a comprehensive map of the binder landscape makes it especially powerful for applications where a large number of diverse binders are needed, such as for targeting multiple epitopes on a single antigen or for developing cross-reactive agents against a panel of related targets.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Design and Construction of the CeVICA Nanobody Library**

A synthetic nanobody library was designed based on a comprehensive analysis of 298 unique camelid nanobody sequences from the Protein Data Bank (PDB298). The analysis revealed four highly conserved framework regions (frame1-4) separated by three hypervariable CDRs. The consensus sequences of the framework regions were used as templates for library construction. The CDRs were fully randomized using NNB degenerate codons (where N is A, T, C, or G, and B is C, G, or T), which encode all 20 amino acids with a slight bias towards hydrophobic residues. The CDR lengths were chosen to match the most common lengths observed in natural nanobodies: 7 amino acids for CDR1, 5 for CDR2, and 6, 9, 10, or 13 for CDR3. The library was constructed in three stages via PCR and ligation, with CDR2 randomized first, followed by CDR1, and finally CDR3, to impose a diversity hierarchy that mirrors the natural order of CDR diversity (CDR3 > CDR1 > CDR2). The final library DNA contained an upstream T7 promoter for in vitro transcription, a 3xMyc tag for selection, and a spacer sequence to enable ribosome display. The library had a full-length diversity of 3.68 × 10^11 per µg of DNA.

**Example 2: Ribosome Display Selection and Background Reduction**

The CeVICA library was subjected to ribosome display selection against two targets: EGFP and the SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD. Before the final CDR3 randomization, a pre-selection step was performed on a library with only CDR1 and CDR2 randomized. This library was displayed and selected against the C-terminal Myc tag. This step enriched for full-length, in-frame sequences, increasing their proportion from 25.3% to 51.9%. The final library, with all three CDRs randomized, was then used for three rounds of selection against the immobilized targets. An optimized PCR protocol was used during library recovery to minimize CDR shuffling. After three rounds, the proportion of stop-free, full-length nanobody sequences in the output library increased by 2.3-fold compared to the input, confirming the successful enrichment of functional binders.

**Example 3: Computational Identification of Binders via CDR-Directed Clustering**

High-throughput sequencing of the post-selection libraries for RBD and EGFP yielded 19,223 and 14,500 unique nanobody sequences, respectively. A custom computational pipeline was used to cluster these sequences based on CDR similarity. A pairwise match score was calculated for CDR1 and CDR2 of each sequence pair using the BLOSUM62 matrix. Sequences with a combined match score above a defined threshold were grouped into clusters. This analysis identified 862 unique clusters for RBD, 71 for EGFP, and 52 shared clusters (background binders). One representative sequence from each of the top 14 RBD clusters (by size) was synthesized and tested. ELISA assays confirmed 11 binders (3 strong, 8 weak), and pseudovirus neutralization assays identified 6 potent neutralizers.

**Example 4: Affinity Maturation of RBD-Binding Nanobodies**

Six RBD-binding nanobodies (SR1, SR2, SR4, SR6, SR8, SR12) were subjected to affinity maturation. Error-prone PCR was used to introduce random mutations across their full-length sequences, creating a mutagenized library with a diversity of 4.18 × 10^10. This library was subjected to three rounds of stringent ribosome display selection against the RBD. Pre- and post-selection libraries were sequenced, and a position-wise amino acid profile was generated for each nanobody family. Putative beneficial mutations were identified as those with a significant increase in frequency in the post-selection population. Multiple variants containing combinations of these mutations were synthesized. Several variants, including SR4t6, SR6c3, and SR12c3, showed improved binding and/or neutralization compared to their parents. SR6c3 was found to be a particularly potent neutralizer.

**Example 5: Biophysical Characterization of CeVICA Nanobodies**

The biophysical properties of several CeVICA-derived nanobodies were evaluated. Size exclusion chromatography showed that >90% of SR12, SR18, and SR6c3 existed as monomers. The impact of CDR cysteines was assessed by non-reducing SDS-PAGE. While some nanobodies with CDR cysteines formed dimers over time, this did not impair their function; in fact, a stored sample of SR6c3 showed enhanced binding and neutralization, which was reversed by a reducing agent, suggesting that disulfide-linked dimers are functional. Thermal stability was assessed by differential scanning fluorimetry, revealing melting temperatures of 72°C for both SR6c3 and SR6v15, which is comparable to natural nanobodies. Remarkably, both nanobodies showed excellent refolding after complete thermal denaturation at 98°C, with SR6c3 even exhibiting a heated/non-heated ELISA ratio >1, indicating a potential gain in function upon refolding.

**Example 6: Engineering of Highly Potent Multimeric Neutralizing Agents**

To further enhance neutralization potency, the high-affinity nanobody SR6v15 was converted into dimeric (SR6v15.d) and trimeric (SR6v15.t) constructs by linking multiple copies in tandem. These constructs were compared to monomeric, dimeric, and trimeric versions of a known neutralizing nanobody, Nb21, in a pseudovirus neutralization assay. The dimeric SR6v15.d was the most potent CeVICA-derived agent, with an IC50 of 0.329 nM, which is comparable to the trimeric Nb21.t (IC50 of 0.244 nM). This demonstrates that the CeVICA platform can be used to engineer not only high-affinity monomers but also highly potent multimeric therapeutic agents.

**Example 7: Discovery of Rare, Variant-Specific Binders from Low-Ranked Clusters**

To validate the comprehensiveness of the CDR-directed clustering approach, 24 additional nanobodies were selected from clusters of varying ranks, including very low-ranked clusters (e.g., SR38 from a cluster of size 5). These nanobodies were tested for binding to wild-type RBD and the N501Y and E484K variants. Nineteen showed positive binding by ELISA, and five showed >20% neutralization at 1 µM for at least one variant. Notably, SR38, from the lowest-ranked cluster, showed strong binding to the N501Y RBD and potent neutralization of the N501Y and E484K pseudoviruses, outperforming two previously described animal-derived nanobodies. This highlights the power of the CeVICA platform to identify rare, high-value binders that would be missed by random sampling or simple frequency-based selection.
