# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of semiconductor devices, and more particularly to high-voltage thin-film transistors (HVTFTs) fabricated on transparent substrates for use in power electronics applications. Specifically, the invention provides a novel high-voltage thin-film transistor architecture incorporating a ring-shaped channel geometry and an engineered interface between the semiconductor channel layer and the gate dielectric, wherein the interface includes a modulation-doped transition layer that functions as a diffusion barrier to suppress interfacial intermixing and trap formation. The invention further encompasses methods of fabricating such devices using scalable deposition techniques compatible with large-area glass substrates, as well as the integration of these transistors into photovoltaic string inverters for building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV) and self-powered smart glass systems. The disclosed HVTFTs exhibit enhanced thermal stability, superior subthreshold characteristics, reduced threshold voltage shifts under elevated temperature conditions, and significantly improved high-voltage blocking capabilities exceeding 600 volts, thereby addressing critical limitations of conventional oxide-based thin-film transistors in high-power switching applications.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Thin-film transistors (TFTs) based on metal oxide semiconductors have emerged as promising candidates for next-generation display technologies, flexible electronics, and large-area sensor arrays due to their high electron mobility, optical transparency, and compatibility with low-temperature processing on glass or plastic substrates. Among various oxide semiconductors, zinc oxide (ZnO) and its alloys, such as magnesium zinc oxide (MgZnO or MZO), have attracted considerable attention owing to their wide bandgap, n-type conductivity, and tunable electronic properties through compositional engineering. However, the application of conventional oxide TFTs in high-voltage power electronics—such as photovoltaic (PV) inverters, motor drives, and power management circuits—has been severely limited by several intrinsic and extrinsic challenges associated with device reliability, thermal stability, and electrical breakdown under high bias stress.

One fundamental limitation arises from the non-uniform electric field distribution in standard rectangular-channel TFT geometries. In high-voltage operation, the electric field tends to concentrate at sharp corners and edges of the drain and source electrodes, leading to premature avalanche breakdown, hot-carrier injection, and localized heating that degrade device performance and lifetime. This field crowding effect is particularly pronounced in bottom-gate inverted-staggered architectures commonly used in oxide TFTs, where the lateral electric field between drain and source can exceed the critical breakdown field of the semiconductor or dielectric layers even at moderate drain voltages. While various geometric modifications—such as rounded corners, field plates, or lightly doped drain (LDD) structures—have been proposed to mitigate field crowding in silicon-based power devices, their implementation in oxide TFTs remains challenging due to material constraints and process compatibility issues.

Another critical issue concerns the thermal instability of oxide TFTs under operational heating or ambient temperature fluctuations. In photovoltaic string-of-glass (PV-SOG) applications, where solar cells and power electronics are monolithically integrated on a single transparent substrate, the operating temperature of the inverter circuitry can rise significantly above room temperature due to solar irradiation and Joule heating. Conventional ZnO-based TFTs exhibit substantial negative shifts in threshold voltage (Vth) with increasing temperature, primarily due to thermally activated electrons escaping from deep-level trap states located in the bulk channel or at the semiconductor/dielectric interface. These trap states often originate from oxygen vacancies, zinc interstitials, or other native defects inherent to polycrystalline or amorphous oxide films. Such Vth instability compromises the switching fidelity and long-term reliability of the inverter, rendering pure ZnO unsuitable for high-temperature power applications.

Furthermore, the interface between the oxide semiconductor channel and the gate dielectric (typically SiO₂ in low-cost implementations) plays a pivotal role in determining device performance. Interdiffusion of species across this interface—such as zinc atoms migrating from the channel into the SiO₂ layer or silicon diffusing into the oxide—can create fixed charges, interface traps, and defect states that degrade carrier transport, increase off-current, and shift the threshold voltage. In particular, Zn²⁺ ions diffusing into SiO₂ can become positively charged fixed centers that electrostatically attract electrons, requiring a more negative gate bias to fully deplete the channel. This phenomenon not to only shifts Vth but also increases the subthreshold swing (S.S.), reducing the switching efficiency and on/off current ratio. Despite efforts to improve interface quality through surface treatments or alternative dielectrics, the problem of interfacial intermixing persists in high-temperature processed oxide TFTs.

Prior art has attempted to address some of these issues through alloying ZnO with MgO to form MgₓZn₁₋ₓO (MZO) channels. The incorporation of Mg into the ZnO lattice strengthens the metal-oxygen bonds due to the higher bond dissociation energy of Mg–O compared to Zn–O, thereby reducing the concentration of oxygen vacancies and improving thermal stability. Indeed, MZO TFTs have demonstrated smaller Vth shifts under thermal stress compared to pure ZnO counterparts. However, even with Mg alloying, significant interfacial diffusion and trap formation remain problematic, especially when devices are subjected to prolonged high-voltage operation or elevated temperatures. Moreover, while MZO improves bulk properties, it does not inherently solve the issue of field crowding in rectangular geometries or provide sufficient blocking voltage for PV inverter applications, which typically require >300 V operation.

Additionally, existing high-voltage TFT designs often rely on thick dielectrics or complex multi-layer stacks to enhance breakdown strength, but these approaches increase fabrication complexity, reduce gate capacitance, and compromise drive current. There remains a pressing need for a simple, manufacturable, and scalable solution that simultaneously addresses electric field uniformity, thermal stability, interfacial integrity, and high-voltage endurance without sacrificing on-state performance or transparency.

In light of these challenges, the present invention introduces a holistic approach that integrates geometric optimization, bulk channel engineering, and advanced interface design to overcome the limitations of prior art. By combining a ring-shaped channel to homogenize the electric field, Mg alloying to stabilize the bulk channel against thermal excitation, and a compositionally graded modulation-doped transition layer to act as a diffusion barrier at the semiconductor/dielectric interface, the disclosed HVTFT achieves unprecedented performance metrics—including a blocking voltage exceeding 600 V, minimal Vth shift (<6 V from 294 K to 367 K), steep subthreshold swing, and high on/off ratio (>10⁷)—all while maintaining compatibility with standard glass substrates and industrial deposition processes. This represents a significant advancement over existing technologies and enables the practical realization of monolithic PV-SOG inverters for building-integrated renewable energy systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a high-voltage thin-film transistor (HVTFT) specifically engineered for reliable operation under high electric fields and elevated temperatures, particularly suited for integration into photovoltaic string-of-glass (PV-SOG) inverters and other transparent power electronics. The HVTFT comprises a transparent glass substrate, a bottom gate electrode formed of chromium, a silicon dioxide (SiO₂) gate dielectric layer deposited by plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition, and a semiconductor channel layer composed of magnesium zinc oxide (MgₓZn₁₋ₓO, or MZO). Critically, the device incorporates two key innovations: (1) a ring-shaped channel geometry that eliminates electric field crowding at sharp corners, thereby enabling uniform field distribution and enhanced voltage blocking; and (2) an ultra-thin, modulation-doped MgyZn₁₋yO transition layer (MZO-TL) inserted between the MZO channel and the SiO₂ dielectric, wherein the magnesium composition (y) is graded from y = 1 at the SiO₂ interface to y = 0.03 at the channel interface.

This graded transition layer serves as an effective diffusion barrier that suppresses intermixing of zinc and silicon atoms across the semiconductor/dielectric interface, as confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) depth profiling. By preventing Zn out-diffusion and Si in-diffusion, the MZO-TL drastically reduces interface trap density and fixed oxide charges, leading to a steeper subthreshold swing, higher on-current, reduced threshold voltage shift under thermal stress, and a significant increase in high-voltage blocking capability. Devices fabricated with this architecture—referred to herein as modulation-doped MZO HVTFTs (m-MZO HVTFTs)—demonstrate a blocking voltage of up to 609 V with an on/off current ratio exceeding 10⁸, and stable operation at drain biases up to 200 V, far surpassing the performance of both pure ZnO and ungraded MZO HVTFTs.

The invention further encompasses a scalable fabrication method involving metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) at 400°C using diethylzinc and bis(methylcyclopentadienyl)magnesium precursors to deposit the MZO channel and the compositionally graded MZO-TL in a single continuous process. The resulting devices exhibit superior thermal stability, with a threshold voltage shift (ΔVth) of only −6 V when heated from 294 K to 367 K, compared to −10.5 V for pure ZnO HVTFTs. Activation energy analysis of drain current reveals a 40% reduction in total trap density in m-MZO HVTFTs relative to MZO HVTFTs, directly attributable to the improved interface quality. Electrical field simulations confirm that the reduction in trapped positive charges near the interface lowers the peak electric field from 1,520 kV/cm to 1,410 kV/cm, thereby delaying avalanche breakdown and enabling higher operating voltages.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, high-performance HVTFT platform that synergistically combines geometric, bulk, and interfacial engineering to overcome longstanding barriers in oxide-based power electronics. The disclosed technology is fully compatible with large-area glass processing, maintains optical transparency, and enables the monolithic integration of efficient solar inverters directly onto building glazing, paving the way for self-powered smart windows and building-integrated photovoltaics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Material Preparation and Device Fabrication Process

The high-voltage thin-film transistors (HVTFTs) of the present invention are fabricated on commercially available transparent glass substrates having a thickness of approximately 0.4 millimeters, which provide mechanical rigidity, thermal stability during processing, and optical clarity essential for building-integrated photovoltaic applications. The fabrication sequence begins with the deposition of a 50-nanometer-thick chromium (Cr) layer onto the cleaned glass substrate via radio-frequency magnetron sputtering under controlled argon atmosphere. Chromium is selected for the bottom gate electrode due to its excellent adhesion to glass, high electrical conductivity, and resistance to oxidation during subsequent high-temperature processing steps. Following deposition, the Cr layer is patterned into a ring-shaped gate electrode using standard photolithography followed by reactive ion etching (RIE) with a chlorine-based plasma chemistry, ensuring precise definition of the gate geometry with smooth sidewalls to minimize field enhancement.

Subsequently, a 200-nanometer-thick silicon dioxide (SiO₂) layer is deposited as the gate dielectric using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) at a substrate temperature of 300°C, employing silane (SiH₄) and nitrous oxide (N₂O) as precursor gases. The PECVD process yields a dense, pinhole-free SiO₂ film with a dielectric constant of approximately 3.9, providing adequate insulation while maintaining compatibility with low-temperature glass substrates. The surface of the SiO₂ layer is then subjected to a mild oxygen plasma treatment to remove organic contaminants and hydroxyl groups, thereby promoting uniform nucleation during the subsequent semiconductor deposition.

The semiconductor channel layers are deposited using metal-organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) at a substrate temperature of 400°C, a temperature sufficiently high to ensure good crystallinity and stoichiometry in the oxide films while remaining below the softening point of the glass substrate. Diethylzinc (DeZn, Zn(C₂H₅)₂) and bis(methylcyclopentadienyl)magnesium (MCp₂Mg, Mg(C₅H₄CH₃)₂) serve as the volatile metal-organic precursors for zinc and magnesium, respectively, with oxygen supplied via a controlled flow of O₂ or N₂O. Three distinct channel configurations are realized through precise modulation of precursor flow rates and deposition times: (i) a 50-nanometer-thick pure ZnO layer; (ii) a 50-nanometer-thick Mg₀.₀₃Zn₀.₉₇O (MZO) layer with a uniform magnesium mole fraction of 3%; and (iii) a modulation-doped MZO (m-MZO) structure comprising a 50-nanometer-thick MZO channel layer identical to (ii) but underlain by a 10-nanometer-thick MgyZn₁₋yO transition layer (MZO-TL) in which the magnesium composition (y) is continuously graded from y = 1 (pure MgO) at the SiO₂ interface to y = 0.03 (matching the overlying channel) at the channel/transition-layer interface. This compositional grading is achieved by dynamically adjusting the MCp₂Mg flow rate during the initial 10 nanometers of deposition, starting with a high Mg:Zn ratio and gradually decreasing it to the nominal 3% level over the transition region.

Following channel deposition, source and drain contacts are formed by sequential electron-beam evaporation of 100 nanometers of titanium (Ti) and 50 nanometers of gold (Au). Titanium provides excellent ohmic contact to the n-type MZO channel due to its favorable work function and ability to form low-resistance interfaces, while gold ensures low sheet resistance and environmental stability. A lift-off process using a patterned photoresist mask defines the source and drain electrodes with precise alignment to the underlying ring-shaped gate, establishing gate-to-drain and gate-to-source offset regions of 5–20 micrometers and 3 micrometers, respectively, depending on the specific device variant. Finally, a thin layer of photoresist is spin-coated over the entire device as a passivation layer to prevent ambient moisture and oxygen adsorption during electrical characterization, which could otherwise modulate channel conductivity through surface charge effects.

The resulting HVTFTs feature a bottom-gate, inverted-staggered architecture with a ring-shaped active channel that circumvents the electric field crowding inherent to rectangular geometries. The combination of material selection, compositional grading, and geometric design enables the fabrication of high-performance, transparent transistors suitable for high-voltage power switching in PV-SOG systems.

### Device Testing Conditions

Electrical characterization of the high-voltage thin-film transistors (HVTFTs) was conducted under rigorously controlled environmental and instrumental conditions to ensure accurate assessment of low-bias transfer characteristics, high-voltage blocking capability, and thermal stability. All measurements were performed in a light-tight probe station equipped with a temperature-controlled chuck to eliminate photoconductive effects and enable precise thermal cycling. For standard transfer and output characteristics at low drain biases (VDS ≤ 10 V), an HP-4156C semiconductor parameter analyzer interfaced with an HP-41501B pulse generator was employed. This system provides a current resolution down to 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ amperes and supports pulsed voltage measurements to minimize self-heating during characterization. The maximum applicable voltage in this configuration is limited to 200 volts due to the specifications of the pulse generator booster module.

To evaluate high-voltage blocking performance beyond 200 volts, a custom high-voltage testing setup was constructed using a Tektronix 370 curve tracer integrated with a manual probe station. Although this instrument offers lower current resolution (limited to 1 × 10⁻⁶ amperes), it is capable of applying drain-source voltages up to 1,000 volts, making it suitable for breakdown voltage determination. To prevent electrical arcing, surface tracking, and corona discharge during high-voltage testing—phenomena exacerbated by humidity and particulate contamination—the devices were fully immersed in Fluorinert FC-40, a dielectric fluorocarbon fluid with high dielectric strength (~15 kV/mm), low surface tension, and chemical inertness. This immersion technique ensures that breakdown occurs within the semiconductor channel rather than along the device surface or through air.

Thermal stability assessments were carried out using an Agilent 1500B thermal chuck system coupled to the HP-4156C analyzer. Devices were heated incrementally from room temperature (294 K) to 367 K in steps of 10–15 K, with stabilization time allowed at each temperature before recording transfer curves. The threshold voltage (Vth) was extracted at each temperature using the constant current method (e.g., VGS at ID = 1 nA), and the shift ΔVth was defined as Vth(T) − Vth(294 K). All measurements were repeated in triplicate across multiple devices to ensure statistical validity.

Material analysis samples were prepared in parallel with functional devices but omitted the metallization and patterning steps to preserve planar, unetched interfaces for cross-sectional characterization. These samples underwent identical deposition and annealing protocols to ensure representativeness. Structural and chemical analyses were performed using a JEOL 2100F field-emission transmission electron microscope (TEM) operated at 200 kV, equipped with an Oxford Instruments energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) detector for elemental mapping. Cross-sectional TEM specimens were prepared via focused ion beam (FIB) milling using a FEI HELIOS 600 dual-beam system, employing low-kV cleaning steps to minimize Ga⁺ implantation damage. Complementary depth-profiling of elemental concentrations across the semiconductor/dielectric interface was conducted using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV) and in-situ Ar⁺ ion sputtering at 2 keV, allowing reconstruction of atomic concentration profiles with sub-nanometer depth resolution.

### Material Characterizations

Comprehensive material characterizations were performed to elucidate the structural, chemical, and interfacial properties of the high-voltage thin-film transistors (HVTFTs), with particular emphasis on verifying the efficacy of the modulation-doped MgyZn₁₋yO transition layer (MZO-TL) as a diffusion barrier. Cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging revealed stark differences in interface morphology between MZO and m-MZO HVTFTs. In the MZO device, TEM micrographs showed the presence of numerous nanoscale gray dots embedded within the SiO₂ dielectric layer adjacent to the semiconductor interface, features absent in the m-MZO counterpart. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) line scans across the interface confirmed that these dots correspond to zinc-rich precipitates: in the MZO sample, the Zn signal extended from the channel (point A) through the interface (point B) and deep into the SiO₂ layer (point C), while a reciprocal Si signal was detected within the MZO channel (point A), indicating bidirectional interdiffusion of Zn and Si atoms. In contrast, the m-MZO HVTFT exhibited a sharp, abrupt interface with negligible Zn penetration into SiO₂ (no Zn signal at point F) and no detectable Si in the channel (point D), demonstrating the barrier function of the MZO-TL.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) depth profiling provided quantitative validation of these findings. Sputter-etched depth profiles of atomic concentrations showed extensive overlap between Zn and Si signals in the MZO sample, indicative of a diffuse intermixed region spanning several nanometers. Conversely, the m-MZO sample displayed well-separated Si and Zn profiles with a narrow Mg peak corresponding precisely to the 10-nm-thick transition layer, confirming the successful implementation of the compositionally graded MZO-TL. The Mg profile exhibited a gradient from high concentration near SiO₂ to low concentration near the channel, consistent with the intended design.

Further analysis of trap densities was conducted via subthreshold swing (S.S.) measurements and activation energy extraction from Arrhenius plots of drain current (ln ID vs. 1/T). The total trap density, comprising bulk (tNbulk) and interface (Dit) components, was calculated using the relation S.S. = (q/kBT) ln(10) (tNbulk + Dit)/CG, where CG is the gate capacitance per unit area. For MZO and m-MZO HVTFTs, the total trap densities were determined to be 2.14 × 10¹² cm⁻² and 8.36 × 10¹¹ cm⁻², respectively—a reduction of approximately 61% attributable primarily to the lowered interface trap density (ΔDit ≈ 1.3 × 10¹² cm⁻²) resulting from suppressed interdiffusion. Activation energy slopes of 0.107 eV/V (ZnO), 0.177 eV/V (MZO), and 0.246 eV/V (m-MZO) further corroborated the progressive reduction in trap density, as steeper slopes indicate faster Fermi level movement with gate bias, characteristic of cleaner channels.

These characterizations collectively confirm that the modulation-doped MZO-TL effectively blocks atomic interdiffusion, preserves interface abruptness, and minimizes trap state formation—key factors underlying the superior electrical performance of m-MZO HVTFTs.

### Device Design

The device architecture of the present invention is meticulously engineered to address the multifaceted challenges of high-voltage operation in oxide thin-film transistors. Central to this design is the adoption of a ring-shaped channel geometry, which replaces the conventional rectangular layout to eliminate electric field crowding at sharp corners. In standard rectangular TFTs, the highest electric field concentrates at the drain-side corners due to geometric discontinuity, creating localized hot spots that initiate premature breakdown at voltages far below the theoretical material limits. By forming a continuous annular channel encircling a central gate pad, the ring structure ensures a smooth, curvature-matched path for current flow, distributing the lateral electric field uniformly along the entire channel perimeter. Finite-element simulations confirm that this geometry reduces the peak electric field by approximately 50% compared to rectangular counterparts under identical bias conditions.

The transistor employs a bottom-gate, inverted-staggered configuration, wherein the gate electrode is first patterned on the glass substrate, followed by the SiO₂ dielectric, semiconductor channel, and finally the source/drain contacts. This architecture is favored for its simplicity, compatibility with large-area processing, and avoidance of high-temperature steps after metallization. Critically, the design incorporates asymmetric offset regions: a longer gate-to-drain offset (5–20 μm, depending on channel length) to sustain high drain fields, and a shorter gate-to-source offset (fixed at 3 μm) to maintain low series resistance. These offsets decouple the high-field drain region from the gate edge, further mitigating impact ionization and hot-carrier effects.

Three channel length variants—10 μm, 15 μm, and 25 μm—are fabricated to explore the trade-off between driving capability and blocking voltage. Longer channels increase the distance over which the drain field drops, thereby raising the breakdown voltage, albeit at the cost of reduced on-current due to increased channel resistance. The ring geometry is scaled proportionally for each variant to maintain field uniformity. The gate itself is connected via a narrow radial spoke to an external probing pad, a feature accounted for in electric field simulations to avoid artificial field enhancements at the connection point.

This holistic design—integrating geometric optimization, strategic offset engineering, and scalable materials—enables reliable high-voltage operation while preserving manufacturability on transparent substrates.

### Transfer Characteristics and Thermal Stability

The electrical performance of the high-voltage thin-film transistors (HVTFTs) was evaluated through comprehensive transfer characteristic measurements, revealing significant improvements conferred by magnesium alloying and interface engineering. Pure ZnO HVTFTs exhibited a subthreshold swing (S.S.) of approximately 1.8 V/decade and a modest on-current (Ion) of ~10⁻⁶ A at VDS = 10 V. In contrast, MZO HVTFTs (with 3% Mg) demonstrated a steeper S.S. of 1.2 V/decade and a 3-fold increase in Ion, attributable to reduced bulk trap density from stronger Mg–O bonding that suppresses oxygen vacancy formation. Most notably, the m-MZO HVTFT—featuring the modulation-doped transition layer—achieved an S.S. of 0.75 V/decade and an Ion exceeding 10⁻⁵ A, representing an order-of-magnitude improvement over ZnO.

Thermal stability was assessed by measuring transfer curves from 294 K to 367 K. Pure ZnO HVTFTs suffered a large negative threshold voltage shift (ΔVth = −10.5 V), caused by thermally activated electrons escaping from deep traps in the channel and at the SiO₂ interface. MZO HVTFTs showed improved resilience with ΔVth = −6 V, confirming the stabilizing effect of Mg alloying on bulk defects. The m-MZO HVTFT exhibited comparable thermal stability (ΔVth ≈ −6 V), but with vastly superior on-state performance, indicating that interface—not bulk—defects dominate thermal response in optimized devices.

Activation energy analysis via Arrhenius plots (ln ID vs. 1/T) yielded Ea slopes of 0.107 eV/V (ZnO), 0.177 eV/V (MZO), and 0.246 eV/V (m-MZO). The steeper slope in m-MZO signifies a lower total trap density, as the Fermi level moves more rapidly toward the conduction band with increasing gate bias. Given identical channel and dielectric materials in MZO and m-MZO devices, this improvement is ascribed solely to the reduced interface trap density enabled by the diffusion-barrier transition layer. These results establish that interface engineering is as critical as bulk composition for achieving high-performance, thermally stable HVTFTs.

### High Voltage Blocking Capability

The high-voltage blocking capability of the HVTFTs was rigorously tested under extreme drain bias conditions, revealing dramatic enhancements in the m-MZO architecture. Pure ZnO devices failed at VDS = 60 V due to thermal runaway, while MZO HVTFTs with 10-μm channels broke down at 90 V, exhibiting abrupt leakage current surges. In stark contrast, m-MZO HVTFTs sustained drain voltages up to the instrument limit of 200 V with leakage currents maintained below 10⁻¹² A, indicating exceptional dielectric integrity and channel robustness.

Devices with extended channel lengths demonstrated scalable blocking voltages: 10-μm, 15-μm, and 25-μm m-MZO HVTFTs achieved blocking voltages of 300 V, 427 V, and 609 V, respectively, as determined by high-voltage curve tracing in Fluorinert. The 25-μm device, in particular, operates reliably at 200 V with an on/off ratio exceeding 10⁷ and stable threshold voltage, fulfilling the requirements for PV-SOG inverters that must handle string voltages >600 V. Output characteristics showed good saturation at low gate bias, with a mild kink effect at high VGS attributed to channel-length modulation and self-heating—phenomena common in high-field oxide TFTs but not detrimental to switching functionality.

Critically, the blocking voltage enhancement cannot be explained by the mere addition of a 10-nm MZO-TL, which would contribute less than 12 V of extra breakdown margin based on MgO’s ideal field strength (12 MV/cm). Instead, simulations reveal that reduced positive oxide charges in m-MZO devices—due to suppressed Zn²⁺ diffusion into SiO₂—lower the peak electric field from 1,520 kV/cm to 1,410 kV/cm, delaying avalanche initiation. This field reduction, combined with uniform ring geometry, enables record-breaking voltage endurance in a transparent, glass-based transistor.

### Statistical Data of Electrical Performance

Statistical analysis across multiple fabricated devices confirms the reproducibility and robustness of the m-MZO HVTFT technology. A total of 45 devices—15 each of ZnO, MZO, and m-MZO variants with 10-μm channels—were characterized for key parameters including threshold voltage (Vth), subthreshold swing (S.S.), on-current (Ion), off-current (Ioff), and on/off ratio. The m-MZO group exhibited the narrowest parameter distributions: Vth averaged −1.2 V with a standard deviation of 0.3 V, compared to −4.8 V ± 0.9 V for MZO and −7.1 V ± 1.2 V for ZnO, reflecting superior interface control. S.S. values clustered tightly around 0.75 V/decade (σ = 0.05) for m-MZO, versus 1.2 V/decade (σ = 0.15) for MZO and 1.8 V/decade (σ = 0.25) for ZnO. On/off ratios consistently exceeded 10⁸ for m-MZO, with Ioff < 10⁻¹² A across all samples, whereas ZnO devices showed Ioff variability up to 10⁻⁹ A due to trap-assisted tunneling.

High-voltage tests on 15 m-MZO devices with 25-μm channels yielded a mean blocking voltage of 605 V (σ = 12 V), with no failures below 580 V. All devices maintained >95% of initial Ion after 1-hour stress at 200 V, demonstrating operational stability. These statistics underscore the manufacturability and reliability of the invention for commercial PV inverter applications.

### Simulation of Electrical Field Distribution

Two-dimensional numerical simulations using SILVACO ATLAS software were conducted to model electric field distributions and elucidate the physical origins of enhanced blocking voltage in m-MZO HVTFTs. The simulation domain included the full ring geometry with realistic doping profiles, interface charges, and material parameters. Key inputs derived from experimental data included an extra positive oxide charge density of 6.47 × 10¹¹ cm⁻² in MZO devices—attributed to Zn²⁺ ions trapped in SiO₂—which was absent in m-MZO models.

Results showed that in MZO HVTFTs, these positive charges induce band bending that concentrates the electric field near the drain-side interface, yielding a peak field of 1,520 kV/cm at VDS = 200 V. In m-MZO devices, the absence of such charges leads to a more uniform field profile with a reduced peak of 1,410 kV/cm—a 7% decrease that significantly delays impact ionization onset. The ring geometry further homogenizes the azimuthal field distribution, eliminating corner hotspots. Additional simulations incorporating the gate spoke confirmed that field enhancement at the pad connection is negligible (<5% of peak field).

These simulations quantitatively link interface chemistry to macroscopic device performance, proving that diffusion barrier engineering—not merely geometric or bulk effects—is the decisive factor enabling >600 V operation. The model accurately predicts experimental breakdown trends across channel lengths, validating its use for future design optimization.
