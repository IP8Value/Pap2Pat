# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of prosthetic devices, and more specifically to advanced sensory systems for upper limb prostheses. The invention provides a novel multi-modal prosthetic fingertip sensor capable of simultaneously detecting proximity, contact, and force magnitude, while also enabling classification of the spatial location and angular orientation of applied forces. This sensor is particularly suited for integration into myoelectric prosthetic hands to enable closed-loop sensory feedback systems that restore tactile and proprioceptive information to upper limb amputees. The technical field encompasses the intersection of biomedical engineering, neural interfaces, microelectromechanical systems (MEMS), and machine learning for sensor fusion applications in prosthetic rehabilitation. The invention addresses critical limitations in current prosthetic technology by providing a compact, robust, and information-rich sensing solution that can interface with peripheral nerve stimulation systems to deliver physiologically appropriate sensory feedback to users of prosthetic limbs.

The invention specifically pertains to the design and implementation of integrated sensor systems within prosthetic fingertips that combine infrared (IR) proximity sensing with barometric pressure measurement to create a comprehensive tactile sensing platform. This multi-modal approach enables the detection of object proximity before contact occurs, precise determination of initial contact events even at near-zero force levels, accurate measurement of force magnitudes across a wide dynamic range from 0 to 50 Newtons, and sophisticated spatial localization capabilities that can distinguish between multiple contact locations and angles of incidence on the fingertip surface. The technical field further extends to the methodologies for processing and fusing data from multiple sensor modalities using advanced computational techniques including Gaussian process regression and supervised machine learning algorithms to extract meaningful information about environmental interactions. The invention represents a significant advancement in the field of prosthetic sensory restoration by providing a practical, manufacturable, and clinically relevant solution that bridges the gap between sophisticated robotic sensing capabilities and the stringent requirements of prosthetic device integration, including considerations for durability, power consumption, communication protocols, and compatibility with existing prosthetic control architectures.

## BACKGROUND

The development of upper limb prosthetic devices has witnessed remarkable progress over recent decades, with modern prosthetic hands now capable of incorporating up to six independent actuators, achieving closing speeds of less than one second, and generating grip forces as high as 100 Newtons, thereby enabling effective performance of everyday activities. Concurrently, advances in myoelectric control algorithms have significantly improved the intuitiveness and functionality of these devices, moving beyond traditional direct control methods to more sophisticated feedforward control paradigms that are increasingly being adopted in clinical practice through various commercial partnerships. Despite these substantial advancements in prosthetic actuation and control, a critical deficiency remains in the field: the lack of effective sensory feedback systems that can restore somatosensory information to the user. Current prosthetic hands are essentially "numb," providing no direct awareness of tactile interactions between the prosthetic hand and the environment, which represents a fundamental limitation given the well-established importance of somatosensory information for effective grasping and manipulation tasks.

The absence of sensory feedback in prosthetic devices creates significant challenges for users, who must rely entirely on visual monitoring to assess grip strength, object contact, and manipulation success. This visual dependency not only increases cognitive load but also limits the ability to perform tasks requiring fine motor control or when visual access is restricted. Research has consistently demonstrated that the restoration of sensory feedback can dramatically improve prosthetic function, leading to enhanced object discrimination, improved manipulation capabilities, reduced cognitive burden, and increased sense of embodiment of the prosthetic device. The most promising approaches to sensory restoration involve interfacing with the peripheral nervous system, which serves as a natural portal to the afferent pathways that normally convey sensory information from the hand to the brain. The peripheral nervous system exhibits somatotopic organization, where nerve fascicles innervating specific areas of the hand form distinct clusters within the nerve trunk, enabling empirical mapping of electrode sites in peripheral nerve implants to specific skin regions of the hand.

Several neural interface technologies have shown promise in human trials for sensory restoration. Transversal multichannel intrafascicular electrodes have demonstrated the ability to distinguish different force levels in upper limb amputees, though long-term stability remains a concern. The Utah Slant Electrode Array, a well-established device for both recording and stimulating peripheral nerves, has been successfully implanted in upper limb amputees for periods up to 30 days, eliciting over 80 distinct sensory percepts and enabling feedforward control of virtual robotic hands. The flat interface nerve electrode (FINE) represents the most mature peripheral neural interface technology, allowing subjects to discriminate and match sensation intensity, recognize changes in stimulation magnitude, and demonstrate improved object discrimination, manipulation, and embodiment when provided with sensory restoration. However, these remarkable achievements have been limited by the simplicity of the sensors used to generate the feedback signals. Most studies have employed basic force-sensitive resistors, such as Tekscan FlexiForce A201 sensors, which can only measure normal loads within a limited range of 0 to 4 Newtons and provide no information about the spatial location or angular orientation of applied forces.

The limitations of current prosthetic sensors extend beyond their inability to provide spatial information. Most commercially available prosthetic hands and research prototypes lack comprehensive sensor suites that can provide both proprioceptive information (digit position) and tactile information (forces applied to digits), which are essential components of any closed-loop prosthetic limb system. While numerous tactile sensors have been developed in both robotics and prosthetics literature, significant barriers remain to their integration into self-contained prosthetic hands. These barriers include compatibility with digital communication systems, multiplexing capabilities for multiple sensors, and practical wiring solutions throughout the device. Critically, none of the existing proximity sensors can reliably classify both spatial position and angular orientation of forces, which is essential for ensuring that sensory restoration can occur reliably and repeatedly even when external forces are applied off-center or at non-normal angles to the fingertip surface. During natural grasping tasks, digits conform around objects using various grasp postures, resulting in forces that vary significantly in both spatial location and angular orientation relative to the fingertip surface. A reliable tactile sensor must be capable of detecting these off-centered and non-normal loads to provide relevant sensory information back to the user.

Previous attempts to address these limitations have included various sensor technologies, from early proximity sensors for robot arm control to more recent implementations for pre-grasp alignment, reactive grasping, point-cloud construction, slip detection, and learning sequential manipulation tasks. However, these solutions have generally failed to achieve the necessary combination of compactness, robustness, multi-modality, and information richness required for practical prosthetic applications. The challenge lies in developing a sensor system that can provide comprehensive tactile information while meeting the stringent requirements of prosthetic device integration, including durability under daily use conditions, compatibility with existing prosthetic control architectures, minimal power consumption, and reliable communication protocols. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by providing a novel multi-modal prosthetic fingertip sensor that combines infrared proximity sensing with barometric pressure measurement in a compact, robust package that can be seamlessly integrated into existing prosthetic hand designs.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel multi-modal prosthetic fingertip sensor system that integrates an infrared (IR) emitter-detector pair with a microelectromechanical system (MEMS)-based barometric pressure sensor to create a comprehensive tactile sensing platform capable of detecting proximity, contact, and force magnitude while simultaneously enabling classification of spatial location and angular orientation of applied forces. The sensor system is housed within a custom-designed prosthetic fingertip that incorporates a cavity for sensor placement and is overmolded with an elastomeric material to create a durable, biocompatible contact surface. The invention leverages the complementary characteristics of the two sensor modalities: the IR proximity sensor provides high sensitivity to near-zero force contact events and proximity information, while the barometric pressure sensor delivers linear force measurements across a wide dynamic range from 0 to 50 Newtons. Through sophisticated sensor fusion techniques, the system overcomes the individual limitations of each sensor type, resulting in a robust, reliable, and information-rich sensing solution specifically designed for prosthetic applications.

The invention comprises a printed circuit board (PCB) assembly containing the IR proximity sensor and barometric pressure sensor arranged along the midline of the prosthetic fingertip, with standard I²C communication protocol ensuring stable and reliable data transmission to the prosthetic hand controller. The elastomeric overmolding, preferably fabricated from a liquid silicon polymer such as Dragon Skin 10, provides mechanical robustness while maintaining sensitivity to external forces and enabling the transmission of both optical and pressure signals to the underlying sensors. The system's multi-modality enables detection of object proximity before contact occurs, precise determination of initial contact events even at force levels approaching zero Newtons, accurate measurement of force magnitudes across the full operational range of prosthetic hands, and sophisticated spatial localization capabilities that can distinguish between five distinct spatial locations and three different angles of incidence on the fingertip surface.

A key aspect of the invention is the implementation of advanced sensor fusion algorithms that combine the raw signals from both sensor modalities to produce calibrated force measurements and spatial classification results. The invention employs Gaussian process regression to fuse the IR and barometric sensor readings into a single calibrated force signal with high accuracy (R² = 0.99), effectively compensating for the nonlinear response characteristics of the IR sensor and the limited sensitivity of the barometric sensor at very low force levels. Additionally, the invention implements supervised machine learning algorithms, including support vector machines (SVM) and convolutional neural networks (CNN), to classify the spatial location and angular orientation of applied forces with high accuracy (96% and 89% respectively for spatial location and angular orientation using SVM classifiers).

The invention addresses critical limitations in current prosthetic technology by providing a practical solution that bridges the gap between sophisticated robotic sensing capabilities and the stringent requirements of prosthetic device integration. The compact design, robust construction, and standard communication protocols ensure compatibility with existing prosthetic hand platforms, while the rich sensory information enables advanced control paradigms including shared control systems and neural interface integration for sensory restoration. The proximity sensing capability enables grasp planning and pre-grasp alignment, while the comprehensive force and spatial information provides the necessary feedback for closed-loop control systems and physiologically appropriate sensory feedback through peripheral nerve stimulation. The invention represents a significant advancement in prosthetic sensory restoration by providing a manufacturable, clinically relevant solution that can dramatically improve prosthetic function and user experience.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive multi-modal prosthetic fingertip sensor system that addresses the critical need for advanced sensory feedback in upper limb prosthetic devices. The system architecture comprises several key components working in concert to deliver rich tactile information while maintaining compatibility with existing prosthetic hand platforms and neural interface technologies. The core of the invention lies in the strategic integration of two complementary sensor modalities—an infrared (IR) proximity sensor and a MEMS-based barometric pressure sensor—within a custom-designed prosthetic fingertip structure that optimizes both mechanical performance and sensory capabilities.

The physical construction of the prosthetic fingertip begins with the design of a custom printed circuit board (PCB) that houses the primary sensor components. The PCB is specifically engineered to accommodate both the IR proximity sensor (preferably a VCNL4010 integrated circuit) and the barometric pressure sensor (preferably an MS5637-02BA03 MEMS device) along the midline of the fingertip structure. This arrangement ensures optimal coverage of the fingertip surface while maintaining a compact profile suitable for integration into existing prosthetic hand designs. The choice of these specific sensor components is driven by their integration of auxiliary circuitry—including instrumentation amplifiers, analog-to-digital converters, and standard bus interfaces—into small packages that minimize the overall footprint and complexity of the sensor system. Both sensors communicate via the I²C protocol, which provides a standardized, reliable communication interface that can be easily integrated with existing prosthetic hand controllers and microcontroller systems.

The prosthetic fingertip body is designed based on established prosthetic hand geometries, such as the Bebionic v2 hand manufactured by RSL Steeper, to ensure compatibility with existing prosthetic platforms. The fingertip body incorporates a precisely dimensioned cavity that accommodates the sensor PCB while maintaining structural integrity and aesthetic consistency with the original prosthetic design. The cavity is positioned to optimize the sensing area coverage while protecting the electronic components from mechanical damage during normal use. The fingertip bodies are initially prototyped using standard three-dimensional (3D) printing techniques, which allows for rapid iteration and customization based on specific prosthetic hand requirements or user preferences.

The critical innovation in the physical construction lies in the overmolding process that encapsulates the sensor PCB within an elastomeric material. The preferred elastomer is a liquid silicon polymer such as Dragon Skin 10, selected for its optimal combination of low viscosity during the molding process and mechanical robustness after curing. The low viscosity ensures complete filling of the mold cavity and intimate contact between the elastomer and sensor components, while the cured material provides excellent durability, flexibility, and biocompatibility for prosthetic applications. The overmolding process involves creating a custom mold that precisely matches the external geometry of the prosthetic fingertip while accommodating the internal sensor cavity. Before pouring the liquid silicon into the mold, a vacuum is applied to completely remove air bubbles from the polymer mixture, ensuring uniform material properties and eliminating potential voids that could compromise sensor performance or mechanical integrity.

The elastomeric overmolding serves multiple critical functions in the sensor system. First, it provides a durable, biocompatible interface between the prosthetic hand and the external environment, protecting the underlying electronic components from moisture, dust, and mechanical damage. Second, it acts as a compliant medium that transmits external forces to the underlying barometric pressure sensor while maintaining the optical path required for the IR proximity sensor to function effectively. Third, the elastic properties of the material enable the detection of both normal and shear forces through the deformation patterns that develop during contact events, which contributes to the system's ability to classify spatial location and angular orientation of applied forces. The thickness and mechanical properties of the elastomer are carefully optimized to balance sensitivity requirements with durability considerations, ensuring reliable performance across the full operational range of the prosthetic hand.

The electronic architecture of the sensor system includes additional components to facilitate data acquisition, processing, and communication. A multiplexing PCB is designed to handle the I²C signals from multiple sensors (two signals per finger, with capacity for up to ten signals in total for a complete hand system) and interface with a host computer or prosthetic hand controller. The preferred implementation utilizes an Arduino microcontroller platform, which provides sufficient processing power for real-time sensor data processing while maintaining compatibility with existing prosthetic control systems. The Arduino firmware performs several critical functions: it calculates proximity values from the raw IR sensor data, implements calibration and temperature compensation algorithms for the barometric pressure sensor (using manufacturer-provided algorithms), and manages the communication protocol between the sensors and the host system.

The data processing pipeline begins with raw signal acquisition from both sensor modalities. The IR proximity sensor provides continuous measurements of the distance to nearby objects based on the reflection of infrared light, while the barometric pressure sensor measures the internal pressure within the sealed fingertip cavity, which changes in response to external forces compressing the elastomeric material. The raw signals undergo initial preprocessing to remove noise and artifacts, including application of low-pass filters to eliminate high-frequency interference and high-pass filters to isolate contact events from the IR proximity signal. The processed signals are then transmitted to the host system via serial USB interface or other appropriate communication protocols, where they can be visualized in real-time, stored for offline analysis, or used directly for prosthetic control applications.

The sensor fusion methodology represents a critical aspect of the invention's functionality. The complementary nature of the two sensor modalities—IR proximity sensing providing high sensitivity at very low force levels and barometric pressure sensing providing linear response across the full force range—enables the creation of a comprehensive force measurement system that overcomes the limitations of either sensor type alone. The invention employs Gaussian process regression as the primary sensor fusion technique, which provides a non-parametric approach to modeling the complex relationship between the raw sensor readings and actual applied force. The Gaussian process is defined by a mean function and a covariance function (kernel function), with the radial basis function (RBF) kernel proving particularly effective for this application. The training process involves presenting the Gaussian process with input data comprising concatenated raw IR and barometric sensor values, along with corresponding target force measurements obtained from a calibrated external load cell. After normalization of all values, the Gaussian process learns the underlying relationships in the data and can then predict force values for new input combinations with high accuracy (R² = 0.99).

For spatial localization and angular orientation classification, the invention implements supervised machine learning algorithms that treat these as distinct classification problems. The spatial location classification problem involves distinguishing between five different contact locations on the fingertip surface: center, 2.5 mm distally, 2.5 mm proximally, 2.5 mm medially, and 2.5 mm laterally. The angular orientation classification problem involves distinguishing between three different angles of incidence: 0°, +20°, and -20° relative to the normal vector of the sensor PCB. Both classification problems are addressed using support vector machine (SVM) classifiers and convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures, with the SVM approach demonstrating superior performance due to the relatively small dataset size and the risk of overfitting with more complex neural network models.

The feature extraction process for the SVM classifiers involves identifying key characteristics from the raw sensor signals that correlate with spatial location and angular orientation. The most effective feature identified is the ratio of IR sensor values to barometric sensor values, which captures the differential response characteristics of the two sensor modalities to off-center and angled loading conditions. Additional features include the maximum and minimum force values recorded during each contact event, as well as temporal characteristics of the loading and unloading cycles. The SVM classifiers are implemented with appropriate kernel functions (polynomial kernel for angular orientation classification and RBF kernel for spatial location classification) and optimized penalty factors (C = 1 for angular orientation, C = 8 for spatial location) determined through experimental validation.

The CNN architecture provides an alternative approach that processes raw sensor data directly without requiring manual feature engineering. The network consists of two 2D convolution layers followed by a flattened layer and a dense output layer with softmax activation. For spatial location classification, the output layer contains five neurons corresponding to the five possible locations, while for angular orientation classification, the output layer contains three neurons corresponding to the three possible angles. The convolution layers automatically learn relevant features from the raw sensor data, capturing the complex spatiotemporal patterns that emerge during contact events at different locations and angles.

The experimental characterization of the sensor system involves comprehensive testing using a materials testing machine (Instron MTS Insight II) equipped with a calibrated load cell (250 N capacity). The testing protocol includes applying controlled loads ranging from 1 to 50 N at various spatial positions and angles of incidence, with custom 3D-printed alignment fixtures ensuring precise positioning of the prosthetic fingertip relative to the loading probe. Each test condition involves multiple repetitions (typically 10 contact events per condition) to ensure statistical reliability and account for natural variability in the system response. The testing covers the full operational envelope of the sensor system, including the transition from proximity detection through initial contact to maximum force application, as well as the unloading phase.

The data analysis methodology includes careful segmentation of individual loading and unloading cycles from the continuous data stream. This involves identifying peak force events and extracting fixed-length windows (typically 180 samples for force calibration, 150 samples for classification tasks) centered on each peak. The segmented data are then normalized or standardized as appropriate for the specific analysis task, with zero-mean, unit-variance standardization applied for classification tasks to ensure consistent performance across different force levels. Cross-validation techniques (six-fold cross-validation in the preferred implementation) are employed to prevent overfitting and provide reliable estimates of classifier performance.

The integration of the sensor system with neural interface technologies represents a key application area for the invention. The rich sensory information provided by the multi-modal sensor system can be mapped to appropriate stimulation parameters for peripheral nerve interfaces such as the flat interface nerve electrode (FINE) or Utah Slant Electrode Array. The calibrated force signal provides a direct mapping to stimulation intensity, while the spatial location and angular orientation information can be used to select appropriate electrode sites for stimulation based on the somatotopic organization of the peripheral nerve. Additionally, the proximity sensing capability opens possibilities for novel sensory paradigms, such as mapping proximity information to non-physiological percepts that could enhance user awareness of the prosthetic hand's environment.

The system's compatibility with existing prosthetic control architectures is ensured through the use of standard communication protocols (I²C for sensor communication, USB serial for host communication) and modular design principles that allow for easy integration with different prosthetic hand platforms. The compact size and low power consumption of the sensor components make the system suitable for battery-powered prosthetic devices, while the robust construction ensures reliable performance under real-world usage conditions. The invention thus provides a practical, manufacturable solution that bridges the gap between advanced robotic sensing capabilities and the stringent requirements of clinical prosthetic applications.

## CONCLUSION

The present invention represents a significant advancement in the field of prosthetic sensory restoration by providing a novel multi-modal prosthetic fingertip sensor system that addresses critical limitations in current prosthetic technology. Through the strategic integration of infrared proximity sensing and barometric pressure measurement within a robust, compact prosthetic fingertip design, the invention delivers comprehensive tactile information that enables both advanced prosthetic control and physiologically appropriate sensory feedback. The system's ability to detect proximity, contact, and force magnitude across a wide dynamic range (0-50 N), while simultaneously classifying spatial location and angular orientation of applied forces, provides unprecedented richness of sensory information compared to existing prosthetic sensors.

The invention's technical achievements are demonstrated through rigorous experimental characterization and validation, showing high accuracy in force calibration (R² = 0.99) using Gaussian process regression and excellent classification performance for spatial location (96% accuracy) and angular orientation (89% accuracy) using support vector machine classifiers. These results confirm the system's capability to provide reliable, repeatable sensory information even under challenging conditions involving off-center and angled loading scenarios that commonly occur during natural grasping tasks. The complementary nature of the two sensor modalities effectively overcomes the individual limitations of each sensor type, with the IR proximity sensor providing high sensitivity at near-zero force levels and the barometric pressure sensor delivering linear response across the full operational range.

The practical significance of the invention extends beyond its technical specifications to its compatibility with existing prosthetic platforms and neural interface technologies. The use of standard communication protocols (I²C), compact component selection, and robust construction methods ensures seamless integration with current prosthetic hand designs, while the rich sensory information enables advanced control paradigms including shared control systems and closed-loop feedback mechanisms. Most importantly, the invention provides the necessary sensory foundation for effective sensory restoration through peripheral nerve stimulation, with the calibrated force signal enabling appropriate intensity mapping and the spatial classification capabilities supporting somatotopically appropriate electrode selection.

Future development directions for the invention include real-time implementation of the sensor fusion and classification algorithms to enable immediate feedback for prosthetic control and sensory restoration applications. The proximity sensing capability opens opportunities for investigating novel sensory paradigms that could enhance user awareness and control, while the comprehensive force and spatial information supports the development of more sophisticated shared control systems that can adapt prosthetic behavior based on environmental interactions. The invention's modular design and standardized interfaces facilitate adaptation to different prosthetic hand platforms and user requirements, ensuring broad applicability across the prosthetic rehabilitation field.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a practical, manufacturable solution that bridges the critical gap between sophisticated robotic sensing capabilities and the stringent requirements of clinical prosthetic applications. By delivering rich, reliable sensory information in a compact, robust package compatible with existing prosthetic systems, the invention represents a significant step toward restoring natural sensory function to upper limb amputees and improving their quality of life through enhanced prosthetic performance and embodiment.
