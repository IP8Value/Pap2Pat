# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to non-destructive evaluation (NDE) methods for composite materials, and more particularly to a high-resolution ultrasonic inspection system and associated signal processing techniques for the detection, visualization, and precise geometric quantification of foreign object defects within woven carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP) laminates. The invention specifically addresses the technical challenge of accurately sizing small-scale manufacturing-induced inclusions—such as polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE or Teflon) films—embedded between plies of a multi-layered composite structure using pulse-echo immersion ultrasound. The method leverages advanced data preprocessing, spatial smoothing via two-dimensional Gaussian filtering, resolution enhancement through Fourier-based interpolation, and a novel edge detection algorithm based on the magnitude of the spatial gradient of the processed C-scan image to achieve sub-millimeter accuracy in defect diameter measurement. This invention is particularly suited for quality assurance in aerospace, automotive, wind energy, and other high-performance industries where the structural integrity of composite components is critical and where even minute manufacturing anomalies can significantly compromise mechanical performance and service life.

### 2. Description of Related Art

Composite materials, especially carbon fiber reinforced polymers (CFRPs), have become indispensable in modern engineering due to their exceptional strength-to-weight ratio, fatigue resistance, and tailorable anisotropic properties. Unlike isotropic metals, composites allow designers to orient fibers to optimize load-bearing capacity along specific directions, enabling lighter and more efficient structures. The aerospace industry has been a major adopter; for instance, the Boeing 787 Dreamliner comprises approximately 50% composite materials by weight, with primary airframe structures fabricated from CFRP laminates. However, the manufacturing of such laminates—often involving manual layup, resin infusion (e.g., Vacuum Assisted Resin Transfer Molding, or VARTM), and autoclave curing—is inherently susceptible to the introduction of defects. Common manufacturing flaws include delaminations, fiber breakage, resin-rich or resin-poor regions, voids, and foreign object debris (FOD), such as release films, peel plies, or tape fragments inadvertently left between plies during assembly. These defects act as stress concentrators, significantly reducing tensile, compressive, and interlaminar shear strengths, thereby shortening component lifespan and posing safety risks.

Given the high cost of composite parts and stringent regulatory requirements—particularly in aviation—the need for reliable, non-destructive inspection techniques is paramount. The global non-destructive testing (NDT) market, valued at $8.3 billion in 2018, is projected to reach $12.6 billion by 2024, driven largely by the proliferation of advanced materials that cannot be destructively tested without prohibitive expense. Among NDT modalities, ultrasound has emerged as the most widely used method for composite inspection due to its safety, portability, relatively low cost, and ability to penetrate thick sections while providing depth-resolved information. Conventional ultrasonic approaches include through-transmission, pitch-catch, and pulse-echo configurations, with C-scan imaging being the standard for planar defect mapping.

Prior art in ultrasonic defect characterization has explored various signal processing and image analysis strategies. Benammar et al. employed signal processing to estimate delamination depth in CFRP with reasonable accuracy (within 4–8% of actual depth). Poudel et al. developed artificial neural network (ANN)-based classifiers to detect Teflon inclusions, achieving high detection rates but offering limited insight into defect geometry. Similarly, Barry et al. used ANNs to distinguish between bonding (e.g., Mylar) and non-bonding (e.g., Teflon) defects but did not quantify defect size. Other researchers have attempted geometric characterization: Amenabar et al. compared ultrasound, thermography, shearography, and X-ray CT for detecting simulated delaminations (Teflon inserts), reporting a 15% lateral area error (≈2 mm diameter error for a 30 mm defect) with conventional ultrasound. Poudel et al. later demonstrated that through-transmission acoustography could reduce diameter measurement error to 0.4 mm for 20 mm inclusions, outperforming through-transmission ultrasound (1.2 mm error). However, these methods require access to both sides of the part, limiting applicability to field inspections or complex geometries.

More recent work has focused on high-frequency phased array ultrasound and advanced algorithms. Mohammadkhani et al. used wavelet transforms with 5 MHz and 10 MHz transducers on unidirectional CFRP panels containing 6 mm square inclusions, achieving depth prediction errors as low as 2% but overestimating Teflon inclusion area by 43.8%. Ma et al. proposed a signal correlation algorithm for circular delaminations (12.7 mm diameter) in unidirectional laminates, reporting diameter measurement errors between 0.175 mm and 0.475 mm. Notably, both studies utilized highly controlled, autoclave-cured unidirectional composites, which exhibit minimal internal scattering compared to woven fabrics. Woven CFRP, commonly used in industrial applications due to its drapeability and impact resistance, presents a far more challenging inspection environment due to the periodic undulation of fiber tows, which generates complex, time-varying structural noise that obscures defect signals.

Conventional defect sizing in ultrasound often relies on the “6 dB drop” method, wherein the defect boundary is defined as the locus where signal amplitude falls to half (−6 dB) of its peak value over the defect. While simple, this technique is highly sensitive to noise, gain settings, and material heterogeneity, leading to significant over- or under-estimation, especially for small or shallow defects. Edge detection methods from computer vision—such as Sobel, Canny, or Laplacian operators—have been explored but are rarely applied to raw ultrasonic C-scans due to low signal-to-noise ratios and grainy textures inherent in composite data. Moreover, existing literature lacks robust, automated methods for sub-millimeter defect sizing in woven laminates using single-sided, pulse-echo ultrasound—a configuration essential for in-service inspection.

Thus, despite advances, there remains a critical unmet need for a non-destructive technique capable of **precisely quantifying the size of small (sub-6 mm), shallow, and deep-seated foreign object defects in woven CFRP laminates using only single-sided access**. Prior methods either lack geometric accuracy, require dual-sided access, are limited to idealized unidirectional materials, or fail to address the unique noise characteristics of woven architectures. The present invention directly addresses these limitations by integrating optimized data acquisition, physics-informed preprocessing, noise suppression, resolution enhancement, and a novel gradient-based edge detection framework to achieve unprecedented sizing accuracy—demonstrating an average diameter error of just 0.11 mm across defect sizes ranging from 1.59 mm to 12.7 mm, regardless of depth within a 12-ply laminate.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive, non-destructive method and system for the high-fidelity detection and precise geometric quantification of foreign object defects within woven carbon fiber reinforced polymer laminates using pulse-echo immersion ultrasound. The invention overcomes the limitations of prior art by introducing a multi-stage signal processing pipeline that enhances defect visibility, suppresses structural noise inherent to woven composites, improves effective spatial resolution beyond the native scan grid, and applies a novel edge detection algorithm to accurately delineate defect boundaries without presupposing shape or requiring dual-sided access.

In one aspect, the invention comprises acquiring ultrasonic A-scan data across a raster grid on a single surface of a composite laminate using a spherically focused transducer operating at a frequency of at least 7.5 MHz, preferably in an immersion tank to ensure consistent acoustic coupling. The raw time-domain signals are then preprocessed to correct for minor surface topography variations by aligning all A-scans to a common front-wall reference using a polynomial surface fit derived from detected front-wall echo times. Subsequently, a depth gate is applied to isolate the ultrasonic response corresponding to the expected ply interface where a defect may reside, using a modified Maximum Gated Amplitude (MGA) approach that selects the maximum signal amplitude within a time window spanning three-quarters of the average inter-ply distance, without taking the absolute value of the signal to preserve phase information critical for edge definition.

In another aspect, the invention applies a two-dimensional Gaussian smoothing filter to the gated C-scan image to attenuate high-frequency noise and spurious intensity fluctuations caused by fiber tow undulations and resin heterogeneity. The filter parameters—standard deviation set to 5/3 of the native scan step size and a finite support of ±5 steps—are optimized to balance noise reduction against preservation of true defect edges, ensuring less than 0.5% loss of integrated signal energy.

In a further aspect, the invention enhances the effective spatial resolution of the C-scan beyond the physical scanning grid through sequential one-dimensional Fourier transform-based interpolation along both orthogonal spatial dimensions. This upsampling—preferably by a factor of five in each direction—generates a high-density representation of the defect region without amplifying noise, as interpolation is performed only after Gaussian smoothing. This step is critical for enabling sub-grid precision in subsequent edge localization.

In yet another aspect, the invention introduces a novel edge detection technique termed Maximum Gradient Transition (MGT), which computes the magnitude of the spatial gradient of the upsampled, smoothed C-scan image using high-order (fourth-order accurate) central finite differences. The resulting gradient magnitude map exhibits sharp peaks precisely at locations of maximal intensity transition—i.e., the true physical boundary between the foreign object and the surrounding composite matrix. The defect perimeter is then reconstructed by systematically identifying these gradient maxima along radial projections from an interior seed point, yielding a closed contour that accurately reflects the defect’s actual geometry, irrespective of its shape regularity.

The invention further provides a method for quantifying defect size by computing an effective diameter from the enclosed area of the MGT-derived contour, enabling direct comparison with ground-truth measurements obtained via optical metrology. Experimental validation demonstrates that this approach achieves an average absolute diameter error of 0.11 mm (0.004 inches) across twelve test specimens containing circular PTFE inclusions of nominal diameters 1.59 mm, 3.18 mm, 6.35 mm, and 12.7 mm, embedded at three different depths (between plies 3–4, 6–7, and 9–10) in a (0/30/60/0/45/0)s woven CFRP laminate fabricated via VARTM. This represents a significant improvement over prior art, which reports errors ranging from 0.175 mm to over 2 mm for similarly sized defects.

Importantly, the invention is specifically tailored for woven composites, whose complex microstructure generates substantial structural noise that confounds conventional analysis. By combining physics-aware preprocessing, targeted noise suppression, resolution enhancement, and a shape-agnostic edge detector, the invention enables reliable detection and sizing of defects as small as 1.59 mm—smaller than those typically reported in literature—and maintains consistent accuracy across varying depths, with only a minor increase in error for near-surface defects attributable to fixed gain settings.

The disclosed method is fully compatible with standard industrial ultrasonic inspection hardware and can be implemented via custom software modules integrated into existing C-scan platforms. Future automation of the interior seed point selection will render the technique fully autonomous, making it suitable for real-time quality control in composite manufacturing and in-service structural health monitoring.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a non-destructive evaluation method for precisely determining the size and location of foreign object defects within woven carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP) laminates using pulse-echo immersion ultrasound combined with an advanced signal processing pipeline. The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, including materials, system configurations, data acquisition protocols, and computational algorithms, sufficient to enable any person skilled in the art of non-destructive testing or composite materials engineering to practice the invention without undue experimentation. It should be understood that the invention is not limited to the particular embodiments described herein, as modifications and variations falling within the spirit and scope of the appended claims will be apparent to those of ordinary skill in the art.

The invention begins with the fabrication of representative test specimens containing known foreign object defects. In a preferred embodiment, twelve CFRP laminates are manufactured using a plain weave 3K carbon fiber fabric (6 oz. areal weight) supplied by ACP Composites, with a symmetric stacking sequence of (0/30/60/0/45/0)s, resulting in a total of twelve plies. The laminates are fabricated using the Vacuum Assisted Resin Transfer Molding (VARTM) process, wherein dry fabric plies are laid up on a flat tool, sealed within a vacuum bag, and infused with a two-part epoxy system comprising Proset INF 114 resin and Proset 211 hardener, cured according to the manufacturer’s recommended thermal cycle to achieve optimal mechanical properties. Each laminate is trimmed to a nominal dimension of 50.8 mm × 50.8 mm (2 in × 2 in) using a diamond-tipped tile saw.

Embedded within each laminate is a single foreign object defect made of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), chosen for its acoustic impedance mismatch with the CFRP matrix and its common occurrence as release film debris in manufacturing. Twelve circular PTFE inserts are fabricated using a precision cutting plotter (Silhouette Cameo) from a 0.05 mm (0.002 in) thick PTFE sheet, with nominal diameters of 12.7 mm (1/2 in), 6.35 mm (1/4 in), 3.18 mm (1/8 in), and 1.59 mm (1/16 in), three specimens per size. Critically, the true geometric dimensions of each PTFE insert are measured prior to embedding using a high-resolution 3D optical profiler (Keyence VR-3000), which employs sub-pixel edge detection to determine effective diameter with a repeatability standard deviation of less than 0.003 mm for 6.35 mm features. Measurements reveal that all fabricated inserts are slightly smaller than nominal, with actual mean diameters of 12.59 ± 0.07 mm, 6.12 ± 0.05 mm, 3.11 ± 0.02 mm, and 1.55 ± 0.05 mm, respectively, underscoring the necessity of ground-truth metrology for validation.

Each PTFE insert is placed between specific ply interfaces during layup: between plies 3 and 4 (counting from the tool side), between plies 6 and 7, or between plies 9 and 10, with one insert per laminate. The laminates are labeled accordingly (e.g., A1 denotes a 12.7 mm nominal insert at the 3–4 interface), resulting in a full factorial test matrix of four sizes × three depths = twelve unique specimens. The acoustic properties of the cured laminate are characterized as having a longitudinal wave speed of 2890 m/s and density of 1370 kg/m³, yielding an acoustic impedance of 3.96 × 10⁶ Pa·s/m³, while PTFE has a wave speed of 1390 m/s, density of 2140 kg/m³, and impedance of 2.97 × 10⁶ Pa·s/m³. Although the impedance mismatch is moderate, the extreme thinness of the PTFE (0.05 mm) relative to the ultrasonic wavelength (0.37 mm in CFRP at 7.5 MHz) presents a significant detection challenge.

Ultrasonic inspection is performed using a custom-built immersion C-scan system. Each specimen is submerged in a water tank and scanned from the tool-side surface only, simulating a realistic single-access inspection scenario. A spherically focused transducer (Olympus V320-SU-F1.50IN-PTF) with a nominal frequency of 7.5 MHz (peak at 7.71 MHz, center at 7.15 MHz, −6 dB bandwidth of 76.22%) and active element diameter of 9.53 mm (3/8 in) is used, with a focal length of 38.1 mm (1.5 in) set to coincide with the mid-plane of the laminate. The transducer is driven by a pulser/receiver (US Ultratek EUT 3160) using a 65 ns negative square wave pulse at 200 V, and received signals are digitized at 160 MHz. The transducer is raster-scanned over a 38.1 mm × 38.1 mm (1.5 in × 1.5 in) area using motorized translation stages (Velmex BiSlide) with a positional resolution of 0.005 mm/step, though the scan step size is set to 0.2 mm (0.008 in) for practical throughput. Custom LabVIEW software controls stage motion, pulse triggering, and data acquisition, storing raw A-scan waveforms for each (x₁, x₂) position.

The core of the invention lies in the post-processing of this raw ultrasonic data to extract precise defect geometry. The first preprocessing step corrects for minor specimen tilt or surface waviness that could misalign depth gates. For each A-scan, the time-of-arrival of the front-wall echo is detected as the first peak exceeding a preset threshold after the initial pulse. A third-order bivariate polynomial surface is fitted to these front-wall times across the scan area, and each A-scan is time-shifted so that the front wall aligns at a common reference time t₀. This yields a corrected dataset F(x₁,k, x₂,l, t̃), where t̃ = t − t₀.

Next, a depth gate is applied to isolate reflections from the target ply interface. The average A-scan across the entire specimen is computed, and the peak corresponding to the ply immediately above the expected defect location (e.g., ply 6 for a defect between plies 6–7) is identified. A gate window is defined starting at this peak and extending over three-quarters of the average peak-to-peak time interval between adjacent plies. Within this gate, the maximum amplitude (not absolute value) is extracted for each spatial location, generating a preliminary C-scan image Ĥ(x₁,k, x₂,l). This modified MGA approach preserves waveform polarity, which aids in distinguishing true defect echoes from structural noise.

To suppress noise and enhance edge definition, a two-dimensional isotropic Gaussian filter is convolved with the MGA image. The filter kernel P(x₁, x₂, μ₁, μ₂, σ₁, σ₂) is defined with σ₁ = σ₂ = (5/3) × 0.2 mm ≈ 0.333 mm, and truncated to a 11×11 pixel neighborhood (±5 steps) to ensure computational efficiency while retaining >99.5% of the kernel’s integral energy. The filtered image F̃(x₁,k, x₂,l) exhibits reduced speckle and smoother intensity transitions at defect boundaries.

Resolution enhancement is then achieved via Fourier-based interpolation. The Gaussian-smoothed image is first interpolated along the x₁-direction using the interpft function in MATLAB (which implements zero-padding in the frequency domain followed by inverse FFT), increasing the sampling density fivefold. The resulting intermediate image H′(x₁,i, x₂,l) is then similarly interpolated along x₂ to produce a final upsampled image H(x₁,i, x₂,j) with grid spacing of 0.04 mm. This step effectively reconstructs high-frequency spatial content lost due to the original 0.2 mm sampling, enabling sub-pixel edge localization.

The key innovation of the invention is the Maximum Gradient Transition (MGT) edge detection algorithm. The magnitude of the spatial gradient G(x₁,i, x₂,j) is computed as the Euclidean norm of the partial derivatives ∂H/∂x₁ and ∂H/∂x₂, approximated using fourth-order central finite differences for high accuracy:

∂H/∂x₁ ≈ [−H(x₁,i+2, x₂,j) + 8H(x₁,i+1, x₂,j) − 8H(x₁,i−1, x₂,j) + H(x₁,i−2, x₂,j)] / (12h)

where h = 0.04 mm is the upsampled grid spacing, and similarly for ∂H/∂x₂. The gradient magnitude map G exhibits sharp ridges precisely at intensity discontinuities—i.e., the physical defect edge.

To extract the defect perimeter, an operator selects an interior seed point within the defect region on the gradient map (any point with normalized intensity between 0.1 and 0.3 suffices). From this seed, radial lines are projected outward in all directions (e.g., every 1°). Along each radial line, the location of maximum gradient magnitude is identified; if multiple adjacent points share the maximum value (due to discretization), their centroid is taken. Connecting these maxima forms a closed contour representing the defect boundary. This contour is shape-agnostic—it adapts to irregularities caused by fiber tow interactions—and requires no assumptions about defect geometry.

Finally, the defect area A_MGT is computed by integrating within the MGT contour, and an effective diameter d̄ = 2√(A_MGT/π) is calculated for comparison with the optically measured true area A_True. Validation across all twelve specimens shows that the MGT method achieves a maximum diameter error of 0.32 mm and an average error of 0.11 mm, substantially outperforming the conventional 6 dB drop method (which yields errors up to 0.8 mm even after filtering). Accuracy is consistent for defects at the 6–7 and 9–10 interfaces, with slightly higher error at the 3–4 interface attributed to fixed gain settings causing signal saturation near the surface—a limitation addressable by adaptive gain control in future implementations.

The invention thus provides a robust, high-accuracy solution for foreign object defect sizing in woven CFRP using industrially feasible pulse-echo ultrasound, enabling improved quality control, structural certification, and life-cycle management of composite components.
