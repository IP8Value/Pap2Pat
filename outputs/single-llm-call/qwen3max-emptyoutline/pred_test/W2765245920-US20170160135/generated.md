# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of optical imaging systems, and more specifically to a novel apparatus and method for snapshot spectral imaging using a static phase-only diffuser in combination with compressed sensing reconstruction algorithms. The invention enables the acquisition of a three-dimensional spectral data cube—comprising two spatial dimensions and one spectral dimension—from a single two-dimensional monochromatic image captured instantaneously by a standard digital camera sensor. This is achieved without the need for moving parts, intermediate image planes, spatial light modulators, or time-sequential filtering mechanisms, thereby significantly simplifying the optical architecture while maintaining high fidelity in spectral and spatial reconstruction. The invention is particularly suited for applications requiring rapid, real-time spectral analysis of dynamic scenes, such as in medical diagnostics, remote sensing, industrial inspection, consumer electronics, and scientific imaging, where conventional spectral imaging techniques are either too slow, too bulky, or too costly for practical deployment.

## BACKGROUND

Spectral imaging—the process of capturing the spatial distribution of light intensity across multiple wavelengths—has become an indispensable tool across a wide array of scientific, industrial, and commercial domains. In biological and medical contexts, spectral imaging enables non-invasive tissue characterization, early disease detection, and real-time monitoring of cellular processes by exploiting the unique spectral signatures of biomolecules and pathological conditions. In agriculture and food safety, it facilitates the identification of contaminants, ripeness assessment, and compositional analysis. In art conservation and archaeology, it allows for the non-destructive analysis of pigments, binders, and underlying sketches. In remote sensing and astronomy, it provides critical information about atmospheric composition, surface materials, and celestial phenomena. Despite its broad utility, traditional spectral imaging systems have been hampered by significant limitations in speed, complexity, and portability.

Conventional approaches to spectral imaging fall into two primary categories: scanning-based and filter-based systems. Scanning systems, such as pushbroom or whiskbroom imagers, acquire spectral data by mechanically scanning either the spatial or spectral dimension over time. While these systems can achieve high spectral resolution, they are inherently unsuitable for imaging dynamic or rapidly changing scenes, as the full data cube is assembled over multiple time intervals. Filter-based systems, on the other hand, employ a sequence of narrowband spectral filters—either manually inserted or electronically tunable—placed in front of a standard image sensor to capture individual spectral bands one at a time. Although this approach avoids mechanical scanning in the spatial domain, it still suffers from temporal limitations, as the total acquisition time scales linearly with the number of spectral bands. Moreover, the use of mosaic color filter arrays (e.g., Bayer patterns) on consumer image sensors introduces substantial light loss due to the blocking of non-matching wavelengths, reducing overall sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio.

To address these shortcomings, researchers have developed snapshot spectral imaging (SSI) architectures that aim to capture the entire spectral cube in a single exposure. Notable examples include the Coded Aperture Snapshot Spectral Imager (CASSI), which uses a coded aperture mask and a dispersive element (such as a prism or grating) to encode spectral information into a 2D measurement. While effective, such systems typically require additional optical components—including relay lenses, intermediate image planes, and precise alignment of the coded aperture—which increase the system’s physical footprint, weight, and manufacturing cost. Furthermore, many SSI designs rely on spatial light modulators or programmable masks, which introduce complexity, power consumption, and potential reliability issues.

In parallel, the advent of compressed sensing (CS) theory has opened new avenues for efficient data acquisition and reconstruction. CS posits that if a signal is sparse or compressible in some transform domain, it can be accurately reconstructed from far fewer measurements than dictated by the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem. This principle has been successfully applied to various imaging modalities, including magnetic resonance imaging, radar, and astronomical observation. In the context of spectral imaging, CS offers a pathway to recover a high-dimensional spectral cube from a low-dimensional 2D measurement, provided the sensing mechanism satisfies certain mathematical conditions—most notably, the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP)—and the signal exhibits sparsity in an appropriate basis.

However, existing CS-based SSI systems often fail to fully integrate the optical design with the computational reconstruction framework in a manner that is both physically realizable and compatible with off-the-shelf digital cameras. Many proposed architectures remain laboratory curiosities, requiring custom optics, specialized sensors, or complex calibration procedures that hinder widespread adoption. There remains a critical need for a spectral imaging solution that is not only snapshot-capable and computationally efficient but also minimally invasive to existing camera hardware—ideally, one that can be retrofitted onto a standard monochromatic digital camera with minimal modification.

The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel optical element—a static, phase-only diffuser placed at the entrance pupil of a standard imaging lens—that simultaneously diffuses and disperses incident light in a wavelength-dependent manner. This single element replaces the need for multiple optical components (e.g., gratings, prisms, coded apertures) while generating a highly randomized, spectrally multiplexed measurement on the sensor plane. When combined with tailored CS reconstruction algorithms that exploit the natural sparsity of spectral cubes in wavelet or framelet domains, this system enables accurate, high-fidelity recovery of the full 3D spectral data from a single grayscale image. Crucially, the invention leverages the inherent properties of photographic scenes—spatial smoothness, spectral continuity, and transform-domain sparsity—to overcome the underdetermined nature of the inverse problem, thereby achieving robust performance even in the presence of noise and model mismatch. By eliminating moving parts, intermediate optics, and color filter arrays, the invention paves the way for compact, low-cost, and high-performance spectral cameras suitable for integration into smartphones, drones, medical endoscopes, and other resource-constrained platforms.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for snapshot spectral imaging that overcomes the limitations of prior art by integrating a specially designed static phase-only diffuser with a monochromatic image sensor and compressed sensing reconstruction algorithms. The core innovation lies in the formulation of a single optical element that performs both spatial diffusion and spectral dispersion, thereby encoding the three-dimensional spectral cube of a scene into a two-dimensional monochromatic image captured in a single exposure. This diffuser, positioned at the entrance pupil of the imaging system, is engineered to produce a wavelength-dependent point spread function that ensures sufficient randomization of the optical response to satisfy the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP) required for stable compressed sensing reconstruction.

The system comprises a standard imaging lens, a monochromatic image sensor devoid of a color filter array, a bandpass spectral filter defining the operational wavelength range, and the aforementioned diffuser. The absence of a color filter array maximizes light throughput and sensor sensitivity, while the diffuser’s phase profile—derived from a randomly permuted, nonlinear saw-tooth function—introduces controlled, wavelength-varying blurring that mixes spatial and spectral information across the sensor pixels. The resulting diffused and dispersed (DD) image contains a linear superposition of all spectral bands, each convolved with a distinct, diffuser-induced kernel.

Reconstruction of the original spectral cube is performed computationally using iterative optimization algorithms, specifically split Bregman iterations, which solve a constrained minimization problem balancing data fidelity against sparsity in a chosen transform domain. The invention supports both 2D framelet and 3D wavelet transforms derived from polynomial and discrete splines, which have been shown to provide highly sparse representations of natural images and their spectral variations. The sensing matrix, which models the forward optical process, is either theoretically computed from the diffuser’s phase profile or empirically calibrated through direct point spread function (PSF) measurements using narrowband spectral filters.

Experimental validation demonstrates that the system can reconstruct a 33-band spectral cube from a single 256×256 monochromatic image with high fidelity, as evidenced by peak signal-to-noise ratios (PSNR) exceeding 15 dB for both RGB and monochromatic reconstructions. The total acquisition time is reduced by orders of magnitude compared to sequential filter-based methods—for example, from over 2.6 seconds to just 10.6 milliseconds—making the system ideal for dynamic scenes. Furthermore, computer simulations using real-world spectral data from human skin tissue confirm the scalability and robustness of the approach across diverse imaging scenarios.

In summary, the invention achieves miniaturization, speed, and simplicity without sacrificing reconstruction quality, thereby enabling the practical deployment of snapshot spectral imaging in a wide range of applications where conventional systems are impractical. The disclosed architecture represents a paradigm shift from hardware-intensive spectral imaging to a co-design of optics and computation, where physical constraints are relaxed through intelligent algorithmic recovery.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Diffuser Design Formulation

The foundation of the present invention rests upon the precise engineering of a phase-only optical diffuser that simultaneously fulfills two critical functions: spatial diffusion and spectral dispersion. Unlike conventional diffusers that scatter light uniformly across all wavelengths, or dispersive elements like prisms and gratings that separate wavelengths without spatial mixing, the diffuser of the present invention is explicitly designed to produce a wavelength-dependent point spread function (PSF) that varies continuously across the operational spectral range. This dual functionality is achieved through a carefully constructed phase profile that imparts a spatially varying optical path difference to incident light, with the magnitude of this difference scaled by the wavelength of the light itself.

The diffuser is positioned at the entrance pupil of the imaging system, which is defined as the image of the aperture stop as seen from the object side. Placing the diffuser at this location ensures that its effect is applied uniformly across the entire field of view, independent of object position, thereby preserving the shift-invariance property of the optical system in the absence of the diffuser. The phase function φ(x) of the diffuser is formulated as a one-dimensional, piecewise-linear function composed of N_d = 400 discrete segments, each of width u_D = 8 µm, spanning a clear aperture of 3.2 mm. This segmentation corresponds to the physical fabrication constraints of available lithographic or laser-writing techniques, while the total aperture matches the diameter of the entrance pupil of the imaging lens used in the experimental setup.

The specific form of the phase function is derived from a randomly permuted, nonlinear saw-tooth waveform. Mathematically, this can be expressed as:

φ(x) = (2π / λ_d) · f(σ(x)),

where λ_d is a design wavelength (typically chosen near the center of the operational band, e.g., 560 nm), σ(x) is a permutation function that reorders the spatial segments to break periodicity and enhance randomness, and f(·) is a nonlinear mapping that introduces curvature into the otherwise linear ramp of a standard saw-tooth. The nonlinearity is crucial: it ensures that the derivative of the phase function—and hence the local dispersion angle—varies non-uniformly across the aperture, which in turn generates a PSF that is not only broadened but also asymmetric and spectrally shifting in a complex, non-repetitive manner.

When polychromatic light passes through this diffuser, each wavelength component experiences a different effective phase profile due to the λ-dependence in the optical path. Consequently, the coherent PSF h(x; λ) for a given wavelength λ is obtained as the inverse Fourier transform of the modified pupil function P(u; λ) = rect(u / D) · exp[iφ(Du; λ)], where D is the pupil diameter, u is the spatial frequency coordinate, and rect(·) denotes the rectangular aperture function. The resulting PSF exhibits a characteristic “smearing” that increases with deviation from the design wavelength λ_d, with shorter wavelengths typically diffracted more strongly than longer ones, though the exact behavior depends on the specifics of the nonlinear mapping f(·).

This wavelength-dependent blurring serves as the encoding mechanism for spectral multiplexing. In the image plane, the intensity recorded by the sensor is the sum over all wavelengths of the object’s spectral radiance convolved with the corresponding PSF, weighted by the system’s spectral sensitivity. Because each PSF is distinct and sufficiently incoherent with respect to others, the resulting measurement matrix A—whose columns correspond to the vectorized PSFs for each spectral band—exhibits low mutual coherence, a key requirement for satisfying the RIP in compressed sensing. The random permutation σ(x) further decorrelates the PSFs, ensuring that no two spectral bands produce highly similar blurring patterns, which would otherwise lead to ill-conditioning in the inverse problem.

Critically, the diffuser is implemented as a static, passive element fabricated from a transparent dielectric material (e.g., fused silica or polymer) using grayscale lithography or direct laser writing. It requires no power, no moving parts, and no real-time control, making it exceptionally robust and suitable for mass production. Its integration into a standard digital camera involves only the replacement of the protective window or dust filter at the sensor plane with the diffuser, or, more commonly, the insertion of the diffuser into the lens mount at the entrance pupil location. This minimal modification preserves the camera’s original form factor and functionality while endowing it with full spectral imaging capability.

### Finally

The reconstruction of the spectral cube from the diffused and dispersed (DD) image is the computational cornerstone of the present invention, and it is here that the synergy between optical design and algorithmic innovation is most evident. The forward model of the imaging system, as derived from the continuous optical principles and discretized for digital implementation, yields a linear relationship Y = A X, where Y is the N_x × N_y measured DD image, X is the N_x × N_y × L spectral cube to be recovered, and A is the N_x N_y × N_x N_y L sensing matrix that encodes the action of the diffuser across all L spectral bands. Given that the number of measurements (N_x N_y) is substantially less than the number of unknowns (N_x N_y L), the system is severely underdetermined, and a naive inversion is impossible. However, by leveraging the inherent sparsity of natural images in appropriate transform domains, the invention enables a stable and accurate reconstruction through the framework of compressed sensing.

The reconstruction process begins with the selection of a sparsifying transform D, which maps the spectral cube X into a coefficient array d such that X = Ψ d, where Ψ is the inverse transform matrix. The invention contemplates two primary classes of transforms: (1) 2D framelet transforms applied independently to each monochromatic slice of the cube, and (2) 3D wavelet transforms that jointly process spatial and spectral dimensions. The 2D framelets, derived from quasi-interpolating polynomial splines, offer tight or semi-tight frames that provide redundant yet stable representations, enhancing sparsity through directional selectivity and multi-scale decomposition. The 3D wavelets, constructed from twelfth-order discrete splines, extend this concept by applying 1D wavelet transforms along the spectral axis to the 2D transform coefficients, thereby exploiting the smoothness of spectral signatures across adjacent bands—a property that is especially pronounced in natural scenes and biological tissues.

With the sparsifying transform in place, the reconstruction problem is cast as a constrained optimization:

minimize ||d||_1 subject to ||A Ψ d − Y||_2 ≤ ε,

where ε is a tolerance parameter related to the expected noise level in the measurement. This ℓ1-minimization problem promotes sparsity in d while ensuring fidelity to the observed data Y. To solve this efficiently, the invention employs split Bregman iterations (SBI), an algorithm that decomposes the problem into alternating subproblems involving soft-thresholding (for sparsity) and least-squares minimization (for data consistency). The SBI update equations are as follows:

d^{k+1} = shrink(Ψ^T A^T (Y − A Ψ d^k) + b^k, c),
b^{k+1} = b^k − (Ψ d^{k+1} − Ψ d^k),

where shrink(·, c) denotes component-wise soft-thresholding with threshold c, b is an auxiliary Bregman variable that enforces the constraint, and k indexes the iteration. The parameters c and μ (implicit in the least-squares step) are tuned based on the noise characteristics and desired trade-off between sharpness and artifacts.

The sensing matrix A may be either theoretically modeled or empirically calibrated. In the theoretical approach, A is constructed by computing the discrete convolution kernels corresponding to the diffuser’s PSF at each wavelength, assuming perfect knowledge of the phase profile and optical parameters. In practice, however, manufacturing imperfections, alignment errors, and sensor non-uniformities necessitate empirical calibration. This is performed by imaging a series of narrowband point sources (e.g., a thin white line on a dark background, filtered through a set of L bandpass filters) at multiple spatial positions, thereby directly measuring the system’s PSF for each spectral band. The resulting empirical A captures all real-world deviations from the ideal model, leading to significantly improved reconstruction accuracy.

The reconstruction pipeline is completed by optional post-processing steps, such as denoising, spectral unmixing, or color rendering. For instance, the reconstructed spectral cube can be converted to standard RGB coordinates using the CIE 1931 color matching functions, enabling direct visualization on conventional displays. The entire process—from raw DD image to full spectral cube or RGB output—can be executed in real time on modern GPUs or embedded processors, making the system suitable for video-rate spectral imaging.

### Exemplary RIP Diffuser Implementation

An exemplary embodiment of the invention was constructed and tested using a commercially available monochromatic CMOS image sensor with a resolution of 256 × 256 pixels and a pixel pitch of 5.5 µm, integrated into a standard digital camera body. The imaging lens was a 50 mm focal length prime lens with an f-number of f/2.8, yielding an entrance pupil diameter of approximately 17.9 mm. However, to match the diffuser’s clear aperture of 3.2 mm, the lens was stopped down to f/15.6, ensuring that the diffuser fully covered the effective aperture. The diffuser itself was fabricated on a 5 mm × 5 mm fused silica substrate using grayscale electron-beam lithography, with a phase depth calibrated to provide a maximum optical path difference of 10 µm at the design wavelength of 560 nm. The phase profile consisted of 400 segments, each 8 µm wide, arranged according to a pseudorandom permutation of a nonlinear saw-tooth function generated by a cryptographic hash algorithm to ensure maximal unpredictability.

The system was operated in a controlled darkroom environment to eliminate ambient light contamination. A bandpass spectral filter with a transmission range of 400–720 nm and 10 nm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) bandpass filters (Thorlabs FB series) were used for calibration and reference measurements. For the snapshot acquisition, the diffuser was permanently mounted in the lens filter thread, and the camera was focused on a test object—a digital “Color Checker” chart displayed on an iPad screen—positioned 88 cm from the lens. The integration time was set to 10.6 ms to avoid sensor saturation while maintaining a high signal-to-noise ratio.

Calibration involved capturing 33 sets of PSF measurements, one for each of the 10 nm-spaced wavelength bands from 400 to 720 nm. In each band, a vertical white line (1 pixel wide) was displayed at 10 different horizontal positions across the iPad screen, and the resulting blurred images were recorded. These measurements were used to construct the empirical sensing matrix A of size 65,536 × 2,162,688 (256×256 measurements vs. 256×256×33 unknowns). The reference spectral cube was obtained by sequentially capturing 256 × 256 images through each of the 33 bandpass filters, with integration times adjusted to maintain consistent peak signal levels.

Reconstruction was performed using both 2D semi-tight framelet and 3D biorthogonal wavelet transforms, with SBI iterations run until convergence (typically 50–100 iterations). The resulting spectral cubes were evaluated against the reference using normalized root-mean-square error (RMSE) and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR). The 2D framelet method achieved a PSNR of 17.67 dB for the RGB image, while the 3D wavelet method yielded 15.92 dB, though the latter showed superior spectral fidelity at specific spatial locations, as evidenced by higher PSNR values in individual spectra (e.g., 17.73 dB at one point vs. 12.11 dB for the 2D method). These results confirm that the diffuser design successfully generates a sensing matrix that satisfies the RIP condition for K-sparse signals, enabling accurate recovery despite the extreme compression ratio of 1:33.

### Apparatus Embodiments

The present invention is not limited to the specific experimental configuration described above but encompasses a broad range of apparatus embodiments tailored to different application domains. In a first embodiment, the system is integrated into a smartphone camera by replacing the infrared cut filter with the phase-only diffuser and disabling the Bayer color filter array in software or hardware. This enables consumer-grade devices to perform real-time spectral analysis for applications such as food freshness assessment, skin health monitoring, or augmented reality object recognition.

In a second embodiment, the system is miniaturized into a medical endoscope or laparoscope, where the diffuser is fabricated directly onto the distal tip of the imaging fiber bundle or CMOS chip. The compact form factor and snapshot capability make it ideal for in vivo spectral diagnosis during surgical procedures, allowing real-time differentiation of healthy and malignant tissue based on hemoglobin oxygenation or collagen content.

In a third embodiment, the system is deployed on unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) for agricultural or environmental monitoring. Here, the diffuser is paired with a high-resolution monochromatic sensor and a telephoto lens, enabling rapid spectral surveys of large areas without the motion artifacts associated with scanning systems. The lightweight and low-power nature of the static diffuser is particularly advantageous in battery-constrained platforms.

In a fourth embodiment, the system is adapted for astronomical use by coupling the diffuser with a cooled scientific CCD and a narrowband pre-filter to isolate specific emission lines (e.g., H-alpha, O-III). The snapshot capability allows for the capture of transient events such as solar flares or supernovae with full spectral information, overcoming the limitations of traditional slit spectrographs.

All embodiments share the core architecture: a monochromatic sensor, a static phase-only diffuser at the entrance pupil, and a computational reconstruction engine. Variations arise in the choice of diffuser parameters (aperture size, phase depth, segmentation), spectral range (UV, visible, NIR), and reconstruction algorithms (real-time vs. offline, 2D vs. 3D transforms), but the underlying principles remain consistent.

### Computer Simulations

To further validate the scalability and robustness of the invention, extensive computer simulations were conducted using a high-dimensional spectral cube acquired from human skin tissue using a VTT spectral imager prototype equipped with a Fabry-Perot interferometer. The original cube, of size 1200 × 1920 × 120 (covering 460–850 nm), was preprocessed by spatial cropping to 1024 × 1024, zero-padding to 1024 × 1024 × 132, and two-level 3D biorthogonal wavelet decomposition. The low-pass sub-cube LLL of size 256 × 256 × 33 was selected for simulation, representing a smoothed, downsampled version of the original data.

The sensing matrix A was generated by theoretically computing the PSF for each of the 33 wavelengths based on the nominal diffuser phase profile. To reduce coherence and improve reconstruction, the columns of A were multiplied by a random sign matrix R (entries ±1), a technique known as random demodulation. The synthetic DD image Y was then computed as Y = A X, and additive Gaussian noise was introduced to simulate realistic sensor conditions.

Reconstruction using the 2D framelet method yielded a PSNR of 18.2 dB for the full cube, with accurate recovery of spectral features such as melanin and hemoglobin absorption peaks. The simulation confirmed that the invention performs well even with real-world, noisy, and high-dimensional data, and that the random demodulation strategy effectively enhances the RIP compliance of the sensing matrix. These results underscore the potential of the invention for clinical applications, where accurate spectral discrimination between benign and malignant lesions is critical.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a complete, practical, and versatile solution for snapshot spectral imaging that bridges the gap between optical physics and computational mathematics, enabling unprecedented access to spectral information in everyday imaging systems.
