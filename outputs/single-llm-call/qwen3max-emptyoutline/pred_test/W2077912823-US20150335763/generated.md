# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of therapeutic agents for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and more specifically to compositions and methods for sequestering high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein from the gastrointestinal tract using DNA-immobilized solid supports. The invention provides a novel approach to ameliorate intestinal inflammation by selectively binding and neutralizing extracellular HMGB1, a key pro-inflammatory mediator implicated in the pathogenesis of IBD, including ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease. The compositions of the invention comprise oligonucleotide sequences with high affinity for HMGB1 covalently immobilized onto biocompatible, non-absorbable particulate carriers such as sepharose beads, which are administered orally to remain within the lumen of the gastrointestinal tract and function as molecular scavengers of HMGB1 without systemic absorption. This localized sequestration strategy effectively reduces luminal and mucosal concentrations of HMGB1, thereby attenuating downstream inflammatory signaling, preserving epithelial barrier integrity, and improving clinical and histological outcomes in experimental models of colitis. The invention further encompasses methods for preparing such DNA-functionalized beads, their use in ex vivo depletion of HMGB1 from biological fluids, and their application as a targeted, non-immunosuppressive therapy for chronic inflammatory conditions driven by DAMP (damage-associated molecular pattern) signaling.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease, represents a significant global health burden characterized by chronic, relapsing inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract. Despite advances in understanding its immunopathogenesis, IBD remains incurable, and current therapeutic strategies—ranging from corticosteroids and immunomodulators to biologic agents targeting cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)—often yield incomplete responses, lose efficacy over time, or are associated with serious adverse effects including systemic immunosuppression and increased risk of infection or malignancy. The etiology of IBD is multifactorial, involving complex interactions between genetic predisposition, dysregulated immune responses, alterations in the gut microbiota, and environmental triggers. A growing body of evidence implicates damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) as critical mediators that perpetuate inflammation in the absence of overt infection. Among these, high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) has emerged as a central alarmin with potent pro-inflammatory properties.

HMGB1 is a highly conserved, non-histone nuclear protein ubiquitously expressed in eukaryotic cells, where it functions in chromatin architecture, DNA repair, and transcriptional regulation. Under homeostatic conditions, HMGB1 resides predominantly in the nucleus. However, during cellular stress, injury, or activation, HMGB1 can be actively secreted by immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells or passively released by necrotic cells into the extracellular space. Once extracellular, HMGB1 acts as a potent DAMP, engaging pattern recognition receptors including Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and the receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE), thereby triggering the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and IL-6, and amplifying the inflammatory cascade. In the context of IBD, elevated levels of HMGB1 have been consistently detected in the inflamed intestinal mucosa and feces of both human patients and experimental animal models. Fecal HMGB1 has been proposed as a non-invasive biomarker of mucosal inflammation, correlating with disease activity. Moreover, functional studies have demonstrated that HMGB1 directly compromises intestinal epithelial barrier function by increasing permeability, facilitating bacterial translocation, and promoting leukocyte recruitment, thereby creating a self-sustaining cycle of inflammation and tissue damage.

The biological activity of extracellular HMGB1 is critically regulated by post-translational modifications, particularly the redox state of its three conserved cysteine residues (C23, C45, and C106). Depending on the local microenvironment, HMGB1 can exist in distinct isoforms: the fully reduced form (all cysteines in thiol state) exhibits chemotactic activity; the disulfide form (C23-C45 disulfide bond with C106 reduced) possesses potent cytokine-stimulating properties; and the terminally oxidized sulfonyl form is biologically inert. In active colitis, both disulfide and fully reduced HMGB1 isoforms are present in the gut lumen, reflecting contributions from both actively secreting immune cells and passively releasing damaged epithelial cells. Additionally, acetylation of lysine residues within HMGB1’s nuclear localization sequences promotes its cytoplasmic translocation and active release, further contributing to extracellular accumulation. Given its central role in driving inflammation and barrier dysfunction, HMGB1 represents a compelling therapeutic target in IBD.

Previous attempts to neutralize HMGB1 have included the use of neutralizing antibodies, small-molecule inhibitors like ethyl pyruvate, and recombinant thrombomodulin. While these approaches have shown efficacy in preclinical models, they face challenges related to cost, stability, potential immunogenicity, and systemic exposure. An alternative strategy leverages HMGB1’s intrinsic, high-affinity binding to DNA structures, including linear duplexes, bent DNA, and four-way junctions. This natural affinity has been exploited in research settings using soluble DNA oligonucleotides to inhibit HMGB1 activity. However, the therapeutic application of free DNA is limited by rapid degradation by nucleases in the gastrointestinal tract, potential off-target immunostimulatory effects via cytosolic DNA sensors (e.g., cGAS-STING pathway), and lack of retention within the intestinal lumen. There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a stable, non-absorbable, and highly specific HMGB1-sequestering agent that can function locally within the gut without systemic bioavailability or unintended immune activation. The present invention addresses this need by immobilizing HMGB1-binding DNA oligonucleotides onto inert, particulate carriers designed for oral administration and luminal retention, thereby creating a “molecular sponge” that selectively depletes pathogenic HMGB1 isoforms from the site of inflammation while avoiding the drawbacks of systemic therapies or soluble nucleic acid-based approaches.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a composition for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease comprising DNA-immobilized solid support particles, wherein the DNA comprises an oligonucleotide sequence having high binding affinity for high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein, and the solid support particles are non-absorbable and configured for oral administration to reside within the gastrointestinal lumen. In one embodiment, the oligonucleotide sequence is selected from the group consisting of S1 (12–20.1), S2 (12–20.2), S3 (12-4WJ.1), and S4 (12-DP.1), or functional equivalents thereof that maintain high-affinity binding to HMGB1. In a preferred embodiment, the oligonucleotide is S2, and the solid support is CNBr-activated sepharose beads with an average particle size of approximately 90 micrometers. The DNA is covalently conjugated to the solid support via a C6 amino linker at the 5′ end, and the oligonucleotide may be modified with phosphorothioate linkages to enhance resistance to nuclease degradation, particularly for linear DNA forms such as S1 and S2.

The invention further provides a method for treating inflammatory bowel disease in a subject in need thereof, comprising orally administering to the subject an effective amount of the aforementioned composition. The method results in the sequestration of extracellular HMGB1 from the gastrointestinal lumen, leading to a reduction in mucosal and systemic levels of HMGB1, attenuation of pro-inflammatory cytokine production (including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β), preservation of intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, improvement in clinical symptoms such as weight loss, and amelioration of histological markers of colonic inflammation and injury. The composition is non-toxic to intestinal epithelial cells, does not induce type I interferon responses, and exhibits specificity for HMGB1 over other DNA-binding proteins such as histone H3 or inflammatory mediators like TNF-α.

Additionally, the invention encompasses a method for ex vivo depletion of HMGB1 from a biological sample, comprising contacting the sample with the DNA-immobilized solid support particles under conditions suitable for HMGB1 binding, followed by separation of the particles from the sample, thereby removing HMGB1 from the sample. This method is applicable to various biological fluids, including serum, fecal extracts, and cell culture supernatants, and is useful for diagnostic purposes or for preparing HMGB1-depleted samples for research or therapeutic applications. The DNA-immobilized particles bind multiple redox and acetylation isoforms of HMGB1, including the pro-inflammatory disulfide form and the chemotactic fully reduced form, and retain binding capacity under acidic conditions (pH 1–2) and in the presence of physiological concentrations of DNase I, ensuring functionality throughout the gastrointestinal tract.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to novel therapeutic compositions and methods based on the selective sequestration of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein using DNA-functionalized, non-absorbable particulate carriers. HMGB1, when present in the extracellular milieu of the gastrointestinal tract during inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), acts as a potent pro-inflammatory alarmin that drives mucosal inflammation, disrupts epithelial barrier function, and contributes to disease progression. By exploiting the natural, high-affinity interaction between HMGB1 and specific DNA structures, the invention provides a localized, non-systemic approach to neutralize this key mediator directly at the site of pathology. The core of the invention lies in the immobilization of carefully selected oligonucleotide sequences onto biocompatible, inert solid supports that are engineered to remain within the intestinal lumen following oral administration, thereby functioning as molecular scavengers that bind and remove pathogenic HMGB1 isoforms without being absorbed into systemic circulation or eliciting unwanted immune responses.

The solid support particles used in the invention are preferably composed of cross-linked polysaccharide matrices such as sepharose, agarose, or dextran, which are well-established in biomedical applications for their chemical stability, low non-specific binding, and biocompatibility. In a particularly preferred embodiment, CNBr-activated sepharose 4 Fast Flow resin is employed due to its high coupling efficiency for amine-containing ligands and its optimal particle size range of 45–165 micrometers, with an average diameter of approximately 90 micrometers. This size range is critical for ensuring that the particles are large enough to avoid uptake by M-cells or penetration through a compromised epithelial barrier, yet small enough to be evenly distributed throughout the intestinal lumen and maximize surface area for HMGB1 binding. The particles are rendered non-absorbable by their polymeric nature and size, ensuring that their action is confined to the gastrointestinal tract, thereby minimizing the risk of systemic side effects.

Covalently attached to these solid support particles are oligonucleotide sequences that have been identified and validated for their high binding affinity to HMGB1. Based on extensive biochemical characterization, four primary DNA constructs were evaluated: two non-immunogenic linear duplexes designated S1 (12–20.1) and S2 (12–20.2), a four-way junction structure S3 (12-4WJ.1), and a kinked duplex S4 (12-DP.1). These sequences were selected from prior art based on reported dissociation constants (Kd) ranging from 1 nM to 22 nM for HMGB1 binding. The oligonucleotides are synthesized with a C6 amino group linker conjugated to the 5′ terminus to facilitate covalent coupling to the activated sepharose matrix via standard carbodiimide chemistry. To enhance stability against enzymatic degradation in the harsh environment of the gut, the linear DNA constructs S1 and S2 are synthesized with phosphorothioate backbone modifications at terminal linkages, which confer resistance to exonuclease activity without significantly altering HMGB1 binding affinity. In contrast, the structured forms S3 and S4 utilize native phosphodiester backbones, as their secondary structures inherently provide some protection against nucleases.

The resulting DNA-immobilized particles, referred to herein as B1 (S1-conjugated), B2 (S2-conjugated), B3 (S3-conjugated), and B4 (S4-conjugated) beads, exhibit distinct binding characteristics. Comprehensive binding assays demonstrate that B1, B2, and B3 beads efficiently capture recombinant HMGB1 in a concentration-dependent manner, with B1 and B2 achieving greater than 90% HMGB1 depletion at optimal bead-to-protein ratios. The binding kinetics are rapid, with saturation occurring within 15–30 minutes at room temperature, making them suitable for dynamic environments like the intestinal lumen. Notably, B1 and B2 beads retain their HMGB1-binding capacity even under highly acidic conditions (pH 1–2), simulating gastric transit, whereas B3 beads lose functionality under such conditions. This acid stability is a crucial feature for orally administered therapeutics, ensuring that the beads remain active upon reaching the site of inflammation in the colon. Furthermore, pre-treatment of B2 beads with physiological concentrations of DNase I (40 ng/mL) does not impair their ability to bind HMGB1, confirming that the phosphorothioate modifications effectively protect the immobilized DNA from degradation.

A key aspect of the invention is the ability of the DNA beads to sequester multiple pathogenic isoforms of HMGB1 present in the inflamed gut. Using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis of HMGB1 captured from fecal samples of colitis mice, it was confirmed that the beads bind both the disulfide HMGB1 (cytokine-inducing) and fully reduced HMGB1 (chemotactic) isoforms. Additionally, the captured HMGB1 was found to be hyper-acetylated at nuclear localization sequences, indicative of active secretion by immune cells, distinguishing it from the hypo-acetylated form passively released during necrosis. This broad specificity ensures comprehensive neutralization of HMGB1’s diverse pro-inflammatory activities. Importantly, the DNA beads exhibit high selectivity for HMGB1, showing no significant binding to other inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α or DNA-binding proteins like histone H3, thereby minimizing off-target effects.

The therapeutic efficacy of the invention was validated in two well-established murine models of colitis: acute dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis and chronic spontaneous colitis in IL-10 knockout (KO) mice. Oral administration of B2 beads (300 µL of a 50% slurry) significantly ameliorated disease severity in both models. In DSS-treated mice, B2 bead administration resulted in markedly reduced weight loss (−0.8 ± 0.4 g vs. −3.8 ± 0.5 g in controls over 8 days), lower serum and fecal HMGB1 levels, decreased colonic expression of IL-6 and IL-1β mRNA, improved colon morphology with preserved crypt architecture, and significantly reduced histological inflammation scores (3.8 ± 2.1 vs. 10.8 ± 1.8 in controls). Similarly, in IL-10 KO mice, chronic B2 bead treatment over six weeks led to sustained weight gain, reduced systemic HMGB1, diminished colonic cytokine expression, and less severe histological damage compared to control bead-treated animals. Control experiments with B4 beads, which lack HMGB1-binding capacity, failed to produce any therapeutic benefit, confirming that the observed effects are specifically attributable to HMGB1 sequestration.

Safety and mechanistic studies further support the clinical potential of the invention. Cytotoxicity assays using human intestinal epithelial cell lines (Caco-2 and HeLa) demonstrated that B2 beads do not induce cell death, as measured by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release, even at high concentrations and prolonged exposure times. Moreover, the beads do not stimulate type I interferon (IFN) production in macrophages, as assessed by quantitative PCR for IFN-β mRNA, thereby avoiding potential pro-inflammatory or autoimmune complications associated with nucleic acid sensing pathways. The inert nature of the DNA oligonucleotides themselves was confirmed by their inability to induce TNF release from human macrophages or synergize with bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), underscoring their suitability for use in the microbe-rich gut environment.

### EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS

The preparation and characterization of the DNA-immobilized beads, as well as the in vitro and in vivo validation of their therapeutic efficacy, were conducted according to the following detailed protocols. CNBr-activated sepharose 4 Fast Flow resin was obtained from GE Healthcare and used as the solid support matrix. Oligonucleotides corresponding to sequences S1 (12–20.1), S2 (12–20.2), S3 (12-4WJ.1), and S4 (12-DP.1) were synthesized by Genemed Synthesis, Inc., with a C6 amino linker at the 5′ end for S1, S2, S3, and S4. Phosphorothioate modifications were incorporated into the terminal linkages of S1 and S2 to confer nuclease resistance, while S3 and S4 were synthesized with standard phosphodiester backbones. Conjugation of DNA to the sepharose beads was performed by incubating 8 nmol of S1 or S2 (48 µg) or 4 nmol of S3 or S4 (180 µg) with 1 mL of drained CNBr-activated sepharose beads in 0.1 M NaHCO3 buffer (pH 8.3) for 2 hours at room temperature with gentle rotation, followed by quenching with 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0) to block unreacted sites. Coupling efficiency was determined by measuring the absorbance at 260 nm of the supernatant before and after conjugation, confirming that greater than 90% of the input DNA was immobilized.

For binding assays, recombinant human HMGB1 was expressed in E. coli and purified to homogeneity using established protocols, with endotoxin levels verified to be less than 10 pg/mg protein via Chromogenic Limulus Amebocyte Lysate Assay. Binding capacity was assessed by incubating fixed amounts of DNA beads (5 µL drained volume) with increasing concentrations of HMGB1 (0–3 µg) in 50 µL PBS for 2 hours at room temperature. Bound HMGB1 was separated by centrifugation, and free HMGB1 in the supernatant was quantified by Western blot using anti-HMGB1 antibodies. Kinetic studies involved incubating 20 µL of DNA beads with 500 ng HMGB1 for 0–960 minutes, with samples taken at specified intervals. Acid stability was tested by incubating beads with HMGB1 in PBS adjusted to pH 1, 2, or 7.0 for 1 hour, while DNase resistance was evaluated by pre-treating B2 beads with 40 ng/mL DNase I for 30 minutes at 37°C prior to HMGB1 binding assays.

In vivo studies utilized female BALB/c mice (8–12 weeks old) for acute DSS-induced colitis, induced by administering 4% DSS in drinking water for 5 days followed by normal water for 3 days. B2 or control beads (300 µL of 50% slurry in PBS) were administered orally on days 0, 1, 2, 4, and 6. For chronic colitis, female IL-10 KO mice on a C57BL/6J background were treated with B2 beads every other day starting at 12 weeks of age for six weeks. Body weight was monitored daily or every other day, and mice were euthanized at defined endpoints. Colons were excised, weighed, and processed for histology (H&E staining) and RNA extraction. Histological scoring was performed blinded using a validated system assessing inflammation severity, extent, crypt damage, and percentage involvement, with scores ranging from 0 to 14. Cytokine mRNA levels (IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-β) were quantified by quantitative RT-PCR using gene-specific primers and normalized to HPRT expression. Serum and fecal HMGB1 levels were measured by ELISA or Western blot.

Ex vivo capture experiments involved incubating fecal extracts from DSS-colitis mice or serum from cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced septic mice with DNA beads for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by washing and analysis of bead-bound HMGB1 by Western blot. For LC-MS/MS analysis of HMGB1 isoforms, HMGB1 was captured from colitis fecal samples using B1 or B2 beads, eluted with 8 M urea, and subjected to trypsin or GluC digestion. Redox states and acetylation status were determined using established mass spectrometric methods. Cytotoxicity was assessed by incubating Caco-2 or HeLa cells with increasing concentrations of B2 beads for up to 24 hours and measuring LDH release in the supernatant. Immunogenicity was evaluated by stimulating human primary macrophages or RAW 264.7 cells with DNA oligos or beads and measuring TNF or IFN-β production by ELISA or qPCR, respectively. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with NIH guidelines. Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with p < 0.05 considered significant.
