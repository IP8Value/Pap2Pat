# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to environmental monitoring systems, and more specifically to a low-cost, portable, Internet-of-Things (IoT) enabled sensing and vision platform for measuring human-centric thermal exposure in outdoor urban environments. The system integrates meteorological sensing with computer vision capabilities to simultaneously capture microscale thermal conditions—specifically Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT)—and pedestrian behavior, including the use of shaded versus sun-exposed areas. The invention is particularly suited for deployment in hot, dry climates where solar radiation dominates thermal comfort and where hyperlocal variations in heat exposure significantly influence human activity patterns. The system employs a combination of off-the-shelf sensors, custom hardware design, edge computing, and machine learning algorithms to provide accurate, real-time, privacy-preserving data on both environmental conditions and public space utilization without requiring manual observation or high-cost instrumentation. The technical domain encompasses biometeorology, urban climatology, embedded systems engineering, computer vision, and machine learning for environmental sensing applications.

## BACKGROUND

Urban heat poses a growing threat to public health, productivity, and social well-being, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions experiencing rapid urbanization and climate change. Traditional approaches to monitoring urban heat rely heavily on air temperature measurements obtained from weather stations, which are often located in non-representative settings such as airport tarmacs or open fields. These measurements fail to capture the microscale thermal conditions experienced by pedestrians at street level, where built form, surface materials, vegetation, and solar geometry interact to create highly heterogeneous thermal environments. Consequently, air temperature alone is insufficient for assessing human thermal stress or guiding interventions such as shade provision, cooling infrastructure, or urban planning policies.

A more physiologically relevant metric for evaluating human thermal exposure is the Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT), which quantifies the total radiant heat load from both shortwave (solar) and longwave (terrestrial) radiation incident on a human body. MRT is a key input to established thermal comfort indices such as the Physiologically Equivalent Temperature (PET) and the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI). Accurate MRT measurement typically requires expensive, bulky instrumentation—such as the six-directional radiometer setup based on three orthogonal net radiometers—that captures hemispherical radiation fluxes and computes MRT using the Stefan-Boltzmann law. While scientifically robust, this method is cost-prohibitive (often exceeding $15,000 per unit) and impractical for widespread deployment across urban landscapes.

As a lower-cost alternative, globe thermometers—typically black or gray spherical devices containing a temperature sensor—have been used to estimate MRT by approximating the radiative exchange of a human body. However, these instruments suffer from several limitations: they exhibit slow thermal response times, their accuracy is sensitive to wind speed, globe size, color, and material properties, and they can produce significant errors when partially shaded or exposed to asymmetric radiation fields. Empirical conversion models exist to derive MRT from globe temperature, but these are often calibrated under specific environmental conditions and do not generalize well across diverse urban contexts. Furthermore, existing low-cost MRT sensing platforms lack integration with behavioral data, making it difficult to correlate thermal conditions with actual human presence and activity.

Concurrently, methods for monitoring pedestrian activity in public spaces have evolved from manual counting to automated sensor-based approaches. Computer vision techniques, particularly those leveraging deep learning, now enable robust object detection and crowd estimation from video feeds. Models such as YOLO (You Only Look Once) and advanced shadow detection networks like BDRAR (Bi-directional Feature Pyramid with Recurrent Attention Residual Modules) can identify individuals and classify them as being in sun or shade. However, these systems are typically deployed independently of environmental sensors, resulting in a disconnect between observed behavior and the concurrent microclimate. Moreover, privacy concerns limit the storage and transmission of raw imagery, necessitating on-device processing that discards visual data after extracting quantitative metrics.

Despite advances in both thermal sensing and computer vision, no integrated system currently exists that combines low-cost, accurate MRT estimation with real-time, privacy-preserving pedestrian behavior analysis in a compact, field-deployable IoT platform. Existing solutions are either too expensive for city-scale deployment, lack the necessary environmental fidelity, or fail to link thermal exposure with human activity patterns. This gap impedes evidence-based urban heat mitigation strategies, such as optimizing the placement of shade structures or evaluating the effectiveness of cooling interventions. The present invention addresses these shortcomings by introducing a novel system that synergistically fuses meteorological sensing, edge AI, and cloud connectivity to deliver actionable insights into the interplay between urban microclimate and human behavior.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a low-cost, portable, smart Internet-of-Things (IoT) weather station and vision system, herein referred to as MaRTiny, designed to simultaneously measure hyperlocal Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT) and monitor pedestrian use of shaded versus sun-exposed areas in outdoor urban environments. The system comprises a compact hardware platform integrating meteorological sensors—including a custom gray globe thermometer, air temperature probe, relative humidity sensor, UV sensor, and anemometer—with a low-power edge computing device equipped with a camera and artificial intelligence capabilities. Meteorological data are collected at one-minute intervals and transmitted via Wi-Fi to a cloud-based database, while the vision system performs real-time pedestrian detection and shade classification using deep learning models, preserving privacy by discarding raw images after processing and transmitting only aggregated counts.

A key innovation of the invention is a machine learning-based correction model that significantly improves the accuracy of MRT estimation from low-cost sensor inputs. Traditional empirical models that convert globe temperature to MRT are prone to errors due to sensor limitations, environmental heterogeneity, and partial shading. The present invention overcomes this by training a supervised learning algorithm—preferably a Support Vector Machine (SVM) with a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel—on paired datasets of low-cost sensor readings and high-fidelity MRT measurements obtained from a reference instrument. The trained model uses inputs such as globe temperature, air temperature, relative humidity, and UV intensity to predict MRT with a root mean square error (RMSE) of approximately 4°C, substantially outperforming conventional physics-based conversions.

The vision subsystem employs a two-stage deep learning pipeline: first, a YOLOv3 object detection model identifies pedestrians and other objects of interest in the camera feed; second, a BDRAR-based shadow detection network generates a binary shade map of the scene. By computing the intersection over union (IOU) between the lower half of each pedestrian’s bounding box and the shade map, the system determines whether an individual is experiencing the cooling effect of shade. This approach accounts for the fact that full-body shade is not required for thermal relief and mitigates errors caused by self-cast shadows. The system achieves a pedestrian detection precision of 95% and a shade classification accuracy of 80%.

The entire MaRTiny system is powered by a single 20-watt source, operates autonomously without human intervention, and costs less than $200 to construct, making it suitable for large-scale deployment by municipalities, researchers, and citizen scientists. Data are securely transmitted to a cloud database using the MQTT protocol over Wi-Fi, enabling real-time monitoring and long-term analysis. By correlating high-resolution MRT data with pedestrian behavior, the invention provides unprecedented insights into how people respond to microscale thermal conditions, thereby supporting data-driven urban planning, public health interventions, and climate adaptation strategies in hot, dry climates.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT) Sensing

The core environmental sensing capability of the present invention centers on the accurate and cost-effective measurement of Mean Radiant Temperature (MRT), a critical parameter for assessing human thermal comfort in outdoor settings. MRT represents the uniform temperature of an imaginary blackbody enclosure that would result in the same net radiant heat exchange as the actual non-uniform environment. It integrates contributions from all directions of shortwave solar radiation and longwave terrestrial radiation emitted by surrounding surfaces such as buildings, pavements, and vegetation. In hot, dry climates, MRT can exceed air temperature by more than 30°C in direct sunlight, making it the dominant factor influencing perceived heat stress.

Traditional high-accuracy MRT measurement employs the six-directional method, which utilizes three orthogonal net radiometers to measure incoming and outgoing shortwave and longwave radiation fluxes in six hemispherical directions. These fluxes are then combined using the Stefan-Boltzmann law, weighted by directional factors that account for the cylindrical geometry of the human body, to compute MRT. While this method is considered the gold standard, its implementation requires specialized, expensive equipment (typically costing over $5,000 per radiometer) and is unsuitable for widespread or mobile deployment.

To address this limitation, the present invention adopts a low-cost globe thermometer approach, enhanced by both hardware design and machine learning correction. The system incorporates a custom-built globe thermometer consisting of a standard 38 mm diameter gray ping-pong ball, chosen for its acrylic gray color that closely approximates the average albedo of human skin and typical summer clothing. This globe is affixed directly over a high-precision digital temperature probe, ensuring minimal thermal lag and consistent contact. The gray coloration is critical, as it balances absorption across the solar spectrum and avoids the overestimation common with black globes under high solar irradiance.

Air temperature is measured using a separate, downward-facing white radiation shield—a small cup-shaped enclosure that reflects solar radiation and promotes natural ventilation around the temperature probe. This configuration minimizes radiative heating of the sensor, yielding a more accurate ambient air temperature reading. Relative humidity is captured by a capacitive polymer sensor, while wind speed is measured using a lightweight, three-cup anemometer powered by a voltage step-up circuit from the main 5V supply. Additionally, a UV sensor provides supplementary data on solar intensity, which aids in distinguishing between high-radiation clear-sky conditions and overcast scenarios.

Raw sensor data—including globe temperature, air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, and UV index—are sampled at a high frequency (approximately 80 readings per minute) by an Arduino Uno microcontroller. These readings are buffered and averaged over one-minute intervals to reduce noise and align with typical meteorological reporting standards. The averaged data are then transmitted via UART serial communication to a NodeMCU microcontroller equipped with an ESP8266 Wi-Fi module. The NodeMCU handles secure cloud communication using the MQTT protocol, authenticating with AWS DynamoDB through stored PEM certificate files and transmitting the meteorological data payload every minute.

However, direct conversion of globe temperature to MRT using conventional empirical models—such as those based on the ISO 7726 standard or recent formulations by Vanos et al. (2021)—introduces significant errors in real-world urban settings. These errors arise from multiple sources: spatial offsets between the globe and reference instruments leading to differential shading, slow thermal response of the globe during rapidly changing conditions (e.g., passing clouds), and inaccuracies in low-cost sensor outputs. To overcome these challenges, the present invention introduces a machine learning-based MRT estimation framework that learns the complex, non-linear relationship between the available sensor inputs and true MRT.

This framework treats MRT estimation as a supervised regression problem. Training data are generated by co-locating the MaRTiny system with a high-fidelity MRT reference instrument (e.g., the MaRTy platform) under diverse environmental conditions, including varying solar angles, cloud cover, and urban configurations. The resulting dataset pairs low-cost sensor readings (globe temperature, air temperature, humidity, UV) with ground-truth MRT values. A Support Vector Machine (SVM) with a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel is trained on this dataset, leveraging its ability to model non-linear relationships without requiring extensive feature engineering. The trained model is then deployed on the edge device (e.g., NVIDIA Jetson Nano) to perform real-time inference, converting raw sensor inputs into corrected MRT estimates with significantly reduced error—demonstrated to achieve an RMSE of 4°C compared to the 10°C error of uncorrected empirical methods.

This hybrid approach—combining purpose-built low-cost hardware with intelligent software correction—enables the invention to deliver MRT measurements that are both scientifically credible and economically scalable, filling a critical gap in urban climate monitoring infrastructure.

## EXAMPLES

### System Overview

The MaRTiny system is implemented as a fully integrated, field-deployable IoT platform designed for autonomous operation in outdoor urban environments. The physical embodiment consists of a weatherproof enclosure housing all electronic components, mounted on a standard tripod or fixed pole at a height of approximately 1.5 meters to simulate pedestrian-level exposure. The system is divided into two primary functional modules: the meteorological sensing unit and the vision processing unit, both powered by a shared 20-watt DC power supply (5V/4A).

The meteorological sensing unit is built around an Arduino Uno microcontroller, which interfaces with four key sensors: (1) a DS18B20 digital temperature sensor embedded within a 38 mm gray acrylic globe for globe temperature measurement; (2) a second DS18B20 sensor housed within a downward-facing white plastic cup to measure shaded air temperature; (3) a DHT22 capacitive sensor for relative humidity; (4) a Davis anemometer modified for low-voltage operation to measure wind speed; and (5) a GUVA-S12SD UV sensor for solar UV intensity. All sensors are calibrated against reference instruments prior to deployment. The Arduino samples these sensors at 1 kHz, buffers the data, and computes one-minute averages, which are then transmitted via UART to a NodeMCU ESP8266 microcontroller.

The NodeMCU serves as the communication hub, managing secure data transmission to the cloud. It stores AWS IoT Core credentials and root certificates in its flash memory, establishing a TLS-encrypted connection to an AWS DynamoDB NoSQL database using the MQTT protocol. Every minute, upon receiving the averaged meteorological data from the Arduino, the NodeMCU formats the payload as a JSON object and publishes it to a dedicated MQTT topic, ensuring reliable, low-latency data ingestion even under intermittent network conditions.

The vision processing unit is centered on an NVIDIA Jetson Nano edge AI computer, configured in 10W performance mode to support real-time deep learning inference. A 5-megapixel MIPI camera module is mounted adjacent to the meteorological sensors, providing a wide-angle field of view of the surrounding area. Video frames are captured at 15 frames per second and processed in real time using a GStreamer pipeline optimized for low latency. The Jetson Nano runs two concurrent deep learning models: a YOLOv3-Darknet object detector fine-tuned for pedestrian identification, and a pre-trained BDRAR network for pixel-level shadow segmentation. Raw images are never stored or transmitted; instead, the system extracts only the number of detected pedestrians, their bounding box coordinates, and a binary classification of sun/shade status based on overlap with the shadow map. These anonymized metrics are formatted and sent to the same AWS DynamoDB instance via the Jetson’s onboard USB Wi-Fi adapter, synchronized with the meteorological data stream.

The entire system is designed for modularity and scalability. Additional sensors (e.g., for particulate matter or noise) can be added to the Arduino bus without redesign, and the vision pipeline can be extended to detect other objects such as bicycles, pets, or umbrellas. Power consumption is carefully managed, with the total draw remaining under 18W, allowing for solar-powered operation in remote locations. The compact form factor (approximately 20 cm × 20 cm × 30 cm) and sub-$200 bill of materials make MaRTiny ideal for dense deployment across parks, sidewalks, and transit corridors, enabling city-scale mapping of thermal exposure and human behavior.

### Machine Learning Algorithm Development

The development of the machine learning algorithm for MRT estimation constitutes a central inventive aspect of the present system. Recognizing that low-cost sensors introduce systematic and stochastic errors that cannot be fully corrected by physics-based models, the inventors formulated MRT prediction as a data-driven regression task. The process begins with the creation of a labeled training dataset, wherein the MaRTiny system is co-located with a high-accuracy reference instrument—such as the MaRTy platform, which implements the six-directional radiometer method—for extended periods under diverse meteorological conditions. During this co-deployment, both systems log synchronized data: MaRTiny records its suite of low-cost sensor readings (globe temperature T_g, air temperature T_a, relative humidity RH, UV index, and wind speed U), while MaRTy provides ground-truth MRT values computed via the Stefan-Boltzmann equation.

From this paired dataset, feature vectors X = [T_g, T_a, RH, UV] are extracted at one-minute intervals, with corresponding labels y = MRT_true. The inventors evaluated multiple machine learning architectures, including linear regression, polynomial regression, artificial neural networks (ANNs), and support vector machines (SVMs) with various kernels. Initial experiments revealed that linear models failed to capture the non-linear dependence of MRT on globe temperature, particularly under high solar irradiance where convective cooling effects become significant. ANNs with ReLU activation functions showed promise but required substantial computational resources for training and were prone to overfitting on the limited dataset.

The optimal solution was found to be an SVM with a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, which offers a favorable balance of accuracy, generalization, and computational efficiency. The RBF kernel implicitly maps the input features into a high-dimensional space where non-linear relationships become linearly separable, enabling the model to learn complex corrections for sensor biases and environmental confounders. Hyperparameters—including the regularization parameter C and the kernel coefficient gamma—were tuned using five-fold cross-validation on a subset of the training data, with performance evaluated using root mean square error (RMSE) on a held-out test set.

The final trained SVM model takes the four meteorological inputs and outputs a corrected MRT estimate. When deployed on the Jetson Nano, the model executes inference in under 10 milliseconds, allowing real-time MRT computation at the edge. Validation on an independent evaluation dataset—collected at a different location and time—demonstrated an RMSE of 4.0°C, a 60% improvement over the 10.0°C error of the uncorrected empirical model. Notably, the model effectively compensated for a known source of error: morning shading of the MaRTiny globe by a nearby palm tree, which caused conventional methods to underestimate MRT by up to 15°C. The SVM learned to recognize this pattern from the correlated drop in UV and subtle changes in air temperature and humidity, adjusting its prediction accordingly.

For the vision subsystem, the pedestrian and shade detection pipeline was developed using a modular deep learning approach. The YOLOv3 object detector was selected for its speed-accuracy trade-off and compatibility with TensorRT optimization. The model was converted from its original Darknet format to a TensorRT engine, reducing inference time from 120 ms to 250 ms per frame down to 250 ms, achieving a stable 4 frames per second on the Jetson Nano—sufficient for pedestrian counting in typical urban scenes. The BDRAR shadow detection network, pre-trained on the SBU Shadow Dataset, was used without fine-tuning due to its robust generalization across lighting conditions. The shade classification logic implements a region-of-interest (ROI) strategy: only the lower 50% of each pedestrian’s bounding box is considered for shade overlap, as this corresponds to the torso and legs—the primary areas affected by ground-level shade. An IOU threshold of 0.4 (i.e., 40% of the ROI overlapping with the shadow map) was empirically determined to maximize agreement with manual annotations, yielding an 80% accuracy in distinguishing sun-exposed from shaded individuals.

### System Evaluation

The MaRTiny system was rigorously evaluated through a series of field experiments conducted over three days in Tempe, Arizona, USA, during peak summer conditions. Two identical MaRTiny units were co-located with a MaRTy reference platform at two distinct sun-exposed sites: an open plaza and a street canyon. Synchronized data logging occurred at 2-second intervals for MaRTy and 1-minute intervals for MaRTiny, with 1-minute averages of MaRTy data used for comparison. Additionally, a custom image dataset was collected for vision system validation, comprising 30 annotated images and short video clips captured at random intervals.

For MRT estimation, the uncorrected MaRTiny system—using the empirical model from Vanos et al. (2021)—exhibited a root mean square error (RMSE) of 10.0°C against MaRTy ground truth. The largest discrepancies occurred during morning hours when a nearby palm tree cast partial shade on the MaRTiny globe but not on the MaRTy radiometers, leading to underestimation of MRT by up to 15°C. After applying the SVM-RBF correction model, the RMSE decreased to 4.0°C across the full diurnal cycle, and to less than 3.0°C when excluding the problematic morning hours. The model demonstrated particular strength in high-irradiance afternoon conditions, where it reduced overestimation errors common with gray globes.

The vision system was evaluated using standard computer vision metrics. For object detection, mean Average Precision (mAP) was calculated across 30 manually annotated images containing 50+ objects. Using an IoU threshold of 0.5, the YOLOv3 model achieved an mAP of 55%, consistent with published benchmarks. Crucially, the Average Precision for the pedestrian class exceeded 85%, confirming suitability for the primary application. Shade detection was assessed on a per-pixel basis using 30 shadow-annotated images, yielding a precision of 90% for the BDRAR network. The integrated pedestrian-in-shade algorithm was tested on a separate set of 50 images, with results compared to manual ground truth. The system achieved an overall accuracy of 80%, with most errors occurring in edge cases where pedestrians stood at the boundary between sun and shade or when occlusions distorted bounding boxes.

Data transmission reliability was verified over 72 continuous hours, with 99.8% of meteorological and vision data packets successfully received by the AWS DynamoDB instance. Power consumption remained stable at 17.5W, confirming compatibility with standard solar charging setups. Privacy preservation was validated through audit logs showing that no raw images or personally identifiable information were ever stored or transmitted.

### Discussion

The MaRTiny system represents a significant advancement in the field of urban climate monitoring by integrating low-cost environmental sensing with privacy-conscious computer vision in a single, deployable platform. Its primary innovation lies not in the novelty of individual components—which are largely drawn from existing open-source hardware and software—but in their synergistic combination and the introduction of machine learning to correct inherent limitations of affordable sensors. This approach enables scientific-grade MRT estimation at a fraction of the cost of traditional systems, democratizing access to human-centric thermal data for municipalities, researchers, and community groups.

The achieved RMSE of 4°C for MRT estimation, while not matching the sub-1°C accuracy of six-directional radiometers, is sufficient for most applied urban planning and public health purposes. Thermal comfort indices such as PET are relatively insensitive to MRT errors below 5°C, and the system’s ability to capture relative spatial and temporal variations in heat exposure is more valuable than absolute precision for decision-making. Moreover, the machine learning correction model is inherently adaptive; as more co-location data are collected across diverse cities and seasons, the model can be retrained to improve generalization and robustness.

The vision system’s 80% accuracy in shade classification, though imperfect, provides a reliable proxy for behavioral response to thermal conditions. Manual observation studies are labor-intensive and subject to observer bias, whereas MaRTiny offers continuous, objective data. Future iterations could incorporate stereo vision or depth sensors to resolve 3D ambiguities, but the current 2D approach strikes a pragmatic balance between accuracy and cost.

Limitations remain. The system assumes static deployment; mobility would introduce motion artifacts and complicate shade mapping. Sensor calibration drift over time may require periodic recalibration against reference instruments. The machine learning model’s performance is contingent on the representativeness of its training data—if deployed in a radically different climate (e.g., humid tropics), retraining would be necessary. Nevertheless, MaRTiny establishes a scalable, extensible framework for urban climate informatics, bridging the gap between environmental science and human behavior in the era of smart cities.
