# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to nuclear safeguards and non-proliferation verification technologies, and more particularly to a method and system for remotely monitoring the fissile isotope inventory and operational status of nuclear reactors—including research reactors, small modular reactors (SMRs), and other sealed-core or continuously refueled reactor systems—by detecting thermalized neutrons emanating from the reactor core at stand-off distances. The invention enables real-time, unattended, and non-intrusive verification of declared nuclear activities by correlating neutron count rates measured outside reactor shielding with reactor thermal power and isotopic composition, thereby providing a robust means to detect undeclared changes in fuel inventory that may indicate diversion of nuclear material for non-peaceful purposes. This technique leverages the physical principle that the neutron flux per unit thermal power within a reactor core is uniquely dependent on the weighted sum of fissile isotope masses present—such as uranium-235, plutonium-239, and plutonium-241—due to their distinct fission cross-sections and energy release per fission event. By deploying an array of large-area thermal neutron detectors at multiple strategic locations around a reactor facility—at distances up to 100 meters or more—and analyzing the temporal correlation between detected neutron rates and declared reactor power, the invention provides a scalable, economical, and tamper-resistant safeguarding mechanism suitable for implementation by international regulatory bodies such as the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA). The system is especially advantageous for monitoring reactor types that pose heightened safeguards challenges, including those with limited inspector access, sealed cores, online refueling capabilities, or use of highly enriched uranium fuels.

## BACKGROUND

Nuclear safeguards are essential to ensuring that nuclear materials and facilities are used exclusively for peaceful purposes, as mandated under international treaties such as the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT) and verified through inspections conducted by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA). Traditional safeguards measures at nuclear power reactors have relied on a combination of physical inventory verification, tamper-indicating seals, video surveillance, and review of operational records. These methods are generally effective for large commercial light-water reactors where fuel assemblies remain intact during operation and are only accessed during scheduled outages. However, emerging reactor technologies—particularly small modular reactors (SMRs) and research reactors—present significant new challenges for conventional safeguards approaches. Many SMRs are designed with sealed, long-life cores that preclude direct sampling or visual inspection of fuel over their operational lifetime, while research reactors often employ online refueling, utilize highly enriched uranium (HEU) or low-enriched uranium (LEU) fuels with high fissile density, and feature accessible hot cells and irradiation facilities that increase the risk of undeclared material diversion.

The IAEA has explicitly identified the development of remote, non-intrusive monitoring technologies as a high priority in its Long-Term Research and Development Plan for Safeguards. Specifically, the agency seeks instruments capable of detecting “process emanations”—i.e., physical signatures such as radiation, heat, or particulates—that correlate with nuclear fuel cycle activities without requiring internal access to reactor systems. Among potential emanations, neutrons represent a particularly compelling signal because they are directly produced by fission events and cannot be easily shielded or spoofed without significant engineering effort. While neutron detection has long been used internally within reactors for control and safety functions, its application for external, stand-off safeguards monitoring has remained underexplored due to assumptions about signal attenuation through shielding and background interference.

Prior art in neutron-based reactor monitoring has primarily focused on using fast neutrons leaking from reactor cores to infer internal power distribution or fuel burnup profiles. For example, certain studies have employed fast neutron spectrometry outside reactor biological shields to reconstruct axial or radial power densities. However, these approaches typically require complex detector arrays, high-energy resolution spectrometers, or close proximity to the core—conditions that are impractical for routine safeguards deployment at diverse reactor sites worldwide. Moreover, such methods do not explicitly link neutron leakage rates to the absolute or relative inventory of specific fissile isotopes, which is critical for verifying compliance with declared fuel cycles and detecting potential diversion scenarios.

A fundamental limitation of existing external monitoring techniques is their insensitivity to isotopic composition. Reactor thermal power alone does not reveal whether the energy is being generated predominantly by uranium-235 fission (as in fresh LEU fuel) or by plutonium-239 (as in recycled or bred fuel). Since plutonium is a weapons-usable material, the ability to distinguish between these fissile contributors is vital for timely detection of undeclared reprocessing or fuel substitution. Conventional safeguards rely on periodic destructive assay or gamma spectroscopy during refueling outages to assess isotopic content, but these methods are incompatible with sealed-core SMRs or reactors that operate continuously without shutdown.

Furthermore, current unattended monitoring systems—such as video cameras on spent fuel pools or seal integrity sensors—are vulnerable to spoofing or circumvention. An adversary could, for instance, maintain normal reactor power while diverting fuel if external signatures do not reflect changes in core composition. There exists, therefore, a critical need for a safeguards technology that provides continuous, real-time correlation between reactor power and fissile inventory through an externally measurable physical quantity that is inherently tied to the nuclear physics of fission.

The present invention addresses these gaps by demonstrating that thermalized neutrons detected at stand-off distances—even through substantial building infrastructure and reactor shielding—exhibit a count rate per unit thermal power that is directly proportional to a weighted sum of fissile isotope masses in the core. This proportionality arises from the differing microscopic fission cross-sections and energy release per fission among key isotopes like U-235, Pu-239, and Pu-241. Crucially, this relationship holds despite simplifications in modeling neutron transport, as validated by empirical data from the National Research Universal (NRU) research reactor. The invention further shows that deploying multiple large-area thermal neutron detectors at different azimuthal and elevational positions around a reactor facility enables discrimination between genuine core-related signals and local environmental interferences—such as temporary shielding from fuel handling equipment—thereby enhancing reliability and resistance to tampering.

Unlike prior approaches that require spectral unfolding or complex inversion algorithms, the method disclosed herein operates on raw count rates normalized to declared reactor power, making it computationally lightweight and suitable for autonomous, long-term deployment. The use of commercially available, ruggedized thermal neutron detectors—such as boron-lined proportional counters or helium-3 tubes—ensures cost-effectiveness and ease of integration into existing IAEA monitoring frameworks. The system’s sensitivity to kilogram-level changes in fissile inventory within weeks, rather than months, meets or exceeds current IAEA timeliness goals for detecting significant quantities of diverted material.

Thus, the background reveals a clear technological void: the absence of a practical, physics-based, externally deployable system that continuously verifies both reactor operational status and fissile isotope inventory through passive neutron detection. The present invention fills this void by transforming the well-understood principles of reactor neutronics into a field-deployable safeguards tool with demonstrated efficacy at real-world reactor facilities.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for remotely monitoring the fissile isotope inventory and operational integrity of a nuclear reactor by detecting thermalized neutrons emanating from the reactor core at stand-off distances of up to 100 meters or more from the core center. The invention exploits the fundamental physical relationship between the in-core neutron flux per unit thermal reactor power and the weighted sum of fissile isotope masses present in the core, where the weighting factors are derived from each isotope’s thermal neutron fission cross-section and energy released per fission event. By measuring the neutron count rate using one or more large-area thermal neutron detectors positioned outside the reactor’s primary biological shielding and normalizing this rate to the concurrently measured or declared thermal reactor power, the invention enables continuous, non-intrusive verification of the reactor’s declared fuel cycle and detection of undeclared changes in fissile inventory that may indicate material diversion.

In one aspect, the invention comprises deploying at least one thermal neutron detector at a fixed location external to the reactor containment structure, wherein the detector is configured to produce a time-stamped record of neutron interaction events. The detector is preferably a large-area device—such as a boron-coated straw (BCS) array or a helium-3-filled proportional counter—with an active detection area of at least 0.1 square meters to ensure sufficient count rates at stand-off distances. The detector output is processed to generate a time-series of count rates, which are then correlated with the reactor’s thermal power data obtained from plant instrumentation or operator declarations.

In another aspect, the invention includes a method of establishing a baseline relationship between the normalized neutron count rate (i.e., counts per second per megawatt of thermal power) and the expected fissile isotope composition of the reactor core, as determined by reactor physics simulations or historical operational data. Subsequent deviations from this baseline—beyond statistically defined thresholds accounting for environmental and operational variability—are flagged as potential anomalies warranting further investigation.

In a further aspect, the invention employs a network of two or more neutron detectors positioned at different locations around the reactor facility—such as at varying distances, azimuthal angles, or elevations relative to the core—to enable discrimination between true core-related signals and localized interferences. For example, transient reductions in count rate caused by temporary shielding from fuel handling equipment will affect only detectors with line-of-sight to the interference source, whereas genuine changes in core isotopic composition will manifest consistently across all detectors. This multi-detector approach enhances the system’s robustness against both inadvertent operational effects and deliberate attempts to spoof or mask diversion activities.

The invention is particularly suited for application to reactor types that pose heightened safeguards challenges, including research reactors with online refueling capabilities, small modular reactors with sealed cores, and heavy-water-moderated systems that breed plutonium during operation. Empirical validation at the National Research Universal (NRU) research reactor demonstrated that the normalized neutron count rate varied linearly with the simulated weighted fissile isotopic composition over periods of weeks to months, with agreement within 5% between measured and calculated values. The system detected changes equivalent to the addition or removal of approximately 1 kilogram of uranium-235 or plutonium-239 within a timeframe consistent with IAEA timeliness goals for safeguards verification.

Additionally, the invention provides a framework for integrating neutron monitoring data with other safeguards measures—such as design information verification and random physical inspections—to mitigate potential vulnerabilities, such as the placement of extraneous neutron sources or moderator materials near detectors. The simplicity, passivity, and physics-based foundation of the technique make it highly resistant to spoofing, as any attempt to mimic the expected neutron signature would require precise replication of the time-dependent, multi-location correlation pattern inherent to genuine reactor operation.

In summary, the invention offers a novel, field-deployable solution for real-time, remote verification of nuclear reactor fuel cycles through passive neutron detection, thereby significantly enhancing the IAEA’s ability to detect undeclared nuclear activities and prevent the diversion of fissile materials for non-peaceful purposes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a comprehensive system and methodology for the remote, non-intrusive monitoring of nuclear reactor operations and fissile material inventory through the detection of thermalized neutrons at stand-off distances. The following detailed description sets forth the technical foundations, operational procedures, hardware configurations, data processing techniques, and safeguards applications of the invention, with reference to empirical validation conducted at the National Research Universal (NRU) research reactor and extrapolation to other reactor types including small modular reactors (SMRs).

At the core of the invention lies the recognition that the neutron population within a thermal nuclear reactor—and consequently the rate at which neutrons leak from the core and are detectable outside shielding—is not solely a function of reactor thermal power, but also depends critically on the isotopic composition of the fissile material in the core. This dependence arises from the distinct nuclear properties of the primary fissile isotopes: uranium-235 (U-235), plutonium-239 (Pu-239), and plutonium-241 (Pu-241). Each of these isotopes exhibits a unique combination of thermal neutron fission cross-section (σ_f) and energy released per fission event (E_f). As shown in Table 1 of the research paper, U-235 has a thermal fission cross-section of approximately 582.6 barns and releases about 201.7 MeV per fission, whereas Pu-239 has a cross-section of 748.1 barns and releases 210.0 MeV, and Pu-241 has a cross-section of 1011.1 barns with 212.4 MeV per fission. These differences mean that, for a given thermal power output, a core rich in Pu-239 will sustain a lower neutron population than one rich in U-235, because each Pu-239 fission contributes more energy but requires fewer neutrons to sustain the chain reaction due to its higher cross-section. Conversely, a U-235-dominated core requires a higher neutron flux to achieve the same power level.

This physical principle is mathematically captured in Equation (2) of the research paper, which expresses the total thermal power P_tot as:

P_tot = V ⟨φ⟩ Σ_i N_i ⟨σ_f,i⟩ E_f,i

where V is the core volume, ⟨φ⟩ is the spatially and energetically averaged neutron flux, N_i is the number density of the i-th fissile isotope, and the summation runs over all fissile species. Rearranging this equation yields the average neutron flux per unit power:

⟨φ⟩ / P_tot = [V Σ_i N_i ⟨σ_f,i⟩ E_f,i]⁻¹

Since the number density N_i is proportional to the mass m_i of the isotope (via Avogadro’s number and atomic weight), this ratio becomes a function of the fissile isotope masses. Critically, the neutron detector count rate n_det at a stand-off location is proportional to the core neutron population, which in turn is proportional to ⟨φ⟩ V / ⟨v⟩ (where ⟨v⟩ is the average neutron speed). Thus, under steady-state conditions and assuming constant detector efficiency and geometry, the normalized count rate n_det / P_tot is directly proportional to ⟨φ⟩ / P_tot and therefore serves as a proxy for the weighted fissile isotopic composition.

The invention implements this principle through a system comprising one or more large-area thermal neutron detectors deployed at fixed external locations around a reactor facility. Thermal neutron detectors are preferred because they offer high intrinsic efficiency for neutrons in the thermal energy range (~0.025 eV), and because the majority of neutrons leaking from a thermal reactor—after moderation by the core reflector, coolant, and surrounding structures—arrive at external locations with energies in the epithermal to thermal range. The detectors used in the empirical validation include two types: (1) a Boron-Coated Straw (BCS) detector consisting of 49 individual boron-10-coated aluminum straws arranged in seven tubes, each 1 meter long and 2.54 cm in diameter, providing a total active area of 0.18 m²; and (2) a B10+ detector comprising seven stainless steel tubes lined with enriched boron-10 and filled with a mixture of helium-3, argon, and carbon dioxide, also presenting an active area of 0.18 m². Both detector types are commercially available, ruggedized for field deployment, and capable of operating unattended for extended periods.

Each detector is connected to a data acquisition system that records individual neutron interaction events in list mode with precise time stamps (e.g., using a National Instruments cRIO real-time controller or a Quaesta Instruments Neutron Pulse Monitor). This high-resolution temporal data allows for post-processing into time-series histograms of count rate versus time, with binning intervals as short as 10 seconds or as long as several hours, depending on the required statistical precision and operational context. Concurrently, the reactor’s thermal power is obtained from plant process instrumentation—typically derived from the product of coolant mass flow rate and temperature rise across the core—and synchronized with the neutron data via a common time reference (e.g., GPS or network time protocol).

The operational procedure begins with a calibration phase during which the system establishes a baseline relationship between the normalized neutron count rate (n_det / P_tot) and the expected fissile inventory. This baseline may be derived from reactor physics simulations (e.g., using the TRIAD neutron diffusion code referenced in the research paper) that track isotopic evolution over time based on declared fuel loading, burnup, and refueling schedules. Alternatively, in the absence of simulation data, the baseline can be established empirically by averaging the normalized count rate over a period of stable operation with known fuel composition. Once the baseline is set, ongoing measurements are compared against it to detect statistically significant deviations.

A key innovation of the invention is the use of multiple detectors at spatially separated locations to enhance signal discrimination and tamper resistance. In the NRU validation, one detector was placed inside the reactor building approximately 17 meters from the core but two floors below and behind substantial shielding (45 cm water, 45 cm steel, 700 cm high-density concrete), while another was located in a trailer outside the building at 69 meters distance with less intervening material (30–300 cm water, 120–30 cm steel, 270–15 cm concrete). Despite the greater distance, the external detector recorded a higher count rate due to reduced shielding, and more importantly, it exhibited transient 50% reductions in signal during online refueling operations when a fuel flask was positioned atop the reactor, temporarily blocking neutron leakage paths. The internal detector, shielded from this line-of-sight interference, maintained a stable count rate. By comparing the two signals in real time, the system could distinguish between genuine core changes (which affect both detectors similarly) and local obstructions (which affect only one). This multi-perspective approach is essential for reliable anomaly detection in complex operational environments.

The invention further incorporates environmental monitoring and correction mechanisms to account for factors that may influence neutron detection efficiency, such as atmospheric pressure, temperature, and humidity, which affect moderator density and thus neutron thermalization. However, empirical data from the NRU tests showed that even without explicit environmental corrections, the scatter in the n_det / P_tot ratio was dominated by operational variations (e.g., minor power fluctuations, fuel movements) rather than counting statistics, indicating that the method is robust under real-world conditions. Advanced implementations may integrate auxiliary sensors (e.g., barometers, thermometers) to apply real-time corrections or use machine learning algorithms to model and subtract environmental noise.

From a safeguards perspective, the invention provides several critical advantages. First, it enables continuous, unattended monitoring without requiring internal access to the reactor, making it ideal for sealed-core SMRs or facilities with restricted inspector presence. Second, it directly verifies the isotopic composition of the core, not just power levels, thereby detecting scenarios where an operator might maintain declared power while substituting or removing fissile material. Third, the system’s reliance on fundamental nuclear physics makes it inherently difficult to spoof; for example, placing an external neutron source near a detector would produce a constant or decaying signal, not one that correlates precisely with reactor power transients during startup, shutdown, or load-following. Similarly, adding moderator around a detector to compensate for reduced core neutron output would alter the energy response in a detectable way, especially if multiple detectors with different moderator configurations are used.

The sensitivity of the system has been quantified through NRU data: a change of approximately 1 kg in U-235 inventory produces a ~4% shift in n_det / P_tot, while 100 g of Pu-239 causes a ~0.5% shift. Given typical count rates of tens to hundreds of counts per minute and power measurement uncertainties of <1%, the system can detect kilogram-level diversions within days to weeks, well within the IAEA’s 90-day timeliness goal for plutonium diversion. Moreover, the method is scalable to other reactor types, as demonstrated by simulations of molten salt reactors (MSRs) and high-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGRs), which show significant time-dependent variation in weighted isotopic composition due to online fuel addition or burnup effects.

In practice, the invention would be deployed as part of an integrated safeguards approach. Neutron monitoring data would be transmitted securely to IAEA headquarters for analysis, with alerts triggered by deviations exceeding predefined thresholds. Random physical inspections could verify detector integrity and calibration, while design information verification would ensure that detector locations are optimized for maximum signal-to-noise ratio and minimal vulnerability to masking. The system’s passive nature—requiring no reactor modifications or operator intervention—ensures compatibility with diverse regulatory regimes and minimizes operational burden on facility operators.

In conclusion, the detailed embodiment of the invention represents a paradigm shift in nuclear safeguards: from periodic, intrusive inspections to continuous, physics-based remote monitoring. By transforming the unavoidable neutron emanations from reactor cores into a precise metric of fissile inventory, the invention provides a powerful new tool for verifying the peaceful use of nuclear energy and deterring proliferation.
