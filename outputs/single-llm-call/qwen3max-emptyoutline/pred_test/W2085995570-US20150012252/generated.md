# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of genomic analysis and, more specifically, to methods and systems for detecting copy number variations (CNVs) in single cells using low-coverage whole genome sequencing data. The invention provides a bioinformatics pipeline that corrects for whole genome amplification (WGA)-induced biases, particularly those associated with GC content, and accurately identifies CNVs and aneuploidies at the single-cell level. The disclosed method is particularly suited for clinical applications such as pre-implantation genetic diagnosis/screening (PGD/PGS), noninvasive prenatal diagnosis using fetal nucleated red blood cells (NRBCs), and cancer screening through the detection of circulating tumor cells. The invention further encompasses computational algorithms for GC bias correction, binary segmentation for breakpoint localization, and dynamic threshold determination for signal filtering, thereby enabling high sensitivity and specificity in CNV detection even under conditions of limited DNA input and low sequencing coverage.

## BACKGROUND

Copy number variations (CNVs) are structural alterations in the genome involving gains or losses of DNA segments larger than 1 kilobase (kb). These variations have been implicated in a wide spectrum of human diseases, including developmental disorders such as 22q11.2 deletion/duplication syndrome and Cri-du-Chat syndrome (5p deletion), as well as various forms of cancer. Traditional approaches to CNV detection have relied on bulk cell analyses, which average genomic signals across heterogeneous populations and thus obscure cell-to-cell variability. However, the advent of single-cell genomics has revealed that somatic mosaicism and clonal evolution—particularly in oncology—are driven by CNVs that may be present in only a subset of cells. Consequently, single-cell resolution has become essential for understanding disease etiology, tracking tumor evolution, and enabling precise diagnostic interventions.

In clinical reproductive medicine, single-cell analysis plays a critical role in pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) and pre-implantation genetic screening (PGS), where individual blastomeres or trophectoderm cells are biopsied from embryos to assess chromosomal integrity prior to implantation. Similarly, noninvasive prenatal testing (NIPT) is increasingly exploring the potential of isolating rare fetal nucleated red blood cells (NRBCs) from maternal circulation, each representing a single fetal cell whose genome can be interrogated for pathogenic CNVs without invasive procedures. In oncology, the detection of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) from peripheral blood offers a liquid biopsy approach for early cancer diagnosis and monitoring, again requiring robust single-cell genomic analysis.

A major technical challenge in single-cell CNV analysis stems from the necessity of whole genome amplification (WGA), which is required to generate sufficient DNA from a single cell for downstream sequencing or hybridization. Common WGA methods, such as degenerate oligonucleotide-primed PCR (DOP-PCR) or multiple displacement amplification (MDA), introduce significant amplification biases that distort the true copy number landscape. These biases are strongly correlated with local genomic features, particularly GC content. Regions with extremely low (<40%) or high (>48%) GC content are consistently under-amplified or over-amplified, respectively, leading to false-positive or false-negative CNV calls. Additional confounding factors include proximity to telomeres and centromeres, repetitive elements, and chromatin structure, all of which can impede uniform amplification.

Historically, array comparative genomic hybridization (aCGH) has been employed for single-cell CNV profiling, wherein test and reference DNA are differentially labeled and co-hybridized to oligonucleotide arrays. However, aCGH suffers from limited resolution, dependence on predefined probe locations, and susceptibility to WGA-induced noise that cannot be fully normalized by reference-based comparison. More recently, massively parallel sequencing (MPS), also known as next-generation sequencing (NGS), has emerged as a superior alternative due to its genome-wide, unbiased coverage and quantitative nature. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) enables digital counting of sequence reads mapped to genomic intervals, providing a direct measure of relative copy number.

Despite these advantages, standard WGS-based CNV detection tools—such as SegSeq, CNVnator, or Control-FREEC—were primarily designed for bulk DNA samples and assume relatively uniform sequencing depth. When applied to WGA-amplified single-cell data, these tools exhibit poor performance due to uncorrected amplification artifacts. For instance, SegSeq employs a comparative genomic strategy akin to aCGH, using a control sample to normalize case data. While this mitigates some technical variance, it fails to account for systematic, sequence-dependent biases inherent to WGA, particularly when both case and control undergo independent amplification. Moreover, fixed thresholds for calling CNVs often misclassify regions with extreme GC content as aberrant, inflating false positive rates.

Several attempts have been made to address GC bias in sequencing data. Loess regression and polynomial smoothing are commonly used to model and correct read depth as a function of GC content. However, these methods typically assume that the majority of the genome is diploid and that deviations are sparse, an assumption that breaks down in highly aneuploid or mosaic samples. Furthermore, in low-coverage single-cell sequencing (typically <10% genome coverage), the sparsity of reads exacerbates statistical noise, making conventional correction strategies unstable.

Thus, there remains a critical need for a specialized, robust, and clinically applicable bioinformatics framework tailored to single-cell CNV detection from WGA-MPS data. Such a method must explicitly model and correct WGA-induced GC bias, adaptively determine significance thresholds based on local genomic context, and precisely localize breakpoints even with limited sequencing depth. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel weighted correction strategy, a refined binary segmentation algorithm, and a dynamic thresholding mechanism that collectively achieve high sensitivity and specificity in CNV detection at the single-cell level.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive bioinformatics method and system for the detection of copy number variations (CNVs) and aneuploidies in single cells using low-coverage whole genome sequencing data generated after whole genome amplification (WGA). The method is specifically designed to overcome the limitations imposed by WGA-induced amplification biases, particularly those associated with genomic GC content, which have historically compromised the accuracy of single-cell CNV analysis.

The core of the invention comprises three integrated computational components: (1) a GC bias correction module that employs a weighted normalization strategy to adjust read counts based on local GC content; (2) a binary segmentation algorithm optimized for low-coverage data to accurately identify CNV breakpoints; and (3) a dynamic threshold determination mechanism that sets adaptive significance cutoffs for CNV calls based on the empirical distribution of corrected read counts within GC-matched genomic windows.

In the first component, the method begins by dividing the reference genome into non-overlapping observation windows of approximately 150 kb, dynamically adjusted to ensure uniform expected read counts based on simulated mappability. For each window, the relative read number (RRN) is computed as the ratio of observed reads to the genome-wide average. A strong correlation between RRN and GC content is observed, with both GC-poor and GC-rich regions exhibiting systematic under- or over-amplification. To correct this, a weighting coefficient is derived for each window based on the average RRN of all windows sharing the same GC content (rounded to the nearest 1%). This coefficient is then used to rescale the raw read count, yielding a corrected read number (CRN) and, subsequently, a corrected relative read number (CRN normalized to the global mean). This weighted correction strategy effectively removes over 99.9% of GC-related bias, as quantified by a novel GC-bias index.

The second component utilizes a binary segmentation algorithm to partition the genome into segments of constant copy number. The algorithm initiates by identifying candidate breakpoints through a run-test that evaluates the statistical significance of differences in CRN between adjacent genomic regions. The top 3,000 most significant candidate breakpoints are retained. An iterative merging process then refines this set by sequentially removing the least significant breakpoint (i.e., the one with the highest p-value) until all remaining breakpoints meet a stringent statistical threshold. This approach enhances breakpoint resolution and reduces over-segmentation, which is particularly important in low-coverage settings where noise can mimic true transitions.

The third component introduces a dynamic threshold for final CNV calling. Rather than applying a fixed fold-change cutoff (e.g., 1.5 for duplication, 0.5 for deletion), the method calculates lower and upper quantiles (e.g., 5th and 95th percentiles) of the CRN distribution within subsets of windows that share similar GC content. A segment is classified as a deletion if its average CRN falls below the lower quantile for its GC class, and as a duplication if it exceeds the upper quantile. This context-aware thresholding dramatically improves specificity by accounting for residual variance that is GC-dependent but not indicative of true CNVs.

The invention has been validated using seven single-cell samples with known karyotypes, including cells from individuals with Down syndrome, Cri-du-Chat syndrome, and microduplications, as well as trophectoderm cells from blastocysts. Using only 4–9.5% genome coverage (approximately 10 million 50-bp reads per cell), the method achieved 99.63% sensitivity and 97.71% specificity for CNVs larger than 1 Mb. In silico simulations further demonstrated that the method can detect CNVs as small as 500 kb with reasonable sensitivity and localize breakpoints within a median distance of 70 kb. Comparative analysis against SegSeq revealed that the present invention significantly outperforms existing methods in specificity, particularly in GC-extreme regions where conventional comparative strategies fail.

The invention is broadly applicable to any scenario requiring CNV detection from minute DNA quantities, including but not limited to: pre-implantation genetic screening of embryos, noninvasive prenatal diagnosis via fetal NRBCs, liquid biopsy for cancer detection through circulating tumor cells, and fundamental research into somatic mosaicism and cellular evolution. The method is implemented as a standalone bioinformatics pipeline that processes raw sequencing reads through alignment, bias correction, segmentation, and dynamic calling, thereby providing a turnkey solution for single-cell genomic analysis in both research and clinical settings.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for detecting copy number variations (CNVs) and aneuploidies in single cells using low-coverage whole genome sequencing data. The method is specifically engineered to address the unique challenges posed by whole genome amplification (WGA), which is an indispensable step in single-cell genomics but introduces significant sequence-dependent biases that compromise the fidelity of CNV detection. Central to the invention is a multi-stage bioinformatics pipeline that integrates GC bias correction, binary segmentation for breakpoint localization, and dynamic threshold determination to achieve high accuracy even under conditions of limited DNA input and sparse sequencing coverage.

The method begins with the acquisition of single cells from a biological source, such as peripheral blood, blastocyst trophectoderm, or other tissues. Single cells are isolated using micromanipulation techniques, such as mouth-controlled pipetting under a microscope for nucleated blood cells, or laser-assisted biopsy for embryonic cells. Each isolated cell is lysed and subjected to WGA using a commercially available kit, such as the GenomePlex Single Cell Whole Genome Amplification Kit (Sigma Aldrich), which employs degenerate oligonucleotide-primed PCR (DOP-PCR) to amplify the entire genome. The amplified DNA is then quality-controlled by PCR amplification of housekeeping genes to ensure successful WGA before proceeding to library preparation.

Sequencing libraries are prepared from the WGA products with a target insert size of approximately 350 bp and subjected to single-end 50 bp sequencing on a high-throughput platform such as the Illumina HiSeq2000. The resulting raw sequencing data typically yields between 10 to 13 million reads per single cell, corresponding to a genome coverage of 4% to 9.5% when mapped to the human reference genome (e.g., HG18/NCBI Build36). Adapter sequences are removed from the raw reads, and the cleaned reads are aligned to the reference genome using a short-read aligner such as SOAP2, allowing up to two mismatches. Low-quality alignments, including PCR duplicates (identical read pairs) and non-uniquely mapped reads, are subsequently filtered out to retain only high-confidence mappings.

Following alignment, the reference genome is partitioned into non-overlapping observation windows to serve as the basic units for copy number analysis. The window size is critically optimized to balance two competing demands: sufficient resolution to capture CNVs and adequate read depth to mitigate stochastic noise. Given that long genomic segments (>300 kb) exhibit relatively homogeneous GC content, the average window size is set to approximately 150 kb to preserve GC heterogeneity while ensuring statistical robustness. However, rather than using fixed-size windows, the invention employs a dynamic windowing strategy based on simulated mappability. Specifically, the reference genome is computationally fragmented into simulated 50-bp reads, which are then realigned to the genome under the same parameters as the experimental data. Windows are constructed such that each contains an equal number of uniquely mappable simulated reads (e.g., 140,000), thereby normalizing for regional differences in mappability due to repeats or low-complexity sequences. This results in a set of 18,743 observation windows that vary in physical length but are comparable in expected read count, enhancing the reliability of downstream comparisons.

For each observation window, several key statistics are computed: the number of uniquely mapped sequencing reads, the GC content of the reference sequence within the window, and the GC content of the mapped reads themselves. The relative read number (RRN) is defined as the ratio of the observed read count in a window to the global average read count across all autosomal windows. In a diploid genome without bias, the RRN should approximate 1.0. However, empirical analysis of WGA-amplified single cells from a healthy individual (YH) reveals a pronounced U-shaped relationship between RRN and GC content, with RRN values significantly below 1.0 in both GC-poor (<40%) and GC-rich (>48%) regions. This pattern is consistent across replicates and confirms that WGA introduces systematic, GC-dependent amplification bias.

To correct this bias, the invention introduces a weighted correction strategy. First, the genome-wide distribution of RRN is stratified by GC content, rounded to the nearest 1% increment. For each GC bin, the average RRN is computed across all windows falling within that bin. This average serves as a GC-specific expectation of amplification efficiency. The weighting coefficient for a given window is then defined as the inverse of the average RRN for its GC bin, effectively normalizing the observed read count to what would be expected in a bias-free amplification. The corrected read number (CRN) for the window is obtained by multiplying the raw read count by this weighting coefficient. The corrected relative read number (CRN_norm) is subsequently calculated by dividing the CRN by the global average CRN across autosomes, yielding a normalized metric that should center around 1.0 in diploid regions post-correction.

The efficacy of this correction is quantified using a novel GC-bias index, defined as the average absolute deviation of RRN from its GC-dependent loess-smoothed expectation. Application of the weighted correction reduces the GC-bias index by over 99.9%, demonstrating near-complete removal of GC-related artifacts. Post-correction, the relationship between CRN_norm and GC content becomes flat, confirming that the method successfully decouples true copy number signal from amplification bias.

With bias-corrected data in hand, the method proceeds to identify CNV breakpoints using a binary segmentation algorithm tailored for low-coverage data. The algorithm operates in two phases: initialization and iterative merging. During initialization, a run-test is applied at each window boundary to assess whether the CRN_norm values on the left and right sides are drawn from the same distribution. Specifically, for each window i, the algorithm considers 100 windows upstream and 100 windows downstream, computes a p-value for the difference in central tendency, and ranks all windows by this p-value. The top 3,000 windows with the smallest p-values are selected as initial candidate breakpoints.

In the iterative merging phase, the algorithm evaluates the statistical support for each candidate breakpoint. For each breakpoint, the left and right adjacent segments are compared using another run-test, and the p-value of this comparison is recorded. The breakpoint with the largest p-value (i.e., the least significant difference between segments) is removed, and the two adjacent segments are merged into a single segment. This process repeats iteratively until all remaining breakpoints have p-values below a predetermined cutoff. The cutoff is determined empirically from control samples (e.g., the two YH single cells) by identifying the p-value that minimizes false positives while preserving known diploid regions. This adaptive approach prevents over-segmentation, a common pitfall in noisy single-cell data, and ensures that only statistically robust breakpoints are retained.

Following segmentation, the genome is represented as a series of contiguous segments, each with a constant average CRN_norm. The final step is to classify these segments as deletions, duplications, or neutral (diploid) based on their CRN_norm values. Here, the invention departs from conventional fixed-threshold methods by implementing a dynamic threshold determination scheme grounded in the central limit theorem. The rationale is that, even after GC correction, residual variance in CRN_norm may persist and be dependent on GC content. Therefore, instead of using universal cutoffs (e.g., CRN_norm < 0.7 for deletion), the method computes GC-specific thresholds.

For each GC bin (again, 1% increments), the distribution of CRN_norm values across all windows in that bin is analyzed. The lower and upper α-quantiles (e.g., α = 0.05, corresponding to the 5th and 95th percentiles) are calculated. A segment is called a deletion if its average CRN_norm falls below the lower quantile for its constituent windows’ GC bins, and a duplication if it exceeds the upper quantile. This approach dynamically adjusts the calling threshold to the local genomic context, dramatically reducing false positives in regions that, despite correction, exhibit higher baseline variance.

The complete pipeline—encompassing read alignment, dynamic windowing, GC bias correction, binary segmentation, and dynamic thresholding—has been rigorously validated. Seven single-cell samples with confirmed karyotypes were analyzed, including cells from individuals with trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), 5p deletion (Cri-du-Chat), and microduplications on chromosomes 1 and 20, as well as euploid and aneuploid blastocyst cells. Using only ~10 million reads per cell, the method correctly identified all large CNVs (>1 Mb) and aneuploidies, achieving 99.63% sensitivity and 97.71% specificity. Notably, it also detected two sub-megabase duplications (3.94 Mb and 5.47 Mb), demonstrating utility beyond the 1 Mb threshold. In silico simulations confirmed that sensitivity increases with CNV size, reaching ~94% for 3 Mb events, and that breakpoints can be localized within a median of 70 kb.

When compared to SegSeq—a leading CNV detection tool that uses a comparative genomic strategy with a fixed threshold—the present invention exhibits superior specificity (97.71% vs. 16.49% for CNVs >1 Mb). SegSeq’s high false positive rate stems from its inability to fully correct GC bias, as evidenced by spurious calls on GC-extreme chromosomes like 13 and 19. In contrast, the dynamic, GC-aware thresholding of the present invention effectively suppresses these artifacts.

The invention is not limited to the specific parameters described herein. The window size, number of simulated reads per window, GC bin width, quantile thresholds, and segmentation parameters can be tuned based on sequencing depth, WGA method, and desired resolution. Moreover, the core principles—weighted GC correction, adaptive segmentation, and context-dependent thresholding—are generalizable to other species and sequencing platforms. The method is implemented as a modular software pipeline, compatible with standard bioinformatics workflows, and is suitable for integration into clinical diagnostic systems for single-cell genomic analysis.

### Example 2

In a specific embodiment of the invention, the method was applied to detect aneuploidy in trophectoderm cells biopsied from human blastocysts during in vitro fertilization (IVF) procedures. Three single cells (SC3, SC4, SC5) were isolated from blastocysts with known chromosomal abnormalities confirmed by SNP microarray analysis. Each cell underwent WGA using the DOP-PCR method, followed by low-coverage whole genome sequencing (~10 million 50-bp reads). The sequencing data was processed through the full bioinformatics pipeline as described.

For sample SC3, which was known to be monosomic for chromosome 18, the pipeline correctly identified a genome-wide reduction in CRN_norm across chromosome 18, with an average CRN_norm of 0.52, well below the dynamic deletion threshold for the GC content of that chromosome. Similarly, SC4, which was trisomic for chromosome 21, exhibited a CRN_norm of 1.48 across chromosome 21, exceeding the duplication threshold. SC5, a euploid control, showed no significant deviations from diploid CRN_norm across all chromosomes. The dynamic threshold for chromosome 18 (GC-poor, average ~38%) was set at the 5th percentile of CRN_norm in 38% GC bins, which was 0.65, while for chromosome 21 (moderate GC, ~41%), the 95th percentile was 1.35. Thus, the calls were unambiguous and consistent with the gold standard.

This example illustrates the clinical utility of the invention in pre-implantation genetic screening, where accurate detection of whole-chromosome aneuploidies is critical for selecting viable embryos. The method’s ability to operate at low coverage reduces sequencing costs, making it economically feasible for routine clinical use. Furthermore, the dynamic thresholding ensures that aneuploidy calls are not confounded by chromosome-specific GC effects, a common issue in WGA-based assays.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The present invention has broad industrial applicability across multiple sectors of the biomedical and biotechnology industries, particularly in clinical diagnostics, reproductive medicine, oncology, and genomic research. The method provides a robust, cost-effective, and scalable solution for copy number variation (CNV) and aneuploidy detection at the single-cell level, addressing a critical unmet need in precision medicine and personalized healthcare.

In the field of reproductive medicine, the invention is directly applicable to pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) and pre-implantation genetic screening (PGS) in assisted reproductive technology (ART) clinics. By enabling accurate detection of chromosomal abnormalities in single trophectoderm or blastomere cells biopsied from embryos, the method facilitates the selection of euploid embryos for transfer, thereby improving implantation rates, reducing miscarriage risk, and preventing the birth of children with severe genetic disorders. The low-coverage sequencing requirement (as low as 4% genome coverage) significantly reduces the cost per sample compared to high-coverage alternatives, making the technology accessible to a wider range of fertility clinics and patients. Moreover, the high specificity (>97%) minimizes false-positive diagnoses that could lead to the unnecessary discarding of viable embryos.

In prenatal diagnostics, the invention offers a pathway toward truly noninvasive testing by enabling CNV analysis from a single fetal nucleated red blood cell (NRBC) isolated from maternal blood. Current noninvasive prenatal tests (NIPT) rely on cell-free fetal DNA, which is a mixture of maternal and fetal fragments and is limited in its ability to detect sub-chromosomal CNVs. In contrast, a single intact fetal cell provides a complete diploid genome, allowing for comprehensive CNV screening. The invention’s ability to handle minute DNA inputs and correct WGA artifacts makes it uniquely suited for this application, potentially revolutionizing prenatal care by eliminating the need for invasive procedures like amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling.

In oncology, the invention supports liquid biopsy applications through the detection of circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in peripheral blood. CTCs are rare, often comprising fewer than one cell per milliliter of blood, and their genomic analysis can provide real-time insights into tumor heterogeneity, treatment response, and resistance mechanisms. The method’s high sensitivity allows for the identification of CNVs and aneuploidies in individual CTCs, enabling the tracking of clonal evolution and the early detection of aggressive subpopulations. This capability is invaluable for guiding targeted therapy decisions and monitoring minimal residual disease.

In the pharmaceutical and biotechnology industry, the invention can be integrated into drug development pipelines to study the genomic stability of cell lines, assess the genotoxicity of compounds, and characterize patient-derived xenografts or organoids at single-cell resolution. Academic and research institutions will benefit from the method’s ability to uncover somatic mosaicism in neurodevelopmental disorders, aging, and other conditions where cellular heterogeneity plays a key role.

From a commercial perspective, the invention can be deployed as a cloud-based software-as-a-service (SaaS) platform, a standalone desktop application, or integrated into existing next-generation sequencing analysis suites. Diagnostic laboratories can adopt the pipeline as a CE-IVD or FDA-cleared assay for single-cell genomic testing. The modular design allows for customization to different WGA chemistries, sequencing platforms, and species, ensuring broad market penetration.

In summary, the invention bridges a critical gap between the promise of single-cell genomics and its practical implementation in clinical and industrial settings. By delivering high accuracy, low cost, and operational simplicity, it enables the widespread adoption of single-cell CNV analysis across diverse applications, ultimately contributing to improved health outcomes and scientific discovery.
