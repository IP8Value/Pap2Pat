# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of machine learning and signal processing, particularly in the domain of radar-based human presence detection and people counting within enclosed or semi-enclosed environments. More specifically, the invention relates to a novel deep learning methodology that integrates a newly developed loss function—termed the Label-Aware Ranked (LAR) loss—with radar signal preprocessing and temporal smoothing techniques to significantly improve the accuracy and robustness of people counting systems operating under challenging real-world conditions. The invention is especially suited for deployment in transportation contexts such as automotive cabins, train compartments, buses, and other confined spaces where precise occupancy estimation is critical for applications including automated climate control (HVAC), safety monitoring, energy efficiency optimization, and intelligent cabin management. Unlike conventional computer vision-based approaches that rely on optical sensors and are susceptible to privacy violations, lighting variations, and adverse weather conditions, the present invention leverages millimeter-wave Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) radar technology, which operates reliably in darkness, fog, smoke, and other visually obstructive environments while preserving individual anonymity. The core innovation resides not merely in the use of radar hardware but in the architectural and algorithmic enhancements applied during the training and inference phases of a deep neural network, wherein the LAR loss function explicitly encodes the ordinal nature of the people counting task—i.e., that labels representing “three people” are inherently closer to “two” or “four” than to “zero” or “five”—into the learned embedding space. This is achieved through a mathematically grounded formulation that enforces both intra-class compactness and inter-class angular uniformity aligned with label ranking, thereby transforming what is traditionally treated as a regression problem into a structured metric learning task. Furthermore, the invention incorporates an exponential moving average filter during inference to stabilize predictions over time, capitalizing on the physical reality that human occupancy in a vehicle cabin typically changes incrementally rather than abruptly. The technical field thus spans interdisciplinary domains including radar signal processing, deep metric learning, supervised embedding optimization, time-series smoothing, and embedded artificial intelligence for edge-computing platforms in automotive and transportation systems.

## BACKGROUND

People counting—the task of estimating the number of individuals present in a given spatial volume—has long been recognized as a foundational capability for smart environments, with applications ranging from retail analytics and public safety to building automation and vehicular comfort systems. Historically, this task has been addressed using a variety of sensor modalities, among which optical cameras have dominated due to their rich spatial information and mature computer vision pipelines. However, camera-based systems face significant limitations in real-world deployment: they raise serious privacy concerns, especially in private or sensitive settings such as personal vehicles or public transit; they perform poorly under low-light conditions, glare, or occlusions; and they are fundamentally compromised by environmental factors like rain, fog, or dust. These shortcomings have motivated the exploration of alternative sensing technologies, with radar emerging as a compelling candidate owing to its all-weather operability, immunity to lighting conditions, and inherent privacy preservation—since radar returns do not yield identifiable visual features of individuals.

Among radar types, Frequency Modulated Continuous Wave (FMCW) radars operating in the millimeter-wave spectrum (e.g., 60 GHz or 77 GHz bands) are particularly well-suited for short-range human sensing due to their high resolution, compact form factor, and low power consumption. In such systems, the raw intermediate frequency (IF) signal generated by mixing transmitted and received chirps undergoes a standardized preprocessing pipeline to extract meaningful representations for downstream analysis. This typically involves organizing sampled IF data into a two-dimensional matrix indexed by fast-time samples (range dimension) and slow-time chirps (Doppler/velocity dimension), applying windowing functions (e.g., Hamming windows) to suppress spectral sidelobes, performing moving target indication (MTI) filtering to remove static clutter such as vehicle structures, and computing two-dimensional Fast Fourier Transforms (2D-FFTs) to generate Range-Doppler Images (RDIs). These RDIs, often represented as multi-channel inputs comprising real and imaginary components from multiple receive antennas, serve as the primary input to deep neural networks trained to map radar signatures to occupancy counts.

Despite these advances, radar-based people counting remains challenging, particularly in dense or confined scenarios like automotive interiors. The human body reflects radar energy from multiple points—torso, limbs, head—leading to complex, overlapping micro-Doppler signatures that can cause signal instability and false detections. Moreover, mutual occlusion among occupants, limited spatial resolution of low-cost radar chips, and multipath interference from cabin surfaces further degrade performance. Traditional signal processing methods, such as peak detection in range-Doppler maps or clustering of point clouds, struggle to disambiguate these effects, especially when distinguishing between, for example, two closely seated adults versus three children.

To overcome these limitations, recent work has turned to deep learning (DL), which can implicitly learn robust feature representations from raw or preprocessed radar data. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have been successfully applied to RDI inputs for tasks including human detection, activity recognition, and occupancy estimation. However, a critical gap persists in how the learning objective is formulated. Most existing approaches treat people counting as either a classification problem (with discrete output classes for counts 0 through N) or a regression problem (with a continuous output later rounded to the nearest integer). In the classification paradigm, standard cross-entropy loss treats all misclassifications equally—confusing “3” with “0” incurs the same penalty as confusing “3” with “4”—despite the latter being far more tolerable in practical applications like HVAC control. In the regression paradigm, Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss penalizes deviations quadratically but fails to exploit the inherent ordinal structure of the labels. Neither approach leverages the fact that the people counting task possesses a natural ranking: labels are not arbitrary categories but ordered integers where semantic proximity correlates with numerical adjacency.

Deep Metric Learning (DML) offers a promising alternative framework by learning an embedding space where distances between samples reflect their semantic relationships. Classical DML losses such as Triplet Loss, Multiclass N-Pair Loss, and Constellation Loss have proven effective in image retrieval and face recognition by pulling together embeddings of the same class and pushing apart those of different classes. However, these methods were designed for nominal classification tasks with no assumed ordering among classes. When applied naively to people counting, they treat “2” and “5” as equally dissimilar to “3,” ignoring the ordinal hierarchy. Consequently, the resulting embedding spaces lack the structured geometry needed to support fine-grained regression or tolerant prediction (e.g., accepting ±1 error as acceptable).

Furthermore, even when accurate on individual frames, radar-based predictors often exhibit temporal instability—oscillating between adjacent counts due to minor signal fluctuations—because they process each frame independently without considering the physical continuity of human presence. While post-processing filters like median or moving average smoothing are sometimes applied, they are heuristic and not integrated into the learning process. A more principled approach would incorporate temporal coherence directly into the inference mechanism, leveraging the fact that occupancy changes are typically gradual.

Thus, there exists a clear need for a unified solution that (1) respects the ordinal nature of people counting labels during training, (2) shapes the embedding space to reflect label ranking through geometric constraints, and (3) stabilizes predictions over time using a theoretically sound temporal filter. The present invention addresses these unmet needs through the introduction of the Label-Aware Ranked (LAR) loss function combined with exponential smoothing at inference, specifically optimized for radar-based people counting in challenging vehicular environments.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for improved people counting in enclosed environments using radar sensing and deep learning, characterized by the integration of a novel loss function—Label-Aware Ranked (LAR) loss—and a temporal smoothing mechanism to enhance both accuracy and stability. The invention overcomes the limitations of prior art by explicitly modeling the ordinal relationship among occupancy labels during the training of a deep neural network, thereby inducing a structured embedding space where the angular separation between embeddings corresponds to the numerical distance between their associated labels. This is achieved through a mathematically rigorous loss formulation that combines normalized feature embeddings with a logarithmic weighting scheme based on inter-label distances, ensuring that embeddings of neighboring counts (e.g., 2 and 3) are closer in the embedding space than those of distant counts (e.g., 0 and 5). The LAR loss is derived from principles of deep metric learning but extends beyond conventional approaches by incorporating label ranking—a property heretofore unexploited in metric learning for regression-like tasks.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a radar-based sensing system configured to capture raw intermediate frequency signals from a scene containing zero to N individuals, where N is a predetermined maximum occupancy (e.g., five in an automotive cabin). The system includes a preprocessing module that converts the raw radar data into a multi-channel Range-Doppler Image representation through a sequence of operations including data framing, windowing, moving target indication, and 2D Fast Fourier Transform. This processed radar data is then fed into a deep neural network—preferably a convolutional architecture with multiple layers of feature extraction and pooling—trained using the LAR loss in conjunction with a regression loss such as Mean Squared Error.

In another aspect, the LAR loss function is defined such that for a batch of input samples, each associated with an integer label representing the number of people, the loss minimizes the cosine similarity between embeddings of the same label while maximizing the angular separation between embeddings of different labels in proportion to the logarithm of their absolute label difference. Specifically, the loss employs normalized feature vectors so that similarity is measured via angular distance on a unit hypersphere, and it introduces a multiplier term log(Δl), where Δl is the absolute difference between the anchor label and a negative sample’s label, to modulate the repulsive force between dissimilar classes. This ensures that the embedding space is not only discriminative but also ranked: the mean embeddings of labels 0, 1, 2, ..., N are arranged in order around the hypersphere with approximately uniform angular spacing, a configuration proven via Jensen’s inequality and trigonometric analysis to minimize the LAR loss.

In a further aspect, the invention incorporates an exponential moving average (EMA) filter during inference to smooth the network’s predictions over consecutive time frames. Given that human occupancy in a vehicle cabin rarely changes instantaneously—typically increasing or decreasing by at most one person per several seconds—the EMA filter leverages temporal continuity to suppress spurious fluctuations and produce more reliable estimates. The smoothed prediction at time k is computed as a weighted average of the current raw prediction and the previous smoothed value, with the weight determined by a decay factor that balances responsiveness and stability.

The combined effect of the LAR loss and EMA smoothing yields substantial improvements over state-of-the-art methods. Experimental validation on a real-world dataset of 95,000 radar frames collected inside a vehicle cabin with occupancy levels from zero to five demonstrates that the proposed approach achieves an accuracy of 83.0% (exact count match) and a neighboring-label accuracy of 99.9% (±1 error tolerance), representing gains of +6.7% and +2.1%, respectively, over the best-performing baseline using Constellation loss with MSE and EMA. These results confirm that embedding space structuring through label-aware ranking significantly enhances predictive performance in ordinal regression tasks, particularly in noisy, real-world sensing conditions.

Accordingly, the invention provides a complete, end-to-end solution for privacy-preserving, weather-independent people counting that is robust, accurate, and suitable for deployment in resource-constrained edge devices within automotive and transportation systems. The methodology is generalizable to other ordinal regression problems beyond people counting, wherever label ranking can be leveraged to improve embedding geometry and prediction fidelity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

The following detailed description sets forth illustrative embodiments of the present invention, providing a comprehensive account of the system architecture, signal processing pipeline, neural network design, loss function formulation, training methodology, and inference procedure. While the primary application context is people counting inside a vehicle cabin using a 60 GHz FMCW radar sensor, the underlying principles are applicable to any environment where ordinal regression from radar or similar time-series sensor data is required.

The system begins with a radar sensor unit, preferably an Infineon XENSIV™ 60 GHz radar chip mounted on the internal upper portion of the vehicle’s front windshield, oriented to cover the entire passenger compartment. This placement ensures broad coverage of seating areas while minimizing occlusion from dashboard structures. The radar operates by transmitting a sequence of linear frequency-modulated chirps and receiving reflected signals from objects within its field of view. For each chirp, the mixer generates an intermediate frequency (IF) signal proportional to the time delay (and hence range) of the reflecting target. Over a frame period consisting of N_C chirps (e.g., 64 chirps), N_S samples per chirp (e.g., 128 samples) are acquired, yielding a raw data cube of dimensions N_S × N_C × N_RX, where N_RX is the number of receive antennas (e.g., 3 antennas, producing 6 channels when real and imaginary parts are separated).

This raw data undergoes a standardized preprocessing chain to produce a feature-rich input for the neural network. First, the data is organized into a 2D dataframe of size N_S × N_C for each receive channel. To enhance velocity resolution, a slow-time integration may be performed, though in the preferred embodiment, the full chirp sequence is retained. Next, a moving target indication (MTI) filter is applied along the chirp dimension to remove static clutter—such as reflections from the vehicle’s interior surfaces—by subtracting the mean across chirps, effectively acting as a high-pass filter in the Doppler domain. Subsequently, Hamming windows are applied independently along the sample (fast-time) and chirp (slow-time) dimensions to reduce spectral leakage and sidelobe artifacts in the subsequent Fourier transforms. Finally, a 2D Fast Fourier Transform (2D-FFT) is computed across both dimensions, converting the time-domain data into a Range-Doppler Image (RDI) where peaks correspond to targets at specific ranges and radial velocities. The real and imaginary components of this complex-valued RDI are concatenated across antennas to form a 6-channel input tensor of size H × W × 6, where H and W correspond to the number of range and Doppler bins, respectively (e.g., 128 × 64).

This preprocessed radar tensor is then fed into a deep neural network designed for feature extraction and regression. The preferred architecture is a lightweight convolutional neural network inspired by encoder structures used in prior radar-based human sensing works. It consists of three sequential blocks, each comprising a 2D convolutional layer with 32 filters of kernel size 3×3, followed by a ReLU activation function and a 2×2 max-pooling layer with stride 2. This progressively reduces the spatial dimensions while increasing channel depth, capturing hierarchical features from local micro-motions to global occupancy patterns. The output of the third pooling layer is flattened and passed through a fully connected layer with ReLU activation, culminating in a single scalar output representing the predicted number of people. During training, this output is used in conjunction with ground-truth labels to compute the composite loss.

The core innovation lies in the training objective, which combines two components: a standard regression loss (e.g., Mean Squared Error, MSE) and the novel Label-Aware Ranked (LAR) loss. The MSE loss ensures that the network’s raw output approximates the true count numerically, while the LAR loss operates on the penultimate embedding layer—i.e., the output of the fully connected ReLU layer before the final regression head—to impose geometric structure on the learned representations.

The LAR loss is formulated as follows. Let a batch contain 2 samples per label for labels l ∈ {0, 1, ..., L−1}, where L is the total number of distinct occupancy levels (e.g., L=6 for counts 0–5). For each anchor sample x_a with label l_a, its positive pair x_p shares the same label, and all other samples in the batch serve as negative examples. Let f_i denote the L2-normalized embedding vector of sample x_i, so that ||f_i|| = 1 and the cosine similarity between f_i and f_j equals their dot product f_i · f_j = cos(θ_ij), where θ_ij is the angle between them on the unit hypersphere.

The LAR loss for the anchor is defined as:

L_LAR(a) = −log[ exp(f_a · f_p / τ) / (exp(f_a · f_p / τ) + Σ_{n ≠ a,p} w(l_a, l_n) · exp(f_a · f_n / τ)) ]

where τ is a temperature hyperparameter (typically set to 0.1 for sharper distributions), and w(l_a, l_n) = log(|l_a − l_n| + ε) is a weighting function that increases with the absolute label difference, with ε a small constant (e.g., 1e−6) to avoid log(0). In practice, to ensure numerical stability and emphasize ranking, the multiplier is often simplified to log(Δl) where Δl = |l_a − l_n|, and the logarithm ensures that distant labels exert stronger repulsive forces than nearby ones, but with diminishing returns (due to the concavity of log).

Summing over all anchors in the batch yields the total LAR loss. This formulation encourages three key properties: (1) embeddings of the same label collapse to a single point (θ = 0); (2) embeddings of different labels are pushed apart; and (3) the angular separation between label clusters increases monotonically with their numerical distance. Through mathematical analysis using Jensen’s inequality and properties of the cosine function, it can be shown that the global minimum of this loss occurs when the mean embeddings of the L labels are distributed uniformly around a great circle of the hypersphere, i.e., with angular spacing of 2π/L between consecutive labels, and ordered according to their numerical values. This uniform, ranked arrangement maximizes inter-label separation while respecting ordinal semantics.

During training, the total loss is a weighted sum: L_total = α · L_MSE + β · L_LAR, where α and β are balancing coefficients (e.g., α=1.0, β=0.5). Optimization is performed using stochastic gradient descent with momentum or Adam optimizer, with batch sizes structured to include balanced label representation (e.g., 12 samples per batch for L=6).

At inference time, the system processes radar frames sequentially at a rate of 10 Hz. For each frame, the network produces a raw count prediction ŷ_k. To enhance temporal stability, an exponential moving average (EMA) filter is applied:

ŷ_smoothed[k] = γ · ŷ_k + (1 − γ) · ŷ_smoothed[k−1]

where γ ∈ (0,1] is the smoothing factor (e.g., γ=0.3), and ŷ_smoothed[0] is initialized to the first prediction. The final output is obtained by rounding ŷ_smoothed[k] to the nearest integer. This simple yet effective mechanism exploits the physical constraint that occupancy changes are gradual, thereby reducing jitter without introducing significant lag.

The system was validated on a proprietary dataset comprising 95,000 labeled radar frames recorded in a real vehicle under diverse conditions (different occupants, seating positions, clothing, ambient temperatures). The dataset was split into 76,000 training and 19,000 test frames, with uniform class distribution. Using the described CNN architecture trained with LAR + MSE loss and EMA smoothing, the system achieved 83.0% exact accuracy and 99.9% ±1 accuracy, outperforming all baselines including Triplet, Mc-N-Pair, and Constellation losses, with or without smoothing.

In alternative embodiments, the radar sensor may be replaced with other coherent RF sensors (e.g., UWB radar), the preprocessing pipeline may include additional steps such as beamforming or micro-Doppler spectrogram generation, and the neural network may employ attention mechanisms or recurrent layers for explicit temporal modeling. However, the essential inventive concept—the use of a label-ranking-aware metric learning loss combined with temporal smoothing for ordinal regression from radar data—remains central to all implementations.

The invention thus provides a robust, accurate, and privacy-compliant solution for people counting in transportation and smart environments, with immediate applicability to automotive HVAC control, occupancy-based lighting, safety alerts, and fleet management systems.
