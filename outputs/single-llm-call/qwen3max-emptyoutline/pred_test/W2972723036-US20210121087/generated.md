# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of noninvasive medical monitoring and, more specifically, to systems and methods for estimating intracranial pressure (ICP) and associated hemodynamic parameters without the need for invasive instrumentation. Intracranial pressure is a critical physiological parameter in neurocritical care, with elevated levels indicating potentially life-threatening conditions such as traumatic brain injury, hydrocephalus, intracranial hemorrhage, and other neurological disorders. Current clinical gold standards for ICP monitoring involve the insertion of catheters or microtransducers into the brain parenchyma or ventricular system, procedures that carry significant risks including infection, hemorrhage, and mechanical damage to neural tissue. These risks often preclude routine ICP monitoring in many clinical settings, particularly in pediatric populations, resource-limited environments, or during surgical procedures where continuous ICP assessment could inform perfusion management but is not otherwise indicated. The invention disclosed herein addresses these limitations by providing a robust, computationally efficient, and fully noninvasive framework for estimating both mean ICP and ICP pulse pressure using only minimally invasive or entirely noninvasive measurements of systemic arterial blood pressure and cerebral blood flow velocity. The method leverages a physiologically grounded model of cerebral hemodynamics, combined with a pseudo-Bayesian estimation strategy that accounts for uncertainties in signal timing and patient-specific variability, thereby enabling accurate, real-time, and continuous monitoring of intracranial dynamics without breaching the cranial vault. This approach is particularly suited for deployment in intensive care units, operating rooms, emergency departments, and ambulatory settings, where early detection of intracranial hypertension can significantly improve patient outcomes and guide therapeutic interventions.

## BACKGROUND

Intracranial pressure (ICP) is a fundamental parameter in the assessment and management of patients with neurological injury or disease. Normal ICP in adults ranges from 7 to 15 mmHg, while values exceeding 20–25 mmHg are generally considered pathological and may require urgent intervention to prevent secondary brain injury due to reduced cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), defined as the difference between mean arterial blood pressure (ABP) and ICP. Despite its clinical importance, continuous ICP monitoring remains an invasive procedure, typically requiring the placement of an external ventricular drain (EVD) or an intraparenchymal microtransducer. While EVDs are considered the gold standard due to their accuracy and ability to provide therapeutic cerebrospinal fluid drainage, they are associated with complication rates of 5% to 10%, including ventriculitis, hemorrhage, and malposition. Intraparenchymal sensors, though less prone to infection, can drift over time and cannot be recalibrated once implanted. Moreover, both modalities necessitate neurosurgical expertise for placement, limiting their availability outside specialized centers and discouraging their use in patients with moderate risk profiles or in preventive monitoring scenarios.

These limitations have spurred decades of research into noninvasive ICP (nICP) estimation techniques. Early approaches focused on correlating surrogate markers—such as optic nerve sheath diameter, tympanic membrane displacement, or otoacoustic emissions—with ICP, but these methods lacked sufficient accuracy and reliability for clinical adoption. More recently, model-based strategies have emerged that exploit the biophysical relationship between cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV), measured via transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD), and arterial blood pressure. These models are often derived from the Windkessel theory of vascular hemodynamics, which conceptualizes the cerebral vasculature as a two-element system comprising a resistive component (representing cerebrovascular resistance) and a capacitive component (representing arterial and brain tissue compliance). In this framework, CBFV is viewed as the output of a dynamic system driven by cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP = ABP – ICP). By inverting this model, one can theoretically estimate ICP if ABP and CBFV are known.

However, practical implementation of such model-based nICP methods has faced several challenges. First, the precise timing relationship between ABP and CBFV waveforms is often unknown and varies between individuals due to differences in vascular anatomy, measurement site (e.g., radial vs. central ABP), and signal processing delays. Misalignment of these signals introduces significant errors in parameter estimation. Second, the assumption of stationarity—that cerebrovascular resistance and compliance remain constant over the analysis window—is only approximately valid, and deviations can bias ICP estimates. Third, the inversion problem is inherently ill-posed: multiple combinations of ICP and model parameters can yield similar CBFV predictions, leading to ambiguity in the solution. Prior attempts to address these issues have included averaging bilateral CBFV signals, using fixed time offsets, or applying machine learning models trained on invasive ICP data. While these approaches have shown promise, they often lack generalizability across diverse patient populations, require extensive calibration, or are computationally intensive, rendering them unsuitable for real-time clinical use.

Notably, Kashif et al. proposed a two-element continuous-time model of cerebral hemodynamics and demonstrated its utility in estimating nICP from ABP and CBFV. Their method achieved reasonable accuracy in adult traumatic brain injury patients but was sensitive to temporal misalignment between input signals and did not account for hydrostatic pressure differences between ABP and ICP transducer sites. Subsequent work by Fanelli et al. automated preprocessing steps and introduced signal quality assessment, improving robustness in a pediatric cohort. However, both approaches relied on a single assumed time offset and did not incorporate mechanisms to track ICP changes over time independently of initial assumptions. Furthermore, none of these prior methods provided a means to estimate ICP pulse pressure—the amplitude of ICP waveform pulsatility—which is itself a clinically relevant marker of intracranial compliance and cerebrovascular health.

The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a comprehensive, two-stage estimation framework that explicitly accounts for uncertainty in signal timing, incorporates prior physiological knowledge in a flexible Bayesian manner, and includes a tracking mechanism to follow dynamic ICP changes without persistent reliance on initial priors. Additionally, the invention uniquely enables the noninvasive estimation of ICP pulse pressure in a training-free, patient-specific fashion, thereby providing a more complete picture of intracranial dynamics than mean ICP alone. This represents a significant advancement over the state of the art, offering a pathway toward a truly noninvasive, continuous, and clinically actionable ICP monitoring system.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, noninvasive method and system for estimating intracranial pressure (ICP) and ICP pulse pressure using only measurements of radial arterial blood pressure (rABP) and cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV). The method is based on a discrete-time approximation of a two-element Windkessel model of cerebral hemodynamics, augmented with a first-order autoregressive (AR) process to model temporal evolution of mean ICP. The core innovation lies in a two-stage estimation framework that combines a pseudo-Bayesian approach with a Kalman filter-like tracking mechanism to achieve high accuracy while minimizing dependence on initial assumptions.

In the first stage, a baseline ICP estimate is established by analyzing multiple consecutive data windows (e.g., five 20-beat segments). For each window, a likelihood distribution over candidate ICP values and time offsets is computed by evaluating the residual error between measured CBFV and CBFV predicted by the hemodynamic model across a range of physiologically plausible ICP values (e.g., 0 to mean rABP) and time offsets constrained by physiological principles (e.g., CBFV systolic peak leading rABP peak, diastolic alignment within ±3 samples). This likelihood is then combined with a preset prior distribution—specifically, a mixture of two truncated Gaussian distributions modeling low and high ICP scenarios—to generate a posterior distribution. The median of this posterior serves as the window-specific ICP estimate. The baseline is formed by averaging these posterior medians across the initial windows.

In the second stage, subsequent ICP estimates are computed using only the likelihood distribution (i.e., a uniform prior) to reduce reliance on the initial prior. To mitigate the increased noise susceptibility of this approach, a tracking framework is employed. Changes in ICP from one window to the next are estimated both from the likelihood-derived point estimates and from the AR model prediction. These two change estimates, along with their associated variances, are fused in a Kalman filter-like manner, assigning greater weight to the more certain estimate. The filtered change is then added to the previous ICP estimate to produce the updated value, with the baseline serving as the initial condition.

Additionally, the invention provides a method for estimating ICP pulse pressure. The same likelihood distribution used for mean ICP estimation is leveraged to compute window-specific estimates of the model parameters (resistance and compliance). These parameters, along with the optimal time offset, are used to reconstruct the ICP waveform in the frequency domain by solving the hemodynamic model for the pulsatile components of ABP and CBFV. A heuristic harmonic selection scheme ensures robustness against noise, and the inverse Fourier transform yields an estimate of the ICP pulse waveform, from which pulse pressure is derived.

The system requires only rABP and CBFV as inputs, both of which are routinely available in critical care settings. The method is fully automated, computationally efficient, and does not require patient-specific calibration against invasive ICP measurements. It has been validated on clinical data from pediatric patients with diverse neurological conditions, achieving a mean error of 0.6 mmHg and root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 3.7 mmHg for mean ICP estimation, and a bias of -1.2 mmHg with RMSE of 4.0 mmHg for ICP pulse pressure estimation—performance comparable to that of concurrent invasive ICP monitors. The invention thus enables reliable, continuous, and noninvasive monitoring of both static and dynamic aspects of intracranial pressure, facilitating improved management of patients at risk for intracranial hypertension.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a system and method for noninvasive estimation of intracranial pressure (ICP) and ICP pulse pressure using a model-based, pseudo-Bayesian approach that integrates physiological modeling with statistical inference. The method operates on time-synchronized waveforms of radial arterial blood pressure (rABP) and cerebral blood flow velocity (CBFV), which are acquired using standard clinical monitoring equipment—namely, an arterial line for rABP and transcranial Doppler ultrasound for CBFV. While the use of an arterial line constitutes a minimally invasive procedure, it is widely accepted in neurocritical care and carries substantially lower risk than intracranial instrumentation. Future implementations may substitute noninvasive ABP measurements (e.g., from oscillometric cuffs or photoplethysmography) to render the entire system fully noninvasive.

The foundational principle of the invention is the representation of cerebral hemodynamics as a linear time-invariant system governed by a two-element Windkessel model. In this model, cerebral blood flow velocity q(t) is related to cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), defined as the difference between central arterial blood pressure p_a(t) and intracranial pressure p_i(t), through a first-order differential equation:

q(t) = (1/R) * [p_a(t) - p_i(t) - R*C*dq(t)/dt]

where R denotes cerebrovascular resistance and C represents the aggregate compliance of the cerebral arterial tree and brain parenchyma. This continuous-time model is discretized using first-order finite differences to yield a two-tap finite impulse response (FIR) filter formulation suitable for digital signal processing:

q[n] = α * (p_a[n] - p_i) + β * (q[n] - q[n-1])

Here, q[n] and p_a[n] are the discrete-time samples of CBFV and rABP, respectively, at sampling index n; p_i is the mean ICP assumed constant over the estimation window; and α and β are model parameters related to R and C by α = 1/R and β = f_s*R*C, where f_s is the sampling frequency. The simplification of treating ICP as a constant mean value (ignoring its pulsatility) during the initial estimation phase is justified by the relatively small magnitude of ICP pulse pressure compared to mean ICP in most clinical scenarios and by the focus on estimating the dominant hemodynamic driver of CBFV.

To account for the fact that rABP is a peripheral measurement and may not perfectly reflect central ABP, and to accommodate unknown physiological and instrumental delays between the rABP and CBFV signals, the invention introduces a time offset variable d. This offset represents the number of samples by which the rABP waveform is shifted relative to CBFV to best align the underlying hemodynamic events. The estimation routine systematically scans over a range of physiologically plausible time offsets. Specifically, the scan range is constrained such that, on average, the systolic peak of CBFV leads that of rABP—a consequence of the Windkessel dynamics wherein flow accelerates rapidly at systole onset—while ensuring that the diastolic troughs of both waveforms are aligned within a tolerance of three sampling intervals (approximately 25 ms at 125 Hz sampling). This dual constraint ensures that the scanned offsets reflect realistic vascular transit times and measurement artifacts without being overly restrictive.

For each candidate combination of mean ICP value I and time offset d within their respective scan ranges, the model parameters α and β are estimated in a least-squares sense. Given an N-sample estimation window, the CBFV prediction error vector e is defined as:

e = q - H(I,d) * θ

where q is the N×1 vector of measured CBFV samples, H(I,d) is an N×2 regression matrix constructed from the time-shifted rABP and CBFV difference terms, and θ = [α, β]^T is the parameter vector. The least-squares solution is:

θ̂(I,d) = H(I,d)^† * q

where ^† denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. The residual error norm ||e||^2 is then computed as a measure of model fit. A likelihood distribution L(I,d) is defined over the ICP-offset space as a normalized exponential function of the negative residual error:

L(I,d) = (1/S_L) * exp(-||e||^2 / (2σ^2))

where S_L is a normalization constant ensuring that the total probability sums to one, and σ^2 is a scaling factor that may be set based on expected noise levels. This formulation assigns high likelihood to (I,d) pairs that produce accurate CBFV predictions and low likelihood to poor fits.

To obtain a marginal likelihood distribution over ICP alone, the joint distribution L(I,d) is summed over all scanned time offsets:

L(I) = Σ_d L(I,d)

This marginalization effectively integrates out the uncertainty in time alignment, yielding a robust one-dimensional distribution of ICP likelihood. From this distribution, a point estimate I_L (e.g., the median) and an associated variance σ^2_L can be derived, representing the noninvasive ICP estimate and its uncertainty under a uniform prior assumption.

The invention employs a two-stage estimation process to balance initial robustness with long-term adaptability. In the first stage, a baseline ICP estimate I_B is established. This is done by computing a posterior distribution for each of the first M_b (e.g., five) consecutive 20-beat data windows. The posterior is formed by multiplying the likelihood L(I) with a preset prior distribution Pr(I):

Pr(I | data) = (1/S_P) * Pr(I) * L(I)

The chosen prior is a mixture of two truncated Gaussian distributions:

Pr(I) = w_1 * N_trunc(I; μ_1, σ_1^2) + w_2 * N_trunc(I; μ_2, σ_2^2)

where N_trunc denotes a Gaussian distribution truncated to the ICP scan range [0, mean rABP], with parameters μ_1 = 13.6 mmHg, σ_1 = 10 mmHg modeling low ICP, and μ_2 = 50 mmHg, σ_2 = 20 mmHg modeling high ICP. The weights w_1 = 0.8 and w_2 = 0.2 reflect the relative prevalence of normal versus elevated ICP in clinical populations. This prior is intentionally broad and conservative, assigning non-negligible probability to extreme ICP values to ensure generalizability across diverse pathologies. The median I_C of the posterior distribution is taken as the window’s ICP estimate, and the baseline I_B is the average of I_C over the M_b windows.

In the second stage, subsequent ICP estimates are computed using only the likelihood-derived point estimate I_L (i.e., uniform prior) to avoid persistent bias from the initial prior. However, to maintain stability, a tracking framework is implemented that fuses the observed ICP change ΔI_obs = I_L[m] - I[m-1] with a model-predicted change ΔI_pred from a first-order AR process:

I[m] = γ * I[m-1] + v_m

where γ is set to 1 to model rapid ICP changes, and v_m is white noise with variance σ_v^2. The predicted change is simply ΔI_pred = I[m-1] - I[m-1] = 0 when γ=1, but its variance accumulates as σ^2_pred = σ_v^2. The observed change has variance σ^2_obs = σ^2_L[m] + σ^2_L[m-1]. The fused change estimate is:

ΔI[m] = (σ^2_pred * ΔI_obs + σ^2_obs * ΔI_pred) / (σ^2_obs + σ^2_pred)

which simplifies to a weighted average favoring the more certain estimate. The final ICP estimate is then:

I[m] = I[m-1] + ΔI[m]

with I[M_b] = I_B. This Kalman-like update reduces the impact of noisy likelihood estimates while allowing the system to track genuine ICP trends.

For ICP pulse pressure estimation, the invention utilizes the joint likelihood distribution L(I,d) to select the optimal time offset d_0 that maximizes L(I,d) for the estimated mean ICP. The corresponding model parameters α and β (or R and C) are then used to solve the pulsatile form of the hemodynamic model in the frequency domain. Mean-subtracted rABP and CBFV waveforms are decomposed into heart beat-aligned wavelets, and their discrete Fourier transforms (DFTs) are computed. The ICP pulsatility spectrum P_i(jhΩ_0) is derived for each harmonic h as:

P_i(jhΩ_0) = P_a(jhΩ_0) - R*(1 + jhΩ_0*R*C)*Q(jhΩ_0)

A heuristic selects only those harmonics whose spectral magnitude is less than or equal to the previous harmonic, ensuring monotonic decay and rejecting noise-dominated high-frequency components. The inverse DFT of the selected harmonics yields an estimated ICP pulse waveform, from which pulse pressure is calculated as the peak-to-peak amplitude.

The system is designed for real-time operation, with all computations—signal preprocessing, windowing, model solving, Bayesian updating, and tracking—implemented as causal, deterministic algorithms. Validation on clinical data demonstrates performance comparable to invasive monitors, with the added benefits of noninvasiveness, no calibration requirement, and the ability to estimate both mean and pulsatile ICP components. This comprehensive approach represents a significant advance in noninvasive neuromonitoring, with broad applicability in critical care, surgery, and emergency medicine.
