# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The prediction of protein three-dimensional structure from its amino acid sequence—commonly referred to as the protein folding problem—represents one of the most enduring and consequential challenges in molecular biology, biochemistry, and computational biophysics. Since the seminal hypothesis by Anfinsen that the native structure of a protein is determined solely by its primary sequence under physiological conditions, significant scientific effort has been devoted to developing methods capable of accurately predicting this structure without reliance on experimentally derived templates or homologous sequences. Despite decades of progress, the development of a universally accurate, computationally efficient, and physically grounded ab initio method for protein structure prediction remains an unmet goal, particularly for proteins lacking detectable homologs or for which experimental structural data are unavailable.

Traditional approaches to protein structure prediction fall broadly into two categories: template-based modeling (TBM) and free modeling (FM), also known as ab initio prediction. Template-based methods, including homology modeling and threading, leverage evolutionary information by aligning the target sequence to known structures in protein databases. These methods achieve high accuracy when suitable templates exist but fail entirely in the absence of detectable homology—a scenario that affects a substantial fraction of proteomes, especially in non-model organisms or for novel protein folds. In contrast, ab initio methods attempt to predict structure from first principles, relying on physical energy functions, statistical potentials, or machine learning models trained on physical or evolutionary features. While recent advances in deep learning, such as AlphaFold2 and RoseTTAFold, have dramatically improved prediction accuracy by integrating co-evolutionary signals with deep neural networks, these systems still depend heavily on multiple sequence alignments and large databases of homologous sequences, thereby limiting their applicability in truly de novo contexts.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations represent a classical ab initio approach grounded in Newtonian mechanics and empirical force fields. By numerically integrating the equations of motion for all atoms in a protein system, MD can, in principle, sample the conformational landscape and converge to the native state. However, the computational cost of atomistic MD is prohibitive for most practical applications, often requiring weeks to months of supercomputing time even for small proteins, due to the femtosecond time steps needed to resolve bond vibrations and the millisecond-to-second timescales of folding. Coarse-grained models reduce this burden by simplifying the representation of the protein, but they often sacrifice atomic detail and physical fidelity. Moreover, many MD-based protocols incorporate biasing potentials or restraints derived from experimental data or predicted contacts, thereby blurring the line between true ab initio simulation and knowledge-assisted modeling.

Beyond computational expense, a fundamental limitation of many existing methods is their opacity: they function as “black boxes” that produce structural predictions without elucidating the underlying physical mechanisms driving folding. This lack of interpretability hinders the ability to understand how specific sequence features—such as charge distribution, hydrophobicity patterns, or post-translational modifications—contribute to structural outcomes, particularly under non-standard environmental conditions like extreme pH, temperature, or ionic strength. Consequently, there exists a critical need for a physically transparent, computationally efficient, and truly ab initio method that not only predicts protein structure but also reveals the dynamic interplay of forces governing the folding process.

The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel simulation framework rooted in the principles of non-equilibrium thermodynamics, drift-diffusion kinetics, and electrostatics. Unlike conventional MD, which tracks every atom over infinitesimal time steps, the invention employs a coarse-grained yet physically rigorous model that captures the essential forces acting on protein substructures—namely, electrostatic attraction/repulsion, dielectric displacement forces arising from polar solvent interactions, thermal diffusion, and global entropy changes. By modeling the motion of residues and secondary structure elements as drift-diffusion processes driven by energy gradients, the invention achieves remarkable computational efficiency, enabling high-accuracy tertiary structure prediction on a standard desktop computer in minutes rather than days or weeks. Furthermore, the method explicitly incorporates the role of water as a polar medium that mediates interactions between charged and nonpolar regions, thereby capturing key aspects of hydrophobic collapse and solvation without resorting to empirical potentials or statistical priors.

Crucially, the invention includes a streamlined secondary structure prediction algorithm that operates directly on the physicochemical properties of the amino acid sequence—charge, hydrophobicity, and polarity—without requiring evolutionary information or machine learning training. This algorithm identifies alpha helices and beta sheets based on local balances between electrostatic repulsion and hydrophobic attraction, reflecting the physical competition between these forces during folding. The predicted secondary structures are then treated as rigid bodies during tertiary folding, significantly reducing the conformational search space while preserving structural integrity. The entire process—from sequence input to final 3D structure—is governed by explicit physical equations, allowing researchers to trace the folding trajectory, quantify energy changes, and interrogate the contribution of individual forces at each step.

This approach not only matches or exceeds the accuracy of contemporary ab initio methods on benchmark datasets such as CASP9 but also provides unique mechanistic insights into folding pathways and environmental sensitivity. For instance, the model naturally explains why certain hydrophobic segments fail to aggregate despite favorable energetics: intervening charged residues generate strong electric fields that attract polar water molecules, which in turn exert a repulsive displacement force on nonpolar regions, preventing collapse. Such phenomena are difficult to capture with statistical models but emerge naturally from the physics-based formalism of the present invention. By bridging the gap between computational speed, physical realism, and interpretability, the invention opens new avenues for studying protein folding in diverse biological contexts, including drug design, enzyme engineering, and the investigation of misfolding diseases.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, ab initio method for predicting the three-dimensional structure of a protein from its amino acid sequence using a physics-based simulation framework that integrates principles of drift-diffusion kinetics, electrostatics, and thermodynamics. The method operates without reliance on homologous templates, statistical potentials, or machine learning models trained on known structures, thereby enabling truly de novo prediction for proteins lacking evolutionary relatives. Central to the invention is a computational model that treats protein folding as a non-equilibrium process driven by the balance of multiple physical forces acting on residues and secondary structure elements, including electrostatic Coulomb forces, dielectric displacement forces arising from polar solvent interactions, thermal diffusion forces, and global entropy changes associated with conformational compaction.

In accordance with the invention, the amino acid sequence of the target protein is first analyzed to assign physicochemical properties—specifically, net charge and hydrophobicity—to each residue. These properties are mapped onto the backbone alpha carbon atoms, which serve as the primary sites for force calculation and motion. The method then employs a two-stage prediction process: (1) a streamlined secondary structure prediction algorithm that identifies alpha helices and beta sheets based on local sequence features, and (2) a tertiary structure simulation that models the drift and diffusion of protein substructures under the influence of the aforementioned physical forces.

The secondary structure prediction algorithm systematically scans the amino acid sequence to detect regions where hydrophobic and electrostatic forces are in near balance, a condition indicative of secondary structure formation. For alpha helix prediction, the algorithm identifies segments bounded by hydrophilic residues within a six-residue window and evaluates the net charge, charge product, and hydrophobic character of the intervening residues according to predefined criteria. If hydrophobic attraction is unopposed by sufficient electrostatic repulsion, an alpha helix is predicted; otherwise, the segment may form a beta sheet or remain unstructured. Beta sheet prediction involves scanning for five-residue windows of unstructured residues and applying thresholds based on the sum of absolute charges and hydrophobic character. This algorithm achieves high accuracy—approximately 70% for alpha helices and 66% for beta sheets on a residue-by-residue basis—and correctly identifies core secondary structures with even greater precision.

Once secondary structures are identified, they are treated as immutable rigid bodies during the tertiary folding simulation. Their collective charge and hydrophobicity are aggregated and assigned to representative points, while unstructured regions are modeled as flexible chains. The tertiary simulation proceeds by iteratively computing the net force and torque acting on each alpha carbon bond pair. The force calculation incorporates four key components: (i) the Coulomb force between charged residues, computed using the dielectric constant of water for distant interactions and adjusted for local environments; (ii) the dielectric displacement force, which arises from the attraction of polar water molecules to regions of high electric field and consequently pushes nonpolar residues toward lower-field regions; (iii) a thermal diffusion force that introduces stochastic motion consistent with the fluctuation-dissipation theorem; and (iv) a global entropy term that modulates the mobility of residues based on the overall compactness of the protein, with entropy typically decreasing upon folding.

At each time step, the bond pair experiencing the greatest net torque is selected to undergo rotational motion, with the angular displacement determined by the drift speed (product of mobility and force) and the lever arm length. To prevent the simulation from becoming trapped in local minima dominated by strong pairwise interactions, the method includes a “freezing” mechanism: if the same bond pair generates the maximum torque for five consecutive time steps, its motion is disabled, effectively locking in the formed secondary structure. This mimics the early formation of autonomous folding units observed in natural proteins. The simulation continues until the protein reaches a low-energy conformation characterized by diminishing energy fluctuations.

The resulting tertiary structure may be further refined through a brief molecular dynamics relaxation step using standard force fields such as AMBER10, which improves root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) accuracy by approximately 2–3% by optimizing local geometry and dielectric interactions. The entire process—from sequence input to final 3D structure—requires less than one minute of CPU time on a standard desktop computer for proteins up to 150 residues, with larger proteins (e.g., 320 residues) completing in comparable timeframes with slightly reduced accuracy.

The invention has been validated against benchmark datasets including CASP9, demonstrating RMSD values averaging 4.9 Å for proteins ranging from 30 to 157 amino acids, with performance comparable to state-of-the-art ab initio methods. Notably, the model accurately reproduces the folding of the Villin headpiece (1VII) with an RMSD of 3.7 Å, approaching the inherent uncertainty of the experimental NMR structure. The method’s sensitivity to environmental conditions—such as pH and temperature—allows for real-time tracking of structural changes in response to ambient perturbations, a capability absent in template-based or purely statistical approaches.

In summary, the invention provides a fast, accurate, and physically interpretable method for ab initio protein structure prediction that elucidates the mechanistic role of fundamental forces in folding. By operating on first principles and avoiding reliance on homologous data, it enables structural prediction for novel proteins, facilitates the study of folding pathways, and supports applications in drug discovery, protein engineering, and the analysis of disease-related mutations.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a comprehensive, physics-based computational framework for predicting the three-dimensional structure of a protein from its amino acid sequence without the use of homologous templates, statistical potentials, or machine learning models trained on known protein structures. The method is founded on the principles of non-equilibrium thermodynamics, drift-diffusion kinetics, and electrostatics, and it explicitly models the physical forces that govern protein folding, including electrostatic interactions, dielectric displacement effects mediated by polar solvents, thermal diffusion, and global entropy changes. The following detailed description outlines the theoretical foundations, computational implementation, and operational workflow of the invention.

The method begins with the input of a protein’s amino acid sequence, which serves as the sole source of information for the prediction. Each amino acid in the sequence is assigned two key physicochemical properties: net charge and hydrophobicity. The net charge is determined based on the ionization state of the residue at the specified ambient conditions (e.g., pH 7.4 for physiological conditions), taking into account both side chain and backbone contributions. Standard pKa values and protonation states are used, and in cases of extreme pH or non-standard conditions, more sophisticated charge assignment protocols may be employed, though such refinements are not required for baseline operation. Hydrophobicity is assigned using established scales from the literature, such as those found in biochemical reference tables, which quantify the relative preference of each residue for nonpolar versus aqueous environments. These properties are then mapped onto the backbone alpha carbon atoms of the protein chain, with the charge and hydrophobicity of each residue attributed to its corresponding alpha carbon. This coarse-graining strategy reduces computational complexity while preserving the essential physical characteristics that drive folding.

The prediction process is divided into two sequential stages: secondary structure prediction and tertiary structure simulation. The secondary structure prediction stage employs a rule-based algorithm that scans the amino acid sequence to identify regions likely to form alpha helices or beta sheets based solely on local sequence features. The algorithm operates by detecting transitions between hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues and evaluating the balance of forces within short sequence windows.

For alpha helix prediction, the algorithm initiates a scan upon encountering a hydrophilic residue following a hydrophobic segment. A search window of up to six subsequent residues is examined. If another hydrophilic residue is found within this window, the intervening segment is evaluated for helix propensity. The evaluation is based on three quantitative criteria derived from the summed charge, charge product, and hydrophobic character of the residues in the segment. Specifically, if the net hydrophobic attraction is sufficiently strong and not counteracted by electrostatic repulsion from charged residues, an alpha helix is predicted. The presence of one or more charged residues in critical positions can generate a repulsive dielectric displacement force—mediated by the influx of polar water molecules toward the charged region—that prevents hydrophobic collapse and thereby inhibits helix formation. In such cases, the segment may instead form a beta sheet or remain unstructured. This mechanism explains observed phenomena such as the conversion of alpha helices to beta sheets in disease-associated mutations, as seen in Alzheimer’s fibril formation.

Beta sheet prediction follows a complementary approach. The algorithm scans the sequence for residues not already assigned to an alpha helix. Upon identifying an unstructured residue, it examines the next four consecutive residues to determine if all are unstructured. If so, a five-residue window is established, and the sum of absolute charges and the sum of hydrophobic character are computed. If the charge sum is below a threshold (e.g., <0.3) and the hydrophobic sum exceeds a minimum value (e.g., >0.1), the segment is predicted to form a beta sheet. This reflects the tendency of beta sheets to form in regions with moderate hydrophobicity and low net charge, where extended conformations are stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonding and side chain packing.

Once secondary structures are identified, they are treated as rigid, immutable units during the tertiary folding simulation. Their collective charge and hydrophobicity are aggregated and assigned to representative points (e.g., the centroid of the secondary structure), and internal degrees of freedom are frozen. Unstructured regions are modeled as flexible polypeptide chains with full rotational freedom about backbone bonds. The tertiary simulation models the protein as a collection of interacting substructures whose motion is governed by a drift-diffusion equation derived from non-equilibrium thermodynamics.

The core of the simulation is the computation of the net force acting on each alpha carbon bond pair. Four distinct forces are considered:

1. **Electrostatic (Coulomb) Force**: The force between two charged residues is calculated using Coulomb’s law, \( F_{\text{elect}} = \frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_w r^2} \), where \( q_1 \) and \( q_2 \) are the charges, \( r \) is the distance between them, and \( \epsilon_w \approx 78\epsilon_0 \) is the dielectric constant of water. This approximation is valid for solvent-exposed residues; for buried residues in secondary structures, local dielectric effects are accounted for during the final MD relaxation step.

2. **Dielectric Displacement Force**: This force arises from the interaction between polar water molecules and electric fields generated by charged residues. When a charged residue creates a strong electric field, polar water is drawn toward it, displacing nonpolar (hydrophobic) residues away from the high-field region. The displacement force is modeled as \( F_{\text{disp}} = -\frac{4\beta|q|^2}{r^5} \), where \( \beta \) is a constant that encapsulates the volume and permittivity differences between water and nonpolar protein regions. This force is critical for explaining why hydrophobic residues do not always aggregate, even when thermodynamically favorable.

3. **Thermal Diffusion Force**: This stochastic force accounts for random thermal motion and is derived from the Einstein relation, \( \nu = \sqrt{D/\tau} \), where \( D \) is the diffusivity and \( \tau \) is the time step. The corresponding force is \( F_{\text{thermal}} = \gamma\sqrt{kT} \), where \( \gamma \) is a mobility-related constant and \( kT \) is the thermal energy. This force introduces noise that prevents the simulation from becoming trapped in local energy minima.

4. **Global Entropy Change**: While not a direct force, the change in global entropy upon folding modulates the mobility \( \mu \) of residues according to the relation \( \mu = \frac{D\nabla\ln(n_p)}{\nabla\{VP - ST + \sum_j \phi_{L_j}n_j\} + kT\nabla\ln(n_p)} \), where \( S \) is entropy, \( T \) is temperature, and other terms represent various energy contributions. As the protein folds and becomes more compact, entropy typically decreases, reducing mobility and slowing conformational changes.

The net force on each bond pair is converted to torque by multiplying by the lever arm length. At each time step, the bond pair with the greatest net torque is selected to undergo rotational motion. The angular displacement is determined by the drift speed \( \mu F \) and the time step duration. To mimic the early stabilization of secondary structures, a “freezing” rule is applied: if the same bond pair produces the maximum torque for five consecutive time steps, its motion is disabled, effectively locking in the formed structure.

The simulation proceeds iteratively, with the protein conformation updated at each step and the energy tracked via the work done by forces (\( \Delta E = -F \cdot \theta \cdot r_0 \)). Energy fluctuations are large initially but diminish as the structure converges. The process terminates when energy changes fall below a threshold, indicating a stable low-energy state.

The final structure may be refined using a short molecular dynamics relaxation with a standard force field (e.g., AMBER10), which optimizes bond angles, dihedrals, and local dielectric interactions, improving RMSD by 2–3%. The entire pipeline runs in under one minute on a desktop PC for proteins up to 150 residues.

The invention has been validated on diverse proteins, achieving average RMSD of 4.9 Å and accurately modeling environmental sensitivity. It provides a transparent, interpretable, and efficient solution to the ab initio protein folding problem.
