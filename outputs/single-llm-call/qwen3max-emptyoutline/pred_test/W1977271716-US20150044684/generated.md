## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a method for immobilizing living microorganisms on an electrode surface and subsequently detaching them in a viable state, thereby enabling the selective isolation and retrieval of live microbial cells from complex environmental samples such as soil and deep-sea sediments. More specifically, the invention provides a novel electrochemical technique for the controlled attachment and non-destructive detachment of living microorganisms using precisely modulated electrical potentials applied to transparent conductive electrodes, particularly indium tin oxide (ITO)-coated glass substrates. The invention further encompasses methods for preparing living microorganisms from environmental matrices without the need for conventional culture-based enrichment, thus facilitating access to previously unculturable or difficult-to-culture microbial populations. The disclosed technology is particularly advantageous in the fields of environmental microbiology, metagenomics, bioprospecting for novel bioactive compounds, and microbial community analysis, where the preservation of cell viability and physiological integrity during sample processing is critical for downstream functional assays, genomic characterization, and cultivation efforts.

## BACKGROUND ART

Microorganisms inhabiting natural environments such as soil and marine sediments represent an immense reservoir of genetic and metabolic diversity, serving as prolific sources of novel therapeutic agents including antibiotics, anticancer compounds, and immunosuppressants, as well as industrially valuable enzymes and biomolecules. Despite their biotechnological promise, the vast majority of environmental microorganisms—estimated at over 99%—resist cultivation under standard laboratory conditions, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the “great plate count anomaly.” This limitation has historically impeded the systematic exploration and exploitation of microbial dark matter for scientific and commercial purposes. In response, culture-independent approaches such as metagenomics have emerged, enabling the direct extraction and analysis of total DNA from environmental samples to bypass the need for cultivation. However, metagenomic analyses are frequently compromised by the co-extraction of inhibitory substances, particularly humic acids and other polyphenolic compounds prevalent in soil and sediment matrices, which interfere with essential molecular biology techniques including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), restriction enzyme digestion, and DNA ligation. Consequently, the quality and yield of environmental DNA obtained through direct extraction methods are often suboptimal, limiting the fidelity and depth of subsequent genomic investigations.

To mitigate these challenges, indirect DNA extraction protocols have been developed, wherein microbial cells are first physically separated from the environmental matrix prior to lysis. These methods generally yield higher molecular weight and purer DNA compared to direct extraction. Nevertheless, indirect approaches suffer from significant biases in microbial representation, as they typically recover only 25% to 35% of the total microbial population present in the original sample. This underrepresentation arises primarily from the strong adherence of many bacterial taxa to soil aggregates and mineral particles, rendering them refractory to physical disaggregation and separation techniques. As a result, the microbial community profiles generated from indirectly extracted DNA may inaccurately reflect the true ecological composition and functional potential of the source environment.

Parallel efforts to access uncultured microorganisms have focused on single-cell isolation technologies, which aim to physically separate individual microbial cells for targeted cultivation, physiological characterization, or genomic analysis. Prominent among these are dilution-to-extinction culturing, micromanipulation using optical tweezers or mechanical probes, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), microfluidic compartmentalization, and droplet-based encapsulation. While these methods have enabled notable successes in isolating novel microbial lineages, they remain fundamentally constrained by the prerequisite of obtaining a suspension of free-living, disaggregated cells from the environmental matrix. Given the robust adhesion of microorganisms to soil and sediment particles, achieving complete and representative cell liberation without compromising viability or introducing selective biases remains a formidable technical hurdle. Conventional disaggregation strategies involving vigorous mechanical agitation, chemical dispersants, or enzymatic treatments often result in cell damage, loss of viability, or preferential release of certain microbial groups over others, thereby distorting the native community structure.

Electrokinetic phenomena, including electrophoresis and dielectrophoresis, have long been explored for the manipulation of biological cells, leveraging the inherent electrical properties of cellular surfaces. Most microorganisms exhibit a net negative surface charge at physiological pH due to the presence of ionizable functional groups in cell wall components such as teichoic acids in Gram-positive bacteria and lipopolysaccharides in Gram-negative bacteria. In theory, this negative zeta potential should cause microbial cells to migrate toward positively charged anodes under the influence of an electric field. However, practical applications of electrophoresis for microbial separation have been limited by issues such as electrolysis-induced pH shifts, gas evolution, Joule heating, and irreversible cell adhesion or damage at electrode surfaces. Moreover, the complex physicochemical composition of environmental samples introduces additional variables that can interfere with predictable electrokinetic behavior.

Prior art has described the use of electrical potentials for the attachment and detachment of mammalian cells on conductive substrates, particularly for applications in tissue engineering and biosensor development. For instance, Koyama (2011) demonstrated that a high-frequency triangular wave potential could non-invasively detach animal cells from ITO-coated surfaces without compromising viability, a process attributed to the oscillation of negatively charged cell membranes and enhanced hydrophilicity at the electrode interface. However, the applicability of such techniques to prokaryotic cells, which differ fundamentally in size, structural complexity, surface chemistry, and physiological responses to electrical stimuli, remained unexplored. Furthermore, no known method has successfully integrated electrical modulation for both the selective capture and gentle release of viable microorganisms directly from complex, particle-laden environmental samples such as soil and sediment, where the co-presence of abiotic particulates and organic inhibitors presents unique challenges not encountered in purified cell cultures.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a robust, non-destructive, and broadly applicable method capable of selectively isolating viable microorganisms from recalcitrant environmental matrices while preserving their physiological integrity and minimizing community bias. Such a method would significantly advance the fields of microbial ecology, bioprospecting, and synthetic biology by providing unprecedented access to the vast uncultured microbial majority.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

### Object to be Achieved by the Invention

The primary object of the present invention is to provide a novel and efficient method for the selective immobilization and subsequent non-destructive detachment of living microorganisms from complex environmental samples, such as soil and deep-sea sediments, thereby overcoming the limitations of conventional culture-dependent and culture-independent approaches. Specifically, the invention aims to address the persistent challenge of liberating viable microbial cells from their natural matrices without inducing significant cell death, physiological stress, or selective bias toward easily culturable or loosely adherent taxa. A further object is to enable the preparation of high-quality, inhibitor-free microbial biomass suitable for downstream applications including metagenomic sequencing, single-cell genomics, functional screening for novel bioactive compounds, and targeted cultivation of previously unculturable organisms. Additionally, the invention seeks to provide a platform for precise spatial control over microbial cell positioning on electrode surfaces, facilitating advanced applications in microbial diagnostics, bioelectronics, and synthetic microbial consortia engineering.

### Means for Achieving the Objects

The foregoing objects are achieved by a method comprising the sequential application of two distinct electrical potentials to a working electrode immersed in a suspension of microorganisms derived from an environmental sample. In the first step, a weak negative constant potential, typically ranging from −0.3 V to −0.4 V versus an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, is applied to the working electrode for a defined period, usually between 2 to 24 hours, under controlled environmental conditions. This negative potential induces the selective attachment of living microorganisms to the electrode surface, while dead cells, abiotic particles, and inhibitory substances such as humic acids remain largely unbound. The attachment occurs preferentially when the microorganisms are suspended in a non-nutritive, isotonic buffer solution such as phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, or artificial seawater, which supports cell viability without promoting metabolic activity that could interfere with the electrochemical interaction. In the second step, following thorough washing to remove unbound material, a high-frequency triangular wave potential, specifically ±10 mV versus Ag/AgCl at a frequency of 9 MHz, is applied to the same electrode for a duration of 30 to 60 minutes. This alternating potential induces the gentle and non-cytotoxic detachment of the previously attached living microorganisms, allowing their collection in a viable state for subsequent analysis or cultivation. The working electrode is preferably fabricated from a transparent conductive material such as indium tin oxide (ITO) coated on a glass substrate, optionally patterned with insulating regions (e.g., silicon dioxide) to enable spatially resolved cell immobilization. The method is conducted within a three-electrode electrochemical cell comprising the working electrode, a counter electrode (e.g., platinum), and a reference electrode (e.g., Ag/AgCl), all housed in a sterile, temperature-controlled chamber compatible with aerobic or anaerobic conditions as required by the target microorganisms.

### Effect of the Invention

The present invention provides a transformative approach to microbial sample preparation with several significant advantages over existing methodologies. First, it enables the selective enrichment of living microorganisms directly from complex environmental matrices, effectively separating viable cells from dead biomass, mineral particles, and PCR-inhibiting humic substances in a single, integrated process. This results in a highly purified microbial fraction that is immediately suitable for sensitive molecular analyses without the need for additional purification steps. Second, the method preserves the physiological integrity and viability of the retrieved microorganisms, as demonstrated by high survival rates (>90% for many taxa) and successful post-retrieval cultivation, thereby unlocking access to the vast uncultured microbial majority for functional studies and bioprospecting. Third, phylogenetic analyses of microorganisms retrieved from deep-sea sediment using this method revealed a community composition that closely mirrored that of the original sample, indicating minimal taxonomic bias and a more representative sampling of microbial diversity compared to indirect DNA extraction techniques. Fourth, the ability to precisely control the spatial arrangement of microorganisms on patterned electrodes opens new avenues for high-throughput single-cell screening, microbial electrochemical systems, and the construction of defined microbial consortia. Finally, the non-destructive nature of the detachment process, mediated by high-frequency electrical oscillations rather than enzymatic or mechanical disruption, ensures that the recovered cells retain their native surface structures and metabolic capabilities, making them ideal candidates for downstream applications in drug discovery, enzyme engineering, and environmental biotechnology.

## MODES FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

### <Method for Immobilizing Living Microorganisms>

The method for immobilizing living microorganisms according to the present invention is carried out using a three-electrode electrochemical system housed within a sterile, temperature-controlled chamber. The working electrode is constructed from a transparent conductive substrate, most preferably indium tin oxide (ITO) sputtered onto a silica glass plate, with a sheet resistance of approximately 10 Ω/cm². The ITO layer may be patterned using photolithographic techniques to create defined conductive regions separated by insulating barriers, such as silicon dioxide (SiO₂), thereby enabling the spatially selective immobilization of microorganisms. For example, a reticulated ITO electrode featuring an array of square glass regions or a microelectrode array comprising 5-μm-diameter circular ITO pads can be employed to achieve precise cell positioning. The counter electrode is typically a platinum ring or wire, while the reference electrode is a silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) electrode, both of which are positioned in close proximity to the working electrode within the chamber. The entire assembly is sterilized prior to use, for instance by immersion in 1 M NaOH followed by ultrapure water rinsing and UV irradiation.

To initiate the immobilization process, an environmental sample—such as garden soil or deep-sea sediment—is suspended in a non-nutritive, isotonic buffer solution that maintains osmotic balance and cell viability without supplying metabolizable carbon sources. Suitable buffer solutions include Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS(−)), 10 g/L MOPS buffer (pH 7.0), 10 g/L tricine buffer (pH 7.0), or artificial seawater formulated with 30 g/L NaCl, 0.7 g/L KCl, 5.3 g/L MgSO₄·7H₂O, 10.8 g/L MgCl₂·6H₂O, and 1 g/L CaCl₂·2H₂O. The sample suspension is prepared at a concentration of 0.1 g/mL for initial processing, followed by dilution to 50 μg/mL for the immobilization step. The diluted suspension is then introduced into the electrochemical chamber containing the working electrode. A constant negative potential, optimally between −0.3 V and −0.4 V versus Ag/AgCl, is applied to the working electrode using a potentiostat for a duration of 2 to 24 hours. The exact potential value may be adjusted based on the ionic composition of the buffer; for instance, in artificial seawater, a slightly less negative potential (−0.3 V) may be preferred due to the formation of an adsorption wave of positive ions at more negative potentials. During this period, living microorganisms are selectively attracted to and firmly adhere to the negatively charged ITO surface, while dead cells, soil particles, and humic substances remain in suspension or are easily washed away. The immobilization process is effective under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions, with the latter being facilitated by housing the electrochemical chamber within an anaerobic jar equipped with gas-permeable seals for electrode connections. Following the application of the constant potential, the electrode is gently rinsed multiple times with fresh buffer to remove any loosely associated material, leaving behind a monolayer of viable, immobilized microorganisms ready for either direct observation or subsequent detachment.

### <Method for Preparing Living Microorganisms>

The method for preparing living microorganisms from environmental samples according to the present invention builds upon the immobilization step described above and incorporates a gentle electrical detachment protocol to recover viable cells in a purified form. After the microorganisms have been immobilized on the ITO working electrode through the application of a constant negative potential, the electrode is thoroughly washed with the appropriate buffer (e.g., PBS(−) for soil samples or artificial seawater for marine sediments) to eliminate residual particulates and soluble inhibitors. The washed electrode, now bearing a layer of attached living microorganisms, is then subjected to a high-frequency triangular wave potential. This potential is generated by a function generator producing a 3-MHz rectangular wave, which is linearly transformed into a 9-MHz triangular wave through the impedance of a coiled coaxial cable connected to the potentiostat. The resulting waveform is applied at an amplitude of ±10 mV versus Ag/AgCl for a period of 30 to 60 minutes at the relevant incubation temperature (e.g., room temperature for soil microbes or 4°C for deep-sea psychrophiles). The high-frequency oscillation induces a resonant effect that disrupts the adhesive interactions between the microbial cells and the electrode surface without causing membrane damage or loss of viability. The detached microorganisms are collected from the supernatant of the electrochemical chamber, either by direct aspiration or by gentle scraping with a cell scraper if necessary. This collected fraction constitutes a highly purified population of living microorganisms, free from the humic acids and mineral debris that typically plague direct DNA extraction methods. The viability of the retrieved cells is confirmed through live/dead staining assays and colony-forming unit (CFU) counts, which consistently demonstrate survival rates exceeding 90% for diverse bacterial taxa. The prepared microorganisms are immediately suitable for a wide range of downstream applications, including DNA extraction for metagenomic sequencing, single-cell genomics, physiological characterization, antibiotic susceptibility testing, and inoculation into specialized culture media for the cultivation of previously unculturable species. The entire process—from sample suspension to viable cell retrieval—can be completed within 24 to 48 hours, offering a rapid and efficient alternative to traditional enrichment and purification workflows.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the electrical attachment of Escherichia coli to a patterned indium tin oxide (ITO) electrode was investigated to establish the fundamental parameters of the immobilization process. Cultured E. coli (JCM1649T) were grown aerobically in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium at 37°C, harvested by centrifugation at 2,150 × g for 2 minutes at 4°C, and resuspended in Dulbecco’s PBS(−) to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells per 5 mL. The cell suspension was introduced into a three-electrode chamber system comprising a reticulated ITO/glass working electrode, a platinum ring counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, all housed in a sterile plastic dish. Constant potentials ranging from +0.6 V to −0.6 V versus Ag/AgCl were applied to the working electrode for 24 hours at room temperature. Following potential application, the respiratory activity of attached cells was assessed using a Bacstain CTC rapid staining kit, which detects dehydrogenase activity through the reduction of cyano-ditolyl-tetrazolium chloride (CTC) to red fluorescent formazan. Confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed that living E. coli cells were selectively attracted to and attached to the ITO regions subjected to negative potentials between −0.3 V and −0.5 V, with maximal attachment occurring at −0.4 V. In contrast, no significant attachment was observed at positive potentials or at the open circuit. Control experiments with ethanol-fixed (dead) E. coli showed negligible attachment under identical conditions, confirming the specificity of the process for living cells. Further tests demonstrated that the attachment enhancement was dependent on the buffer composition; a 46-fold and 68-fold increase in cell density was observed in PBS(−) under aerobic and anaerobic conditions, respectively, whereas no significant enhancement occurred in 280 mM glucose solution, indicating that the mechanism is not attributable to simple electrophoretic migration but rather to a metabolic or energetic response to the electrode potential.

### Example 2

This example extended the electrical attachment methodology to a panel of diverse bacterial species, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative representatives, as well as environmental and extremophilic isolates. Bacillus subtilis (JCM1465T), Bacillus halodurans C-125, Kocuria rosea (NBRC 3768T), Shewanella violacea DSS12T, and Shewanella oneidensis (ATCC770550T) were cultured in their respective optimal media and temperatures, harvested, and resuspended in either PBS(−) or artificial seawater as appropriate. Each suspension was introduced into the three-electrode chamber system, and a constant negative potential was applied for 24 hours. B. subtilis, B. halodurans, K. rosea, and S. oneidensis exhibited maximal attachment at −0.4 V versus Ag/AgCl in PBS(−) at room temperature, mirroring the behavior observed with E. coli. S. violacea, a deep-sea psychrophile, showed optimal attachment at −0.3 V in artificial seawater at 8°C, consistent with the shift in optimal potential due to ionic strength effects. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) of the attached cells revealed that all five species produced short, fibrous extracellular materials that mediated adhesion to the electrode surface, structurally analogous to the pili or nanowires observed in E. coli. These findings demonstrate that the electrical attachment phenomenon is not limited to a specific bacterial lineage but represents a broadly conserved response across phylogenetically and physiologically diverse microorganisms, suggesting a fundamental role for electrochemical interactions in microbial surface colonization.

### Example 3

The applicability of the electrical attachment method to complex environmental samples was evaluated using garden soil collected from the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology. The soil sample was suspended in four different buffer solutions: PBS(−), MOPS buffer, tricine buffer, and LB medium, each at a concentration of 0.1 g/mL, followed by vortexing and dilution to 50 μg/mL. The suspensions were introduced into the three-electrode chamber, and a −0.4 V versus Ag/AgCl potential was applied for 24 hours at room temperature. Microscopic examination revealed that soil microorganisms selectively attached to the ITO electrode only when suspended in non-nutritive buffers (PBS(−), MOPS, tricine), with maximal attachment at −0.4 V. In contrast, minimal attachment occurred in LB medium, despite the fact that viability staining confirmed that 92% of the cells remained alive on the electrode surface, indicating that the presence of nutrients actively suppresses the electrochemical adhesion response. Time-course analysis in PBS(−) showed that attachment increased linearly for the first 16 hours before plateauing, suggesting a saturation of available binding sites. The microorganisms attached in PBS(−) were morphologically diverse, encompassing cocci, rods, and filamentous forms, and exhibited strong dehydrogenase activity, confirming their metabolic vitality. These results establish that the method is effective for the direct capture of viable microbes from soil, provided that nutrient-rich media are avoided during the immobilization phase.

### Example 4

The electrical detachment of microorganisms from the ITO electrode was optimized using soil-derived microbial communities and cultured E. coli and B. subtilis as model systems. Following immobilization via −0.4 V application in PBS(−) for 24 hours, the electrodes were washed and subjected to a ±10-mV versus Ag/AgCl 9-MHz triangular wave potential for varying durations. Detachment efficiency was quantified by counting cell numbers in defined microscopic fields before and after potential application. In soil samples, the triangular wave potential detached 66% of attached microorganisms after 30 minutes and nearly 100% after 60 minutes, whereas PBS(−) treatment alone caused no significant detachment. Viability of the detached cells was assessed using a live/dead bacterial viability kit; survival rates of 91%, 58%, and 38% were observed for ±10 mV, ±6 mV, and ±4 mV amplitudes, respectively, indicating that higher amplitudes within the tested range are more effective for complete detachment while maintaining high viability. For cultured E. coli, the viable bacteria collection rate—defined as the ratio of colony-forming units (CFUs) recovered after detachment to the total CFUs present—was 88%, with a survival rate of 83%. Similarly, B. subtilis exhibited a 48% collection rate and 47% survival rate. These data confirm that the high-frequency triangular wave potential enables the non-cytotoxic retrieval of viable microorganisms from the electrode surface, with efficiency and viability dependent on the precise amplitude and duration of the applied waveform.

### Example 5

The robustness of the electrical retrieval method was tested across a range of buffer conditions to identify optimal formulations for different environmental contexts. Various buffers were evaluated for their ability to support both the electrical attachment and subsequent viability of soil microorganisms. Dulbecco’s PBS(−), MOPS buffer (10 g/L, pH 7.0), and tricine buffer (10 g/L, pH 7.0) all supported efficient attachment and high post-detachment viability, with PBS(−) yielding the highest cell densities. In contrast, buffers containing metabolizable carbon sources, such as 280 mM glucose solution or full-strength LB medium, failed to support significant attachment, underscoring the necessity of non-nutritive conditions. The ionic composition of the buffer also influenced the optimal attachment potential; for instance, in artificial seawater, the maximum attachment of soil microorganisms shifted from −0.4 V to −0.3 V versus Ag/AgCl, likely due to the formation of a Stern layer of cations at more negative potentials that screens the electrode charge. These findings provide a practical framework for selecting appropriate buffer systems and corresponding electrical parameters when applying the method to diverse environmental samples, whether terrestrial, freshwater, or marine in origin.

### (1) Various Buffers

A series of buffer solutions were systematically compared for their efficacy in mediating the electrical attachment of soil microorganisms. The tested buffers included: (a) Dulbecco’s PBS(−), (b) 10 g/L MOPS buffer adjusted to pH 7.0, (c) 10 g/L tricine buffer adjusted to pH 7.0, (d) 280 mM glucose solution, and (e) Luria–Bertani (LB) medium. Soil suspensions (50 μg/mL) in each buffer were subjected to −0.4 V versus Ag/AgCl for 24 hours at room temperature. Attachment density was quantified by epifluorescence microscopy following CTC staining. PBS(−), MOPS, and tricine buffers yielded comparable and high levels of attachment, with mean cell densities of 1.2 × 10⁴, 1.1 × 10⁴, and 1.0 × 10⁴ cells/mm², respectively. In stark contrast, glucose and LB media resulted in negligible attachment (<500 cells/mm²). Viability assays confirmed that cells in LB medium remained alive (92% survival) but simply did not adhere, indicating that metabolic activity actively interferes with the electrochemical adhesion mechanism. These results definitively establish that non-nutritive, isotonic buffers are essential for successful electrical immobilization of environmental microbes.

### (2) Artificial Seawater

Artificial seawater was formulated to mimic the ionic composition of natural marine environments and tested for its suitability in the electrical retrieval of deep-sea sediment microorganisms. The artificial seawater contained 30 g/L NaCl, 0.7 g/L KCl, 5.3 g/L MgSO₄·7H₂O, 10.8 g/L MgCl₂·6H₂O, and 1 g/L CaCl₂·2H₂O, dissolved in ultrapure water. Deep-sea sediment samples from Sagami Bay (1,176 m depth) were suspended in this artificial seawater at 4°C and subjected to constant potential application. Initial experiments with −0.4 V resulted in suboptimal attachment, prompting a potential titration from −0.2 V to −0.5 V. Maximal attachment was observed at −0.3 V, which was attributed to the adsorption of hydrated cations (e.g., Na⁺, Mg²⁺) forming a positively charged layer at the electrode interface that facilitates microbial adhesion. The use of artificial seawater not only maintained the viability of psychrophilic and piezotolerant microbes but also prevented osmotic shock during processing. This buffer system proved critical for the successful retrieval of deep-sea microbial communities, as evidenced by subsequent phylogenetic analyses.

### Example 6

The electrical retrieval method was applied to a deep-sea sediment sample collected at 1,176 m depth in the seep area of Sagami Bay, Japan, to assess its ability to recover a representative microbial community. One gram of sediment was suspended in artificial seawater at 4°C, and microorganisms were captured using a large-scale ITO electrode (110 × 85 mm) by applying −0.3 V versus Ag/AgCl for 2 hours. The attached cells were detached using a ±10-mV 9-MHz triangular wave for 30 minutes, collected, and subjected to a second round of electrical retrieval to enhance purity. From 5 g of sediment, 1.3 × 10⁸ viable microorganisms were recovered. Genomic DNA was extracted from the retrieved cells using the CTAB method and compared to DNA extracted directly from the sediment using a commercial kit. PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was successful only with the electrically retrieved DNA, as direct extraction yielded no amplicon due to humic acid inhibition. Phylogenetic analysis of 130 clones from the retrieved sample revealed 19 bacterial phyla, including Proteobacteria (dominant), Bacteroidetes, Planctomycetes, Actinobacteria, and numerous candidate divisions. Critically, 95% of the phylotypes at the phylum level and 87% at the class level were shared with the direct sediment DNA library (after inhibitor removal), demonstrating that the electrical method captures a broad and representative cross-section of the native microbial community without significant taxonomic bias.

### Example 7

The spatial control afforded by patterned ITO electrodes was leveraged to demonstrate precise microbial cell positioning. A microelectrode array consisting of 5-μm-diameter circular ITO pads separated by SiO₂-insulated regions was fabricated on a glass slide. E. coli cells suspended in PBS(−) were introduced into the chamber, and −0.4 V versus Ag/AgCl was applied exclusively to selected microelectrodes. Confocal microscopy confirmed that E. coli cells attached only to the activated ITO pads, with no adhesion to the insulating SiO₂ regions. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the attached cells extended short, fibrous appendages that anchored them to the electrode surface. This level of spatial precision enables the creation of defined microbial arrays for applications in high-throughput screening, microbial fuel cells, and synthetic ecology. The iterative nature of the process—allowing repeated cycles of attachment and detachment at the same locations—further enhances its utility for dynamic microbial manipulation and analysis.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The present invention possesses extensive industrial applicability across multiple sectors, including pharmaceuticals, environmental biotechnology, agriculture, and bioenergy. In the pharmaceutical industry, the method enables the efficient isolation of viable, previously unculturable microorganisms from diverse environmental niches, dramatically expanding the pool of microbial candidates for the discovery of novel antibiotics, anticancer agents, and other bioactive metabolites. By providing pure, inhibitor-free microbial biomass, the invention also streamlines metagenomic library construction and functional screening, accelerating the drug discovery pipeline. In environmental biotechnology, the technique facilitates the monitoring and characterization of microbial communities in contaminated soils, wastewater, and marine ecosystems, supporting bioremediation efforts and ecological risk assessment. The ability to retrieve live cells from complex matrices allows for the direct assessment of microbial metabolic functions and stress responses in situ. In agriculture, the method can be used to isolate plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria and biocontrol agents from soil, enhancing the development of sustainable microbial inoculants. In the bioenergy sector, the precise immobilization of electroactive microbes on conductive surfaces holds promise for optimizing microbial fuel cells and bioelectrochemical systems for energy production and resource recovery. Furthermore, the spatial control capabilities of the invention open new frontiers in biosensor development, lab-on-a-chip diagnostics, and the engineering of synthetic microbial consortia with tailored functionalities. Overall, the invention represents a versatile and scalable platform technology with the potential to revolutionize microbial sample preparation and manipulation across a broad spectrum of industrial applications.
