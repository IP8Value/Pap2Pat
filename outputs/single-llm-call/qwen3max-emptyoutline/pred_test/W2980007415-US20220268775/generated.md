# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of immuno-oncology has undergone a transformative evolution over the past several decades, driven in large part by the discovery and characterization of immune checkpoint molecules that regulate the activation, proliferation, and effector functions of T lymphocytes. Among these, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), also known as CD279, stands as a pivotal regulator of adaptive immunity. First identified in 1992 by Tasuku Honjo and colleagues at Kyoto University, PD-1 is a transmembrane receptor expressed primarily on activated T cells, B cells, natural killer T cells, and myeloid cells. Its physiological role is to dampen immune responses and maintain peripheral tolerance, thereby preventing autoimmune pathology. However, in the context of cancer, this same inhibitory mechanism is co-opted by tumors to evade immune surveillance. Tumor cells and associated stromal cells frequently upregulate the ligands for PD-1—namely, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1, or CD274) and PD-L2 (CD273)—which engage PD-1 on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, leading to T-cell exhaustion, anergy, or apoptosis. This immunosuppressive axis represents a major barrier to effective anti-tumor immunity.

In recognition of the profound therapeutic implications of this pathway, immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1 or PD-L1 have revolutionized the treatment of numerous malignancies, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, and others. These monoclonal antibodies block the interaction between PD-1 and its ligands, thereby reinvigorating exhausted T cells and restoring anti-tumor immune responses. The clinical success of such agents has underscored the critical importance of understanding the dynamics of the PD-1/PD-L1 axis not only as a therapeutic target but also as a source of biomarkers that can inform patient selection, predict treatment response, and monitor disease progression.

Parallel to the development of checkpoint inhibitors, therapeutic cancer vaccines have emerged as another promising modality in immuno-oncology. Unlike checkpoint blockade, which removes inhibitory signals, cancer vaccines aim to actively stimulate the immune system by presenting tumor-associated antigens to antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells (DCs), thereby priming naïve T cells and expanding tumor-specific effector populations. Two prominent approaches in personalized cancer vaccination involve autologous tumor cell vaccines (TCVs) and autologous dendritic cell vaccines (DCVs). In TCV strategies, irradiated autologous tumor cells—often derived from short-term cultures of surgically resected metastases—are administered directly to the patient, relying on endogenous APCs to phagocytose, process, and present tumor antigens. In contrast, DCV approaches involve the ex vivo generation of dendritic cells from the patient’s own monocytes or CD34+ progenitors, followed by loading these DCs with tumor antigens—typically derived from the same autologous tumor cell lines—and reinfusing them as potent, pre-activated APCs capable of efficiently initiating T-cell responses.

Despite their conceptual appeal, the clinical efficacy of cancer vaccines has been variable, and identifying reliable biomarkers to predict which patients will benefit remains a significant challenge. Traditional immunological readouts—such as delayed-type hypersensitivity reactions, ELISPOT assays for interferon-gamma secretion, or flow cytometric analysis of T-cell subsets—are often labor-intensive, lack standardization, and may not correlate robustly with clinical outcomes. Consequently, there is a pressing need for minimally invasive, quantifiable, and clinically actionable biomarkers that reflect the underlying state of anti-tumor immunity and its modulation by therapeutic interventions.

One such candidate biomarker is soluble programmed cell death protein-1 (sPD-1), a circulating form of PD-1 that lacks the transmembrane domain and is detectable in serum and plasma. sPD-1 is believed to arise through alternative splicing of the PDCD1 gene or proteolytic cleavage of membrane-bound PD-1. Its biological function remains incompletely understood, but it has been hypothesized to act as a decoy receptor that modulates PD-1 signaling by competing with membrane-bound PD-1 for ligand binding, or conversely, by stabilizing ligand-receptor interactions. Regardless of its precise mechanism, sPD-1 levels are thought to reflect the degree of T-cell activation and the intensity of ongoing immune regulation via the PD-1 pathway. For instance, interferon-gamma—a hallmark cytokine of Th1-type immune responses—has been shown to upregulate PD-1 expression on lymphocytes, potentially leading to increased shedding or splicing into the soluble form. Thus, in the context of cancer immunotherapy, sPD-1 may serve as a dynamic indicator of the host immune response to tumor antigens and the counter-regulatory mechanisms that seek to suppress it.

Prior studies have explored sPD-1 as a prognostic or predictive biomarker in various malignancies, but results have been inconsistent. In hepatocellular carcinoma, elevated sPD-1 has been associated with worse prognosis, whereas in non-small cell lung cancer treated with epidermal growth factor receptor inhibitors, higher sPD-1 correlated with improved outcomes. In pancreatic cancer, sPD-1 levels showed no clear association with survival but were linked to systemic inflammation and T-cell infiltration. These discrepancies likely stem from differences in tumor biology, therapeutic contexts, assay methodologies, and patient populations. Moreover, most investigations have focused solely on baseline sPD-1 levels, neglecting the potential informational value of longitudinal changes in sPD-1 following therapeutic intervention.

The present invention arises from a detailed retrospective analysis of patients with metastatic melanoma enrolled in a randomized phase II clinical trial comparing autologous DCV and TCV. In this trial, paired serum samples were collected before and after the initial course of vaccination, enabling a unique opportunity to evaluate both baseline sPD-1 concentrations and their temporal dynamics in relation to long-term survival. Contrary to initial expectations, baseline sPD-1 alone was not prognostic across the entire cohort. However, a striking divergence emerged when patients were stratified by treatment arm: among those receiving DCV—but not TCV—a composite biomarker combining baseline sPD-1 levels and the direction of change after vaccination demonstrated strong predictive power for three-year and five-year survival. Specifically, DCV-treated patients were classified into two groups: a “sPD-1 low/decreased” cohort, defined by either very low baseline sPD-1 (<100 pg/mL) or intermediate baseline levels (<200,000 pg/mL) accompanied by a decrease in sPD-1 after vaccination; and a “sPD-1 high/increased” cohort, defined by either extremely high baseline sPD-1 (>200,000 pg/mL) or any increase in sPD-1 post-vaccination. This classification correctly identified 80% of long-term survivors and 86% of short-term non-survivors in the DCV group, suggesting a previously unrecognized interaction between vaccine type, immune checkpoint dynamics, and clinical outcome.

This finding has profound implications for the design and implementation of future cancer vaccine trials. It suggests that not all cellular vaccines elicit immunological effects that are equally reflected in sPD-1 kinetics, and that the predictive utility of sPD-1 is contingent upon the specific mechanism of antigen presentation employed. Furthermore, it provides a rationale for using sPD-1 profiling to enrich clinical trial populations—excluding patients with extremely high baseline sPD-1 who are unlikely to benefit from DCV monotherapy, or selecting those with favorable sPD-1 trajectories for combination strategies with checkpoint inhibitors. The invention thus addresses a critical unmet need in precision immuno-oncology: a simple, blood-based biomarker strategy that can guide therapeutic decision-making and accelerate the development of effective cancer vaccines.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for predicting the likelihood of long-term survival in a patient with metastatic melanoma who is undergoing or is scheduled to undergo treatment with an autologous dendritic cell vaccine (DCV). The method comprises obtaining a first biological sample from the patient prior to administration of the DCV, measuring the concentration of soluble programmed cell death protein-1 (sPD-1) in the first biological sample to determine a baseline sPD-1 level, administering at least one dose of the DCV to the patient, obtaining a second biological sample from the patient after administration of the at least one dose of the DCV, measuring the concentration of sPD-1 in the second biological sample to determine a post-treatment sPD-1 level, calculating the change in sPD-1 concentration between the baseline and post-treatment levels, and classifying the patient into a prognostic category based on a combination of the baseline sPD-1 level and the direction of change in sPD-1 concentration.

More specifically, the method classifies the patient as having a favorable prognosis if the baseline sPD-1 level is less than 100 picograms per milliliter (pg/mL), or if the baseline sPD-1 level is less than 200,000 pg/mL and the post-treatment sPD-1 level is lower than the baseline level. Conversely, the patient is classified as having an unfavorable prognosis if the baseline sPD-1 level is greater than 200,000 pg/mL, or if the post-treatment sPD-1 level is higher than the baseline level regardless of the absolute baseline concentration, provided that the baseline level is not below 100 pg/mL. This classification scheme has been shown to correlate strongly with overall survival in patients treated with DCV, with favorable classification associated with median survival exceeding four years and unfavorable classification associated with median survival of less than two years.

The invention further provides a method for selecting patients for DCV therapy based on their baseline sPD-1 level. In this embodiment, patients with a baseline sPD-1 level greater than 200,000 pg/mL are excluded from DCV treatment due to their poor prognosis, while those with baseline levels below this threshold are considered eligible. Additionally, the invention encompasses a method for monitoring the immunological response to DCV by tracking changes in sPD-1 levels over time, wherein a decrease in sPD-1 following vaccination is indicative of a productive anti-tumor immune response that is not being suppressed by the PD-1/PD-L1 axis.

It is a key aspect of the invention that the predictive value of the sPD-1 biomarker profile is specific to DCV and does not extend to other forms of autologous cancer vaccines, such as autologous tumor cell vaccines (TCVs). This specificity underscores the distinct immunological mechanisms engaged by DCV—namely, the ex vivo loading of tumor antigens onto professional antigen-presenting dendritic cells—which appear to generate immune responses whose regulation is more faithfully reflected in sPD-1 dynamics than those elicited by TCV.

The biological samples used in the methods of the invention are preferably serum or plasma, and sPD-1 concentration is measured using a quantitative immunoassay, such as an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), performed under good laboratory practice (GLP) standards. The post-treatment sample is advantageously obtained approximately one week after the third weekly dose of DCV, although the timing may be adjusted based on clinical protocol.

The invention also provides kits comprising reagents for measuring sPD-1 in biological samples, along with instructions for interpreting the results according to the classification criteria described herein. Such kits may be used in clinical laboratories to support treatment decisions in oncology settings.

In summary, the invention offers a novel, non-invasive, and clinically practical approach to personalizing cancer immunotherapy by leveraging the dynamic interplay between dendritic cell vaccination and the PD-1 checkpoint pathway. By integrating baseline and longitudinal sPD-1 measurements, clinicians can identify patients most likely to derive durable benefit from DCV, thereby optimizing resource allocation, minimizing unnecessary treatment exposure, and improving overall therapeutic outcomes in metastatic melanoma.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to methods and systems for predicting clinical outcomes in patients with metastatic melanoma treated with autologous dendritic cell vaccines (DCVs) through the analysis of soluble programmed cell death protein-1 (sPD-1) levels in biological samples. The core insight underlying the invention is that the prognostic and predictive value of sPD-1 is not static but emerges from the interaction between the patient’s pre-existing immune state—as reflected in baseline sPD-1—and the immunological consequences of DCV administration—as reflected in the direction and magnitude of sPD-1 change following vaccination. This dual-parameter assessment enables a more nuanced and accurate stratification of patients than either parameter alone, particularly within the context of DCV therapy.

To implement the methods of the invention, a clinician or laboratory technician first obtains a biological sample from the patient prior to the initiation of DCV treatment. This “baseline” or “pre-treatment” sample is typically collected during the screening or enrollment phase of a clinical protocol, ideally one week before the first vaccine dose, although the exact timing may vary depending on institutional practices. The biological sample is preferably serum, though plasma may also be used, provided that anticoagulants do not interfere with the sPD-1 assay. The sample is processed according to standard phlebotomy and centrifugation procedures and may be stored frozen at -80°C until analysis to preserve analyte integrity.

The concentration of sPD-1 in the baseline sample is then quantified using a validated immunoassay. In the exemplary embodiment, a quantitative, multiplex enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is employed, such as those commercially available from Raybiotech, Inc. (Norcross, GA), which utilize chemiluminescent or colorimetric detection and are calibrated against recombinant human sPD-1 standards. The assay is performed in accordance with good laboratory practice (GLP) standards to ensure reproducibility and accuracy. Results are reported in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL), a unit that reflects the low physiological abundance of sPD-1 in circulation.

Following baseline sampling, the patient receives the DCV according to a predetermined schedule. The DCV is prepared by isolating peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the patient via leukapheresis, differentiating monocytes into immature dendritic cells using cytokine cocktails (typically granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor [GM-CSF] and interleukin-4 [IL-4]), and then loading these dendritic cells with tumor-associated antigens derived from irradiated autologous tumor cells cultured from surgically resected metastatic lesions. The mature, antigen-loaded dendritic cells are formulated in a suspension containing GM-CSF and administered subcutaneously. A typical dosing regimen involves three weekly injections during the induction phase, followed by booster doses at weeks 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24, though variations are possible.

Approximately one week after the third weekly injection—i.e., at “week 4” relative to the start of treatment—a second biological sample is obtained from the patient. This “post-treatment” or “on-treatment” sample is processed and analyzed for sPD-1 concentration using the same assay conditions as the baseline sample to minimize technical variability. The change in sPD-1 concentration is then calculated by subtracting the baseline value from the week-4 value. A negative result indicates a decrease in sPD-1, while a positive result indicates an increase.

The patient is then classified into one of two prognostic categories using the following algorithm:

- **Favorable Prognosis (“sPD-1 low/decreased”):** The patient is assigned to this category if either (a) the baseline sPD-1 level is less than 100 pg/mL, regardless of the direction of change; or (b) the baseline sPD-1 level is at least 100 pg/mL but less than 200,000 pg/mL, and the week-4 sPD-1 level is lower than the baseline level.

- **Unfavorable Prognosis (“sPD-1 high/increased”):** The patient is assigned to this category if either (a) the baseline sPD-1 level is 200,000 pg/mL or greater, regardless of the direction of change; or (b) the baseline sPD-1 level is less than 200,000 pg/mL but the week-4 sPD-1 level is equal to or greater than the baseline level, provided that the baseline level is not below 100 pg/mL.

This classification is not merely descriptive but has direct clinical implications. Patients in the favorable prognosis group exhibit significantly longer overall survival, with a majority surviving beyond three years and a median survival exceeding four years. In contrast, patients in the unfavorable prognosis group have markedly shorter survival, with a median of less than two years and only a minority reaching the three-year milestone. The statistical significance of this difference (p = 0.0152 by log-rank test) supports the use of this classification as a predictive tool in clinical decision-making.

Importantly, the invention recognizes that this sPD-1-based classification is specific to DCV and does not apply to other autologous vaccine platforms, such as tumor cell vaccines (TCVs). In TCV, where irradiated whole tumor cells are injected without ex vivo manipulation, the same sPD-1 criteria fail to stratify patients by survival outcome. This distinction highlights a fundamental difference in how DCV and TCV engage the immune system: DCV, by delivering pre-processed antigens via professional APCs, appears to induce T-cell responses whose regulation is tightly coupled to PD-1 dynamics, whereas TCV relies on in vivo antigen uptake and presentation, which may involve different regulatory circuits or kinetic profiles.

The invention also contemplates the use of baseline sPD-1 alone as a screening tool to exclude patients unlikely to benefit from DCV. Specifically, patients with baseline sPD-1 levels exceeding 200,000 pg/mL consistently exhibit poor survival when treated with DCV, suggesting that their immune systems are already in a state of profound exhaustion or suppression that cannot be overcome by antigen-specific vaccination alone. Enriching clinical trial populations by excluding such patients may enhance the observed efficacy of DCV and reduce exposure to ineffective therapy.

From an immunological perspective, the invention aligns with current models of tumor immune microenvironments. Very low baseline sPD-1 (<100 pg/mL) may indicate an “immune desert” phenotype, where few tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes are present, and thus minimal PD-1-mediated suppression exists. In such cases, DCV can effectively prime a de novo anti-tumor response without immediate counter-regulation. Extremely high baseline sPD-1 (>200,000 pg/mL) may reflect a “highly inflamed but suppressed” state, where abundant tumor-reactive T cells are present but functionally inhibited by chronic PD-1 signaling; here, DCV alone may be insufficient, and combination with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade would be more appropriate. Intermediate levels with a post-vaccination decrease in sPD-1 may signify successful vaccine-induced T-cell activation without concomitant upregulation of inhibitory checkpoints—a scenario conducive to durable tumor control.

The methods of the invention may be implemented in a variety of clinical and research settings. Diagnostic laboratories can offer sPD-1 testing as a service, providing reports that include both raw concentrations and interpretive classifications according to the invention’s criteria. Pharmaceutical companies developing DCV products can incorporate sPD-1 profiling into their clinical trial designs as a stratification factor or enrichment strategy. Regulatory agencies may recognize the sPD-1 classification as a surrogate endpoint for accelerated approval in certain contexts.

Furthermore, the invention extends beyond melanoma to other solid tumors where DCV is being investigated, provided that similar sPD-1 dynamics are observed. While the specific thresholds (100 pg/mL and 200,000 pg/mL) were derived from a melanoma cohort, they may serve as starting points for validation in other indications. Assay harmonization across platforms will be essential for broad applicability.

In conclusion, the detailed description herein provides a robust, evidence-based framework for using sPD-1 as a dynamic biomarker in DCV-treated melanoma patients. By integrating pre- and post-treatment measurements into a simple classification rule, the invention enables personalized immunotherapy with improved precision and outcomes.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Retrospective Analysis of sPD-1 in a Randomized Phase II Melanoma Vaccine Trial**

A randomized phase II clinical trial (NCT00948480) enrolled 42 patients with metastatic melanoma to compare the efficacy of autologous dendritic cell vaccine (DCV) versus autologous tumor cell vaccine (TCV). Both vaccines were prepared from short-term cultures of irradiated autologous tumor cells derived from surgically resected metastases. DCV consisted of dendritic cells generated ex vivo from patient monocytes and loaded with tumor antigens, while TCV comprised the irradiated tumor cells themselves. Patients received eight subcutaneous injections over 24 weeks, formulated with granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF). Long-term follow-up revealed superior survival in the DCV arm: median overall survival was 43.4 months versus 20.5 months for TCV, with three-year survival rates of 61% versus 25% (p = 0.018).

As part of correlative science, paired serum samples were collected from 39 of the 42 patients—one week before the first vaccine dose (week-0) and one week after the third dose (week-4). Soluble PD-1 (sPD-1) concentrations were measured using a quantitative ELISA (Raybiotech, Inc.) under GLP conditions. Baseline sPD-1 levels ranged from undetectable (0 pg/mL) to 511,063 pg/mL, with a median of 119,845 pg/mL across all patients. Healthy controls exhibited mean sPD-1 of 595 pg/mL, consistent with literature norms (200–1200 pg/mL).

Initial analysis showed that baseline sPD-1 alone was not prognostic for the entire cohort: patients with baseline sPD-1 <1,200 pg/mL had similar three-year survival (47%) to those with >1,200 pg/mL (35%; p = 0.453). Similarly, the direction of sPD-1 change (increase vs. decrease) from week-0 to week-4 did not predict survival overall.

However, stratification by treatment arm revealed striking differences. Among 17 DCV-treated patients, those with baseline sPD-1 >200,000 pg/mL (n=2) survived less than 10 months, whereas all 15 with baseline <200,000 pg/mL survived at least one year (p = 0.0074). Moreover, among DCV patients with intermediate baseline sPD-1 (100–200,000 pg/mL), those who exhibited a decrease in sPD-1 after vaccination (n=7) had 71% three-year survival (5/7), compared to 0% (0/7) for TCV patients with decreased sPD-1 (p = 0.021).

These observations led to the development of a composite classification:  
- **sPD-1 low/decreased:** baseline <100 pg/mL OR (baseline 100–200,000 pg/mL AND sPD-1 decreased at week-4)  
- **sPD-1 high/increased:** baseline ≥200,000 pg/mL OR (baseline <200,000 pg/mL AND sPD-1 increased or unchanged at week-4)

Applying this to DCV patients:  
- 9/17 (53%) were classified as low/decreased; 8/9 (89%) survived >3 years, median survival >4 years  
- 8/17 (47%) were high/increased; 2/8 (25%) survived >3 years, median survival <2 years  
- Log-rank p = 0.0152; sensitivity for 3-year survival = 80%, specificity = 86%

In contrast, the same classification applied to 22 TCV patients showed no survival difference (p = 0.78), confirming the DCV-specific nature of the biomarker.

**Example 2: Clinical Application for Patient Selection**

A 52-year-old male with stage IV melanoma undergoes resection of a liver metastasis, from which a short-term tumor cell line is established. He is referred for consideration of autologous DCV therapy. A baseline serum sample is drawn and analyzed for sPD-1, yielding a concentration of 250,000 pg/mL. According to the invention’s criteria, this patient is classified as “sPD-1 high/increased” due to baseline >200,000 pg/mL. Given the historical data showing uniformly poor survival in such patients treated with DCV alone, the treating oncologist elects to offer alternative therapy—specifically, combination immunotherapy with anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies—which is more likely to overcome the profound immune suppression indicated by the elevated sPD-1.

**Example 3: Monitoring Vaccine Response**

A 45-year-old female with resected recurrent stage III melanoma enrolls in a DCV trial. Her baseline sPD-1 is 85,000 pg/mL. After three weekly DCV doses, her week-4 sPD-1 is 42,000 pg/mL—a 51% decrease. She is classified as “sPD-1 low/decreased” and predicted to have favorable long-term survival. Indeed, she remains disease-free at 48 months follow-up, supporting the predictive validity of the biomarker.

**Example 4: False Negative Case Illustrating Biomarker Limitations**

A 55-year-old male with ocular melanoma and resected liver metastases has baseline sPD-1 of 7,645 pg/mL, which increases to 10,340 pg/mL at week-4. Per the invention, he is classified as “sPD-1 high/increased” and predicted to have poor survival. However, he later receives anti-PD-1 therapy upon progression and achieves a durable response, surviving beyond five years. This case illustrates that the biomarker predicts outcome for DCV monotherapy but may not apply when subsequent checkpoint blockade is administered, highlighting the importance of treatment context.

These examples demonstrate the practical utility, specificity, and limitations of the sPD-1 classification system as disclosed in the present invention.
