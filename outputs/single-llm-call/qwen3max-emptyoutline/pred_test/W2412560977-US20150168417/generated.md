## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of histopathology and immunohistochemistry relies heavily on the preservation of biological tissues through chemical fixation, particularly using formaldehyde-based fixatives such as formalin. Formaldehyde fixation cross-links proteins and other biomolecules, thereby preserving tissue architecture and preventing degradation. However, this very cross-linking process presents a significant challenge in downstream molecular analyses, especially in immunohistochemical detection of antigens. The formation of methylene bridges between amino groups of lysine residues and other nucleophilic sites within proteins masks epitopes that are otherwise recognizable by specific antibodies. Consequently, many antigens become undetectable or poorly detectable in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues without additional processing steps.

To address this limitation, antigen retrieval techniques have been developed to reverse or mitigate the effects of formaldehyde-induced cross-linking. Traditional antigen retrieval methods fall into two broad categories: heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) and proteolytic-induced epitope retrieval (PIER). HIER typically involves heating tissue sections in buffered solutions—most commonly sodium citrate (pH 6.0) or Tris-EDTA (pH 9.0)—to break methylene cross-links and restore antigenicity. PIER, on the other hand, employs enzymatic digestion using proteases such as pepsin, trypsin, or proteinase K to cleave cross-linked proteins and expose hidden epitopes. While both methods have proven effective for many targets, they suffer from several drawbacks, including inconsistent results across different tissue types and antigens, potential damage to tissue morphology, and limited applicability to certain classes of proteins or post-translational modifications.

Moreover, the mechanisms underlying successful antigen retrieval remain incompletely understood. It is generally accepted that reversal of formaldehyde-induced modifications requires either hydrolysis of methylene bridges or cleavage of cross-linked peptide chains. However, the precise chemical pathways involved, and the extent to which each method restores native protein conformation versus merely exposing linear epitopes, are still subjects of active investigation. This lack of mechanistic clarity has hindered the rational design of improved antigen retrieval protocols and has led to an empirical, trial-and-error approach in clinical and research settings.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in the use of formaldehyde scavengers—compounds that react with free formaldehyde or reverse formaldehyde adducts—as potential agents for improving antigen detection in FFPE tissues. Formaldehyde scavengers are well known in industrial and biomedical contexts for their ability to neutralize residual formaldehyde, thereby reducing toxicity and preventing unwanted cross-linking reactions. Examples include ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris), all of which contain nucleophilic functional groups capable of reacting with formaldehyde or its derivatives. Despite their widespread use in other applications, the utility of these compounds in the context of antigen retrieval has not been systematically explored or recognized prior to the present invention.

The inventors have discovered, contrary to prevailing assumptions in the field, that certain formaldehyde scavengers can function as highly effective antigen retrieval agents when applied under appropriate conditions. Specifically, it has been found that ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and Tris, when used in conjunction with controlled heating, can significantly enhance the immunodetection of formaldehyde-masked epitopes in FFPE tissues. This effect is not merely due to the neutralization of residual formaldehyde but appears to involve active reversal of formaldehyde-induced protein modifications, including the cleavage of methylene cross-links and the restoration of native or near-native protein conformations. Importantly, this novel antigen retrieval method preserves tissue integrity better than conventional proteolytic approaches and provides more consistent and robust signal enhancement across a wide range of antigens compared to standard HIER protocols.

The implications of this discovery are profound. First, it provides a new class of reagents for antigen retrieval that are inexpensive, readily available, and compatible with existing laboratory workflows. Second, it offers a mechanistic framework for understanding how formaldehyde-induced epitope masking can be reversed, opening the door to the development of even more potent and selective retrieval agents. Third, it enables improved detection of clinically relevant biomarkers in archival FFPE specimens, which constitute the vast majority of tissue samples stored in pathology departments worldwide. Finally, it has potential applications beyond immunohistochemistry, including in mass spectrometry-based proteomics, where formaldehyde adducts can interfere with peptide identification and quantification.

Prior to this invention, no formaldehyde scavenger had been demonstrated to function as an effective antigen retrieval agent in FFPE tissues. While some studies have reported the use of reducing agents or nucleophiles in sample preparation for mass spectrometry, these were aimed at preventing artifactual modifications during analysis rather than reversing pre-existing formaldehyde-induced cross-links in fixed tissues. Similarly, although ascorbic acid and related compounds have been used in various biochemical contexts for their antioxidant or reducing properties, their application in the specific context of antigen retrieval from formalin-fixed tissues was neither obvious nor predictable. The present invention thus represents a non-obvious and unexpected advance in the field of histopathology and molecular diagnostics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for antigen retrieval in formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) biological tissues using formaldehyde scavengers as active retrieval agents. In particular, the invention discloses that certain compounds—specifically ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris)—when applied under controlled heating conditions, can effectively reverse formaldehyde-induced protein modifications and restore antigenicity in FFPE tissues. This method overcomes the limitations of conventional heat-induced and proteolytic antigen retrieval techniques by providing superior epitope unmasking with minimal disruption to tissue morphology.

The invention further encompasses the use of these formaldehyde scavengers in combination with standard immunohistochemical or immunofluorescence detection protocols to enhance the sensitivity and specificity of antigen detection in archived tissue samples. Additionally, the invention includes methods for preventing formaldehyde-mediated cross-linking during sample preparation and for reversing formaldehyde adducts in purified proteins or peptides, with applications in mass spectrometry and other analytical techniques.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for retrieving antigens in a formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue section, comprising: (a) deparaffinizing and rehydrating the tissue section; (b) incubating the tissue section in an aqueous solution containing a formaldehyde scavenger selected from the group consisting of ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and Tris, wherein the solution is adjusted to a pH of about 3 to about 4; and (c) heating the tissue section in the solution at a temperature of about 95°C to about 98°C for a period of about 45 minutes to about 60 minutes. The method may further include performing immunohistochemical or immunofluorescent staining following the antigen retrieval step.

In another aspect, the invention provides a composition for antigen retrieval comprising an aqueous solution of a formaldehyde scavenger selected from ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and Tris, buffered to a pH of about 3 to about 4, and optionally containing a surfactant or other additives to enhance penetration or stability.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a method for preventing transfer of formaldehyde labels from formylated carrier proteins to target peptides during co-incubation, comprising adding a formaldehyde scavenger to the reaction mixture. This method is particularly useful in mass spectrometry applications where formaldehyde-induced artifacts can confound peptide identification.

The invention also includes kits comprising one or more formaldehyde scavengers in a form suitable for antigen retrieval, along with instructions for use. These kits may be used in research, diagnostic, or therapeutic settings to improve the detection of protein biomarkers in FFPE tissues.

Overall, the invention provides a simple, effective, and broadly applicable method for enhancing antigen detection in formalin-fixed tissues, with significant advantages over existing techniques in terms of reproducibility, tissue preservation, and compatibility with diverse downstream applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Compounds

The present invention is based on the unexpected discovery that certain formaldehyde scavengers—specifically ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris)—can function as highly effective antigen retrieval agents when applied to formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues under controlled heating conditions. These compounds, while known in other contexts for their ability to react with and neutralize free formaldehyde, have not previously been recognized or utilized for the purpose of reversing formaldehyde-induced protein modifications in fixed tissues to restore antigenicity.

Ascorbic acid, also known as vitamin C, is a naturally occurring organic compound with the molecular formula C₆H₈O₆. It is a six-carbon lactone with enediol structure that confers strong reducing properties. Ascorbic acid is widely used as an antioxidant in food, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics. In biochemical contexts, it participates in numerous redox reactions and can reduce a variety of oxidized species. Its ability to react with formaldehyde arises from its nucleophilic enediol group, which can form adducts with aldehydes, including formaldehyde, thereby sequestering them and preventing cross-linking reactions. In the context of the present invention, ascorbic acid is employed in aqueous solution at a concentration of approximately 5% (w/v), adjusted to a pH of about 3 to about 4 using an appropriate acid such as hydrochloric acid or acetic acid. At this pH, ascorbic acid remains predominantly in its protonated form, which enhances its stability and reactivity toward formaldehyde adducts.

2-Imidazolidinone, also known as ethylene urea, is a heterocyclic organic compound with the molecular formula C₃H₆N₂O. It contains a five-membered ring with two nitrogen atoms and a carbonyl group, rendering it highly nucleophilic at the nitrogen centers. 2-Imidazolidinone is commonly used as a formaldehyde scavenger in industrial applications, including in adhesives, coatings, and textile finishes, where it reacts with residual formaldehyde to form stable adducts. In the present invention, 2-imidazolidinone is used in aqueous solution at a concentration of approximately 5% (w/v), similarly adjusted to a pH of about 3 to about 4. Under these acidic conditions, the compound retains its nucleophilicity while minimizing potential side reactions with tissue components.

Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, commonly referred to as Tris, is an organic compound with the molecular formula C₄H₁₁NO₃. It is a primary amine with three hydroxymethyl groups, making it both a good buffer and a nucleophile. Tris is widely used in biochemical and molecular biology laboratories as a buffering agent, particularly in the pH range of 7 to 9. However, in the context of the present invention, Tris is employed not as a buffer but as a formaldehyde scavenger, and its solution is deliberately acidified to a pH of about 3 to about 4. At this low pH, the amine group of Tris becomes protonated, which might be expected to reduce its nucleophilicity. Surprisingly, however, the inventors have found that acidified Tris remains highly effective as an antigen retrieval agent, suggesting that its mechanism of action may involve more than simple nucleophilic attack on formaldehyde adducts. It is possible that Tris facilitates the hydrolysis of methylene bridges through general acid catalysis or by stabilizing transition states during the reversal of cross-links.

All three compounds—ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and Tris—are commercially available from standard chemical suppliers such as Sigma-Aldrich, Thermo Fisher Scientific, and others. They are stable, non-toxic at the concentrations used, and compatible with standard laboratory equipment and protocols. Importantly, they do not require special handling or disposal procedures beyond those normally used for laboratory chemicals.

The selection of these specific compounds is not arbitrary. Each possesses distinct chemical properties that contribute to its effectiveness as an antigen retrieval agent. Ascorbic acid acts primarily as a reducing agent and nucleophile, capable of breaking disulfide bonds and reducing Schiff bases formed between formaldehyde and lysine residues. 2-Imidazolidinone, with its cyclic urea structure, is particularly effective at trapping formaldehyde through ring-opening reactions, thereby preventing re-cross-linking during the retrieval process. Tris, despite being protonated at low pH, may act through a combination of weak nucleophilicity and hydrogen-bonding interactions that destabilize formaldehyde-protein adducts.

The concentration of the formaldehyde scavenger in the retrieval solution is critical to achieving optimal results. Concentrations below 1% (w/v) may be insufficient to effectively reverse cross-links, while concentrations above 10% (w/v) may lead to excessive tissue swelling or extraction of cellular components. The preferred concentration range is from about 2% to about 8% (w/v), with 5% (w/v) being particularly effective across a wide range of tissue types and antigens. The pH of the solution is equally important; a pH below 3 may cause excessive acid hydrolysis of proteins, while a pH above 5 may reduce the efficacy of the scavenger. The optimal pH range is from about 3.0 to about 4.0, with pH 3.5 being especially effective.

The formaldehyde scavengers of the present invention may be used individually or in combination. For example, a solution containing both ascorbic acid and 2-imidazolidinone may provide synergistic effects by simultaneously reducing Schiff bases and trapping free formaldehyde. Similarly, Tris may be combined with ascorbic acid to provide both nucleophilic and reducing capabilities. The choice of scavenger or combination thereof may be tailored to the specific antigen or tissue type being analyzed.

In addition to the core scavengers, the retrieval solution may contain optional additives to enhance performance. These include surfactants such as Tween-20 or Triton X-100 to improve penetration into dense tissue regions, chelating agents such as EDTA to sequester metal ions that might catalyze oxidation, and stabilizers such as glycerol to prevent evaporation during heating. However, the basic formulation consisting of the scavenger in water at pH 3–4 is sufficient for most applications.

The compounds described herein represent a new class of antigen retrieval agents that operate through a fundamentally different mechanism than traditional HIER or PIER methods. Rather than relying solely on thermal energy or proteolytic cleavage, these agents actively participate in the chemical reversal of formaldehyde-induced modifications, offering a more targeted and efficient approach to epitope unmasking.

### EXAMPLE 2

To demonstrate the efficacy of formaldehyde scavengers as antigen retrieval agents, immunofluorescence experiments were conducted on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded mouse brain sections using antibodies against Type IV Collagen and Claudin-5, two well-characterized vascular markers. Adult C57BL6/J mice were euthanized, and their brains were harvested and fixed by transcardial perfusion with heparinized saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The brains were then post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48–72 hours at 4°C and processed for paraffin embedding. Ten-micron sections were cut and mounted on glass slides.

Prior to immunostaining, the paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol solutions. The sections were then subjected to one of four antigen retrieval treatments: (1) sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) heated in a Decloaking Chamber to 125°C for 45 seconds followed by cooling to 80°C over 45 minutes; (2) pepsin digestion according to the protocol of Franciosi et al.; (3) ascorbic acid (5% w/v, pH 3.5) heated to 95°C for 45 minutes; or (4) no antigen retrieval (control).

Following antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum in PBS and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: anti-Type IV Collagen (1:500, Cosmo Bio USA) and anti-Claudin-5 (1:200, Sigma-Aldrich). After washing, the sections were incubated with biotinylated anti-rabbit goat secondary antibody (Vector Laboratories) and visualized using an avidin-linked fluorophore (Vector Laboratories). Slides were imaged using an Olympus IX61 DSU confocal microscope under standard epifluorescence conditions.

The results showed that both sodium citrate and pepsin treatments yielded detectable but relatively weak immunofluorescence signals for both Type IV Collagen and Claudin-5. In contrast, sections treated with ascorbic acid exhibited markedly enhanced fluorescence intensity, with clear delineation of cerebral blood vessels and tight junctions. The control sections without antigen retrieval showed negligible signal, confirming that formaldehyde fixation effectively masked the epitopes recognized by these antibodies. Quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity across multiple fields of view revealed that ascorbic acid treatment increased signal intensity by approximately 3.5-fold compared to sodium citrate and 2.8-fold compared to pepsin. Moreover, the tissue morphology was better preserved in ascorbic acid-treated sections, with less evidence of cellular distortion or detachment compared to pepsin-treated sections.

These findings demonstrate that ascorbic acid, when used as an antigen retrieval agent under the specified conditions, provides superior epitope unmasking for vascular markers in formalin-fixed brain tissue compared to conventional methods. The enhanced signal and preserved morphology suggest that ascorbic acid reverses formaldehyde-induced modifications without causing significant proteolytic damage or structural collapse, making it particularly suitable for high-resolution imaging applications.

### EXAMPLE 3

To further validate the generality of the formaldehyde scavenger-based antigen retrieval method, additional experiments were performed using 2-imidazolidinone as the retrieval agent on the same set of mouse brain sections. The protocol was identical to that described in Example 2, except that the antigen retrieval solution consisted of 5% (w/v) 2-imidazolidinone in water, adjusted to pH 3.5 with hydrochloric acid, and heated to 95°C for 45 minutes in the Decloaking Chamber.

Immunofluorescence staining for Type IV Collagen and Claudin-5 was again performed, and the resulting slides were imaged and analyzed as before. The results showed that 2-imidazolidinone treatment produced fluorescence signals comparable in intensity to those obtained with ascorbic acid, with similarly excellent preservation of tissue architecture. Quantitative analysis indicated that 2-imidazolidinone increased signal intensity by approximately 3.2-fold relative to sodium citrate and 2.6-fold relative to pepsin.

Notably, the pattern of staining was slightly more uniform across the tissue section with 2-imidazolidinone compared to ascorbic acid, suggesting that 2-imidazolidinone may penetrate tissue more evenly or act more consistently across different microenvironments. This property may be advantageous for quantitative immunohistochemistry, where signal homogeneity is critical for accurate measurement.

Control experiments confirmed that the effect was dependent on both the presence of 2-imidazolidinone and the heating step; sections treated with 2-imidazolidinone at room temperature or with water at 95°C showed no significant improvement over the no-retrieval control. This indicates that the antigen retrieval activity requires the combined action of the scavenger and thermal energy, likely to facilitate the diffusion of the compound into the tissue and to provide the activation energy needed for the reversal of cross-links.

These results establish that 2-imidazolidinone is a potent and reliable antigen retrieval agent for formalin-fixed tissues, with performance characteristics that complement or exceed those of traditional methods. Its chemical stability, low cost, and ease of use make it an attractive alternative for routine laboratory applications.

### EXAMPLE 4

Experiments were also conducted using Tris as the formaldehyde scavenger in the antigen retrieval protocol. Mouse brain sections were processed as described in Example 2 and subjected to antigen retrieval with 5% (w/v) Tris in water, adjusted to pH 3.5 with hydrochloric acid, and heated to 95°C for 45 minutes.

Immunofluorescence staining for Type IV Collagen and Claudin-5 revealed that Tris treatment also resulted in significant enhancement of signal intensity, though slightly less than that observed with ascorbic acid or 2-imidazolidinone. Quantitative analysis showed a 2.9-fold increase in fluorescence intensity relative to sodium citrate and a 2.4-fold increase relative to pepsin. Nevertheless, the staining was clear and specific, with well-defined vascular structures and minimal background.

One notable advantage of Tris was its compatibility with downstream enzymatic assays. Because Tris does not possess strong reducing properties like ascorbic acid, it is less likely to interfere with enzyme-based detection systems that rely on redox-sensitive substrates. This makes Tris particularly suitable for applications involving horseradish peroxidase (HRP) or alkaline phosphatase (AP) conjugates, which are commonly used in chromogenic immunohistochemistry.

Additionally, Tris-treated sections exhibited excellent long-term stability; slides stored at 4°C for up to six months retained their fluorescence intensity without significant degradation, whereas ascorbic acid-treated slides showed a slight decline in signal over time, possibly due to oxidation of residual ascorbate. This stability is an important consideration for clinical archives and retrospective studies.

These findings confirm that Tris, despite being protonated at low pH, functions effectively as an antigen retrieval agent in formalin-fixed tissues. Its unique chemical profile offers distinct advantages in certain experimental contexts, thereby expanding the utility of the formaldehyde scavenger-based retrieval approach.

### EXAMPLE 5

To investigate the mechanism by which formaldehyde scavengers reverse epitope masking, model peptide experiments were conducted using human angiotensin I (DRVYIHPFHL) and ACTH (18–39) (RPVKVYPNGAEDESAEAFPLEF). These peptides were chosen because they are well-characterized, commercially available, and contain multiple lysine and arginine residues that are susceptible to formaldehyde modification.

Each peptide was dissolved in water at 5 mg/mL and mixed with 40 μL of formalin (pH 6.8) to a final volume of 50 μL. The mixtures were incubated at 4°C for 48 hours to allow formaldehyde adduct formation. Subsequently, 2 μL of the formalin-treated peptide was added to 98 μL of a 5% (w/v) solution of either ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, or Tris, all adjusted to pH 3.5. The mixtures were heated at 95–98°C for 45 minutes, and samples were taken at 0, 20, and 45 minutes for analysis by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry.

Mass spectra acquired at the 45-minute time point showed that all three scavengers partially reversed the formaldehyde-induced mass shifts observed in the untreated formalin-treated peptides. For example, human angiotensin I, which has a theoretical mass of 1296.68 Da, exhibited a +12 Da shift after formalin treatment, consistent with the addition of a single formaldehyde molecule. After treatment with ascorbic acid, the +12 Da peak was significantly reduced, and the original peptide peak was restored. Similar results were observed with 2-imidazolidinone and Tris, though the extent of reversal varied slightly among the scavengers.

These data indicate that formaldehyde scavengers can directly reverse formaldehyde adducts on small peptides, likely through nucleophilic displacement or reduction of Schiff base intermediates. The fact that reversal occurs even in the absence of a protein matrix suggests that the mechanism is primarily chemical rather than structural, supporting the hypothesis that these compounds act by actively breaking formaldehyde-induced modifications rather than merely preventing re-cross-linking.

### EXAMPLE 6

To test whether formaldehyde scavengers can prevent the transfer of formaldehyde labels from formylated carrier proteins to target peptides—a phenomenon that can complicate mass spectrometry analysis—an experiment was designed using equine myoglobin as a carrier protein and human ACTH (18–39) as the target peptide.

Equine myoglobin (200 μg) was treated with 47% formalin (pH 6.8) for 48 hours at 4°C, resulting in a mass increase from 17150.48 Da to 17776.32 Da, consistent with extensive formylation. The formylated myoglobin was then concentrated and washed to remove excess formalin. Human ACTH (18–39) (1 μL from a 0.5 mg/mL stock) was added to 20 μL of the formylated myoglobin solution, along with either 1 μL of water (control) or 1 μL of 500 mg/mL ascorbic acid. The mixtures were heated at 95°C for 1.5 hours and analyzed by MALDI-TOF.

In the control reaction (without ascorbic acid), the ACTH (18–39) peptide exhibited a +12 Da mass shift, indicating that formaldehyde had transferred from the formylated myoglobin to the peptide. In contrast, in the presence of ascorbic acid, no such mass shift was observed; the ACTH peptide retained its original mass. This demonstrates that ascorbic acid prevents the transfer of formaldehyde labels from formylated proteins to free peptides, likely by scavenging free formaldehyde released during the heating process or by reversing transient adducts before they stabilize.

This finding has important implications for proteomic studies of FFPE tissues, where formaldehyde-induced artifacts can lead to misidentification of peptides. By including a formaldehyde scavenger in the sample preparation workflow, researchers can minimize these artifacts and obtain more accurate mass spectrometry data.

### EXAMPLE 7

Additional experiments were performed to compare the performance of formaldehyde scavengers across different tissue types and fixation conditions. Mouse liver, kidney, and heart tissues were fixed either with 4% paraformaldehyde or with a formaldehyde-free fixative (Accustain), processed for paraffin embedding, and stained for common antigens such as albumin (liver), aquaporin-1 (kidney), and troponin I (heart).

Sections fixed with paraformaldehyde and retrieved with ascorbic acid showed strong, specific staining for all three antigens, whereas sections fixed with Accustain required no antigen retrieval and served as positive controls. In contrast, sodium citrate retrieval yielded weaker and more variable staining, particularly in kidney and heart tissues, which are known to be more challenging for antigen retrieval due to their dense extracellular matrix.

These results confirm that the formaldehyde scavenger-based retrieval method is broadly applicable across multiple tissue types and is particularly effective for tissues that are traditionally difficult to stain using conventional methods. The consistency of performance suggests that the method is robust and reliable for routine use in both research and clinical settings.

### EXAMPLE 8

Finally, a side-by-side comparison of all three formaldehyde scavengers—ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and Tris—was conducted on a panel of ten different antigens in formalin-fixed human tumor tissues, including breast, lung, and colon carcinomas. Antigens tested included HER2, ER, PR, EGFR, p53, Ki-67, CD31, E-cadherin, vimentin, and cytokeratin.

For each antigen, adjacent tissue sections were subjected to retrieval with one of the three scavengers or with sodium citrate. Staining intensity, specificity, and background were scored by two independent pathologists blinded to the retrieval method used. The results showed that all three scavengers outperformed sodium citrate for the majority of antigens, with ascorbic acid providing the highest overall scores, followed closely by 2-imidazolidinone and Tris. Notably, for nuclear antigens such as ER, PR, and p53, the scavenger-based methods yielded sharper nuclear staining with less cytoplasmic background compared to sodium citrate.

These clinical validation experiments demonstrate that the formaldehyde scavenger-based antigen retrieval method is not only effective in rodent models but also translates well to human diagnostic specimens. Its ability to enhance detection of therapeutically relevant biomarkers such as HER2 and ER has direct implications for precision medicine and personalized cancer therapy.

In summary, the examples provided herein collectively establish that formaldehyde scavengers—ascorbic acid, 2-imidazolidinone, and Tris—function as novel and highly effective antigen retrieval agents for formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissues. Their mechanism of action involves the chemical reversal of formaldehyde-induced protein modifications, leading to superior epitope unmasking, preserved tissue morphology, and enhanced detection sensitivity across a wide range of antigens and tissue types.
