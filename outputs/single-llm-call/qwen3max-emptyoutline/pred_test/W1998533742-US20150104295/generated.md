# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

In the field of turbomachinery, structural components such as blisks—integrated bladed disks—are increasingly fabricated using advanced materials and manufacturing techniques that yield extremely lightweight and stiff configurations. While these innovations enhance aerodynamic efficiency and reduce mass, they often result in structures with inherently low mechanical damping. This deficiency in intrinsic damping renders such components highly susceptible to resonant vibrations when subjected to periodic excitations, which are common in rotating machinery due to interactions with stator vanes, combustion pulses, or other engine-order forcing functions. Prolonged exposure to high-amplitude resonant vibrations can lead to high-cycle fatigue failure, a critical reliability concern in aerospace and power generation applications where component integrity is paramount.

To mitigate this risk, various passive and active damping strategies have been explored over the decades. Among passive approaches, friction-based mechanisms—such as blade-to-blade contact interfaces or dedicated friction ring dampers—have been widely implemented. These rely on energy dissipation through Coulomb friction at contact surfaces under relative motion. However, their performance is highly sensitive to normal preload, surface conditions, temperature, and wear, leading to inconsistent damping behavior over time and operational cycles. Viscoelastic damping treatments, which involve bonding layers of polymer-based materials to vibrating surfaces, offer an alternative but are often limited by temperature sensitivity, aging effects, and added mass penalties that may be unacceptable in high-speed rotating systems.

More recently, piezoelectric-based shunt damping has emerged as a promising technique for vibration suppression in lightly damped structures. In this approach, piezoelectric transducers—typically patches made of lead zirconate titanate (PZT) ceramics—are bonded to the host structure. When the structure vibrates, the piezoelectric elements experience strain, generating an electrical charge proportional to the mechanical deformation via the direct piezoelectric effect. By connecting these transducers to an external electrical network, the mechanical energy of vibration is converted into electrical energy and subsequently dissipated as heat within the network. This method offers the advantage of being non-intrusive from an aerodynamic standpoint (when properly integrated), scalable, and tunable to specific structural modes.

The simplest form of piezoelectric shunt is the resistive (R) shunt, wherein each transducer is connected across a resistor. While robust and easy to implement, the R-shunt provides only modest damping enhancement because it lacks frequency selectivity; it dissipates energy across a broad frequency range without preferential attenuation at resonance. To improve performance, inductive-resistive (RL) shunts have been developed. In an RL shunt, an inductor is placed in series with a resistor to form a second-order electrical circuit that can be tuned to resonate at the natural frequency of a targeted structural mode. At resonance, the impedance of the electrical circuit matches the electromechanical characteristics of the piezoelectric-structure system, enabling efficient energy transfer and enhanced damping. Theoretically, optimal damping is achieved when the inductance \( L \) satisfies the tuning condition \( \omega_i = 1/\sqrt{LC} \), where \( \omega_i \) is the angular natural frequency of the targeted mode and \( C \) is the capacitance of the piezoelectric patch.

Despite its superior performance, the practical implementation of RL shunt damping in turbomachinery faces significant challenges. Most critically, the required inductance values for typical blade vibration frequencies (often in the range of hundreds of hertz to several kilohertz) and standard PZT capacitances (on the order of nanofarads) are prohibitively large—frequently exceeding one henry. Such large inductors are physically bulky, heavy, and difficult to integrate into compact, high-speed rotating assemblies. Moreover, conventional magnetic-core inductors suffer from saturation, hysteresis losses, and sensitivity to mechanical shock and centrifugal loads, making them unsuitable for harsh rotating environments.

To circumvent these limitations, synthetic inductors—active electronic circuits based on operational amplifiers, known as gyrators—have been employed in laboratory settings to emulate large inductance values using smaller passive components. While effective in controlled experiments, gyrator-based synthetic inductors require external power supplies, introduce noise and stability concerns, and are fundamentally incompatible with the stringent reliability, safety, and maintenance requirements of real-world rotating machinery. Active components are particularly problematic in rotating frames due to the difficulty of providing stable power and signal transmission across rotating-stationary interfaces, as well as the increased risk of failure under high g-forces and thermal cycling.

An alternative strategy, switched shunt damping, uses semiconductor switches (e.g., MOSFETs) to dynamically reconfigure the electrical network in synchrony with the structural vibration. This approach can achieve broadband or multimodal damping without requiring large inductors and is less sensitive to detuning. However, it demands precise phase-locked switching synchronized to the vibration waveform, which becomes exceedingly complex when multiple modes are simultaneously excited—a common scenario in turbomachinery. Furthermore, like synthetic inductors, switched shunts rely on active electronics and real-time control, rendering them impractical for passive, fail-safe operation in rotating systems.

Compounding these challenges is the modal complexity of rotationally periodic structures such as bladed disks. These systems exhibit cyclic symmetry, leading to modal families characterized by nodal diameters—circumferential standing wave patterns with a specific number \( n \) of nodal lines passing through the center. For a disk with \( N \) identical blades, the maximum number of nodal diameters is \( N/2 \) (for even \( N \)). Blade-dominated modes (e.g., first flexural 1F, first torsional 1T) typically appear as nearly flat bands in frequency-nodal diameter plots, indicating weak dependence on \( n \), whereas drum- or disk-dominated modes increase rapidly in frequency with \( n \). Resonance occurs when the excitation frequency—determined by rotor speed \( \Omega \) and the number of stator vanes or other spatial harmonics—satisfies \( \omega_n = |kN \pm n|\Omega \) for integer \( k \). This results in a "zig-zag" resonance line that intersects modal frequency curves at discrete points, identifying critical modes that must be damped.

Crucially, not all modes are equally dangerous; engineering analysis often reveals one or a few dominant resonant modes—characterized by a specific \( n \)—that govern fatigue life under expected operating conditions. This insight enables a targeted damping strategy: rather than attempting to suppress all modes (which would require complex, multi-channel active systems), it is sufficient—and more practical—to focus on the most critical mode(s). However, conventional RL shunt implementations, whether using independent loops per transducer or global networks, fail to exploit the spatial symmetry of the targeted mode shape, leading to inefficient use of transducers and excessive inductance requirements.

Prior art has not adequately addressed the need for a purely passive, mechanically robust, and electrically simple shunt damping architecture that leverages the harmonic nature of nodal diameter modes to drastically reduce inductance demands while maintaining high damping performance on the targeted mode. Existing solutions either rely on impractical active components or fail to capitalize on the phase relationships inherent in circumferentially periodic mode shapes. There remains a critical unmet need for a shunt damping system that is compatible with the constraints of rotating turbomachinery—namely, passivity, minimal component count, low mass, and insensitivity to moderate manufacturing variations—while delivering effective, mode-selective vibration suppression.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The present invention provides a novel passive shunt damping system for rotationally periodic structures, particularly applicable to turbomachinery components such as bladed disks, drums, or integrally bladed rotors (blisks). The system utilizes an array of piezoelectric transducers strategically interconnected into a reduced number of parallel electrical loops, exploiting the spatial harmonic characteristics of a targeted vibration mode with a specific number \( n \) of nodal diameters. This configuration achieves equivalent or near-equivalent damping performance on the targeted mode compared to conventional independent-loop RL shunts, while reducing the required inductance by a factor of approximately \( 4n^2 \), thereby enabling the use of compact, passive inductors suitable for integration into rotating machinery.

In accordance with the invention, a set of \( 4n \) piezoelectric transducers—where \( n \) is the number of nodal diameters of the targeted vibration mode—is affixed to the host structure at predetermined locations that align with the antinodes of the mode shape. Each transducer is configured to operate in a bending-sensitive mode, such as the \( d_{31} \) piezoelectric coupling mode, wherein strain along the longitudinal axis of the patch generates charge across its thickness. Critically, the polarization direction of adjacent transducers is alternated in a specific pattern that corresponds to the sign changes in curvature exhibited by the targeted mode across successive lobes of the circumferential displacement pattern.

For a mode with \( n \) nodal diameters, the circumferential displacement varies harmonically as \( \cos(n\theta) \) or \( \sin(n\theta) \), where \( \theta \) is the angular position. Over one full revolution, there are \( 2n \) regions of positive curvature alternating with \( 2n \) regions of negative curvature. The invention arranges the \( 4n \) transducers such that every pair of transducers separated by an angular distance of \( \pi/n \) radians experiences opposite curvature during vibration of the targeted mode. By mounting these opposing pairs with inverted electrical polarization—i.e., reversing the orientation of the piezoelectric material’s poling direction—the electrical charges generated by mechanical strain add constructively when the transducers are connected in parallel.

Specifically, the transducers are grouped into two parallel electrical loops, each containing \( 2n \) transducers. Within each loop, transducers are connected such that those experiencing positive curvature in the cosine mode (for example) are wired in parallel with those experiencing negative curvature but having inverted polarization, ensuring that the net voltage across the loop is maximized for the targeted mode. The two loops are electrically independent and each is connected to its own RL shunt circuit comprising a single inductor and a single resistor. Due to the parallel connection of \( 2n \) transducers, the effective capacitance of each loop is \( C_{\text{loop}} = 2nC \), where \( C \) is the capacitance of a single transducer. The inductance \( L_{\text{loop}} \) required to tune the RL circuit to the natural frequency \( \omega_n \) of the targeted mode is then given by \( L_{\text{loop}} = 1/(\omega_n^2 \cdot 2nC) \).

In contrast, a conventional independent-loop RL shunt employing the same \( 4n \) transducers would require \( 4n \) separate inductors, each tuned according to \( L_{\text{ind}} = 1/(\omega_n^2 C) \). The total inductance demand—considered in terms of physical volume, mass, and magnetic core requirements—is thus reduced by a factor of \( (4n) / (2 \cdot L_{\text{loop}}/L_{\text{ind}}) = 4n^2 \). For example, in a bladed disk with \( n = 7 \) nodal diameters, the inductance requirement per loop is reduced by a factor of 196, transforming an impractical 0.7 H inductor into a manageable 3.6 mH component—well within the range of commercially available passive inductors.

The invention is particularly advantageous when applied to structures where aerodynamic interference must be avoided. In turbomachinery, piezoelectric patches are preferably mounted on non-aerodynamic surfaces such as the inner rim of a bladed drum, the underside of a platform, or the root fillet region, away from the airflow path. The disclosed interconnection scheme ensures that even when transducers are constrained to a limited spatial region (e.g., a continuous annular band), they can still be effectively utilized for damping a specific nodal diameter mode by leveraging their relative angular positions and controlled polarization.

In practice, the number of available transducers may not exactly equal \( 4n \), but may be a multiple thereof (e.g., \( 8n, 12n \)), in which case the grouping into parallel loops scales accordingly. Alternatively, if the transducers are initially installed with uniform polarization—as was the case in the experimental bladed drum described in the research paper—the invention accommodates this constraint by increasing the number of independent loops. For instance, with 28 uniformly polarized patches targeting a mode with \( n = 7 \), the ideal \( 4n = 28 \) transducers cannot be split into two loops of 14 with alternating polarization. Instead, the patches are divided into four groups of 7, each group connected to its own RL shunt. Although this increases the number of inductors from two to four, the inductance per loop is still reduced by a factor of \( n^2 = 49 \) compared to independent loops, representing a substantial practical improvement.

The resistance value in each RL loop is selected to maximize energy dissipation at resonance. The optimal resistance \( R_{\text{opt}} \) is derived from the total effective electromechanical coupling factor \( K_n^2 \) of the targeted mode, which quantifies the fraction of modal strain energy convertible to electrical energy. For the parallel-loop configuration, \( K_n^2 \) is computed as the sum of the individual coupling factors of all transducers in the loop, adjusted for their spatial alignment with the mode shape. In many implementations, the internal resistance of the inductor (especially if a synthetic inductor is temporarily used for testing) may exceed \( R_{\text{opt}} \), in which case an external resistor is omitted, and the inductor’s parasitic resistance provides sufficient damping.

The invention has been validated on both simplified test articles and realistic turbomachinery components. In a circular plate clamped at the center, 12 PZT patches were arranged to target the first mode with \( n = 3 \) nodal diameters. Using the parallel-loop configuration (two loops of six patches each), the system achieved damping performance comparable to 12 independent RL loops, but with inductance reduced from 12 discrete 0.5 H units to two 14 mH inductors. Frequency response function (FRF) measurements using laser vibrometry confirmed equivalent attenuation of both sine and cosine variants of the targeted mode.

In a more representative application, a monobloc stainless steel bladed drum with 76 integrally machined blades—exhibiting extremely low inherent damping (\( \xi \approx 10^{-4} \))—was instrumented with 28 PZT patches bonded to the inner surface of the blade support rim. The patches operated in \( d_{31} \) mode and were initially installed with uniform polarization. Two critical modes were targeted: the blade-dominated 1F7 mode (\( n = 7 \)) and the drum-dominated D4 mode (\( n = 4 \)).

For the 1F7 mode, the 28 patches were grouped into four parallel loops of seven patches each, based on spatial analysis of the mistuned mode shape. Although substantial blade mistuning—caused by manufacturing tolerances—introduced non-harmonic components into the mode shape, degrading the ideal performance, the parallel shunt still provided significant damping localized primarily to the 1F7 resonance, unlike the independent shunt which affected multiple nearby 1F modes indiscriminately. This selectivity is advantageous when only one mode is critical, as it avoids unnecessary energy dissipation at non-resonant frequencies.

For the D4 mode, which is less sensitive to blade mistuning due to its drum-dominated character, the mode shape remained nearly harmonic. The patches were reconfigured into four loops aligned with the D4 antinodes. The parallel shunt achieved damping performance nearly identical to the independent shunt, with inductance reduced from 28 × 1 H to 4 × 140 mH. Cumulative RMS response metrics confirmed effective suppression of the targeted resonance without perturbing adjacent modes.

The invention is robust to moderate levels of structural mistuning—inevitable in real manufacturing—because the parallel-loop design inherently averages the response over multiple sectors. While extreme mistuning (as in the experimental drum with >2% frequency scatter) can degrade performance by breaking the assumed harmonic symmetry, the system remains functional and selective. Future refinements may incorporate adaptive grouping algorithms or hybrid topologies to further enhance mistuning tolerance.

Importantly, the entire system operates passively, requiring no sensors, controllers, power supplies, or rotating electrical contacts. The inductors and resistors are stationary components that can be housed in the engine casing, connected to the rotating transducers via slip rings or wireless inductive coupling if necessary, though in many architectures the shunt network may also rotate with the disk. The use of standard passive components ensures long-term reliability, thermal stability, and compliance with aviation safety standards.

In summary, the invention provides a breakthrough in passive vibration control for turbomachinery by intelligently interconnecting piezoelectric transducers to exploit modal symmetry, thereby achieving high-performance, mode-selective damping with dramatically reduced hardware complexity and mass. This enables the practical deployment of piezoelectric shunt damping in rotating systems where active or synthetic solutions are infeasible, significantly enhancing component life and operational safety.
