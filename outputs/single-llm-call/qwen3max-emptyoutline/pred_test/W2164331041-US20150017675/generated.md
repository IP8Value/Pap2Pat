## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of cellular delivery systems has long sought efficient and non-invasive methods to transport biologically active molecules—including peptides, nucleic acids, proteins, and small-molecule therapeutics—across the plasma membrane of mammalian cells. Traditional transfection reagents often rely on endocytic mechanisms that can lead to sequestration of cargo within endosomes or lysosomes, thereby limiting bioavailability and functional efficacy in the cytosol or nucleus. In this context, cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) have emerged as promising vectors due to their ability to traverse biological membranes through both energy-dependent and energy-independent pathways, enabling direct cytosolic delivery without reliance on classical endocytosis.

Among the most extensively studied CPPs is the Model Amphipathic Peptide (MAP), originally designed by Steiner and colleagues as a synthetic construct with the amino acid sequence KLALKLALKALKAALKLA-NH₂. This peptide adopts an α-helical conformation wherein hydrophobic residues align along one face of the helix while positively charged lysine residues occupy the opposing face, conferring amphipathicity that facilitates interaction with lipid bilayers. Early investigations into MAP’s behavior revealed unexpected cell-penetrating properties, prompting extensive research into the mechanisms governing its internalization. Initial studies suggested a non-endocytic uptake mechanism based on observations of cellular entry even at 0°C or under conditions of metabolic inhibition; however, subsequent work demonstrated that MAP internalization may involve both endocytic and non-endocytic routes depending on experimental conditions and cell type.

A critical limitation of conventional CPP-based delivery strategies lies in their lack of responsiveness to intracellular physiological states. Most CPP-cargo conjugates enter cells irrespective of redox status, metabolic activity, or disease phenotype, thereby limiting their utility in targeted therapeutic or diagnostic applications. To address this shortcoming, researchers have explored redox-sensitive linkers—particularly disulfide bonds—that remain stable extracellularly but are cleaved upon exposure to the reducing environment of the cytosol, which is enriched in reduced glutathione (GSH). Such constructs enable conditional release of cargo only after successful internalization, enhancing specificity and minimizing off-target effects.

Building upon these principles, Hällbrink and coworkers developed a fluorescence-based assay using a disulfide-linked CPP-cargo construct wherein a fluorophore-quencher pair was separated upon intracellular reduction, allowing real-time monitoring of disulfide cleavage. While effective for tracking internalization kinetics, this approach did not account for potential modulation of CPP uptake itself by cellular redox state—a factor that could significantly influence both delivery efficiency and signal interpretation.

The present invention addresses these gaps by introducing a novel redox-responsive peptide construct termed “reductide,” which integrates a dual-fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) system with a redox-labile disulfide bond linking a cell-penetrating moiety to a non-penetrating reporter peptide. Reductide enables simultaneous assessment of two distinct biological events: (1) cellular uptake of the CPP-conjugate, monitored via a TAMRA fluorophore attached to the penetrating peptide, and (2) intracellular reduction of the disulfide linker, detected through dequenching of a FAM fluorophore on the cargo peptide. Critically, the invention demonstrates that both uptake and reduction are modulated by the cellular redox environment, thereby transforming reductide from a passive delivery vehicle into an active sensor of intracellular redox dynamics.

This dual functionality represents a significant advancement over existing redox probes such as roGFP or monochlorobimane, which report solely on redox potential without providing information about membrane permeability or uptake kinetics. Moreover, unlike viability assays like Alamar Blue™, reductide signal is not confounded by cytotoxicity, as evidenced by discordant responses between reductide fluorescence and cell death markers under oxidative stress conditions. The ability of reductide to reflect both redox state and uptake efficiency opens new avenues for redox-guided drug delivery, high-throughput screening of redox-modifying compounds, and real-time monitoring of adaptive cellular responses to pro-oxidant or antioxidant stimuli.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

As used herein, the term “reductide” refers to a synthetic peptide construct comprising a cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) covalently linked via a disulfide bond to a non-cell-penetrating reporter peptide, wherein the CPP is labeled with a first fluorophore (e.g., TAMRA) and the reporter peptide is labeled with a second fluorophore (e.g., FAM) such that fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) occurs between the two fluorophores when the disulfide bond is intact, and FRET is abolished upon reduction of the disulfide bond, resulting in detectable emission from the second fluorophore.

The phrase “cell-penetrating peptide” or “CPP” denotes a short peptide capable of translocating across the plasma membrane of mammalian cells independently of classical endocytic pathways or in combination with them, typically characterized by a net positive charge and/or amphipathic structure. Non-limiting examples include Model Amphipathic Peptide (MAP), TAT, penetratin, and transportan.

The term “non-cell-penetrating peptide” refers to a peptide that lacks intrinsic ability to cross the plasma membrane unaided and requires conjugation to a CPP or other delivery vector for intracellular access.

“Redox state” or “cellular redox state” encompasses the balance between oxidizing and reducing equivalents within a cell, commonly reflected by the ratio of reduced glutathione (GSH) to oxidized glutathione (GSSG), the NAD⁺/NADH ratio, or the activity of redox-sensitive enzymes such as thioredoxin and glutaredoxin. It may also refer to the overall reducing capacity of the intracellular environment.

“Disulfide bond” means a covalent linkage formed between two cysteine thiol groups (–S–S–), which is stable under oxidizing extracellular conditions but readily reduced to free thiols (–SH) in the reducing intracellular milieu.

“Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)” is a distance-dependent energy transfer mechanism between a donor fluorophore and an acceptor chromophore, wherein excitation of the donor leads to non-radiative energy transfer to the acceptor if the two are in close proximity (typically <10 nm), resulting in quenching of donor emission and/or sensitized emission from the acceptor.

“TAMRA” refers to 5(6)-carboxytetramethylrhodamine, a red-emitting fluorophore with excitation/emission maxima near 555/580 nm, commonly used as a FRET acceptor or quencher.

“FAM” denotes fluorescein amidite, a green-emitting fluorophore with excitation/emission maxima near 495/520 nm, frequently employed as a FRET donor.

“Glutathione” includes both reduced glutathione (GSH), the predominant intracellular thiol antioxidant, and oxidized glutathione (GSSG), its disulfide dimer formed during redox reactions.

“Redox-modifying agent” is any chemical compound that alters the cellular redox state, including but not limited to antioxidants (e.g., N-acetylcysteine, selenomethionine), pro-oxidants (e.g., hydrogen peroxide, diamide), and inhibitors of redox enzyme systems (e.g., L-buthionine sulfoximine, which inhibits GSH synthesis).

### Invention

The present invention provides a redox-responsive peptide construct, designated reductide, that functions as a dual-reporter system for simultaneously monitoring cellular uptake and intracellular reduction in living cells. Reductide comprises a cell-penetrating peptide (CPP)—specifically, a cysteine-modified variant of Model Amphipathic Peptide (C-MAP)—conjugated via a disulfide bond to a non-cell-penetrating reporter peptide (CLKANL). The C-MAP moiety is labeled at its N-terminus with 5(6)-carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA), while the CLKANL peptide is labeled at its N-terminus with fluorescein amidite (FAM). In the intact reductide molecule, TAMRA acts as a quencher of FAM fluorescence through FRET due to their close spatial proximity enforced by the disulfide linkage. Upon cellular internalization and exposure to the reducing intracellular environment, the disulfide bond is cleaved, separating the two fluorophore-labeled peptides and abolishing FRET, thereby restoring FAM fluorescence. Concurrently, TAMRA fluorescence remains detectable regardless of disulfide status, serving as a direct readout of cellular uptake.

Critically, the invention discloses that both the uptake of reductide and the rate of its intracellular reduction are modulated by the cellular redox state. Cells in a more reduced state (e.g., pretreated with N-acetylcysteine) exhibit accelerated uptake and enhanced FAM signal development, whereas cells in an oxidized state (e.g., treated with chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene or hydrogen peroxide) show delayed uptake and diminished FAM fluorescence. This redox dependence extends beyond mere disulfide reduction kinetics, as demonstrated by experiments wherein reductide was added before or after redox perturbation: only when redox state was altered prior to reductide exposure was a significant difference in FAM signal observed, indicating that redox status influences the initial translocation step itself.

Furthermore, the invention establishes that reductide responds dynamically to pharmacological modulation of redox pathways. Screening against a library of 84 redox-modifying compounds revealed that the majority of antioxidants increased reductide signal, while certain pro-oxidants decreased it, with some compounds exhibiting concentration-dependent biphasic effects—acting as antioxidants at low doses and pro-oxidants at high doses. Notably, reductide signal correlated strongly with established redox biosensors such as glutaredoxin-1 fused to redox-sensitive green fluorescent protein (Grx1-roGFP) under oxidative stress conditions, validating its fidelity as a redox reporter.

The invention thus provides a novel platform for: (1) real-time, non-destructive assessment of cellular redox state in live cells; (2) high-throughput screening of redox-modulating drugs; (3) redox-guided delivery of therapeutic or imaging agents via CPP-disulfide conjugates; and (4) investigation of the interplay between membrane translocation mechanisms and intracellular redox homeostasis.

### Materials and Methods

Reductide was synthesized using standard Fmoc solid-phase peptide synthesis protocols. Two separate peptide chains were prepared: (1) Cys-Lys-Leu-Ala-Leu-Lys-Leu-Ala-Leu-Lys-Ala-Leu-Lys-Ala-Ala-Leu-Lys-Leu-Ala-amide (C-MAP), corresponding to a cysteine-extended variant of the Model Amphipathic Peptide, and (2) Cys-Leu-Lys-Ala-Asn-Leu (CLKANL), a non-cell-penetrating control peptide. Following cleavage from resin and deprotection, the C-MAP peptide was labeled at its N-terminal amine with 5(6)-carboxytetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) using carbodiimide coupling chemistry, while the CLKANL peptide was similarly labeled with fluorescein amidite (FAM). The purified TAMRA-C-MAP and FAM-CLKANL peptides were then oxidatively coupled in aqueous buffer under controlled pH and oxygen tension to form a disulfide bond between their respective cysteine residues, yielding the final reductide construct. The product was purified by reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry.

Human BJ fibroblasts, IMR90 fibroblasts, and rat H9c2 neonatal cardiomyocytes were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 2 mM minimum essential amino acids. For stable expression of the redox biosensor Grx1-roGFP, H9c2 cells were transduced with retroviral particles generated from PLAT-E packaging cells transfected with pLPCX-Grx1-roGFP plasmids. Transduced cells were selected using puromycin (4 µg/mL) over 15 population doublings, with stable integration verified by fluorescence microscopy and Western blotting.

For acellular validation, reductide (1 µM) was incubated in Tris-buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.4) containing defined concentrations of reduced glutathione (GSH) and/or oxidized glutathione (GSSG), maintaining a total glutathione pool of 5 mM. Fluorescence was measured over time using a Synergy HT plate reader with excitation/emission settings of 485/528 nm for FAM and 530/590 nm for TAMRA.

In cellular assays, cells seeded in 96-well plates were pretreated with redox-modifying agents—including N-acetylcysteine (NAC), 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene (CDNB), L-buthionine sulfoximine (BSO), diamide, or hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)—for specified durations, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then incubated with reductide (1 µM) in fresh medium. Fluorescence was quantified at designated time points. In reversal experiments, cells were first loaded with reductide, washed, and then exposed to redox agents to isolate the effect on reduction versus uptake.

Live-cell imaging was performed on BJ fibroblasts plated on glass chamber slides using an Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope equipped with a stage-top incubator maintaining 37°C and 5% CO₂. Sequential imaging of TAMRA and FAM channels was conducted over 4.5 hours with 200 ms exposure per frame.

Flow cytometry was carried out on IMR90 fibroblasts treated with H₂O₂ or NAC, followed by reductide incubation for 3, 15, or 30 minutes. Cells were trypsinized, resuspended in phenol red-free medium, stained with DAPI for viability gating, and analyzed on a flow cytometer detecting TAMRA (590/40 nm) and FAM (530/30 nm) emissions.

For comparative analysis with roGFP, H9c2-Grx1-roGFP cells were imaged using a BD Pathway 855 High Content Bioimager with 405 nm and 488 nm laser excitation. Ratiometric images (405/488 emission ratio) were generated in ImageJ after background subtraction and correlated well-by-well with subsequent reductide FAM signals from the same plate.

Monochlorobimane assays were performed in parallel using a commercial glutathione detection kit, with fluorescence read at 390/478 nm. Alamar Blue™ viability assays were conducted according to manufacturer instructions with 540/590 nm detection.

### Statistical Analysis

All quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates. Comparisons between two experimental groups were evaluated using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For experiments involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed followed by post-hoc Tukey’s multiple comparisons test to determine significance between specific pairs. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and a p-value less than 0.01 was deemed highly significant. Correlation analyses between reductide FAM signal and roGFP ratiometric values were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0).

## Results

### Effects of GSH/GSSG on Reductide Redox-Dependent Fluorescence

To validate the redox sensitivity of reductide in a controlled acellular environment, the construct was incubated in Tris-buffered saline (TBS, pH 7.4) containing varying ratios of reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG), with the total glutathione pool maintained at 5 mM. In the presence of GSH alone (5 mM), reductide exhibited a time-dependent increase in FAM fluorescence (excitation/emission: 485/528 nm), reaching a plateau within 20 minutes, consistent with rapid disulfide reduction and separation of the FAM- and TAMRA-labeled moieties. When GSSG was introduced into the buffer—thereby lowering the GSH/GSSG ratio and shifting the redox potential toward oxidation—the rate of FAM signal development was markedly slowed, and the maximum fluorescence intensity achieved was significantly reduced in a GSSG concentration-dependent manner. For instance, buffers containing 2 mM GSH and 1.5 mM GSSG (corresponding to a calculated redox potential of approximately –164 mV) yielded only ~60% of the maximal FAM signal observed in 5 mM GSH (redox potential < –200 mV). In the complete absence of GSH, no increase in FAM fluorescence above baseline was detected, confirming that thiol-mediated reduction is essential for signal generation.

Concurrently, TAMRA fluorescence (530/590 nm) was found to be dependent on GSH concentration but insensitive to time. Specifically, TAMRA emission intensity increased with higher GSH levels, likely due to improved solubility or conformational stability of the reduced C-MAP-TAMRA fragment, yet this signal did not evolve over the 20-minute observation period. This temporal dissociation between FAM (time-dependent) and TAMRA (time-independent) signals confirms that FAM dequenching specifically reports disulfide reduction, while TAMRA serves as a static marker of peptide presence. These findings establish that reductide faithfully responds to the thermodynamic driving force of the glutathione redox couple, as predicted by the Nernst equation, and is suitable for probing redox environments that mimic physiological intracellular conditions.

### Flow Cytometry

Flow cytometric analysis of IMR90 fibroblasts provided single-cell resolution of reductide uptake and processing under defined redox conditions. Cells pretreated with the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC, 4 mM) exhibited significantly higher TAMRA fluorescence intensity compared to vehicle-treated controls at all time points (3, 15, and 30 minutes post-reductide addition), indicating enhanced cellular association or internalization of the C-MAP-TAMRA moiety in a reduced state. In contrast, cells exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, 600 µM) showed attenuated TAMRA signal, consistent with impaired uptake under oxidative stress. FAM fluorescence, though detectable, displayed lower magnitude and reduced temporal resolution relative to TAMRA, a phenomenon attributed to the active export of the reduced FAM-CLKANL peptide via exocytic vesicles, as previously observed in live imaging. Importantly, no increase in DAPI-positive (dead) cells or side-scatter changes indicative of membrane damage was observed in any treatment group, confirming that reductide exposure and redox perturbations at these concentrations do not induce acute cytotoxicity. These data corroborate plate reader and microscopy results, demonstrating that redox state modulates both the quantity and kinetics of reductide internalization at the single-cell level.

### Reductide Response to a Small Library of Redox Modifying Compounds

To evaluate the utility of reductide in pharmacological screening, BJ fibroblasts were exposed to 84 compounds from the Screen-Well™ REDOX library (Enzo Life Sciences) at a uniform concentration of 50 µM for 24 hours, followed by reductide incubation and FAM signal measurement. Of the 84 compounds tested, 65 (77.4%) induced a statistically significant increase in FAM fluorescence relative to vehicle controls, consistent with an overall reductive shift in cellular redox state. Nine compounds (10.7%) caused a significant decrease in signal, indicative of oxidation, while the remaining 10 elicited no significant change. Notably, several compounds known to possess antioxidant properties—such as carvedilol and GERI-BP002A—unexpectedly suppressed reductide signal at 50 µM, suggesting pro-oxidant activity under these conditions. Upon retesting at lower concentrations (e.g., 10 µM), these same compounds enhanced FAM signal, revealing a concentration-dependent biphasic response characteristic of many redox-active molecules that act as antioxidants at low doses but generate reactive oxygen species or deplete thiols at higher concentrations.

Strikingly, certain compounds that induced overt cytotoxicity—evidenced by cell rounding, detachment, and morphological signs of death—nevertheless produced elevated reductide signals. For example, promethazine HCl and retinyl palmitate caused substantial cell loss yet increased FAM fluorescence by 76% and 123%, respectively, compared to controls. This paradoxical result underscores that reductide signal reflects redox state rather than viability, as dying cells may undergo transient reductive bursts (e.g., via GSH release or metabolic collapse) prior to irreversible damage. This conclusion is further supported by experiments showing that 200–400 µM H₂O₂ treatment for 24 hours increased reductide signal while simultaneously reducing Alamar Blue™ metabolic activity, confirming signal-viability discordance. Thus, reductide serves as a specific redox sensor, uncoupled from general indicators of cell health.

## Discussion

### Reductide Uptake as Well as Reduction Depends on Cellular Redox State

The central discovery of this work is that the cellular internalization of the Model Amphipathic Peptide (MAP)—and by extension, reductide—is not a redox-neutral process but is instead dynamically regulated by the intracellular redox environment. While prior studies assumed that CPP uptake was constitutive and unaffected by metabolic state, our data demonstrate that both the rate of cellular association and the efficiency of disulfide reduction are enhanced in reduced cells and suppressed in oxidized cells. This dual dependence arises because reductide signal development integrates two sequential steps: (1) translocation of the intact disulfide-linked construct across the plasma membrane, and (2) intracellular thiol-mediated cleavage of the disulfide bond. If only step (2) were redox-sensitive, differences in FAM signal between redox conditions would be minimal, given that acellular reduction kinetics in buffers spanning physiologically relevant redox potentials (–164 mV to <–200 mV) are rapid and nearly indistinguishable. However, the pronounced divergence in cellular FAM signals—coupled with earlier TAMRA appearance in reduced cells during live imaging and the attenuation of redox-dependent signal differences when reductide is added before redox perturbation—provides compelling evidence that step (1), uptake, is itself redox-modulated.

This finding has profound implications for CPP-based delivery strategies. It suggests that MAP and similar amphipathic peptides may preferentially accumulate in tissues or cells with a more reduced phenotype—a characteristic often associated with proliferating cells, stem cells, or certain tumor microenvironments. Conversely, oxidative stress conditions (e.g., inflammation, ischemia-reperfusion injury) may limit CPP entry, potentially reducing off-target effects in diseased tissues. The mechanism underlying this redox dependence remains to be fully elucidated but may involve redox regulation of plasma membrane potential. Given that MAP carries a strong net positive charge (+7 at physiological pH), its translocation could be driven by the negative-inside electrical gradient maintained by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and ion channels. Since redox state modulates the activity of potassium and calcium channels—often leading to membrane hyperpolarization under reducing conditions—it is plausible that a more negative intracellular potential enhances electrostatic attraction and translocation of cationic CPPs. Future studies will test this hypothesis by directly manipulating membrane potential and assessing reductide uptake.

### Pro-Oxidants Activate an Antioxidative Response

A particularly intriguing observation was the biphasic response of reductide signal to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) treatment: low-to-moderate doses (200–400 µM for 24 hours) increased FAM fluorescence, whereas higher doses (≥600 µM) suppressed it. This pattern aligns with the well-documented phenomenon of hormesis, wherein mild oxidative stress triggers an adaptive antioxidative response that ultimately enhances cellular reducing capacity. At sublethal concentrations, H₂O₂ activates transcription factors such as Nrf2, which translocates to the nucleus and upregulates antioxidant response element (ARE)-driven genes encoding enzymes like heme oxygenase-1, glutathione S-transferases, and NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1. Concurrently, H₂O₂ can stimulate GSH synthesis, stabilize antioxidant enzyme mRNAs, and enhance thioredoxin system activity—all contributing to a net reductive shift despite the initial oxidant challenge. Only when the pro-oxidant insult exceeds a threshold does the antioxidant defense become overwhelmed, leading to GSH depletion, protein oxidation, and a collapse of redox homeostasis, reflected in diminished reductide signal.

This adaptive response explains why certain “antioxidant” compounds in the screening library paradoxically decreased reductide signal at high concentrations: they likely induced sufficient oxidative stress to activate compensatory mechanisms at low doses but caused net oxidation at higher doses. The ability of reductide to capture this nuanced biology—distinguishing between transient adaptive reduction and chronic pathological oxidation—highlights its superiority over static redox probes. Moreover, because reductide integrates both uptake and reduction, it may serve as a functional readout of cellular fitness, reporting not just redox potential but also the integrity of membrane translocation machinery, which is often compromised in advanced disease states.

In summary, reductide represents a transformative tool that bridges the gap between redox sensing and delivery. Its dual-reporter design, redox-modulated uptake, and insensitivity to cytotoxicity make it uniquely suited for applications ranging from basic redox biology to precision medicine.
