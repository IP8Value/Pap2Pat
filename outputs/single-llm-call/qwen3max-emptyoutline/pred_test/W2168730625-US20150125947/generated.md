# DESCRIPTION

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The present invention provides a microfluidic device comprising a metering trap configured to precisely meter a volume of a liquid droplet without requiring active control over flow rates or timing of fluid actuation. The metering trap is formed within a microchannel having a main channel and two side bypass channels that run parallel to the main channel along a defined segment of its length. The side bypasses are fluidically connected to the main channel at both ends of the trap segment and are dimensioned such that they permit the continuous phase to flow around a trapped droplet while preventing the droplet from entering the bypasses. At the downstream terminus of the trap segment, a barrier is positioned within the main channel, extending vertically from the floor of the channel to a height that is substantially equal to the depth of the bypass channels. This barrier serves to increase the Laplace pressure required for a droplet to pass beyond the trap, thereby immobilizing droplets that are shorter than the length of the trap segment. When a droplet longer than the trap segment enters the trap, its rear end blocks the entrance to the bypass channels, thereby preventing the continuous phase from flowing around the droplet. As a result, the pressure drop along the droplet increases, forcing the droplet to advance until its rear end aligns with the entrance to the trap. At this point, the continuous phase can again flow through the bypasses, and the droplet becomes stationary. The front portion of the droplet, which extends beyond the barrier, is then subjected to Rayleigh-Plateau instability due to the high curvature imposed by the narrow gap between the barrier and the ceiling of the channel. This instability causes the droplet to pinch off, leaving behind a precisely metered volume of liquid confined within the trap segment. The volume of the metered droplet is determined solely by the geometric dimensions of the trap—specifically, the length of the trap segment and the cross-sectional area of the main channel—and is independent of the flow rate of the continuous phase or the initial size of the incoming droplet, provided that the incoming droplet is sufficiently large to span the entire length of the trap. This passive metering mechanism enables the reliable generation of uniform droplet volumes without the need for external actuators, pumps, or real-time feedback control systems. In operation, the metering trap may be used in conjunction with a source of sample solution and a source of diluent to generate a series of serial dilutions. For instance, after a sample droplet is metered and trapped within the module, subsequent droplets of diluent may be introduced into the system. Each diluent droplet merges with the trapped sample droplet, homogenizes via internal circulation induced by the flow of the continuous phase through the bypasses, and displaces an equal volume of the mixed solution out of the trap. Repeating this process multiple times yields a geometric progression of concentrations, wherein each successive droplet contains half the concentration of the previous one, assuming equal volumes of sample and diluent. The degree of mixing is enhanced by the shear forces generated as the continuous phase flows through the thin films surrounding the droplet and through the corner gutters of the rectangular channel, promoting rapid homogenization even in the absence of active stirring mechanisms. The metering trap thus represents a foundational building block for constructing more complex microfluidic circuits capable of executing multi-step liquid handling protocols in a fully passive manner.

### Example 2

In another embodiment, the invention includes a merging trap designed to facilitate the controlled coalescence of two or more liquid droplets followed by their release as a single merged droplet, all without reliance on precise temporal or flow-rate control. The merging trap shares structural similarities with the metering trap but incorporates distinct geometric features at both its inlet and outlet termini to enable reversible and robust droplet merging. Specifically, the merging trap comprises a main channel segment flanked by two parallel bypass channels that are open to the main channel only at the extreme ends of the trap. At both the upstream and downstream ends of the main channel segment, side intrusions—recessed cavities or notches—are formed in the channel walls. These intrusions serve as capillary barriers that increase the local Laplace pressure when a droplet attempts to pass through them, thereby immobilizing short droplets within the trap. Unlike the sharp-edged barrier used in the metering trap, the side intrusions are shaped to minimize interfacial deformation and suppress Rayleigh-Plateau-type instabilities, ensuring that a droplet pushed through the trap remains intact rather than fragmenting. During operation, a first droplet that is shorter than the length of the trap enters the module and becomes immobilized near the downstream intrusions due to the increased back-pressure. The continuous phase flows unimpeded through the bypass channels, maintaining a low pressure gradient across the trapped droplet. Subsequently, a second droplet is introduced into the system and flows toward the first. Because the space between the two droplets is filled with continuous phase, and because the bypass channels provide an escape route for this phase, the second droplet can approach and contact the first without being blocked by hydraulic resistance. Upon contact, the two droplets coalesce into a single larger droplet. If the combined volume of the merged droplet exceeds the capacity of the trap—that is, if its length surpasses the distance between the inlet and outlet intrusions—the rear of the merged droplet occludes the entrance to the bypass channels. This occlusion prevents the continuous phase from bypassing the droplet, resulting in a buildup of pressure that propels the merged droplet forward through the downstream intrusions. Once the droplet clears the trap, the bypass channels are re-exposed, and the pressure gradient dissipates, allowing the droplet to continue downstream at a velocity governed by the flow conditions of the main channel. Importantly, the merging process is directional yet symmetric: because intrusions are present at both ends, the trap functions identically regardless of the direction of flow, enabling bidirectional operation. Furthermore, the internal flow field within the merged droplet—characterized by recirculating vortices driven by the motion of the continuous phase in the surrounding films and gutters—ensures rapid mixing of the constituent liquids. This convective mixing is primarily dependent on the distance traveled by the droplet rather than the elapsed time, making it highly compatible with variable or manually controlled flow rates. The merging trap thus enables the deterministic combination of discrete liquid aliquots in a manner that is both passive and reproducible, forming a critical component for applications such to as reagent addition, reaction initiation, or sample normalization in integrated microfluidic assays.

### Example 3

A further example of the invention is embodied in a “lock&shift” trap, a microfluidic module engineered to immobilize individual droplets at predefined locations while enabling their sequential replacement without cross-contamination between successive samples. The lock&shift trap is structurally derived from the metering trap but features a key modification: the side bypass channels are fluidically isolated from the main channel along the central portion of the trap segment, connecting only at the very entrance and exit of the module. This configuration ensures that once a droplet is positioned within the trap, the continuous phase cannot access the region surrounding the droplet except through the narrow gaps at the termini. At the downstream end of the trap, a barrier—either a vertical wall extending upward from the channel floor or a pair of side intrusions similar to those in the merging trap—is employed to generate a localized increase in Laplace pressure that halts the forward motion of short droplets. When a droplet enters the lock&shift trap, it is advected by the continuous phase until it contacts the barrier, at which point it becomes immobilized. The continuous phase continues to flow through the bypass channels, which run parallel to the main channel but remain disconnected along the droplet’s confinement zone, thereby minimizing shear stress on the trapped droplet while maintaining overall system throughput. The defining functionality of the lock&shift trap emerges during the introduction of a subsequent droplet. As the new droplet approaches the trapped one, the continuous phase between them is expelled through the terminally connected bypasses, allowing the incoming droplet to push against the trapped droplet. However, because the bypasses do not intersect the main channel along the body of the trap, direct contact between the two droplets is prevented; instead, hydrodynamic coupling transmits force through the intervening continuous phase, causing the original droplet to be displaced downstream and released from the trap. The incoming droplet then occupies the vacated position, becoming the new trapped sample. This “shift” mechanism ensures that each droplet is individually addressable and replaceable, with no physical contact between consecutive samples, thereby eliminating the risk of carryover or cross-contamination. This feature is particularly advantageous in applications requiring repeated sampling from a single location, such as longitudinal monitoring of microbial cultures, iterative enzymatic reactions, or sequential drug screening. Moreover, arrays of lock&shift traps can be arranged in parallel or series to create spatially organized libraries of droplet-based assays, each containing a unique sample or condition. The passive nature of the locking and shifting operations—governed entirely by channel geometry and interfacial physics—eliminates the need for valves, electrodes, or external actuators, significantly simplifying device operation and enhancing robustness in resource-limited or point-of-care settings. The lock&shift trap thus represents a versatile tool for droplet management in complex microfluidic workflows, combining precise positional control with contamination-free sample exchange.

### Example 4

Another illustrative embodiment of the invention is the delay trap, a microfluidic structure configured to modulate the transit velocity of a droplet relative to the mean flow velocity of the continuous phase, thereby introducing a programmable time delay into droplet-based protocols. The delay trap is constructed with a geometry analogous to the metering trap, featuring a main channel segment accompanied by two parallel bypass channels that run alongside it for a defined length. However, unlike the metering trap, the delay trap lacks any terminal barriers or intrusions; both ends of the trap segment are open and continuous with the upstream and downstream portions of the main channel. When a droplet enters the delay trap, the continuous phase preferentially flows through the lower-resistance bypass channels, effectively shunting a portion of the total flow around the droplet. This redistribution of flow reduces the pressure gradient acting along the length of the droplet, which in turn decreases its propulsion force and slows its forward motion. The magnitude of the delay is determined by the hydraulic resistance of the bypass channels relative to the main channel, which can be tuned by adjusting the width, depth, or length of the bypasses during device fabrication. Experimental validation has demonstrated that appropriately designed delay traps can reduce droplet velocity by up to a factor of two compared to the velocity observed in an unmodified straight channel under identical flow conditions. Crucially, this deceleration occurs without altering the flow rate of the continuous phase or requiring any active intervention, making the delay trap a purely passive timing element. The ability to introduce controlled delays is essential for synchronizing multi-droplet operations in integrated circuits—for example, ensuring that a reagent droplet arrives at a reaction site simultaneously with a sample droplet, or allowing sufficient time for a chemical reaction to proceed before the next processing step. In more advanced configurations, multiple delay traps with varying geometries can be cascaded to create differential delays across different branches of a microfluidic network, enabling sophisticated spatiotemporal control over droplet trajectories and interactions. Furthermore, because the delay effect arises from steady-state hydrodynamic partitioning rather than transient phenomena, it is robust to fluctuations in flow rate and insensitive to minor variations in droplet size. This reliability makes the delay trap particularly suitable for use in manual or gravity-driven systems where precise flow control is impractical. By embedding timing functionality directly into the chip architecture, the delay trap exemplifies the broader principle of encoding protocol logic into physical geometry, thereby reducing operational complexity and enhancing the accessibility of microfluidic technologies for non-specialist users.

### Example 5

An integrated serial dilutor constructed according to the principles of the present invention demonstrates the synergistic combination of multiple passive trap modules to execute a multi-step liquid handling protocol without external control over droplet generation or flow dynamics. This first integrated system, referred to herein as Integrated Serial Dilutor I, comprises three functional zones arranged in a planar microfluidic layout: a buffer metering array, a sample metering unit, and a dilution output array. The buffer metering array consists of ten identical metering traps connected in series along a primary flow path, each separated by a delay trap to ensure adequate spacing and temporal separation between successive buffer droplets. The sample metering unit is a single metering trap positioned orthogonally to the buffer path and accessible via a dedicated inlet. The dilution output array is composed of ten lock&shift traps aligned in a linear fashion downstream of the sample trap. Operation of the device proceeds in three sequential phases, each initiated by switching the direction of continuous phase flow through designated inlets and outlets. In the first phase, continuous phase is introduced through inlet in1 and exits via outlet out1, while a bolus of pure solvent (e.g., water or buffer) is injected through inlet inSol. The continuous phase advects the solvent through the buffer metering array, where each metering trap captures a precisely defined volume V of solvent, leaving ten uniformly sized droplets immobilized in place. In the second phase, flow is redirected from inlet in1 to outlet outSam, and a sample solution is introduced through inlet inSam. This action meters a fixed volume of sample into the dedicated sample trap, completing the preparation of all required reagents. In the third and final phase, continuous phase is flowed from inlet in2 to outlet out2, which drives the buffer droplets sequentially from the metering array toward the sample trap. Each buffer droplet merges with the trapped sample, homogenizes via internal circulation enhanced by bypass flow, and displaces an equal volume of the diluted mixture out of the trap. The resulting droplet, now containing a reduced concentration of sample, travels downstream and is captured by the first lock&shift trap in the output array. Subsequent buffer droplets repeat this process, generating a geometric series of concentrations (e.g., C₀, C₀/2, C₀/4, ..., C₀/2⁹) that are spatially arrayed in the lock&shift traps. Critically, the entire sequence—from buffer metering to final dilution capture—is executed using only three flow-direction switches, with no requirement for precise timing, flow-rate regulation, or droplet-on-demand actuation. The fidelity of the dilution series is ensured by the geometric definition of droplet volumes in the metering traps and the deterministic merging and displacement mechanics of the sample trap. This level of integration illustrates how elementary passive modules can be hierarchically assembled into functional systems capable of performing complex biochemical workflows, such as dose-response curve generation or calibration standard preparation, in a format suitable for point-of-care diagnostics or field-deployable assays.

### Example 6

A second, more advanced integrated system—designated the DOMINO system—exemplifies a fully autonomous serial dilution generator that operates exclusively through directional flow switching and requires no pre-metered input droplets or external droplet formation mechanisms. The DOMINO architecture is built from repeating units, each consisting of a metering trap paired with a downstream merging trap, arranged in a linear cascade. All traps share identical geometric parameters, ensuring consistent volumetric behavior across the array. The system is fabricated in a single-layer polycarbonate chip using CNC milling, with all critical features defined by channel depth, width, and intrusion placement. Operation unfolds in three distinct stages, each corresponding to a specific flow direction through the chip’s manifold of inlets and outlets. In the initialization stage, continuous phase flows from inlet in1 to outlet out1 while a large bolus of solvent (>1.375 µL) is introduced via inlet inSol. This flow pushes the solvent through the entire array of metering traps, each of which captures a fixed volume V (e.g., 275 nL), leaving precisely metered solvent droplets locked in every metering trap. Concurrently, the merging traps remain empty. In the second stage, flow is reversed, directed from inlet in2 to outlet out2. This reversal transports the solvent droplets from the metering traps into the adjacent merging traps, where they become immobilized due to the capillary barriers formed by the side intrusions. Simultaneously, a sample solution is introduced through inlet inSam and metered into the first metering trap as a droplet of volume 2V. At the conclusion of this stage, the system holds a 2V sample droplet in the first metering trap and V-volume solvent droplets in each merging trap. In the third and execution stage, flow is restored in the original direction (in1 to out1). The 2V sample droplet is driven forward: the first metering trap captures V of the sample, allowing the remaining V to enter the first merging trap, where it coalesces with the resident solvent droplet. The merged droplet, now of volume 2V and concentration C₀/2, is released and advected to the next unit. There, the process repeats: V is metered into the second metering trap, and V proceeds to merge with the next solvent droplet, yielding C₀/4, and so on down the line. After traversing all N units, the system outputs an array of droplets with concentrations C₀/2, C₀/4, ..., C₀/2ᴺ, each precisely metered and spatially resolved. Notably, the entire protocol is executed with only three flow-direction changes—no pumps, valves, or sensors are needed. The robustness of the system has been quantified: volume variability of trapped droplets remains below 3% at fixed flow rates and below 5% across a threefold range of flow rates (e.g., 100–300 µL/h), demonstrating insensitivity to operator-induced flow inconsistencies. This performance starkly contrasts with conventional T-junction droplet generators, where similar flow variations cause significant droplet size fluctuations. The DOMINO system thus embodies the core innovation of the invention: the hard-wiring of complex fluidic logic into static geometry, enabling sophisticated biochemical manipulations through simple, user-friendly operations such as flipping a switch or tilting a chip.

### Example 7

The invention further encompasses methods for fabricating the disclosed microfluidic devices using scalable, low-cost manufacturing techniques compatible with mass production and point-of-care deployment. In a preferred embodiment, the devices are fabricated from polycarbonate sheets using computer numerical control (CNC) milling, a subtractive process that precisely carves channel features into a polymer substrate. The milled layer, containing all trap geometries—including main channels, bypasses, barriers, and intrusions—is then thermally bonded to a flat, unstructured polycarbonate cover plate under controlled temperature (e.g., 130°C) and pressure (e.g., 30 minutes of compression) to form sealed microchannels. This single-sided structuring approach eliminates the need for multi-layer alignment or complex lithographic processes, significantly reducing fabrication complexity and cost. Alternative manufacturing methods include hot embossing, where a master mold (fabricated via CNC or photolithography) is used to imprint channel patterns into a thermoplastic film, and injection molding, which enables high-throughput replication of the device geometry in commodity plastics such as cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) or poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA). All these techniques are compatible with the geometric constraints of the trap modules, which rely on well-defined depth transitions (e.g., ¼-depth bypasses, ¾-height barriers) and smooth intrusions that can be faithfully reproduced in thermoplastics. The choice of material is also critical: polycarbonate and similar polymers exhibit excellent optical clarity for droplet visualization, sufficient chemical resistance for aqueous and oil-based systems, and surface properties that can be tuned via plasma treatment or coating to ensure preferential wetting by the continuous phase (e.g., hexadecane). Device interfacing is achieved by inserting stainless steel needles (e.g., 21-gauge) into laser-drilled or punched ports, which are then connected to syringes or reservoirs via polyethylene tubing. In gravity-driven implementations, reservoirs may be elevated to provide hydrostatic pressure, eliminating the need for pumps altogether. The compatibility of the design with planar, single-layer fabrication not only lowers production costs but also enhances reliability by minimizing delamination risks and interfacial defects. Furthermore, the modular nature of the trap architecture allows for rapid prototyping and customization: new functions can be added by simply appending additional trap types to an existing layout without redesigning the entire fluidic network. This manufacturability, combined with operational simplicity, positions the invention as a viable platform for disposable diagnostic cartridges, educational kits, and field-deployable analytical tools in low-resource settings.

### Example 8

The disclosed microfluidic platforms find utility in a wide range of applications spanning clinical diagnostics, environmental monitoring, synthetic biology, and fundamental research. In point-of-care diagnostics, the passive trap modules can be pre-loaded with dried reagents (e.g., antibodies, enzymes, chromogenic substrates) that are reconstituted upon contact with a patient sample droplet. For instance, a blood sample introduced into a DOMINO-like array could be automatically serially diluted and delivered to an array of immunoassay zones, each containing a different capture antibody, enabling multiplexed detection of biomarkers from a single finger-prick volume. Similarly, in microbiology, the lock&shift trap enables long-term observation of bacterial colonies or yeast cultures within immobilized droplets, with periodic replacement of growth medium via incoming droplets to sustain viability over days—ideal for antibiotic susceptibility testing or evolutionary studies. In molecular biology, the merging trap facilitates on-chip PCR setup by combining template DNA, primers, and master mix in precise stoichiometries without cross-contamination, while delay traps can be used to synchronize thermal cycling steps with reagent addition. Environmental applications include the on-site detection of pollutants, where a water sample is processed through a metering trap to isolate a known volume, mixed with a detection reagent in a merging trap, and analyzed colorimetrically in a lock&shift array. The robustness of the system to variable flow rates makes it especially suited for use with hand-operated syringes or gravity-fed reservoirs in remote areas. Additionally, the invention supports digital microfluidics concepts by enabling “droplet programming”—where the sequence of operations is encoded in the spatial arrangement of traps—allowing non-experts to perform complex assays by simply loading samples and initiating flow. Future extensions may integrate capillary valves to automate flow-direction switching, creating truly autonomous chips that require only sample deposition to initiate a full assay protocol. The versatility, scalability, and passive operation of the disclosed technology thus establish a new paradigm for microfluidic system design, bridging the gap between laboratory-grade precision and field-deployable simplicity.

### Example 9

The theoretical foundation underlying the operation of all trap modules resides in the dominance of capillary forces over viscous and inertial effects in microscale two-phase flows, characterized by a low capillary number (Ca ≪ 1). In this regime, the shape and motion of droplets are governed primarily by interfacial tension and channel geometry, rendering them insensitive to moderate variations in flow rate or timing. The pressure jump across a droplet interface is dictated by the Young-Laplace equation, ΔP = γ(1/R₁ + 1/R₂), where γ is the interfacial tension and R₁, R₂ are the principal radii of curvature. In confined rectangular channels, the curvature is strongly influenced by the channel cross-section, particularly at constrictions such as barriers or intrusions. By engineering these constrictions to impose a critical pressure threshold for droplet passage, the traps exploit capillary resistance as a binary switch: droplets shorter than the trap length experience insufficient driving pressure to overcome the barrier and remain immobilized, while longer droplets generate enough pressure via continuous-phase occlusion to advance. The bypass channels play a dual role: they provide a low-resistance path for the continuous phase, reducing the effective pressure gradient on trapped droplets, and they enable hydrodynamic communication between droplets during merging or shifting events. The stability of the trapped state is further enhanced by the suppression of inertial effects (low Reynolds number) and gravitational settling (small Bond number), ensuring that droplets remain stationary unless actively displaced by flow. Mixing within droplets arises from internal circulatory flows induced by the velocity gradients in the thin continuous-phase films surrounding the droplet and in the corner gutters of the rectangular channel—phenomena well-documented in droplet microfluidics literature. These flows scale with droplet velocity but are sufficiently vigorous even at low speeds to achieve homogenization over distances of a few millimeters. The predictability of droplet behavior in this regime allows for rational design of trap dimensions based on first principles, with volume accuracy limited primarily by fabrication tolerances rather than operational variability. Experimental validation confirms that normalized volume standard deviations remain below 5% across a threefold flow-rate range, underscoring the robustness of the geometric encoding strategy. This physics-based approach distinguishes the invention from active microfluidic systems that rely on real-time feedback or precise actuation, offering instead a deterministic, fail-safe framework for droplet manipulation rooted in the immutable laws of capillarity and hydrodynamics.
