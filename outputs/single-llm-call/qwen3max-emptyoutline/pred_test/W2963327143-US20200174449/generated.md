# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of electromagnetic wave manipulation has long relied upon conventional optical elements—such as lenses, mirrors, polarizers, and beam splitters—that are engineered at macroscopic scales significantly larger than the wavelength of the incident radiation. These traditional components operate on well-established principles of geometric optics and wave interference, enabling precise control over amplitude, phase, and polarization states of light or other forms of electromagnetic (EM) radiation. However, their bulkiness, limited integration potential, and inherent constraints in miniaturization have motivated a paradigm shift toward subwavelength-scale engineering of optical functionality. Over the past two decades, this drive has catalyzed the emergence of metamaterials and metasurfaces—artificially structured materials composed of subwavelength unit cells designed to interact with EM fields in ways unattainable by natural media.

Metamaterials, initially conceptualized to achieve exotic properties such as negative refractive index, enabled phenomena like sub-diffraction imaging and cloaking. Photonic crystals, another class of periodic dielectric structures, offered bandgap engineering for controlling light propagation. Yet both approaches often required complex three-dimensional architectures or suffered from narrow operational bandwidths due to reliance on resonant mechanisms. Metasurfaces—a two-dimensional counterpart to metamaterials—rose to prominence as a more practical alternative, offering planar, ultrathin platforms capable of imparting spatially varying phase, amplitude, and polarization responses across an interface. Early metasurface implementations leveraged plasmonic metallic nanostructures to sculpt wavefronts via localized surface plasmon resonances. While effective in principle, these designs were plagued by high ohmic losses, particularly in the visible and near-infrared regimes, severely limiting their efficiency and applicability in real-world systems.

To circumvent these limitations, researchers transitioned toward all-dielectric metasurfaces based on high-refractive-index materials such as silicon, titanium dioxide, and gallium nitride. These platforms exploit Mie-type electric and magnetic dipole resonances within subwavelength scatterers to achieve full 2π phase coverage, enabling functionalities like flat lensing, holography, vortex beam generation, and polarization conversion. Despite notable advances, dielectric metasurfaces remain fundamentally constrained by their reliance on predefined geometric building blocks—typically cylinders, rectangles, ellipses, or V-shaped antennas—whose dimensions are tuned to adjust resonant behavior. This parametric design approach restricts the available degrees of freedom, making it exceedingly difficult to simultaneously optimize multiple performance metrics such as efficiency, bandwidth, angular tolerance, and polarization independence. Moreover, resonant operation inherently limits spectral response to narrow bands, typically less than 10% relative bandwidth (Δλ/λ), thereby hindering deployment in broadband applications such as telecommunications, spectroscopy, or multi-spectral imaging.

A further challenge lies in the fabrication complexity associated with nanoscale patterning of high-index dielectrics, especially when targeting visible wavelengths where feature sizes must be below 100 nm. Techniques like electron-beam lithography, focused ion beam milling, or deep-UV photolithography are expensive, slow, and incompatible with large-area or three-dimensional structuring. Consequently, while laboratory demonstrations of advanced metasurfaces abound, scalable, cost-effective manufacturing of high-performance devices remains elusive. This gap between theoretical promise and practical realization underscores the need for a new design philosophy—one that transcends the limitations of intuitive, geometry-driven approaches and instead directly optimizes material distribution to meet target electromagnetic responses.

In parallel, additive manufacturing—commonly known as 3D printing—has matured into a versatile fabrication modality capable of producing complex, freeform structures with resolutions spanning from micrometers to millimeters. Recent advances in polymer-based 3D printing, including stereolithography (SLA), digital light processing (DLP), and fused deposition modeling (FDM), now enable the creation of low-loss dielectric components tailored for microwave, millimeter-wave, and even terahertz frequencies. Polymers such as high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS), and poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) exhibit low dielectric loss tangents (tan δ < 0.003) and moderate refractive indices (n ≈ 1.5–1.6) in these spectral ranges, making them ideal candidates for passive EM devices. Crucially, 3D printing allows monolithic integration of intricate internal geometries that would be impossible to achieve with layer-by-layer lithographic techniques, opening the door to truly volumetric electromagnetic design.

However, the full potential of additive manufacturing for EM device fabrication cannot be realized without a corresponding evolution in design methodology. Traditional forward-design approaches—where a human designer selects a candidate geometry and iteratively refines its parameters—are ill-suited for exploiting the vast design space afforded by 3D-printable continuous media. The combinatorial explosion of possible material distributions renders manual optimization infeasible. To address this, inverse design methods rooted in computational electromagnetics have emerged as a powerful alternative. Inverse design formulates the device synthesis problem as a constrained optimization task: given desired input and output field conditions, find the spatial distribution of permittivity ε(r) that minimizes the discrepancy between actual and target electromagnetic behavior. By treating Maxwell’s equations as physical constraints within an optimization framework, inverse methods can discover non-intuitive, highly efficient structures that defy conventional design rules.

Pioneering work in this domain includes topology optimization, adjoint variable methods, and objective-first algorithms, which have been successfully applied to integrated photonics, on-chip wavelength division multiplexers, and optical isolators. Yet most prior demonstrations have been confined to guided-wave systems (e.g., silicon photonic circuits) where boundary conditions are well-defined and simulation domains are compact. Extending inverse design to free-space metadevices—operating under plane-wave illumination and radiating into open space—introduces significant challenges related to modeling radiation conditions, enforcing periodicity or aperiodicity, and handling vectorial polarization effects. Furthermore, experimental validation of inverse-designed free-space devices has been scarce, particularly in the millimeter-wave regime where scalable fabrication is feasible but rigorous characterization demands specialized equipment.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a unified platform that integrates physics-driven inverse electromagnetic design with scalable additive manufacturing to produce high-efficiency, broadband, polarization-controllable metadevices for free-space applications. Such a platform must overcome the narrowband limitations of resonant metasurfaces, eliminate the inefficiencies of plasmonic losses, leverage the full three-dimensional design freedom of 3D printing, and demonstrate robust experimental performance across a wide frequency range. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel methodology for designing and fabricating millimeter-wave metadevices—including polarization beam splitters, polarization-independent beam benders, and flat metalenses—using an inverse electromagnetic algorithm coupled with consumer-grade fused deposition modeling (FDM) 3D printing of low-loss polymer. This approach enables unprecedented control over diffraction orders, focal properties, and polarization responses while achieving efficiencies exceeding 60% and relative bandwidths greater than 25%, thereby establishing a new benchmark for practical, manufacturable metadevices.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive platform for the design, fabrication, and deployment of high-performance electromagnetic metadevices operating in the millimeter-wave spectrum, though scalable to other frequency ranges including microwave, terahertz, and potentially optical regimes through appropriate material selection and resolution scaling. Central to this invention is an inverse electromagnetic design methodology that directly optimizes the spatial distribution of dielectric permittivity within a bounded design region to achieve prescribed input-output field transformations, without reliance on preconceived geometric motifs or resonant scattering elements. This approach enables the creation of metadevices that exhibit superior efficiency, broad operational bandwidth, and advanced polarization control compared to conventional metasurfaces or diffractive optics.

Specifically, the invention encompasses three primary classes of metadevices: (1) polarization-selective meta-gratings that diffract orthogonal linear polarizations into opposite diffraction orders; (2) polarization-independent meta-gratings that uniformly bend incident radiation into a single designated diffraction order regardless of input polarization state; and (3) flat, subwavelength-thick metalenses that focus normally incident plane waves into cylindrical wavefronts converging at user-specified focal distances. All devices are fabricated using additive manufacturing—specifically fused deposition modeling (FDM)—with high-impact polystyrene (HIPS), a low-cost, low-loss thermoplastic polymer exhibiting a real relative permittivity of approximately 2.3 and a loss tangent below 0.003 across the 26–38 GHz band. The binary nature of the fabricated structures—comprising only air (ε = 1) and HIPS (ε = 2.3)—is explicitly enforced during the inverse design process, ensuring manufacturability while maintaining high fidelity to simulated performance.

The inverse design algorithm employed herein is based on the objective-first method, which reformulates Maxwell’s equations as a constrained nonlinear optimization problem. The electric field **E** and permittivity distribution ε are alternately optimized under fixed boundary conditions representing the desired electromagnetic functionality. For meta-gratings, periodic boundary conditions are imposed along the grating vector direction, and the target output consists of plane waves in specific diffraction orders (e.g., m = +1 for one polarization, m = −1 for the orthogonal polarization). For metalenses, perfectly matched layer (PML) boundaries are used to simulate open-space radiation, and the target field corresponds to a cylindrical wave emanating from a prescribed focal point. The algorithm iteratively converges to a binary permittivity map that satisfies the functional requirements while adhering to material constraints.

Experimental validation confirms that the fabricated devices achieve diffraction efficiencies exceeding 54% for both polarizations in the polarization splitter, with measured rejection ratios above 5 dB, and polarization-independent bending efficiencies surpassing 70% with minimal power leakage into higher diffraction orders—outperforming conventional blazed gratings by a factor of two in suppression of undesired modes. The metalenses demonstrate tight focusing at distances of 2λ and 15λ with full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) spot sizes approaching the diffraction limit, corresponding to numerical apertures of 0.8 and 0.36, respectively. Critically, all devices exhibit broadband operation: the polarization splitter functions over a 33% relative bandwidth (27–38 GHz), the polarization-independent bender operates up to 55% bandwidth (26–44 GHz), and the metalenses maintain focusing capability from 28 to 40 GHz.

The invention thus establishes a generalizable framework wherein any free-space electromagnetic transformation definable via input and output field distributions can be physically realized through inverse design and 3D printing. By decoupling device performance from resonant physics and instead leveraging propagation-phase accumulation within optimized dielectric pathways, the disclosed metadevices overcome the narrowband limitations of prior art. Furthermore, the use of consumer-grade 3D printing ensures low-cost, rapid prototyping and scalability to large apertures, facilitating integration into radar systems, wireless communications, imaging arrays, and sensing platforms. The methodology is not restricted to millimeter waves; due to the scale invariance of Maxwell’s equations, identical designs can be transposed to other spectral domains provided that suitable low-loss, additively manufacturable dielectrics with subwavelength feature resolution are available.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to electromagnetic metadevices designed via an inverse electromagnetic optimization framework and fabricated using additive manufacturing techniques, specifically for operation in the millimeter-wave frequency range (30–300 GHz), though adaptable to other spectral regions. The core innovation lies in the synergistic combination of a physics-constrained inverse design algorithm with scalable 3D printing of low-loss polymer materials to produce metadevices that achieve high efficiency, broad bandwidth, and sophisticated polarization control without relying on resonant meta-atoms or plasmonic effects. Below, the invention is described in detail with reference to its design methodology, material selection, fabrication process, device architectures, and experimental performance.

The inverse design process begins by defining a rectangular design domain Ω of dimensions L_x × L_y × L_z, where L_z corresponds to the device thickness (typically ≤2λ at the center frequency), and L_x, L_y define the lateral extent. For two-dimensional simulations (assuming infinite extent along the z-axis for transverse magnetic (TM) or transverse electric (TE) polarization), the domain reduces to L_x × L_y. The goal is to determine a spatially varying relative permittivity distribution ε_r(x,y) ∈ {1, ε_HIPS}, where ε_HIPS ≈ 2.3, such that an incident plane wave with specified polarization and wavevector **k**_in is transformed into a desired output field **E**_out at the exit boundary of Ω. This is formulated as a constrained optimization problem:

minimize ||**E**_sim − **E**_target||²  
subject to ∇ × ∇ × **E** − ω²μ₀ε(**r**)**E** = 0 in Ω,  
and **E** = **E**_in on Γ_in,  
and appropriate radiation or periodic boundary conditions on Γ_out,

where **E**_sim is the field computed via finite-element or finite-difference time-domain simulation for a candidate ε(**r**), and **E**_target encodes the desired functionality. The objective-first algorithm solves this by alternating between solving Maxwell’s equation for fixed ε (yielding **E**) and updating ε via gradient-based or heuristic optimization to reduce the mismatch with **E**_target, while enforcing binarization through projection filters or thresholding operations.

For polarization beam splitters, the target functionality requires that a normally incident plane wave polarized along the y-direction (parallel polarization, TM mode with H_z component) be diffracted predominantly into the m = +1 order, while a wave polarized along z (perpendicular polarization, TE mode with E_z component) is diffracted into m = −1. The grating period L_y is set by the grating equation L_y sinθ = mλ, where θ is the desired deflection angle (e.g., ±30°). The design domain spans one period in y and multiple wavelengths in x (thickness direction). Periodic boundary conditions are applied along y, and Bloch ports or scattering boundary conditions are used along x. The inverse algorithm generates a complex, non-periodic internal structure of HIPS and air that imparts a spatially varying phase profile φ(y) tailored separately for each polarization, effectively creating a polarization-dependent blazed grating without geometric symmetry.

In the case of the 30° polarization splitter, the optimized device has a thickness of ~2λ (1.8 cm at 33 GHz) and a period of 1.8 cm. The resulting permittivity map exhibits no obvious geometric regularity—unlike conventional metasurfaces composed of rotated nanopillars or elliptical holes—but instead features intricate, interwoven channels of polymer that guide and delay the wave differently depending on its vectorial nature. Full-wave simulations reveal that the phase accumulation is primarily propagation-based rather than resonance-based, with total phase shifts exceeding 6π in dense regions, enabling full 2π coverage despite the modest index contrast (Δn = 0.52). This non-resonant mechanism underpins the observed 33% relative bandwidth (27–38 GHz), far exceeding typical Mie-resonant metasurfaces.

Fabrication is performed using a commercial FDM 3D printer with 0.4 mm nozzle diameter, yielding feature sizes down to ~0.5 mm—sufficient for millimeter-wave operation where λ ≈ 8–11 mm. HIPS filament is extruded layer-by-layer to build the binary structure, with infill density set to 100% for solid regions and 0% for voids. Post-processing includes acetone vapor smoothing to reduce surface roughness, though measurements indicate that minor imperfections contribute only modestly to efficiency loss (simulated 90% vs. measured 76% for parallel polarization). The final device measures 2 cm (x) × 7.2 cm (y) × 8 cm (z), with four grating periods along y to approximate infinite periodicity.

Characterization employs a vector network analyzer (VNA) connected to transmitting and receiving horn antennas. The source antenna is positioned >100λ away to ensure plane-wave incidence, and the receiver scans angles from −40° to +40° in 2° steps across 26–38 GHz. Measured far-field patterns confirm polarization splitting: at 33 GHz, parallel polarization peaks at +30° with 76% transmission efficiency, perpendicular at −30° with 54%. Rejection ratios (peak-to-sidelobe) exceed 5 dB, validating high diffraction purity. Broadband maps show consistent performance across the band, with slight efficiency roll-off at band edges due to dispersion in phase accumulation.

A second polarization splitter with ±15° deflection uses a larger period (3.5 cm) and exhibits asymmetric bandwidth: parallel polarization operates from 22–44 GHz, while perpendicular is limited to 31–37 GHz due to excitation of a guided resonance that reverses bending direction outside this range. This illustrates how inverse design can unintentionally couple to guided modes, but also how such effects can be harnessed for polarization discrimination.

The polarization-independent bender is designed to send both polarizations to m = +1 at 30°. Its internal structure lacks mirror symmetry, yet achieves balanced response through co-optimized phase profiles. Compared to a triangular blazed grating of identical thickness, the inverse-designed device reduces power in unwanted orders by factors of 2.8 (perpendicular) and 2.0 (parallel), achieving >70% efficiency into m = +1 across 26–38 GHz and extending to 44 GHz. Rejection ratios reach 12.4 dB, demonstrating superior mode purity.

For metalenses, the design domain is aperiodic, with PML boundaries along x and y to absorb outgoing radiation. The target field is a cylindrical wave E_z(r) ∝ r^(−1/2) exp(ikr), centered at (x_f, y_f) = (f, 0), where f is the focal length (2λ or 15λ). The inverse algorithm sculpts a phase profile φ(x,y) that compensates for the quadratic phase of a spherical wave, but does so through volumetric dielectric pathways rather than surface height modulation. The resulting lens is flat (constant thickness ~10 cm, or ~12λ at 38 GHz) yet focuses via internal refraction.

Two lenses are fabricated: one with f = 1.5 cm (2λ), aperture 1.5 cm; another with f = 12 cm (15λ), aperture 2.5 cm. Both are 10 cm tall (z-dimension). Measurements using a scanning probe antenna map the intensity in the focal plane. The short-focus lens achieves FWHM = 0.5 cm (theoretical diffraction limit ~0.48 cm), NA = 0.8; the long-focus lens yields FWHM = 1.1 cm (theoretical ~0.94 cm), NA = 0.36. Efficiency exceeds 60% across 28–40 GHz, confirming broadband focusing. The slight deviation from theory arises from finite aperture effects and fabrication tolerances.

In summary, the invention demonstrates that inverse electromagnetic design coupled with additive manufacturing enables a new class of metadevices that transcend the limitations of conventional optics and resonant metasurfaces. By treating the entire volume as a design variable and optimizing directly for function, the disclosed approach yields high-performance, broadband, polarization-engineered components that are low-cost, scalable, and readily integrable into next-generation electromagnetic systems.
