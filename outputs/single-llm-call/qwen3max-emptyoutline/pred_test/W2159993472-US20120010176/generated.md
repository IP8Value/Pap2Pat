# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of therapeutics for neuromuscular disorders, particularly Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD). More specifically, the invention provides methods and pharmaceutical compositions for the treatment of DMD through the upregulation of endogenous utrophin expression in skeletal and cardiac muscle tissue. The invention is based on the discovery that certain small-molecule compounds, including but not limited to nabumetone, can activate the human utrophin A promoter, thereby increasing the transcription, translation, and functional expression of utrophin protein in muscle cells. Utrophin, a naturally occurring autosomal homologue of dystrophin, is capable of compensating for the absence or dysfunction of dystrophin in DMD patients when sufficiently expressed throughout the sarcolemma of myofibers. The invention further encompasses the use of repurposed, already-approved pharmaceutical agents as safe, orally bioavailable, and cost-effective therapeutics for DMD, circumventing many of the delivery, immunogenicity, and regulatory hurdles associated with gene therapy, protein replacement, or oligonucleotide-based approaches. The disclosed methods are applicable to all patients with DMD regardless of the specific mutation in the dystrophin gene, offering a universal therapeutic strategy that addresses the root cause of the disease—loss of structural integrity at the sarcolemma—by restoring a functional analog of dystrophin via pharmacological activation of an endogenous compensatory pathway.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a severe, progressive, and ultimately fatal X-linked recessive disorder that affects approximately 1 in 3,500–5,000 male births worldwide. It is caused by mutations in the DMD gene located on the X chromosome, which encodes the large cytoskeletal protein dystrophin. Dystrophin plays a critical role in maintaining the structural and functional integrity of skeletal and cardiac muscle fibers by forming a mechanical link between the intracellular actin cytoskeleton and the extracellular matrix through its association with the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC). In the absence of functional dystrophin, the DAPC fails to assemble properly at the sarcolemma, leading to membrane instability, increased susceptibility to mechanical stress during muscle contraction, and recurrent cycles of muscle fiber damage, necrosis, and attempted regeneration. Over time, the regenerative capacity of satellite cells is exhausted, and damaged muscle tissue is progressively replaced by fibrotic and adipose tissue, resulting in profound muscle weakness, loss of ambulation typically by early adolescence, respiratory insufficiency requiring ventilatory support, cardiomyopathy, and premature death often in the third or fourth decade of life.

Despite decades of research, there remains no cure for DMD, and current standard-of-care treatments—such as corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone or deflazacort)—offer only modest symptomatic relief and delay disease progression without addressing the underlying molecular defect. Emerging therapeutic strategies have focused on restoring dystrophin expression or function through various modalities, including exon-skipping antisense oligonucleotides (e.g., eteplirsen, golodirsen), stop-codon readthrough agents (e.g., ataluren), and gene therapy using adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors to deliver micro-dystrophin constructs. However, these approaches face significant limitations. Exon-skipping and nonsense suppression therapies are mutation-specific, applicable only to subsets of patients (e.g., ~13% for exon 51 skipping), and require lifelong, repeated administration of expensive biologics. Gene therapy, while promising, is constrained by the enormous size of the full-length dystrophin cDNA (~14 kb), which exceeds the packaging capacity of AAV vectors, necessitating the use of truncated, potentially less functional variants. Moreover, AAV-mediated delivery carries risks of immune responses against the viral capsid or the transgene product, especially in patients who have never expressed dystrophin and may recognize it as foreign. Additionally, the high cost, complex manufacturing, and limited re-dosing potential of gene therapies pose substantial barriers to widespread clinical implementation.

In this context, utrophin upregulation has emerged as a compelling alternative therapeutic strategy for DMD. Utrophin is an autosomal paralog of dystrophin encoded by the UTRN gene on chromosome 6 in humans. It shares 74% amino acid sequence identity with dystrophin and exhibits a nearly identical domain architecture, including N-terminal actin-binding domains, a central rod domain composed of spectrin-like repeats, and a C-terminal domain that binds to β-dystroglycan and other components of the DAPC. During fetal development, utrophin is widely expressed along the sarcolemma of developing myofibers, where it performs a role analogous to dystrophin. Postnatally, however, utrophin expression becomes restricted primarily to the neuromuscular junctions (NMJs) and myotendinous junctions (MTJs) in mature skeletal muscle, while remaining constitutively expressed in non-muscle tissues such as the liver, kidney, lung, and blood vessels. Critically, because utrophin is naturally expressed in DMD patients throughout life—even in the absence of dystrophin—it is recognized as “self” by the immune system, thereby avoiding the risk of immune-mediated rejection that plagues dystrophin-replacement strategies.

Extensive preclinical studies in the mdx mouse model of DMD—a strain harboring a nonsense mutation in exon 23 of the Dmd gene—have demonstrated that forced overexpression of utrophin in skeletal and cardiac muscle can fully compensate for the lack of dystrophin, preventing muscle pathology, restoring membrane integrity, normalizing calcium homeostasis, reducing inflammation and fibrosis, and significantly improving muscle strength and lifespan. Transgenic mdx mice expressing high levels of utrophin under muscle-specific promoters exhibit a near-complete rescue of the dystrophic phenotype. Similarly, viral vector-mediated delivery of utrophin transgenes or artificial transcription factors targeting the utrophin promoter has yielded robust therapeutic benefits in animal models. These findings strongly support the hypothesis that even modest, sustained upregulation of endogenous utrophin throughout the sarcolemma—rather than just at synaptic regions—could provide clinically meaningful protection in DMD patients.

However, translating utrophin upregulation into a viable therapy has proven challenging. Early attempts using biologics such as heregulin (a neuregulin-1 fragment that activates the utrophin promoter via the ERBB2/ERBB3 signaling pathway) or TAT-fused microutrophin proteins showed promise in vitro and in vivo but faced obstacles related to poor bioavailability, short half-life, and the need for invasive or frequent administration. Small-molecule approaches offer a more attractive route due to their potential for oral dosing, favorable pharmacokinetic profiles, and established safety data. Yet, identifying potent and selective utrophin modulators has been hampered by the lack of robust, scalable screening platforms. Prior efforts to develop utrophin-promoter reporter assays were limited by low signal-to-noise ratios, poor reproducibility, or incompatibility with high-throughput formats.

Drug repositioning—the strategy of identifying new therapeutic uses for existing approved drugs—presents a powerful opportunity to accelerate the development of utrophin-based therapies. By screening libraries of compounds with known human safety, pharmacokinetics, and regulatory approval status, researchers can bypass much of the early-stage drug discovery pipeline, significantly reducing time, cost, and risk of failure. The Prestwick Chemical Library, which consists of over 1,100 FDA- or EMA-approved drugs and natural compounds, represents an ideal resource for such a screen. Indeed, successful repositioning campaigns have yielded new indications for drugs in diverse areas, including oncology, neurodegeneration, and infectious disease.

Against this backdrop, the inventors developed a novel, stable, cell-based luciferase reporter assay driven by the human utrophin A promoter in C2C12 murine myoblasts. This assay was rigorously validated for sensitivity, dynamic range, and statistical robustness (Z-factor > 0.5), making it suitable for high-throughput screening. Using this platform, the inventors screened the Prestwick Chemical Library and identified multiple hit compounds capable of dose-dependent activation of the utrophin A promoter. Among these, nabumetone—a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) approved for the treatment of osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis—emerged as a lead candidate. Independent validation confirmed that nabumetone significantly increases endogenous utrophin A mRNA and protein levels in muscle cells without evidence of cytotoxicity at therapeutically relevant concentrations. Given its established safety profile, oral bioavailability, and potential dual benefit from both utrophin upregulation and anti-inflammatory activity, nabumetone represents a highly promising repurposed therapeutic for DMD. The present invention thus provides the first demonstration of a clinically approved small molecule capable of pharmacologically upregulating utrophin, offering a universal, mutation-agnostic, and rapidly translatable treatment strategy for this devastating disease.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides methods and pharmaceutical compositions for the treatment of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and related muscular dystrophies by upregulating the expression of utrophin in skeletal and cardiac muscle tissue. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that certain small-molecule compounds, particularly those already approved for human use, can activate the human utrophin A promoter, leading to increased transcription and translation of endogenous utrophin protein. Utrophin, when expressed at sufficient levels throughout the sarcolemma of myofibers, can functionally substitute for the missing or defective dystrophin in DMD, thereby restoring the integrity of the dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC), stabilizing the muscle membrane, and ameliorating the pathological features of the disease.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method of treating DMD in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of a compound that activates the utrophin A promoter. In a preferred embodiment, the compound is selected from the group consisting of nabumetone, and pharmaceutically acceptable salts, esters, or prodrugs thereof. In another embodiment, the compound is administered orally, and is formulated in a pharmaceutical composition suitable for systemic delivery.

In another aspect, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising a therapeutically effective amount of a utrophin A promoter-activating compound, such as nabumetone, in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient. The composition may be formulated for oral, parenteral, or other routes of administration, and may be provided in unit dosage forms such as tablets, capsules, or solutions.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a method of identifying compounds capable of activating the utrophin A promoter, comprising contacting a cell stably transfected with a reporter construct comprising the human utrophin A promoter operably linked to a reporter gene (e.g., luciferase) with a test compound, and measuring the activity of the reporter gene as an indicator of promoter activation. This screening method enables the rapid identification of novel utrophin modulators, including repurposed drugs and natural products.

The invention further provides for the use of nabumetone in the manufacture of a medicament for the treatment of DMD. Additionally, the invention contemplates combination therapies wherein a utrophin-upregulating compound is administered alongside other therapeutic agents, such as corticosteroids, anti-fibrotics, or agents that enhance utrophin mRNA stability or sarcolemmal localization, to achieve synergistic effects.

Importantly, the therapeutic approach of the invention is mutation-independent, meaning it is applicable to all DMD patients regardless of the type or location of their dystrophin gene mutation. This universality, combined with the favorable safety and pharmacokinetic profile of repurposed compounds like nabumetone, offers a significant advantage over mutation-specific therapies currently in development. The invention thus represents a major advance in the treatment of DMD, providing a practical, scalable, and rapidly deployable therapeutic strategy that addresses the fundamental pathophysiology of the disease through the activation of a natural compensatory mechanism.

## DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “Duchenne muscular dystrophy” or “DMD” refers to a genetic disorder characterized by progressive muscle degeneration and weakness due to mutations in the DMD gene, resulting in the absence or severe deficiency of functional dystrophin protein in skeletal and cardiac muscle. DMD is inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern and primarily affects males.

The term “utrophin” refers to the protein encoded by the UTRN gene in humans, which is a structural and functional homologue of dystrophin. Utrophin exists in two major isoforms, utrophin A and utrophin B, generated from distinct promoters. Utrophin A is the predominant isoform in skeletal muscle and is the focus of the present invention.

“Utrophin A promoter” refers to the genomic DNA sequence upstream of the transcription start site of the utrophin A isoform that regulates its expression. In the context of this invention, the utrophin A promoter includes at least the 2.3 kilobase fragment of the human utrophin A promoter that has been shown to confer regulated expression in muscle cells, as described in the Examples.

“Promoter activation” means an increase in the transcriptional activity of a promoter, resulting in elevated levels of mRNA transcribed from the associated gene. In the assays described herein, promoter activation is measured indirectly by quantifying the activity of a reporter gene (e.g., luciferase) operably linked to the promoter of interest.

A “therapeutically effective amount” refers to the quantity of a compound or composition that, when administered to a subject, is sufficient to produce a desired therapeutic effect, such as an increase in utrophin expression, improvement in muscle function, or slowing of disease progression, without causing unacceptable adverse effects.

“Pharmaceutically acceptable” refers to those compounds, materials, compositions, and/or dosage forms that are, within the scope of sound medical judgment, suitable for use in contact with the tissues of human beings and animals without excessive toxicity, irritation, allergic response, or other problem or complication, commensurate with a reasonable benefit/risk ratio.

“Pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient” includes any and all solvents, dispersion media, coatings, antibacterial and antifungal agents, isotonic and absorption delaying agents, and the like that are compatible with pharmaceutical administration. The use of such media and agents for pharmaceutically active substances is well known in the art.

The term “subject” or “patient” refers to a human or non-human animal, including mammals such as mice, rats, dogs, cats, livestock, and non-human primates, that is being treated for or is at risk of developing DMD or a related muscular dystrophy.

“Mutation-independent” therapy refers to a treatment approach that is effective regardless of the specific genetic mutation causing the disease, as opposed to mutation-specific therapies such as exon-skipping oligonucleotides that target only particular mutation types.

“Repurposed drug” or “repositioned drug” means a pharmaceutical compound that has been previously approved by a regulatory authority (e.g., the U.S. Food and Drug Administration or the European Medicines Agency) for a specific indication, but is now being used or proposed for use in a different therapeutic context.

“Reporter gene” refers to a gene whose product is easily detectable and quantifiable, and which is used as a surrogate marker for the activity of a regulatory element (e.g., a promoter) to which it is linked. Common reporter genes include luciferase, green fluorescent protein (GFP), and β-galactosidase.

“Stable cell line” refers to a population of cells that have been genetically modified to integrate a foreign DNA construct (e.g., a reporter construct) into their genome, allowing for consistent and heritable expression of the transgene over multiple generations.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to the treatment of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) through the pharmacological upregulation of utrophin, a naturally occurring dystrophin homologue that can functionally compensate for the absence of dystrophin in muscle fibers. The core insight of the invention is that small-molecule compounds, particularly those already approved for human use, can be employed to activate the endogenous utrophin A promoter, thereby increasing the production of utrophin protein throughout the sarcolemma of myofibers. This approach leverages the body’s own compensatory mechanisms to restore structural integrity to the muscle membrane, mitigate the downstream pathological consequences of dystrophin deficiency, and ultimately improve clinical outcomes in DMD patients.

The invention is predicated on several key scientific and technical advances. First, the inventors developed a highly robust and sensitive cell-based assay for monitoring utrophin A promoter activity. This assay utilizes a stable C2C12 murine myoblast cell line (designated C2C12utrn) that has been engineered to express a luciferase reporter gene under the control of a 2.3 kb fragment of the human utrophin A promoter. The C2C12 cell line was chosen because it is a well-characterized model of skeletal muscle differentiation and expresses relevant transcriptional machinery for muscle-specific gene regulation. The 2.3 kb promoter fragment was selected based on prior evidence that it contains critical cis-regulatory elements necessary for basal and inducible expression in muscle cells. The stable integration of the reporter construct ensures consistent and reproducible signal output, while the use of luciferase as a reporter provides a wide dynamic range, high sensitivity, and compatibility with high-throughput screening formats.

Second, the assay was rigorously validated for its suitability in high-throughput drug discovery. Key parameters such as cell seeding density, solvent tolerance (particularly to dimethyl sulfoxide, DMSO, commonly used for compound solubilization), incubation time, and statistical robustness were optimized. The Z-factor—a statistical parameter used to assess the quality of high-throughput screening assays—was determined to be 0.6, which falls within the acceptable range (0.5–1.0) for robust assays. The percentage coefficient of variation (% CV) of control wells was consistently below 10%, indicating low intra-plate variability. Trichostatin A (TSA), a histone deacetylase inhibitor known to upregulate utrophin, was identified as a suitable positive control, exhibiting a clear dose-response relationship with an EC50 of 1.7 nM in the 384-well format.

Third, using this validated assay, the inventors conducted a high-throughput screen of the Prestwick Chemical Library, a collection of 1,120 compounds comprising 90% FDA- or EMA-approved drugs and 10% natural compounds with known biological activity. The screen was performed at a single concentration (2 µg/ml, approximately 5 µM) and identified 20 initial hits (1.8% of the library) that induced at least 20% upregulation of luciferase activity relative to vehicle controls. To eliminate false positives and confirm dose-dependent activity, these hits were subjected to secondary screening across a 15-point concentration range (0.5 ng/ml to 8 µg/ml). Fourteen compounds (70% of the initial hits) demonstrated clear dose-response curves, confirming their ability to activate the utrophin A promoter. Among these, nabumetone—a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) approved for the treatment of osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis—exhibited potent activity with a high fold-change and no evidence of cytotoxicity at effective concentrations.

Fourth, the inventors performed independent validation of nabumetone’s effect on endogenous utrophin expression using orthogonal methodologies. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) with a custom TaqMan assay specific for utrophin A mRNA confirmed that treatment of wild-type C2C12 cells with 25 µM nabumetone resulted in a statistically significant 1.8-fold increase in utrophin A transcript levels compared to vehicle-treated controls. Western blot analysis using a well-characterized monoclonal antibody (mancho 3 clone 8A4) further demonstrated that prolonged exposure (4 days) to nabumetone led to a 1.2-fold increase in utrophin protein levels. Although the magnitude of protein upregulation was somewhat lower than that observed at the mRNA level—potentially due to post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms or the slow turnover of the large (~400 kDa) utrophin protein—the observed increase is considered therapeutically relevant. Preclinical studies in mdx mice have shown that even a 1.5- to 2-fold increase in utrophin protein can significantly ameliorate the dystrophic phenotype, including improvements in muscle histology, strength, and resistance to eccentric contraction-induced damage.

The mechanism by which nabumetone activates the utrophin A promoter is not yet fully elucidated. Nabumetone is a prodrug that is metabolized in the liver to its active form, 6-methoxy-2-naphthylacetic acid (6-MNA), which functions as a non-selective inhibitor of cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, with a slight preference for COX-2. While COX inhibition leads to reduced prostaglandin synthesis and anti-inflammatory effects, it is unclear whether this mechanism is directly linked to utrophin upregulation. It is possible that nabumetone or its metabolite modulates signaling pathways that converge on the utrophin promoter, such as those involving histone acetylation, kinase cascades, or transcription factor activation. Alternatively, the effect may be indirect, mediated through changes in the cellular redox state, calcium signaling, or inflammatory milieu. Regardless of the precise mechanism, the empirical observation of utrophin upregulation provides a strong rationale for therapeutic development.

The advantages of the present invention are manifold. First, it offers a universal treatment for DMD, applicable to all patients irrespective of their dystrophin mutation type. Second, it utilizes a small-molecule drug with a well-established safety profile, extensive pharmacokinetic data, and oral bioavailability, thereby avoiding the complexities of biologic delivery, immunogenicity risks, and high manufacturing costs associated with gene or protein therapies. Third, the repurposing strategy dramatically accelerates the path to clinical testing, as Phase I safety trials are unnecessary, and Phase II/III efficacy trials can be initiated more rapidly. Fourth, the anti-inflammatory properties of nabumetone may provide additional therapeutic benefit, given that chronic inflammation is a well-documented contributor to DMD pathology. Finally, the invention establishes a platform for the discovery of additional utrophin modulators, either through further screening of compound libraries or through medicinal chemistry optimization of lead compounds.

### Pharmaceutical Compositions and Methods of Administration

The present invention provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising a therapeutically effective amount of a utrophin A promoter-activating compound, such as nabumetone, in combination with one or more pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, diluents, or excipients. These compositions are formulated to ensure stability, bioavailability, and patient compliance, and may be adapted for various routes of administration depending on the clinical context and desired pharmacokinetic profile.

Nabumetone is commercially available as an oral tablet (e.g., 500 mg or 750 mg) for the treatment of arthritis. For the treatment of DMD, the dosage may be adjusted based on preclinical efficacy data, human pharmacokinetic parameters, and safety considerations. In the in vitro studies described herein, the effective concentration of nabumetone was 25 µM. Extrapolating to human dosing, this concentration is well below the peak plasma levels achieved with standard clinical doses (which correspond to approximately 100–200 µM in total body fluid volume). Therefore, it is anticipated that existing formulations of nabumetone may be used with minimal or no modification for DMD therapy. However, the invention also encompasses the development of novel formulations designed to optimize muscle tissue penetration, prolong half-life, or reduce gastrointestinal side effects commonly associated with NSAIDs.

Pharmaceutical compositions of the invention may be prepared using standard techniques known in the art. For oral administration, the active compound may be formulated as tablets, capsules, powders, granules, or liquid suspensions. Solid dosage forms may include fillers (e.g., lactose, microcrystalline cellulose), binders (e.g., starch, polyvinylpyrrolidone), disintegrants (e.g., croscarmellose sodium), lubricants (e.g., magnesium stearate), and coating agents (e.g., hydroxypropyl methylcellulose). Liquid formulations may contain solubilizers, preservatives, and flavoring agents.

For parenteral administration (e.g., intravenous, subcutaneous, or intramuscular injection), the compound may be dissolved or suspended in a sterile aqueous or non-aqueous vehicle, such as saline, dextrose solution, or polyethylene glycol. Such formulations may include buffers, antioxidants, and tonicity agents to ensure stability and compatibility with biological tissues.

The invention also contemplates controlled-release or extended-release formulations that provide sustained delivery of the active compound over an extended period, thereby maintaining therapeutic concentrations with less frequent dosing. These may be achieved through the use of biodegradable polymers, liposomes, or nanoparticle-based systems.

Administration of the pharmaceutical composition is typically carried out on a chronic basis, given the progressive nature of DMD. The dosing regimen may be once daily, twice daily, or as otherwise determined by clinical response and tolerability. In pediatric patients, dosing may be adjusted based on body weight or surface area.

Combination therapy is another important aspect of the invention. The utrophin-upregulating compound may be co-administered with other agents that target complementary aspects of DMD pathophysiology. For example, concurrent use with corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone) may provide additive or synergistic benefits by simultaneously reducing inflammation and enhancing membrane stability. Similarly, combination with agents that promote utrophin mRNA stability (e.g., compounds targeting the 5′ or 3′ UTRs) or facilitate sarcolemmal localization (e.g., biglycan or other extracellular matrix modulators) could amplify the therapeutic effect. The invention thus provides a flexible platform for multimodal intervention in DMD.

## EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Generation and Validation of the C2C12utrn Stable Cell Line**

The C2C12 murine myoblast cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin. To create a reporter cell line for utrophin A promoter activity, a 2.3 kb fragment of the human utrophin A promoter was amplified by PCR from a CHORI BAC clone (PR1-91J24) using specific primers and cloned into the pGL4.14[luc2/Hygro] vector (Promega) downstream of the luciferase reporter gene. The resulting construct, pGL4.14/utrnAprom, was verified by sequencing.

C2C12 cells were transfected with pGL4.14/utrnAprom using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the medium was replaced, and 48 hours later, cells were trypsinized and re-plated in selection medium containing 250 µg/ml hygromycin B. Hygromycin-resistant colonies emerged after 10–14 days and were isolated using sterile filter paper discs. These colonies were subjected to two rounds of limiting dilution cloning to obtain monoclonal populations. Four clones exhibiting the highest luciferase activity in preliminary assays were selected for further validation. Genomic DNA was extracted from these clones, and PCR amplification using utrophin A promoter-specific primers confirmed the presence of the integrated transgene. The clone with the highest and most consistent luciferase signal, designated C2C12utrn, was expanded and used for all subsequent experiments.

**Example 2: Development and Optimization of the Utrophin Promoter Activation Assay**

Assay conditions were optimized to ensure robust performance in high-throughput format. Cell seeding density was determined by plating C2C12utrn cells at varying densities (600–5,000 cells/well) in 384-well plates and assessing confluence and luciferase activity after 24 hours. A density of 1,200 cells/well (resulting in ~70% confluence at assay endpoint) was selected as optimal. DMSO tolerance was evaluated by treating cells with 0–0.5% DMSO for 24 hours; concentrations up to 0.2% had minimal effect on luciferase activity, while 0.5% reduced activity to 75% of control, supporting the use of 0.1% DMSO as the final solvent concentration in screening.

Four known utrophin modulators—L-arginine (2 mM), okadaic acid (50 nM), heregulin (2 nM), and trichostatin A (TSA, 50 nM)—were tested as potential positive controls. Cells were treated for 24 or 48 hours, and luciferase activity was measured using the Bright-Glo assay. TSA produced the greatest induction (2.5-fold) after 24 hours and exhibited a Z-factor of 0.6, confirming its suitability as a positive control. A dose-response curve for TSA in 384-well format yielded an EC50 of 1.7 nM, with peak activity at 20 nM.

**Example 3: High-Throughput Screening of the Prestwick Chemical Library**

The Prestwick Chemical Library (1,120 compounds) was screened in 384-well plates at a final concentration of 2 µg/ml (0.1% DMSO). C2C12utrn cells were plated 24 hours prior to compound addition and incubated for 24 hours before luciferase measurement. Quality control plates containing 5 nM TSA were run at the beginning and end of the screen. Data were analyzed using ActivityBase software, with hits defined as compounds inducing ≥20% upregulation (approximately 3× the % CV of negative controls). Twenty hits were identified (1.8% of the library).

**Example 4: Hit Confirmation and Dose Optimization**

The 20 initial hits were retested in a 15-point dose-response (0.5 ng/ml to 8 µg/ml) in 384-well format. Fourteen compounds showed dose-dependent activation, with maximum fold-changes ranging from 1.2 to 1.9. Nabumetone was selected for further study due to its high potency, lack of cytotoxicity, and established clinical profile.

**Example 5: Independent Validation of Nabumetone**

Wild-type C2C12 cells were treated with 25 µM nabumetone for 24 hours. RNA was isolated and reverse-transcribed, and utrophin A mRNA levels were quantified by TaqMan qRT-PCR using primers and probe specific for the utrophin A isoform. Nabumetone treatment resulted in a 1.8-fold increase in utrophin A mRNA (p < 0.01, one-way ANOVA). In a separate experiment, cells were treated with 25 µM nabumetone for 4 days, with media and compound refreshed after 2 days. Western blot analysis showed a 1.2-fold increase in utrophin protein (p < 0.05, Student’s t-test), confirming functional upregulation.

These examples collectively demonstrate the feasibility of identifying repurposed drugs that upregulate utrophin and validate nabumetone as a lead candidate for DMD therapy.
