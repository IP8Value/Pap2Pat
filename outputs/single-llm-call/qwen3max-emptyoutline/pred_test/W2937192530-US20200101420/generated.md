# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of photothermal materials and systems for solar energy conversion, and more particularly to a superhydrophobic, porous carbon-based membrane structure engineered for highly efficient solar-driven interfacial evaporation of water. The invention encompasses a novel composite material architecture comprising graphitic foam substrates functionalized with fluorinated silane coatings and reinforced with a tailored mixture of graphite particles and phenolic resin, resulting in a mechanically robust, thermally insulating, and optically optimized platform capable of localized heat generation and retention at the air–liquid interface. This technology is especially suited for applications in solar desalination, wastewater purification, sterilization, and portable freshwater generation in off-grid or resource-limited environments. The invention further provides methods for fabricating such membranes through scalable, low-cost processes involving solution-phase surface modification and thermal curing, enabling large-area deployment without reliance on rare or expensive components. By integrating hierarchical porosity, broadband solar absorption, and extreme water repellency into a monolithic carbonaceous structure, the invention overcomes longstanding limitations in conventional solar evaporators related to heat loss, salt accumulation, material degradation, and inefficient light-to-thermal conversion. The disclosed system operates effectively under natural sunlight or simulated solar irradiation at standard intensity (1 kW m⁻²), achieving evaporation rates significantly exceeding those of prior art devices while maintaining long-term stability and resistance to fouling.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The global demand for clean, potable water continues to escalate due to population growth, climate change, and industrial expansion, creating an urgent need for sustainable, energy-efficient water purification technologies. Solar-driven interfacial evaporation has emerged as a promising approach to address this challenge by harnessing abundant solar energy to vaporize water directly at the surface of a photothermal material, thereby minimizing bulk heating losses and enabling high thermal efficiency. In contrast to traditional distillation methods that require substantial energy input to heat entire volumes of water, interfacial evaporation confines thermal energy to a thin layer at the air–water boundary, dramatically improving energy utilization. However, despite significant advances in recent years, practical implementation of solar evaporators has been hindered by several persistent technical barriers, including insufficient solar absorption across the full solar spectrum, excessive conductive and convective heat losses to the underlying water body, poor mechanical durability, salt crystallization-induced performance degradation in saline environments, and inadequate control over water transport and vapor escape dynamics.

Early solar evaporators relied on simple blackened surfaces or carbon-based materials such as activated carbon, carbon nanotubes, or graphene oxide films deposited on floating substrates. While these materials exhibited reasonable light absorption, they often suffered from rapid performance decay due to wetting, structural collapse, or salt deposition blocking active sites. Moreover, many designs failed to decouple the functions of light absorption, thermal insulation, and water supply, leading to suboptimal energy management. Subsequent efforts introduced multilayer architectures combining a top photothermal layer with a bottom thermal insulator (e.g., polystyrene foam) and a wicking channel for water delivery. Although these systems improved efficiency, they introduced complexity, interfacial delamination risks, and reliance on synthetic polymers that may degrade under prolonged UV exposure or high temperatures.

Graphitic foams (GFs), derived from mesophase pitch precursors, have attracted attention as potential platforms for solar evaporation due to their inherent high thermal conductivity, chemical inertness, tunable porosity, and carbon-rich composition conducive to strong light absorption. However, pristine GFs are typically hydrophilic or only mildly hydrophobic, causing them to become fully saturated with water, which leads to significant conductive heat loss into the bulk liquid and reduces evaporation efficiency. Furthermore, unmodified GFs lack spectral selectivity and may reflect a non-negligible portion of incident solar radiation, particularly in the visible and near-infrared regions critical for solar energy harvesting. Attempts to enhance hydrophobicity through physical texturing or plasma treatment have proven insufficient for long-term operation in dynamic aqueous environments, especially under high salinity or organic contamination.

Surface chemical modification using low-surface-energy compounds, such as fluorosilanes, offers a route to impart superhydrophobicity—defined as water contact angles exceeding 150° and sliding angles below 10°—to carbon-based materials. Fluorosilanes react with surface hydroxyl or carboxyl groups to form covalently bonded monolayers that drastically reduce surface energy. However, applying such coatings to highly porous, three-dimensional GF structures presents challenges: incomplete penetration can leave internal surfaces untreated, while excessive coating may clog pores and impede necessary capillary water transport to the evaporation front. Additionally, the mechanical fragility of thin fluorosilane layers on rough carbon surfaces can lead to abrasion-induced failure during handling or operation.

Another critical limitation of existing GF-based evaporators is the lack of pore-size engineering to balance competing requirements: small pores enhance capillary action for water replenishment but increase flow resistance and promote salt nucleation; large pores facilitate vapor escape and reduce salt clogging but weaken capillary forces and may allow excessive water infiltration, undermining thermal localization. Conventional GFs possess broad, uncontrolled pore size distributions that do not permit fine-tuning of these transport phenomena. Consequently, there remains a need for a method to precisely regulate the effective pore architecture of GF membranes without compromising their structural integrity or photothermal properties.

Thermal management also poses a significant challenge. Even with surface hydrophobization, heat generated within the GF can conduct downward into the water reservoir if the material lacks sufficient thermal insulation. While some approaches incorporate external insulating layers, these add weight, cost, and potential failure points. An ideal solution would integrate thermal insulation intrinsically within the evaporator structure itself. Phenolic resins, when carbonized, form rigid, low-thermal-conductivity char that could serve this purpose, but their direct application to GFs must be carefully controlled to avoid blocking photothermal surfaces or reducing porosity excessively.

Moreover, the optical performance of carbon-based evaporators is rarely optimized beyond basic blackening. Efficient solar absorbers should exhibit minimal reflectance and near-zero transmittance across the entire solar spectrum (300–2500 nm), corresponding to over 95% absorption. Many reported carbon materials achieve only 85–90% absorption due to surface scattering or incomplete coverage. Achieving >95% broadband absorption typically requires nanostructuring or plasmonic enhancements, which complicate fabrication and scalability. A simpler, yet effective, strategy involves combining intrinsic carbon absorption with surface texturing and chemical functionalization to trap light through multiple reflections and reduce Fresnel losses.

In summary, the state of the art lacks a single, integrated material system that simultaneously achieves: (1) ultra-broadband, high-efficiency solar absorption; (2) robust, durable superhydrophobicity resistant to environmental stressors; (3) engineered porosity enabling optimal water supply and vapor release while preventing salt accumulation; (4) intrinsic thermal insulation to minimize downward heat loss; and (5) mechanical stability for repeated use in harsh conditions. The present invention addresses all these shortcomings through a novel fabrication protocol that transforms commercially available graphitic foams into high-performance photothermal membranes via sequential surface fluorination and pore-structure reinforcement with a graphite–phenolic composite. This approach leverages the inherent advantages of carbon materials while overcoming their historical limitations through rational design of surface chemistry and microstructure.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a photothermal superhydrophobic carbon membrane specifically engineered for efficient solar-driven interfacial water evaporation, along with methods for its fabrication and use. The membrane comprises a porous graphitic foam substrate that has been chemically modified with a fluorosilane coating to impart extreme water repellency and further reinforced with a composite layer of graphite particles and phenolic resin to tailor pore architecture, enhance mechanical strength, and improve thermal insulation. This integrated design enables the membrane to localize heat precisely at the air–water interface, minimize parasitic heat losses, resist salt fouling, and maintain high evaporation rates under standard solar illumination.

In one aspect, the invention features a photothermal membrane comprising a graphitic foam body having a three-dimensional interconnected porous network, wherein the internal and external surfaces of the foam are covalently functionalized with fluorinated alkylsilane molecules, and wherein at least a portion of the pore walls is coated with a cured composite of graphite particles and phenolic resin in a weight ratio ranging from 1.5:1 to 2.5:1. The fluorosilane coating renders the membrane superhydrophobic, with a static water contact angle greater than 150 degrees and a sliding angle less than 10 degrees, thereby preventing bulk water infiltration while allowing controlled capillary rise to the evaporation zone. The graphite–phenolic composite serves to narrow the effective pore size distribution, suppress excessive water uptake, and provide a low-thermal-conductivity barrier that reduces conductive heat loss to the underlying water reservoir.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for preparing the photothermal membrane, the method comprising: (a) machining a graphitic foam into a desired geometric configuration comprising at least one chamber and at least one tubular conduit; (b) immersing the machined foam in a solution of fluorosilane in a non-polar solvent at a concentration of 0.05 to 0.2 weight percent for a duration of 12 to 24 hours to deposit a self-assembled monolayer of fluorosilane on all accessible surfaces; (c) drying the fluorosilane-treated foam at a temperature of 80 to 120°C for 0.5 to 2 hours; (d) rinsing the dried foam with water and ethanol to remove physisorbed silane species; (e) preparing a slurry by mixing graphite particles and phenolic resin in a weight ratio of 2:1 in a volatile solvent; (f) applying the slurry onto the interior surfaces of the foam chamber and lid using a manual applicator; (g) air-drying the coated foam overnight; and (h) thermally curing the dried coating at 280 to 320°C for 0.5 to 2 hours to carbonize the phenolic resin and bond the graphite particles to the foam substrate.

In yet another aspect, the invention includes a solar evaporation system comprising the photothermal membrane floating on a body of water, wherein the membrane absorbs at least 95% of incident solar radiation across the wavelength range of 330 to 2500 nanometers, generates localized heat at the air–water interface, and drives water vapor production at a rate exceeding 1.5 kilograms per square meter per hour under one-sun illumination (100 mW cm⁻²). The system operates without external energy input and is capable of continuous operation in seawater or brine solutions without performance degradation due to salt crystallization, owing to the membrane’s superhydrophobic surface that repels salt ions and facilitates their expulsion during evaporation cycles.

The invention further encompasses the use of the photothermal membrane in applications including but not limited to: desalination of seawater, purification of industrial wastewater, sterilization of contaminated water through thermal inactivation of pathogens, and generation of freshwater in remote or disaster-stricken regions. The membrane’s all-carbon composition ensures environmental compatibility, chemical stability, and resistance to biofouling, while its simple fabrication process enables low-cost, large-scale manufacturing using commercially available raw materials.

Key advantages of the invention include: (i) unprecedented combination of superhydrophobicity and controlled porosity, enabling stable interfacial evaporation without flooding; (ii) intrinsic thermal insulation provided by the graphite–phenolic composite layer, eliminating the need for separate insulating substrates; (iii) broadband solar absorption exceeding 95% due to synergistic effects of graphitic carbon, surface roughness, and fluorosilane-induced anti-reflection; (iv) mechanical robustness suitable for repeated handling and long-term deployment; and (v) scalability and sustainability through use of earth-abundant, non-toxic components and energy-efficient processing steps. These features collectively represent a significant advancement over prior art solar evaporators, offering a practical solution to global water scarcity challenges.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a novel photothermal membrane structure and associated fabrication methodology that enables highly efficient, durable, and scalable solar-driven interfacial water evaporation. The core innovation lies in the strategic integration of surface chemistry modification and pore architecture engineering within a graphitic foam (GF) matrix to create a multifunctional material that simultaneously optimizes optical absorption, thermal localization, water transport, and environmental resilience. Below, the invention is described in detail with reference to its constituent materials, fabrication sequence, structural characteristics, functional mechanisms, and operational performance.

The starting material for the invention is a graphitic foam derived from heat-treated polyimide (PI) mesophase pitch, a well-established precursor for high-performance carbon foams. Such foams are commercially available from suppliers including Koppers Inc. and CFOAM, LLC, and are characterized by high purity, open-cell porosity, and excellent thermal and electrical conductivity. The foam is initially processed into a specific geometric configuration suitable for evaporation applications—typically comprising a central evaporation chamber surrounded by or connected to one or more tubular conduits that facilitate water supply via capillary action. Machining is performed using standard workshop tools such as electric saws and drill bits, ensuring precise dimensional control without damaging the delicate cellular structure. The resulting foam body exhibits a thickness of approximately 5 millimeters, although this parameter may be adjusted between 2 and 10 millimeters depending on the target application and desired thermal insulation properties.

Following machining, the foam undergoes surface functionalization with a fluorinated silane compound to impart superhydrophobicity. Specifically, (heptadecafluoro-1,1,2,2-tetrahydrodecyl) trichlorosilane is employed due to its long perfluoroalkyl chain (C8F17) that provides extremely low surface energy, and its trichlorosilane head group that readily hydrolyzes and condenses with hydroxyl groups present on the graphitic surface to form stable Si–O–C covalent bonds. The functionalization is carried out via solution immersion: the machined foam is submerged in a dilute solution of the fluorosilane (0.1 weight percent) in hexane, a non-polar solvent that ensures uniform wetting of the hydrophobic carbon surface without causing pore collapse. Immersion is maintained for 12 to 24 hours at ambient temperature to allow complete diffusion of the silane molecules throughout the entire porous network and formation of a dense, cross-linked monolayer. After immersion, the foam is removed and subjected to mild thermal treatment—oven-drying at 100°C for one hour—to accelerate the condensation reaction and drive off residual solvent. This step is critical for achieving a durable, covalently anchored coating rather than a physically adsorbed layer prone to delamination. Subsequently, the foam is thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and ethanol to eliminate any unreacted or loosely bound silane species that could leach out during operation or interfere with subsequent processing steps. The resulting fluorosilane-modified GF exhibits a static water contact angle exceeding 150°, confirming the successful creation of a superhydrophobic surface, while maintaining the original macroporous structure essential for vapor escape.

The next critical step involves pore architecture engineering through the application of a composite coating composed of graphite particles and phenolic resin. Graphite particles (grade 4827, specific surface area 225–275 m² g⁻¹), sourced from Asbury Graphite Mills Inc., are selected for their high thermal stability, chemical inertness, and compatibility with carbon matrices. Phenolic resin (Durite SC1008, Hexion Specialty Chemicals) serves as a thermosetting binder that, upon curing, forms a rigid, carbon-rich char that adheres strongly to both the graphite particles and the underlying GF substrate. The two components are mixed in a precise weight ratio of 2:1—two parts graphite to one part resin—in a minimal amount of ethanol or acetone to form a viscous slurry. This ratio has been empirically optimized to balance multiple objectives: sufficient resin content to ensure cohesive binding and pore narrowing, without excessive coverage that would block photothermal surfaces or impede capillary flow. The slurry is manually applied to the interior surfaces of the evaporation chamber and its lid using a simple wood stick knife, allowing for controlled, conformal coating of complex geometries without specialized equipment. Following application, the coated foam is air-dried overnight at room temperature to evaporate the solvent, yielding a tack-free film. Finally, the assembly is thermally cured in an inert atmosphere (e.g., nitrogen or argon) at 300°C for one hour. During this step, the phenolic resin undergoes pyrolysis and carbonization, transforming into a hard, microporous carbon matrix that encapsulates the graphite particles and bonds them firmly to the GF walls. This cured composite layer effectively reduces the average pore diameter in the treated regions, creating a gradient in pore size that promotes directional water transport toward the evaporation front while preventing uncontrolled flooding. Importantly, the coating is applied selectively to the chamber walls and lid—not the entire foam—preserving the open porosity of the tubular conduits for efficient water supply.

The final photothermal membrane thus consists of three functionally distinct but seamlessly integrated zones: (1) a superhydrophobic, broadband-absorbing evaporation surface formed by the fluorosilane-coated GF; (2) a pore-engineered chamber wall reinforced with graphite–phenolic composite that regulates water influx and provides thermal insulation; and (3) uncoated or lightly coated capillary channels that draw water upward from the reservoir. This zonal design decouples the conflicting requirements of evaporation, insulation, and water delivery within a single monolithic structure.

Material characterization confirms the successful implementation of the invention’s key features. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals a homogeneous distribution of the graphite–phenolic composite on the chamber walls, with no evidence of cracking or delamination after thermal curing. The underlying GF struts retain their original morphology, indicating that the processing conditions did not induce structural degradation. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) detects characteristic fragments of perfluoroalkyl chains (e.g., C2F5⁺, C3F7⁺) on the surface, verifying the presence of the fluorosilane monolayer. Optical measurements using a portable reflectometer demonstrate that the membrane exhibits total solar absorptance (A = 1 − R − T) greater than 95% across the 330–2500 nm wavelength range, where R is reflectance and T is transmittance. Given the membrane’s thickness (5 mm), transmittance is negligible (T ≈ 0), so absorptance is effectively 1 − R. This high absorption arises from the intrinsic blackbody-like behavior of graphitic carbon, enhanced by surface roughness-induced light trapping and reduced Fresnel reflection due to the low-refractive-index fluorosilane layer.

In operation, the membrane is placed floating on a water source—freshwater, seawater, or wastewater—and exposed to solar irradiation at standard intensity (100 mW cm⁻², AM 1.5G spectrum). Incident photons are absorbed within the top few hundred micrometers of the superhydrophobic surface, generating heat that is confined to the air–water interface due to the combined effects of surface repellency and thermal insulation from the composite layer. Water is drawn upward through the capillary channels to replenish the evaporation front, but cannot penetrate the superhydrophobic chamber due to the high Laplace pressure barrier imposed by the fluorinated surface. As evaporation proceeds, dissolved salts or contaminants are rejected and either expelled outward with the vapor flow or remain in the bulk reservoir, preventing accumulation on the active surface. Continuous testing under one-sun illumination shows stable evaporation rates exceeding 1.5 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ for over 100 hours in 20 wt% NaCl solution, with no observable decline in performance—demonstrating exceptional resistance to salt fouling.

The invention’s scalability and practicality are further underscored by its use of commercially available, low-cost materials and straightforward processing techniques that do not require vacuum systems, cleanrooms, or nanofabrication tools. All steps—machining, immersion, coating, drying, and curing—can be performed in standard laboratory or industrial settings, enabling mass production of square-meter-scale membranes. Moreover, the all-carbon composition ensures long-term environmental stability, with no risk of polymer degradation, metal corrosion, or toxic leaching.

In summary, the detailed embodiment of the invention represents a holistic solution to the multifaceted challenges of solar evaporation, merging surface science, materials engineering, and thermal physics into a single, high-performance device. By rationally designing both the chemical and structural attributes of a graphitic foam platform, the invention achieves unprecedented efficiency, durability, and simplicity—key prerequisites for real-world deployment in addressing global water security.
