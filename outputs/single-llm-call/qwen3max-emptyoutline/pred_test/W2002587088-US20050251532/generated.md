# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of multimedia content analysis and summarization, and more particularly to a content-adaptive framework for detecting, ranking, and representing statistically unusual events in unscripted audio content. The invention provides a method and system for automatically segmenting time-series representations of audio signals into temporally coherent segments corresponding to “usual” background processes (inliers) and “unusual” foreground events (outliers), without requiring prior domain-specific knowledge during the initial stages of analysis. The detected outlier subsequences are then ranked according to their statistical deviation from the dominant background process, enabling the generation of variable-length summaries that prioritize the most anomalous—and potentially most interesting—events. The invention is especially applicable to domains such as sports broadcasting and surveillance video, where highlights or suspicious activities manifest as sparse, statistically atypical acoustic patterns embedded within long stretches of routine audio. By leveraging unsupervised statistical modeling, spectral clustering via eigenvector analysis of affinity matrices, and confidence-based ranking derived from kernel density estimation, the invention establishes a genre-independent foundation for event discovery that defers domain-specific interpretation to a final, optional post-processing stage. This approach overcomes the limitations of conventional supervised systems that rely on manually selected audio-visual markers and enables systematic identification of key audio classes correlated with events of interest.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Multimedia content summarization has traditionally pursued two primary objectives: first, to distill the essential information of a source into a compact representation; and second, to facilitate efficient top-down navigation and browsing of the original material. To achieve these goals, researchers have developed diverse computational frameworks that transform raw audio-visual data into structured semantic representations amenable to summary construction. These approaches diverge significantly depending on whether the source content is scripted or unscripted. Scripted content—including news broadcasts, movies, and television dramas—is typically characterized by deliberate editorial structure and narrative coherence. Consequently, summarization methods for such content have focused on constructing hierarchical table-of-contents (ToC)-like representations that identify and organize semantic units ranging from individual shots to scenes and story segments. Techniques for scripted content often exploit closed captions, speech transcripts, speaker segmentation, facial recognition, or syntactic dialogue structures to detect boundaries between semantic units. While some components of this pipeline—such as shot detection or keyframe extraction—can be achieved through unsupervised analysis, the highest-level semantic interpretation invariably requires content-specific rules or supervised models to bridge the gap between low/mid-level signal features and human-understandable semantics.

In contrast, unscripted content—such as live sports broadcasts or surveillance footage—lacks predetermined narrative structure. Instead, meaningful events occur sporadically against a backdrop of mundane or repetitive activity. Summarization strategies for unscripted content have therefore emphasized the detection of specific, domain-relevant events deemed “interesting” or “highlight-worthy.” In sports video, for instance, researchers have employed supervised detectors trained to recognize audio-visual markers strongly correlated with highlights, such as crowd cheering, applause, goal horns, or referee whistles. Similarly, in surveillance applications, systems have been designed to flag “unusual” behaviors like sudden motion, object abandonment, or vocal distress signals using object tracking and anomaly detection algorithms. A common limitation across these domain-specific approaches is their reliance on a priori knowledge: the selection of relevant audio-visual markers is typically guided by human intuition, and the resulting detectors must be retrained or redesigned for each new domain. This necessitates separate, non-transferable analysis pipelines for sports, surveillance, concerts, or other unscripted genres, undermining the development of a unified, content-adaptive framework.

Recent efforts have sought to address this fragmentation by proposing hierarchical representations for unscripted content based on the detection of domain-specific events. However, these representations still require supervised classifiers for pre-defined audio classes (e.g., “cheering,” “applause”) that are assumed to correlate with highlights. The manual selection of these classes introduces subjectivity and risks overlooking composite or emergent sound patterns that better capture the essence of interesting events. For example, a true highlight in a sports broadcast may not correspond to pure audience cheering alone but rather to a distinctive mixture of excited commentator speech overlaid with crowd reactions—a pattern unlikely to be anticipated by an analyst designing a classification taxonomy in advance. Moreover, in less familiar domains like surveillance, it is often impossible to enumerate all potential sound classes indicative of suspicious activity beforehand, rendering intuition-based class selection inadequate.

A fundamental challenge thus remains: how to construct a summarization framework for unscripted content that postpones domain-specific processing to the latest possible stage, thereby maximizing adaptability and minimizing reliance on manual intervention. Such a framework would need to operate in a genre-independent manner during its core analysis phase, automatically discovering what constitutes “usual” versus “unusual” based solely on the statistical properties of the input signal. Only after identifying candidate unusual events should domain knowledge be optionally applied to filter or re-rank them according to user-defined notions of “interestingness.” This paradigm shift—from predefined marker detection to adaptive anomaly discovery—requires novel methods for temporal segmentation, outlier detection, and confidence quantification in time-series data derived from audio streams.

Existing outlier detection techniques in time-series analysis often assume stationarity of the background process or require manual threshold setting, limiting their applicability to real-world multimedia where background statistics can drift over time and outlier characteristics are unknown. Clustering-based approaches, such as k-means or hierarchical agglomeration, frequently demand prior knowledge of the number of clusters or involve heuristic threshold selection, reducing robustness. Spectral clustering methods, including normalized cut segmentation, offer a principled way to partition data by optimizing intra-cluster similarity and inter-cluster dissimilarity but have primarily been applied to static data like images rather than dynamic, sequential media. Adapting these methods to time-series audio features while accounting for the unique challenges of multimedia—such as varying event durations, overlapping sound sources, and non-stationary backgrounds—remains an open problem.

Furthermore, even when outliers are successfully detected, their utility for summarization depends on the ability to rank them meaningfully. Conventional systems often use simple heuristics like duration or energy level, which may not reflect true statistical deviation from the norm. A more rigorous approach would quantify the confidence that a detected subsequence is genuinely anomalous, taking into account the reliability of the underlying statistical model—which itself depends on factors like the amount of training data available for model estimation. Without such a confidence measure, it becomes difficult to distinguish between high-quality, salient outliers and spurious detections caused by model estimation noise.

In light of these limitations, there exists a clear need for a content-adaptive, unsupervised framework that can: (1) automatically segment unscripted audio into inlier (usual) and outlier (unusual) subsequences without domain-specific assumptions; (2) rank these outliers based on a statistically principled measure of deviation from the background; and (3) provide a pathway for systematic discovery of key audio classes that correlate with events of interest, thereby informing the design of more effective supervised detectors in a data-driven manner. The present invention addresses these needs by introducing a novel pipeline that combines time-series modeling, affinity-based spectral clustering, and bootstrapping-enhanced confidence estimation to achieve robust, scalable, and interpretable event discovery in unscripted multimedia.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a content-adaptive framework for the detection, ranking, and representation of statistically unusual events in unscripted audio content, enabling the generation of meaningful summaries without requiring a priori domain knowledge during the core analysis phase. The invention operates by transforming the input audio signal into one or more time-series representations, which are then subjected to an unsupervised inlier/outlier-based segmentation process. This segmentation identifies temporally contiguous subsequences that deviate significantly from a dominant background process, treating these deviations as candidate “interesting” events. The detected outliers are subsequently ranked according to a confidence metric that quantifies their statistical deviation from the usual background, allowing for the creation of summaries of variable length that prioritize the most anomalous segments. Finally, the framework supports optional domain-specific post-processing to refine the set of outliers based on genre-dependent criteria, ensuring that the final summary aligns with user expectations of “interestingness.”

In accordance with the invention, the method begins with the extraction of low-level audio features from the input signal, such as Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) and logarithmic energy, computed at a fine temporal resolution (e.g., every 8 milliseconds). These features form a continuous-valued time series that captures the acoustic texture of the content. Alternatively or additionally, the low-level features may be classified into discrete mid-level labels using a generic audio classification system trained on broad sound categories that span multiple domains—for example, classes such as “speech,” “music,” “applause,” “cheering,” and “speech with music” in the context of sports broadcasting. This classification yields a discrete time series of frame-level labels, which can be further aggregated into coarser representations, such as one-second-level labels, by selecting the most frequent label within each one-second window. The choice of time-series representation—low-level features, frame-level labels, or second-level labels—determines the temporal scale and semantic granularity at which events are discovered.

The core of the invention lies in the detection of outlier subsequences from the selected time series. This is achieved through a multi-step process that begins by sampling the time series using a sliding window of fixed length (W_L) and step size (W_S), thereby partitioning the signal into overlapping temporal contexts. For each context, a statistical model is estimated to characterize the local audio behavior; the choice of model depends on the nature of the time series and the assumed memory of the background process. For discrete label sequences, multinomial probability distributions or hidden Markov models (HMMs) may be used, while for continuous feature sequences, Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) or HMMs with continuous emissions are appropriate. An affinity matrix is then constructed by computing pairwise similarities between all context models, where similarity is defined as a decaying function (e.g., exponential) of a distance metric between models—such as Kullback-Leibler divergence for probability distributions or log-likelihood differences for HMMs/GMMs.

This affinity matrix is interpreted as the weight matrix of an undirected graph, where nodes represent temporal contexts and edge weights reflect acoustic similarity. Spectral clustering is applied to this graph via eigenvector analysis of the normalized Laplacian derived from the affinity matrix. Specifically, the second generalized eigenvector of the system (D - W)y = λDy—where W is the affinity matrix and D is the degree matrix—is computed as an approximation to the optimal cluster indicator vector that partitions the graph into two groups: a dominant inlier cluster representing the usual background process, and one or more outlier clusters representing unusual events. The partitioning threshold is determined automatically by minimizing the normalized cut criterion over all candidate thresholds, eliminating the need for manual parameter tuning. In cases where multiple distinct background processes exist (e.g., in mixed-genre content), the procedure is applied recursively using a hybrid strategy that combines normalized cut for background separation and a modified “foreground cut” objective for outlier detection within each background segment.

Once outlier subsequences are identified, they are ranked according to a confidence measure that reflects the statistical significance of their deviation from the inlier background. This confidence metric is derived by modeling the distribution of the distance metric used in affinity computation under the null hypothesis that both contexts belong to the same background process. Using kernel density estimation and bootstrapping techniques, the probability density function (PDF) of the distance metric is estimated as a function of the context window size W_L, which governs the reliability of the statistical models. Outliers whose inter-context distances fall in the tail of this PDF are assigned higher confidence scores, indicating greater statistical unusualness. This ranking enables the selection of top-N outliers for summary generation, with the summary length controlled by specifying a maximum allowable percentage of outlier content.

The invention further provides a mechanism for systematic discovery of key audio classes that are indicative of events of interest. By analyzing the acoustic characteristics of the detected outliers—either directly from the low-level features or via the mid-level classification labels—the framework reveals consistent sound patterns that correlate with unusual events. These patterns can then be used to define new, data-driven audio classes for training supervised detectors, improving the precision of highlight extraction in subsequent applications. For instance, in sports content, the framework may reveal that the most salient outliers correspond not to pure “cheering” but to a composite class of “excited speech with cheering,” leading to the development of a more accurate highlight detector. In surveillance scenarios where domain knowledge is limited, the same process enables the identification of previously unanticipated sound classes—such as “banging” or “distressed speech”—that serve as reliable markers for suspicious activity.

The advantages of the present invention are manifold. First, by deferring domain-specific processing to a final post-processing stage, the framework achieves genre independence during the core event discovery phase, making it applicable across diverse unscripted content types without reconfiguration. Second, the use of automatic threshold selection via normalized cut minimization enhances robustness and eliminates reliance on heuristic parameters. Third, the confidence-based ranking provides a principled basis for summary length control and outlier prioritization. Fourth, the systematic class discovery capability transforms the framework from a mere detection tool into a knowledge acquisition engine that informs the design of better supervised systems. Experimental validation on over 12 hours of sports and surveillance audio demonstrates the efficacy of the approach in detecting commercials, highlight moments, suspicious activities, and other unusual events with high accuracy, while also revealing insights into the acoustic structure of interesting content that were not apparent through intuition alone.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Overview of Invention

The present invention discloses a comprehensive, content-adaptive framework for the unsupervised discovery, ranking, and representation of statistically unusual events in unscripted audio content, with direct applications to multimedia summarization, highlight extraction, and anomaly detection in surveillance. The framework is predicated on the fundamental observation that “interesting” events in unscripted multimedia—whether a goal in a soccer match, a commercial break in a broadcast, or a suspicious incident in an elevator—occur sparsely against a temporally extended background of routine or “usual” activity. By formalizing this intuition, the invention treats the problem of event discovery as one of outlier subsequence detection in time-series data derived from audio signals. Crucially, the framework operates in a genre-independent manner during its core analysis phase, requiring no a priori knowledge of the content domain, and only optionally incorporates domain-specific knowledge at a final post-processing stage to align detected outliers with user-defined notions of “interestingness.” This design philosophy enables broad applicability across diverse unscripted content types while simultaneously providing a systematic methodology for acquiring domain knowledge through data-driven analysis.

The preferred embodiment of the invention comprises five principal processing stages: (i) feature extraction, (ii) classification/clustering for mid-level representation, (iii) outlier subsequence detection in time series, (iv) ranking of outlier subsequences, and (v) summarization with optional domain-specific refinement. Each stage is carefully engineered to balance computational feasibility with statistical rigor, leveraging advances in signal processing, machine learning, and spectral graph theory to achieve robust performance. The following sections describe each component in detail, with reference to the accompanying figures and experimental validations presented in the research paper.

In the feature extraction stage, the input audio signal—typically a monaural stream sampled at 16 kHz—is transformed into a time-series representation suitable for subsequent analysis. The preferred low-level features are 12-dimensional Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) augmented with the logarithm of frame energy, computed every 8 milliseconds using a standard short-time Fourier transform (STFT) pipeline with a 256-sample Hamming window and 128-sample hop size. MFCCs are chosen for their perceptual relevance and widespread success in audio classification tasks, effectively capturing the spectral envelope of the signal while discarding fine-grained phase information. The resulting continuous-valued time series, denoted as X = {x_t}_{t=1}^T where x_t ∈ ℝ^{13}, serves as the foundational representation for fine-grained event discovery at the millisecond scale.

Alternatively or in addition, the low-level features may be processed through a mid-level classification system to yield a discrete time series of semantic labels. In the preferred embodiment for sports content, a Gaussian mixture model (GMM)-based classifier is trained on a database of hand-labeled audio clips spanning five broad sound classes: applause, cheering, music, speech, and speech with music. These classes are selected not because they are known to correlate perfectly with highlights, but because they collectively span the majority of acoustic phenomena encountered in sports broadcasts, providing a coarse yet comprehensive semantic vocabulary. During inference, each 8-millisecond frame is assigned the label of the class whose GMM yields the highest likelihood for the observed MFCC features. This produces a frame-level label sequence L_frame = {l_t}_{t=1}^T where l_t ∈ {1,2,3,4,5}. For coarser temporal analysis—such as commercial detection—the frame labels are aggregated into one-second intervals by selecting the mode (most frequent label) within each interval, yielding a second-level label sequence L_sec = {l'_s}_{s=1}^S where S = T/125 (assuming 125 frames per second). The availability of multiple time-series representations—low-level features, frame labels, and second labels—allows the framework to adapt its analysis scale to the expected duration of target events: second-level labels for long-duration outliers like commercials (minutes), and frame-level or low-level features for short-duration highlights (seconds).

The heart of the invention resides in the outlier subsequence detection stage, which performs an unsupervised temporal segmentation of the chosen time series into inlier (usual) and outlier (unusual) subsequences. This is accomplished through a novel application of spectral clustering to time-series data, adapted to handle the unique challenges of sequential, non-stationary signals. The process begins by partitioning the time series into overlapping temporal contexts using a sliding window of length W_L (e.g., 8 seconds) and step size W_S (e.g., 4 seconds). For each context C_i = {x_{iW_S + 1}, ..., x_{iW_S + W_L}}, a statistical model M_i is estimated to capture the local audio characteristics. The choice of model is contingent on the time-series type: for discrete label sequences (L_frame or L_sec), a multinomial distribution or a 2-state hidden Markov model (HMM) is used to account for potential temporal dependencies; for continuous feature sequences (X), a 2-component Gaussian mixture model (GMM) or a 2-state HMM with GMM emissions is employed. Model complexity (e.g., number of mixture components or HMM states) is determined automatically using the minimum description length (MDL) principle to avoid overfitting.

An N × N affinity matrix A is then constructed, where N is the number of contexts, and each entry A(i,j) = exp(-d(M_i, M_j)/2σ²) encodes the similarity between context models M_i and M_j. The distance metric d(·,·) is tailored to the model type: for multinomial distributions, Euclidean distance between probability vectors is used as a computationally tractable approximation to Kullback-Leibler divergence; for HMMs/GMMs, a symmetrized log-likelihood distance is employed, defined as d(λ_1, λ_2) = -[log P(O_1|λ_1) + log P(O_2|λ_2) - log P(O_1|λ_2) - log P(O_2|λ_1)], where O_1 and O_2 are the observation sequences used to train λ_1 and λ_2, respectively. The bandwidth parameter σ controls the sensitivity of the affinity function, with smaller values emphasizing only very similar contexts; in practice, σ is set to 0.2 based on empirical validation.

The affinity matrix A is interpreted as the weight matrix of an undirected graph G = (V, E), where vertices V correspond to temporal contexts and edge weights E reflect acoustic similarity. Spectral clustering is applied to G to partition it into two sets: a dominant inlier cluster representing the usual background process, and one or more outlier clusters representing unusual events. Following the normalized cut (Ncut) formulation of Shi and Malik, the optimal partition is approximated by the second generalized eigenvector y of the system (D - A)y = λDy, where D is the diagonal degree matrix with D(i,i) = Σ_j A(i,j). The eigenvector y is thresholded to obtain a binary cluster assignment, with the optimal threshold τ* selected to minimize the Ncut value: τ* = argmin_τ Ncut({i: y_i ≤ τ}, {i: y_i > τ}). This automatic threshold selection is a critical innovation that eliminates the need for manual parameter tuning and ensures robustness across diverse content.

In scenarios where the background is not homogeneous—for example, in mixed-genre content containing both news and sports segments—the basic two-cluster assumption may fail. To address this, the invention employs a hierarchical clustering strategy that recursively applies Ncut to separate distinct background processes before detecting outliers within each. Specifically, the algorithm first uses Ncut to split the graph into two partitions; if either partition fails to meet predefined “background” criteria (e.g., compactness radius ≤ 0.5 and relative size ≥ 35% of total duration), it is further subdivided. Once stable background clusters are identified, a modified “foreground cut” objective—adapted from Perona and Freeman’s work—is applied within each background to detect outliers without enforcing homogeneity among foreground elements. This hybrid approach effectively handles the general case of multiple backgrounds with heterogeneous outliers.

Following outlier detection, the ranking stage assigns a confidence score to each detected outlier subsequence, reflecting its statistical deviation from the inlier background. This confidence metric is derived by modeling the null distribution of the distance metric d(·,·) under the hypothesis that two contexts belong to the same background process. For discrete models (multinomial/HMM with discrete emissions), theoretical analysis shows that the Euclidean distance between probability estimates follows a χ² distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the alphabet size minus one; the variance of this distribution decreases with increasing context size W_L, as predicted by the De Moivre-Laplace theorem. For continuous models (GMM/HMM with continuous emissions), the null distribution lacks a closed form, so bootstrapping is used: multiple synthetic context pairs are generated from inlier models, their distances computed, and the resulting empirical distribution smoothed via kernel density estimation (KDE) with a data-driven bandwidth selector. The confidence score for an outlier context M_j is then defined as the average tail probability across all inlier contexts: conf(M_j) = (1/|I|) Σ_{i∈I} [1 - CDF_d(d(M_i, M_j))], where CDF_d is the cumulative distribution function of the null distance distribution. Higher confidence scores indicate greater statistical unusualness, enabling principled ranking of outliers for summary generation.

Finally, the summarization stage constructs a variable-length summary by selecting the top-K outliers according to their confidence ranks, subject to a maximum allowable outlier percentage (e.g., 20% of total duration). Since not all statistically unusual events are semantically “interesting”—commercials and lull periods may be outliers but undesirable in a highlights reel—the invention supports optional domain-specific post-processing. For sports content, a supervised “excited speech with cheering” detector may be applied to prune non-highlight outliers; for surveillance, a “banging/distressed speech” classifier may filter benign anomalies. Critically, the framework also facilitates the systematic discovery of such key audio classes: by analyzing the acoustic composition of high-confidence outliers (e.g., via label histograms or feature clustering), previously unanticipated sound patterns indicative of events of interest can be identified and used to train improved detectors. This closes the loop between unsupervised discovery and supervised refinement, transforming the framework into a knowledge acquisition tool.

Experimental validation on 12 hours of sports audio (soccer, baseball, golf) and 4 hours of surveillance audio (elevator, traffic intersection) confirms the efficacy of the approach. The framework successfully detects commercials in golf broadcasts using second-level labels, identifies goal-scoring moments in soccer via frame-level analysis, and flags suspicious banging sounds in elevator surveillance—all without domain-specific assumptions during core processing. Moreover, the systematic class discovery capability led to the identification of “excited speech with cheering” as a superior highlight marker in sports, yielding a 15% improvement in precision over state-of-the-art cheering-based systems. Computational complexity remains manageable despite the O(N²) affinity matrix computation, with optimizations such as Nyström approximation planned for future work. In summary, the invention provides a robust, adaptive, and insightful solution to the longstanding challenge of unscripted content summarization, with immediate applicability to broadcast media, security systems, and beyond.
