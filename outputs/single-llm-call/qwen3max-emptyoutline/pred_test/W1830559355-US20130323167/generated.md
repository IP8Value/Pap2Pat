# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of oncology and, more specifically, in the domain of targeted cancer therapeutics that exploit the unique vascular characteristics of solid tumors to enhance the selective delivery and retention of therapeutic and diagnostic agents. The invention provides a novel method for significantly improving the tumor-specific accumulation of macromolecular drug formulations—including but not limited to liposomal nanoparticles, radiolabeled antibodies, and other high-molecular-weight therapeutic constructs—by co-administering such agents with vascular-active compounds that induce a transient, localized enhancement of vascular permeability within tumor tissue. This approach, herein termed Enhanced Enhanced Permeability and Retention (E2PR), represents a pharmacologically amplified extension of the classical Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect, which has long been recognized as a passive targeting mechanism enabling preferential accumulation of nanoparticles in solid tumors due to their structurally abnormal vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage. The E2PR phenomenon described herein is induced through the systemic or localized administration of pro-inflammatory cytokines, bacterial agents, or microtubule-interacting vascular disrupting agents, all of which provoke a controlled inflammatory or vascular destabilizing response selectively within the tumor microenvironment. The invention is particularly applicable to the treatment of solid tumors that are poorly responsive to conventional chemotherapy due to insufficient intratumoral drug concentrations, including but not limited to glioblastoma multiforme, colorectal adenocarcinoma, and other malignancies characterized by dense stromal barriers or compromised vascular access. Furthermore, the invention encompasses both therapeutic and diagnostic applications, as the same principles that enhance drug delivery also improve the tumor-to-background ratio of imaging agents, thereby facilitating more accurate tumor detection, staging, and monitoring of therapeutic response. The technical field thus spans pharmaceutical formulation science, vascular biology, immuno-oncology, nanomedicine, and molecular imaging, with direct implications for clinical oncology practice and the development of next-generation combination therapies that maximize efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and despite significant advances in surgical techniques, radiation therapy, and systemic treatments, many solid tumors continue to pose formidable therapeutic challenges. A central limitation of conventional chemotherapy is its lack of selectivity: cytotoxic agents distribute broadly throughout the body, damaging healthy tissues and causing dose-limiting toxicities that prevent the administration of sufficiently high drug concentrations to eradicate all malignant cells within a tumor. Over the past several decades, considerable effort has been directed toward developing strategies to improve the therapeutic index of anticancer agents by enhancing their selective delivery to tumor sites while sparing normal tissues. Among these strategies, the exploitation of the Enhanced Permeability and Retention (EPR) effect has emerged as a cornerstone of modern nanomedicine. First described by Maeda and colleagues in the 1980s, the EPR effect refers to the phenomenon whereby macromolecules and nanoparticles of appropriate size (typically between 10 and 200 nanometers in diameter) preferentially accumulate in solid tumors due to the structural and functional abnormalities of tumor-associated blood vessels. Unlike normal vasculature, which features tightly apposed endothelial cells connected by continuous tight junctions and supported by a well-organized basement membrane and pericyte coverage, tumor vasculature is often disorganized, hyperpermeable, and deficient in functional lymphatic drainage. These pathological features create a “leaky” vascular network that allows circulating nanoparticles to extravasate into the tumor interstitium, where they are retained for prolonged periods due to the absence of efficient lymphatic clearance mechanisms.

The clinical validation of the EPR concept is exemplified by the approval of Doxil, a sterically stabilized liposomal formulation of doxorubicin, which demonstrates improved pharmacokinetics and reduced cardiotoxicity compared to free doxorubicin, along with enhanced antitumor activity in certain malignancies such as ovarian cancer and Kaposi’s sarcoma. Numerous other nanoparticle-based therapeutics have since entered clinical development, leveraging the EPR effect to deliver chemotherapeutics, nucleic acids, proteins, and radionuclides to tumor sites. However, the clinical translation of EPR-based strategies has been met with variable success, and it is now widely recognized that the magnitude of the EPR effect can differ substantially between tumor types, individual patients, and even within different regions of the same tumor. Factors such as high interstitial fluid pressure, dense extracellular matrix, heterogeneous vascular perfusion, and variable expression of vascular permeability mediators can all limit the extent of nanoparticle accumulation, thereby constraining therapeutic efficacy. Consequently, there has been growing interest in developing methods to actively modulate the tumor microenvironment to amplify the EPR effect and achieve more robust and predictable drug delivery.

One potential avenue for enhancing vascular permeability in tumors stems from observations in wound healing and infection biology. It is well established that acute inflammation—whether triggered by infection, trauma, or immune activation—leads to a rapid and dramatic increase in vascular permeability at the site of injury. This physiological response, mediated by a cascade of inflammatory mediators including histamine, bradykinin, prostaglandins, and cytokines, serves to facilitate the recruitment of immune cells, antibodies, and plasma proteins to the affected area. Notably, tumors have long been described as “wounds that do not heal,” a metaphor coined by Harold Dvorak to highlight the shared features between tumorigenesis and chronic wound repair, including persistent inflammation, angiogenesis, and extracellular matrix remodeling. Given this conceptual parallel, it is reasonable to hypothesize that inducing an acute inflammatory response specifically within the tumor microenvironment could transiently exacerbate the inherent vascular leakiness of tumors, thereby potentiating the EPR effect.

Support for this hypothesis comes from early studies involving bacterial cancer therapy. Certain anaerobic bacteria, such as Clostridium novyi-NT, have been shown to selectively colonize the hypoxic regions of solid tumors following systemic administration, where they proliferate and induce localized necrosis. In the course of investigating the interaction between C. novyi-NT and host immune responses, researchers observed unexpectedly high accumulation of systemically administered antibodies—not only those specific to bacterial antigens but also non-specific immunoglobulins—in infected tumors. This finding suggested that the bacterial infection was triggering a generalized increase in vascular permeability, independent of antigen-antibody binding. Similarly, the pro-inflammatory cytokine Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) has long been known to induce vascular leakage and hemorrhagic necrosis in tumors when administered systemically, although its clinical use has been limited by severe systemic toxicity at effective doses. Nevertheless, preclinical studies have demonstrated that TNF-α can dramatically alter tumor hemodynamics and enhance the delivery of co-administered agents.

Other classes of vascular-modulating agents have also been explored. Microtubule-interacting compounds such as combretastatin A4 phosphate (CA4P) and vinorelbine are known to disrupt the cytoskeleton of proliferating endothelial cells, leading to rapid vascular shutdown in tumors—a mechanism distinct from permeability enhancement but nonetheless capable of altering drug distribution. Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), a key mediator of angiogenesis, has also been reported to increase vascular permeability, though its effects are complex and context-dependent. Despite these diverse approaches, no unified strategy has yet been developed to reliably and safely amplify nanoparticle delivery across a broad spectrum of tumor types.

A critical unmet need therefore exists for a method that can consistently and selectively enhance the intratumoral concentration of therapeutic and diagnostic macromolecules without exacerbating systemic toxicity. Such a method would ideally be compatible with existing nanoparticle platforms, applicable to multiple tumor types—including those with poor baseline EPR such as glioblastoma—and translatable to human patients using clinically relevant dosing regimens. The present invention addresses this need by providing a novel pharmacological paradigm—E2PR—that leverages the controlled induction of tumor-selective vascular permeability to dramatically improve the tumor-to-blood and tumor-to-normal-tissue ratios of co-administered macromolecular agents, thereby enabling more effective and safer cancer therapy.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for significantly enhancing the selective accumulation and retention of macromolecular therapeutic or diagnostic agents in solid tumors by co-administering said agents with a vascular-active compound that induces a transient, tumor-localized increase in vascular permeability. This method, designated Enhanced Enhanced Permeability and Retention (E2PR), represents a deliberate pharmacological amplification of the classical EPR effect and results in markedly improved tumor-to-background ratios of the administered macromolecules, leading to superior therapeutic outcomes and diagnostic sensitivity. The vascular-active compounds suitable for inducing E2PR include, but are not limited to, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α), attenuated bacterial strains capable of selective tumor colonization such as Clostridium novyi-NT, and microtubule-interacting vascular disrupting agents such as combretastatin A4 phosphate (CA4P) and vinorelbine. The macromolecular agents that benefit from E2PR encompass a broad range of constructs, including sterically stabilized liposomes (SSLs), radiolabeled antibodies, polymer-drug conjugates, protein therapeutics, and other nanoparticles with hydrodynamic diameters typically ranging from approximately 10 nanometers to over 1 micrometer. The invention is predicated on the discovery that the administration of a vascular-active compound creates a window of enhanced vascular leakiness specifically within the tumor microenvironment, during which co-administered macromolecules extravasate more efficiently and are retained in greater quantities than would occur under baseline EPR conditions. Critically, this enhancement is selective for tumor tissue, with minimal impact on vascular permeability in normal organs, thereby preserving the therapeutic index of the co-administered agent.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a method for treating a solid tumor in a mammalian subject comprising administering to said subject a therapeutically effective amount of a macromolecular drug formulation in combination with a vascular-active compound that induces E2PR. The macromolecular drug formulation may contain a chemotherapeutic agent such as doxorubicin encapsulated within SSLs (e.g., Doxil), a radionuclide such as iodine-131 conjugated to an antibody or entrapped within liposomes, or any other pharmacologically active molecule suitable for macromolecular delivery. The vascular-active compound is administered in an amount sufficient to induce E2PR without causing unacceptable systemic toxicity, and the timing of administration relative to the macromolecular agent is optimized to maximize tumor accumulation—typically, the vascular-active compound is administered concurrently with or up to 12 hours after the macromolecular agent, but not more than 6 hours prior thereto. In another embodiment, the invention provides a method for imaging a solid tumor comprising administering to a subject a diagnostically effective amount of a radiolabeled macromolecular imaging agent in combination with a vascular-active compound, followed by detection of the accumulated radioactivity using imaging modalities such as SPECT/CT. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising a macromolecular agent and a vascular-active compound formulated for co-administration, as well as kits containing separate components for such combined therapy. The method is particularly effective against tumors that are traditionally difficult to treat due to poor drug penetration, including glioblastoma multiforme, where the invention has been shown to overcome the blood-brain barrier and achieve significant therapeutic benefit. By leveraging the E2PR effect, the present invention enables the use of lower doses of cytotoxic agents to achieve complete tumor regression and long-term cures in preclinical models, thereby offering a promising strategy to improve the efficacy and safety of cancer therapy in clinical practice.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is based on the unexpected and advantageous discovery that the intratumoral accumulation of macromolecular therapeutic and diagnostic agents can be dramatically enhanced through the co-administration of vascular-active compounds that induce a controlled, tumor-selective increase in vascular permeability. This phenomenon, termed Enhanced Enhanced Permeability and Retention (E2PR), extends the classical EPR effect by pharmacologically amplifying the inherent vascular abnormalities of solid tumors, resulting in significantly higher tumor-to-blood and tumor-to-normal-tissue ratios of the administered macromolecules. The following detailed description sets forth the various aspects, embodiments, and experimental validations of the invention, illustrating its broad applicability across different tumor models, macromolecular platforms, and vascular-active agents.

### Example 1

In a foundational experiment demonstrating the E2PR effect, subcutaneous CT26 murine colorectal adenocarcinoma tumors were established in BALB/c mice, and the animals were administered either spores of the attenuated anaerobic bacterium Clostridium novyi-NT or a control vehicle via intravenous injection. Concurrently, the mice received a radiolabeled monoclonal antibody specific to liposomase, an enzyme secreted by C. novyi-NT within the hypoxic regions of infected tumors. As anticipated, the anti-liposomase antibody showed marked enrichment in C. novyi-NT-infected tumors, consistent with antigen-specific binding. However, a surprising and pivotal observation was that a radiolabeled non-specific IgG control antibody also accumulated to a significant extent in the infected tumors, albeit at lower levels than the antigen-specific antibody. This indicated that the bacterial infection was inducing a generalized increase in vascular permeability that facilitated the extravasation and retention of circulating immunoglobulins irrespective of their antigen specificity. To confirm that this effect was not limited to a particular antibody isotype or target, a second experiment was conducted using an anti-CD20 antibody, which recognizes a B-cell surface antigen not expressed by CT26 tumor cells. When administered systemically in conjunction with C. novyi-NT spores, the anti-CD20 antibody similarly exhibited enhanced accumulation in the tumors, further supporting the hypothesis that the bacterial infection was creating a non-specific, permeability-enhancing environment within the tumor microvasculature. Biodistribution analyses performed 48 hours post-injection revealed that the radioactivity levels in infected tumors were approximately four-fold higher than those in most normal tissues, including liver, spleen, kidneys, and muscle, thereby demonstrating the tumor selectivity of the observed effect. These findings established that microbial-induced inflammation within tumors could serve as a potent trigger for E2PR, opening the door to the use of other inflammatory stimuli to achieve similar outcomes without the complexities associated with live bacterial therapy.

### Example 2

Building upon the observations with C. novyi-NT, the inventors sought to identify a defined molecular mediator capable of recapitulating the E2PR effect in a more controllable and clinically translatable manner. Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) emerged as a prime candidate due to its well-documented role as a key serum factor responsible for endotoxin-induced vascular permeabilization and its ability to induce hemorrhagic necrosis in tumors upon systemic administration. In a series of experiments, CT26 tumor-bearing mice were injected intravenously with murine TNF-α followed immediately by a radiolabeled murine IgG. Biodistribution studies showed that TNF-α treatment resulted in significant IgG accumulation specifically within the tumors, with minimal uptake in normal tissues. Time-course analyses revealed that the peak tumor accumulation of IgG occurred between 72 and 96 hours post-injection, indicating a relatively slow but sustained permeability response. To evaluate whether the E2PR effect extended to nanoparticle platforms beyond antibodies, sterically stabilized liposomes (SSLs) of approximately 100 nanometers in diameter were prepared and labeled with iodine-125 using a novel chemical entrapment strategy. Specifically, the liposomes were loaded with arginine at low pH, and then incubated with Bolton-Hunter reagent pre-labeled with 125I. The reagent diffused across the lipid bilayer and covalently bound to the encapsulated arginine, becoming trapped within the liposome due to the resulting positive charge. This method achieved high specific activity while minimizing radiation exposure during preparation. When these 125I-labeled SSLs were administered in combination with TNF-α, a striking enhancement in tumor-selective retention was observed, with tumor-to-blood radioactivity ratios reaching as high as 22-fold—substantially exceeding the ratios achieved with radiolabeled IgG under similar conditions. Imaging by SPECT/CT confirmed the biodistribution data and further revealed that the kinetics of SSL accumulation differed from that of IgG, peaking at 24 hours post-injection, which is 48 to 72 hours earlier than the IgG peak. Additional experiments demonstrated that the E2PR effect is particle size-dependent: while free 125I-labeled arginine showed no TNF-α-dependent tumor accumulation, larger particles such as 125I-labeled C. novyi-NT spores (~1 micrometer in diameter) exhibited significant tumor enrichment only when co-administered with TNF-α. This indicated that E2PR reflects a more profound vascular disruption than classical EPR, extending the effective size range for tumor accumulation from ~100 nm up to at least 1 micrometer. Moreover, the E2PR effect was found to be independent of tumor volume, as both small and large subcutaneous tumors in the same animal showed comparable retention of radiolabeled SSLs following TNF-α treatment. The temporal relationship between TNF-α and SSL administration was also optimized: maximal E2PR was achieved when TNF-α was given concurrently with or up to 12 hours after SSL injection, whereas administration of TNF-α 6 hours prior to SSLs failed to induce the effect, suggesting that the vascular window must be open at the time of macromolecule circulation. Finally, other vascular-active agents, including the microtubule disruptors combretastatin A4P and vinorelbine, were shown to induce E2PR, albeit less potently than TNF-α, thereby broadening the scope of compounds suitable for this approach.

### Example 3

To translate the E2PR phenomenon into therapeutic benefit, the inventors evaluated the antitumor efficacy of combining TNF-α with macromolecular drug formulations in the CT26 tumor model. Mice bearing fully established subcutaneous tumors were treated with a single intravenous dose of either Doxil (a clinically approved SSL formulation of doxorubicin) or a radiolabeled IgG conjugated to iodine-131 (chosen for its beta-emitting properties suitable for radiotherapy), administered alone or in combination with TNF-α. As expected, monotherapy with Doxil or 131I-IgG resulted in transient tumor growth delay and modest survival prolongation, but all tumors eventually recurred. In stark contrast, the combination of either agent with TNF-α led to complete tumor regression in all treated animals, with more than 75% achieving long-term cures (>60 days survival with no detectable tumor). Dose-response studies indicated that the curative effect was dependent on the TNF-α dose, as a lower dose (25 μg/kg) failed to produce cures despite extending survival, underscoring the importance of achieving a threshold level of vascular modulation. The therapeutic synergy was further confirmed using 131I-labeled SSLs prepared via the same chemical entrapment method as in Example 2; while the radiolabeled liposomes alone showed antitumor activity, complete regressions and cures were only observed when combined with TNF-α. Importantly, the TNF-α doses used in these studies were either at or below the maximum tolerated dose established in prior clinical trials, suggesting that the regimen could be feasible for human application. These results provided compelling evidence that E2PR not only enhances macromolecule delivery but also translates directly into superior therapeutic outcomes, enabling complete eradication of established tumors with a single treatment cycle—a feat rarely achieved with conventional nanomedicine approaches.

### Example 4

The inventability and clinical relevance of the E2PR approach were further validated in a highly aggressive and therapeutically challenging orthotopic model of glioblastoma multiforme (GBM). GL261 murine glioma cells were stereotactically implanted into the frontal lobe of C57BL/6 mice, where they formed invasive, necrotic tumors closely resembling human GBM in histopathology and lethality, with untreated animals succumbing to disease within approximately 30 days. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) poses a major obstacle to chemotherapy in GBM, severely limiting drug penetration into brain tumors. To test whether E2PR could overcome this barrier, mice with established intracranial tumors were administered 125I-labeled SSLs with or without concurrent TNF-α treatment. SPECT/CT imaging revealed that significant tumor accumulation of the radiolabeled liposomes occurred only in the TNF-α-treated group, demonstrating that TNF-α could transiently disrupt the BBB/BTB (blood-tumor barrier) and enable nanoparticle delivery to brain tumors. In a parallel therapeutic study, mice received a single intravenous dose of Doxil alone, TNF-α alone, or the combination. Neither monotherapy provided substantial survival benefit, with all animals dying by day 50. In contrast, the combination therapy markedly prolonged survival, with some animals living up to 103 days—more than three times longer than controls. Histological examination confirmed extensive tumor necrosis and reduced cellularity in the combination group, consistent with enhanced drug delivery and efficacy. These findings are particularly significant because they demonstrate that E2PR can be effectively applied to central nervous system malignancies, a class of tumors historically refractory to systemic therapy due to the BBB. The success in this stringent model underscores the potential of the invention to address unmet medical needs in oncology and supports its advancement toward clinical evaluation in patients with GBM and other hard-to-treat solid tumors.
