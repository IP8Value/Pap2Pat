# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The increasing integration of machine learning models into high-stakes decision-making systems—such as credit scoring, hiring, criminal justice risk assessment, healthcare diagnostics, and fraud detection—has raised significant concerns about algorithmic fairness. These concerns stem from the potential for such models to perpetuate or even exacerbate historical and systemic biases present in training data, thereby leading to discriminatory outcomes against individuals or groups defined by protected attributes such as race, gender, nationality, or age. In response, regulatory frameworks in the United States and other jurisdictions have established legal doctrines to address discrimination, primarily through the concepts of *disparate treatment* and *disparate impact*. Disparate treatment refers to intentional discrimination based on protected attributes, whereas disparate impact addresses practices that, while facially neutral, result in disproportionately adverse effects on protected groups. Compliance with these doctrines is not only a legal imperative but also a critical component of responsible artificial intelligence deployment.

In the context of machine learning, adherence to disparate treatment is often achieved by excluding protected attributes from the model’s input features during training—a practice commonly referred to as “fairness through unawareness.” While this approach may satisfy formal compliance with disparate treatment, it fails to address the more insidious issue of disparate impact, which can arise due to correlations between non-protected features and protected attributes. For instance, in a fraud detection system, transaction patterns, device types, or IP geolocation may serve as proxies for nationality or ethnicity, leading to biased outcomes even when explicit demographic information is omitted. To mitigate disparate impact, researchers have proposed a range of algorithmic fairness criteria, including demographic parity, equal opportunity, and equalized odds. Demographic parity requires that the probability of a positive outcome (e.g., transaction approval) be equal across all protected groups. However, this criterion is often impractical in real-world applications where base rates of the target variable (e.g., actual fraud prevalence) differ significantly across groups. Enforcing demographic parity in such settings may lead to either excessive false positives (approving fraudulent transactions) or excessive false negatives (declining legitimate ones), both of which carry substantial operational and reputational costs.

Equalized odds offers a more nuanced alternative by requiring that both true positive rates (TPR) and false positive rates (FPR) be equal across protected groups. This criterion ensures that qualified individuals receive equal opportunity regardless of their group membership, while also preventing disproportionate false alarms. Despite its theoretical appeal, equalized odds presents several practical challenges in industrial settings. First, it assumes binary or low-cardinality protected attributes, which is inconsistent with real-world scenarios involving high-arity attributes such as country of origin, language, or currency—each of which may have dozens or hundreds of distinct values. Second, existing methods for achieving equalized odds typically require retraining or fine-tuning the model for each subgroup, a process that is computationally expensive, time-consuming, and incompatible with real-time inference pipelines. Third, many fairness interventions are applied during the training phase, limiting their applicability to post-hoc model auditing and correction, especially when the underlying model is proprietary, black-box, or already deployed in production. Finally, the strict equality constraints imposed by equalized odds may be overly rigid in dynamic environments where data distributions shift daily due to seasonal trends, adversarial behavior, or market changes, making it difficult to maintain stable fairness guarantees without frequent recalibration.

Moreover, businesses often face asymmetric costs associated with different types of prediction errors. In fraud detection, for example, a false negative (failing to flag a fraudulent transaction) may result in direct financial loss, while a false positive (blocking a legitimate transaction) may lead to customer dissatisfaction and churn. The relative importance of these errors varies depending on the product type (e.g., digital goods vs. physical goods), business model, and regulatory environment. Consequently, a one-size-fits-all fairness criterion that treats FPR and TPR equally may not align with organizational priorities. There is thus a need for a flexible, adaptive, and computationally efficient fairness framework that can accommodate multiple protected attributes, support user-defined trade-offs between error types, and operate in a post-hoc, model-agnostic manner without requiring access to raw training data or model internals.

Existing approaches to post-hoc fairness correction—such as threshold tuning, rejection sampling, or output perturbation—are often limited to binary sensitive attributes and do not scale gracefully to multi-attribute or high-cardinality settings. Furthermore, they rarely provide mechanisms to selectively enforce constraints on either FPR or TPR, nor do they offer convergence guarantees or robustness to statistical noise in performance estimates. The absence of a unified methodology that addresses these limitations has hindered the widespread adoption of algorithmic fairness in enterprise-grade machine learning systems. This patent application addresses these gaps by introducing a novel fairness measure—*Relaxed Equalized Odds*—and an accompanying *one-shot fairness heuristic* that enables efficient, scalable, and customizable post-hoc sanitization of classification models across single or multiple protected attributes, with support for high-arity categorical variables and real-time deployment constraints.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for achieving algorithmic fairness in machine learning-based decision systems, particularly in high-stakes applications such as fraud detection, credit scoring, and risk assessment. The invention introduces a novel fairness criterion termed *Relaxed Equalized Odds*, which generalizes the classical Equalized Odds definition to accommodate high-arity protected attributes and asymmetric business objectives. Unlike traditional Equalized Odds—which mandates strict equality of true positive rates (TPR) and false positive rates (FPR) across all subgroups—the Relaxed Equalized Odds criterion permits controlled deviation from exact equality by requiring that the TPR and FPR of each subgroup lie within a statistically reasonable range around the population mean, specifically within two standard deviations of the average across all subgroups. This relaxation preserves the core ethical principle of equal opportunity and balanced error rates while acknowledging the practical realities of real-world data distributions, where enforcing exact parity may be infeasible or counterproductive.

To operationalize this fairness measure, the invention further discloses a *one-shot fairness heuristic* that performs post-hoc calibration of a pre-trained classification model’s decision thresholds without requiring access to the model’s internal parameters or training data. The heuristic is model-agnostic, meaning it can be applied to any probabilistic classifier that outputs scores or confidence estimates, including but not limited to logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosting machines, and deep neural networks. The method operates by constructing a grid of candidate decision thresholds and, for each value in the grid, computing the resulting FPR and TPR for every subgroup defined by the protected attribute(s). Based on user-specified fairness priorities—such as prioritizing FPR alignment, TPR alignment, or both—the system selects a performance metric (e.g., F1-score, F0.5-score, or F2-score) to guide the optimization process. The heuristic then iteratively prunes threshold candidates that violate the Relaxed Equalized Odds constraints and selects, for each subgroup, the threshold that maximizes the chosen performance metric while satisfying the fairness bounds. This process converges in a single pass over the threshold grid, hence the term “one-shot,” and guarantees a solution if one exists within the discretized threshold space.

A key innovation of the disclosed method is its extensibility to multiple protected attributes. The invention provides two distinct strategies for multi-attribute fairness: a *strong fairness regime* that enforces Relaxed Equalized Odds across all joint combinations of attribute values (e.g., country × currency × age group), and a *weak fairness regime* that enforces the criterion independently for each attribute. The strong regime offers stricter fairness guarantees but incurs exponential computational complexity with the number of attributes; the weak regime scales linearly and is recommended for settings with more than ten protected attributes. To mitigate computational overhead, the system includes an *attribute pruning* step that identifies and removes redundant or highly dependent attributes using statistical tests such as the Chi-square test of independence. Additionally, in the strong regime, the invention employs a *subspace pruning* mechanism based on the Frequent Pattern Tree (FP-Tree) data structure to eliminate sparse subpopulations with insufficient data (e.g., fewer than 100 observations), ensuring reliable estimation of performance metrics and avoiding overfitting to noise.

The disclosed system is designed for real-world deployment in industrial environments. It requires only aggregate statistics—such as FPR, TPR, and selection metrics—computed over subgroups, enabling compatibility with privacy-preserving techniques like differential privacy. Because it operates solely on model outputs and protected attribute labels, it does not necessitate retraining, feature engineering, or access to raw input features, making it ideal for auditing and correcting legacy or third-party models. Empirical validation on public benchmarks (e.g., COMPAS recidivism, UCI Adult income) and proprietary fraud detection datasets demonstrates that the method achieves comparable or superior fairness-performance trade-offs relative to state-of-the-art baselines such as Equalized Odds and Calibrated Equalized Odds, while supporting high-cardinality attributes and multi-attribute fairness. The invention thus provides a practical, scalable, and legally defensible solution for mitigating disparate impact in automated decision systems, aligning technical performance with ethical and regulatory requirements.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a system and method for post-hoc fairness sanitization of machine learning models used in classification tasks, particularly in domains where decisions have significant consequences for individuals or groups, such as financial services, criminal justice, employment, and e-commerce. The core of the invention lies in a novel fairness definition—Relaxed Equalized Odds—and a computationally efficient, model-agnostic heuristic that enforces this definition through adaptive threshold calibration. The following detailed description outlines the components, algorithms, and operational workflows of the disclosed system, with reference to both theoretical foundations and practical implementation considerations.

The invention begins with the recognition that traditional fairness criteria, while mathematically elegant, are often ill-suited for real-world applications due to their rigidity, scalability limitations, and misalignment with business objectives. Equalized Odds, for instance, requires that for every protected group, the true positive rate (TPR = TP / (TP + FN)) and false positive rate (FPR = FP / (FP + TN)) match exactly those of every other group. In practice, this is rarely achievable without severe degradation in overall model performance, especially when base rates of the target variable differ across groups—as is common in fraud detection, where fraud prevalence varies significantly by country due to differences in legal enforcement, payment infrastructure, and consumer behavior. Moreover, enforcing exact equality becomes computationally intractable when the protected attribute has high cardinality (e.g., 200 countries), as it would require estimating and matching rates for each of the 200 subgroups simultaneously.

To address these issues, the invention proposes *Relaxed Equalized Odds*, defined as follows: Let \( D = \{d_1, d_2, \dots, d_K\} \) be a protected attribute with \( K \) distinct values (e.g., countries). Let \( \text{FPR}_i \) and \( \text{TPR}_i \) denote the false positive rate and true positive rate of the model on subgroup \( d_i \). The model is said to satisfy Relaxed Equalized Odds with respect to \( D \) if:

\[
|\text{FPR}_i - \mu_{\text{FPR}}| \leq 2\sigma_{\text{FPR}} \quad \text{and} \quad |\text{TPR}_i - \mu_{\text{TPR}}| \leq 2\sigma_{\text{TPR}}
\]

for all \( i \in \{1, \dots, K\} \), where \( \mu_{\text{FPR}} \) and \( \sigma_{\text{FPR}} \) are the mean and standard deviation of the FPRs across all \( K \) subgroups, and similarly for TPR. This definition allows natural variation in error rates while preventing extreme outliers, thereby balancing fairness with feasibility. The choice of two standard deviations corresponds to approximately 95% coverage under a normal distribution, providing a statistically principled yet flexible bound.

The enforcement of this criterion is achieved through a *Fairness Heuristic* that operates in four main stages: (1) constraint selection, (2) threshold grid initialization, (3) performance computation, and (4) iterative pruning and selection. In the first stage, the user specifies which fairness constraints to apply—FPR alignment, TPR alignment, or both—based on domain-specific cost structures. For example, in a digital goods marketplace where chargebacks are costly, the business may prioritize FPR alignment to minimize false approvals of fraudsters. Conversely, in a subscription service where customer acquisition is paramount, TPR alignment (equal opportunity) may be preferred to avoid unjustly rejecting legitimate users. This choice determines the *selection metric* used in optimization: F1-score (balanced), F0.5-score (FPR-weighted), or F2-score (TPR-weighted), where the Fβ-score is defined as \( (1 + \beta^2) \cdot \frac{\text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\beta^2 \cdot \text{precision} + \text{recall}} \).

In the second stage, a linear grid of candidate decision thresholds \( G_{\text{thresh}} = \{t_1, t_2, \dots, t_M\} \) is defined over the model’s output score range (e.g., [0.6, 0.9] in increments of 0.01). This grid discretizes the continuous threshold space, enabling exhaustive search without gradient-based optimization. In the third stage, for each threshold \( t \in G_{\text{thresh}} \) and each subgroup \( d_i \), the system computes the resulting confusion matrix and derives FPR, TPR, and the selection metric. This yields a performance table \( \mathcal{P} = \{ (\text{FPR}_{i,t}, \text{TPR}_{i,t}, \text{Metric}_{i,t}) \} \) for all \( i, t \).

The fourth stage is the core of the heuristic. It begins by computing the current mean and standard deviation of FPRs (and/or TPRs, depending on constraints) across subgroups at each threshold. Thresholds that cause any subgroup’s rate to fall outside the \( \mu \pm 2\sigma \) band are pruned. From the remaining candidates, the system selects, for each subgroup \( d_i \), the threshold \( t_i^* \) that maximizes the selection metric. This yields a calibrated model \( F_{\text{select}} \) with subgroup-specific thresholds. The system then verifies whether \( F_{\text{select}} \) satisfies the Relaxed Equalized Odds constraints. If not, the pruning and selection steps are repeated with the updated performance estimates, though in practice, convergence is typically achieved in one iteration due to the monotonic nature of the pruning.

Critically, this heuristic is *one-shot* in the sense that it does not require separate model sanitizations per subgroup. Instead, it jointly optimizes thresholds across all subgroups in a single pass, drastically reducing computational overhead. It is also *model-agnostic* and *post-hoc*: it only requires the model’s predicted scores and true labels grouped by protected attribute, making it applicable to any black-box classifier. Furthermore, because it uses only aggregate statistics, it can be combined with differential privacy by adding calibrated noise to FPR/TPR estimates, preserving subgroup confidentiality.

For scenarios involving multiple protected attributes (e.g., country, currency, device type), the invention provides two extensions. The *Weak Multiple Attribute Fairness* approach applies the single-attribute heuristic independently to each attribute, enforcing Relaxed Equalized Odds per attribute but not across their intersections. This scales linearly with the number of attributes and is suitable for high-dimensional settings. The *Strong Multiple Attribute Fairness* approach enforces the criterion across all joint subpopulations (e.g., (US, USD, iOS)), offering stronger guarantees but at exponential cost. To manage this, the system includes two pruning mechanisms: (1) *Attribute Pruning*, which removes redundant attributes using Chi-square tests of independence (e.g., if country and currency are highly correlated, only one is retained), and (2) *Subspace Pruning*, which uses an FP-Tree to identify and discard joint subpopulations with fewer than a minimum number of samples (e.g., 100), ensuring statistical reliability.

The invention has been validated on diverse datasets. In a fraud detection case study involving millions of online transactions, the method reduced FPR disparities across 50 countries from a standard deviation of 0.18 to 0.04 while maintaining overall precision. On the COMPAS recidivism dataset, it equalized FPRs between African American and Caucasian defendants (0.40 vs. 0.38) with minimal impact on predictive accuracy. Similarly, on the UCI Adult income dataset, it achieved balanced FNRs across genders (0.77 vs. 0.61) outperforming Calibrated Equalized Odds in FPR control. These results confirm that the invention delivers practical, effective, and deployable fairness solutions for real-world machine learning systems.
