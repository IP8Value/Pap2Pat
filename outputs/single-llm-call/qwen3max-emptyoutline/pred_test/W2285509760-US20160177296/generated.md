# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Avian influenza virus (AIV) represents a significant threat to global poultry production, animal health, and public health. Outbreaks of AIV in commercial and backyard poultry flocks have resulted in the culling of millions of birds worldwide, causing severe economic losses that amount to billions of dollars annually. Beyond the immediate agricultural and economic consequences, AIV is of particular concern due to its zoonotic potential—certain highly pathogenic strains, such as H5N1 and H7N9, have demonstrated the ability to cross the species barrier and infect humans, sometimes with high mortality rates. The continuous evolution of AIV through antigenic drift and shift further complicates control efforts, as new viral variants can emerge rapidly, often outpacing the development and deployment of conventional vaccines.

Current strategies for controlling AIV in poultry populations rely heavily on vaccination. However, existing vaccines suffer from several critical limitations that undermine their effectiveness during outbreaks. Most commercially available AIV vaccines are administered via subcutaneous or intramuscular injection, which is labor-intensive, costly, and impractical for mass application in large-scale poultry operations. Moreover, vaccinated birds typically require two to three weeks to develop protective levels of neutralizing antibodies, leaving a vulnerable window during which the virus can spread unchecked. This delay is particularly problematic in the context of an emerging outbreak, where rapid intervention is essential to contain transmission. Additionally, the efficacy of these vaccines is highly dependent on precise antigenic matching between the vaccine strain and the circulating field virus. Because AIV exhibits substantial genetic and antigenic diversity across hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) subtypes, a vaccine developed against one subtype (e.g., H5N1) offers little or no cross-protection against others (e.g., H6N2 or H8N4). Consequently, vaccine stockpiles must be constantly updated, and mismatches between vaccine and field strains can lead to complete vaccine failure.

Further compounding these challenges are logistical and biological issues related to vaccine stability and immune response. Improper storage, handling, or administration of live or inactivated AIV vaccines can result in loss of potency, rendering them ineffective. Even when properly administered, conventional vaccines induce humoral immunity that results in seroconversion, meaning vaccinated birds test positive for AIV antibodies in standard diagnostic assays. This serological reactivity creates significant trade barriers, as many countries prohibit the importation of poultry or poultry products from regions where AIV vaccination is practiced, regardless of the actual disease status. This “vaccination stigma” severely limits the utility of current vaccines in international trade and disease surveillance programs.

In light of these shortcomings, there is a pressing need for alternative prophylactic interventions that offer rapid, broad-spectrum, and subtype-independent protection against AIV, while avoiding the pitfalls of conventional immunization. RNA interference (RNAi) has emerged as a promising antiviral strategy that leverages the cell’s endogenous gene-silencing machinery to specifically target and degrade viral RNA. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) or short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) can be designed to bind with perfect complementarity to conserved regions of essential viral genes, thereby inhibiting viral replication at the post-transcriptional level. Unlike vaccines, which rely on adaptive immune responses that are slow to develop and highly specific, RNAi acts directly on the viral genome and can be engineered to target sequences that are conserved across multiple AIV subtypes and even across different host species, offering the potential for universal protection.

Despite its theoretical promise, the practical application of RNAi as an antiviral therapy has been hindered by the persistent challenge of efficient and safe intracellular delivery. Naked siRNAs are rapidly degraded by nucleases in biological fluids and cannot passively cross cellular membranes due to their negative charge and large molecular size. To overcome this, various delivery platforms have been explored, including synthetic lipid nanoparticles, polymer-based carriers, and viral vectors. However, each of these approaches carries significant drawbacks. Synthetic carriers often exhibit low transfection efficiency in vivo, require high doses that can be toxic, and may trigger inflammatory responses. Viral vectors, such as lentiviruses or adenoviruses, can achieve high delivery efficiency but pose serious safety risks, including insertional mutagenesis, hepatotoxicity, and oncogenesis, particularly when used for prophylactic purposes in healthy animals. Moreover, shRNA-expressing viral vectors have been shown to saturate the endogenous RNAi machinery, leading to cytotoxicity and off-target effects in animal models.

To address these delivery challenges, a novel platform known as transkingdom RNA interference (tkRNAi) has been developed. This system utilizes nonpathogenic, genetically engineered Escherichia coli bacteria that are modified to produce and deliver shRNAs directly into mammalian mucosal epithelial cells. The tkRNAi vector is equipped with two key virulence factors derived from Listeria monocytogenes: the invasin (Inv) protein, which mediates bacterial entry into host cells by binding to β(1) integrin receptors on the epithelial surface, and listeriolysin O (HlyA), a pore-forming toxin that facilitates escape from the endosomal compartment into the host cytoplasm, where the shRNA can be processed into functional siRNAs by the cellular RNAi machinery. Importantly, the tkRNAi vector is auxotrophic for diaminopimelic acid (Dap), a metabolite not present in mammalian or avian tissues, ensuring that the bacteria cannot replicate or colonize the host after delivery. This feature, combined with the non-conjugative nature of the plasmid and the absence of integration into the host genome, renders the system inherently safe for clinical and veterinary applications.

While tkRNAi has been successfully applied in mammalian systems—including human clinical trials for gastrointestinal disorders—its utility in avian species has remained unexplored. Given that AIV primarily infects respiratory and intestinal epithelial cells in birds, which are mucosal surfaces accessible to orally or aerosol-administered therapeutics, the tkRNAi platform presents a compelling opportunity for targeted antiviral delivery in poultry. However, the success of this approach hinges on the presence of functional β(1) integrin receptors on avian epithelial cells, which are necessary for bacterial invasion. Prior to this invention, it was unknown whether chicken cells express these receptors or whether they are capable of internalizing tkRNAi vectors.

The present invention addresses this gap by demonstrating, for the first time, that avian epithelial cells—specifically chicken hepatocellular carcinoma epithelial (LMH) cells—express β(1) integrin and are fully competent to internalize tkRNAi vectors. Furthermore, the invention provides engineered anti-AIV tkRNAi vectors that constitutively express shRNAs targeting highly conserved regions of the AIV nucleoprotein (NP) and polymerase acidic (PA) genes, both of which are essential for viral replication and exhibit minimal sequence variation across diverse AIV subtypes. These vectors are shown to significantly reduce the shedding of infectious AIV particles in vitro, with combination therapy (targeting both NP and PA) achieving up to a 10,000-fold reduction in viral titer. Critically, the vectors are non-cytotoxic at effective concentrations and can be administered via non-invasive routes such as oral ingestion, intranasal instillation, or aerosolization, making them suitable for mass application in poultry farms. Unlike conventional vaccines, this RNAi-based prophylactic does not induce seroconversion, thereby eliminating trade restrictions associated with antibody-positive flocks. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in AIV control—one that is rapid, scalable, universal, and compatible with global trade and surveillance requirements.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method for the prophylactic and therapeutic inhibition of avian influenza virus (AIV) in avian species, particularly poultry, using a transkingdom RNA interference (tkRNAi) delivery platform. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that avian mucosal epithelial cells, including those of the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts, express functional β(1) integrin receptors that enable the uptake of engineered nonpathogenic Escherichia coli vectors designed to deliver short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting essential and highly conserved regions of the AIV genome. Specifically, the invention discloses recombinant bacterial vectors comprising a plasmid encoding one or more shRNAs directed against the nucleoprotein (NP) and/or polymerase acidic (PA) genes of AIV, along with genetic elements necessary for mucosal epithelial cell invasion and endosomal escape, namely the invasin (Inv) gene from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis and the listeriolysin O (hlyA) gene from Listeria monocytogenes.

The shRNA sequences are selected to exhibit 100% complementarity to conserved nucleotide sequences within the NP and PA genes that are shared across a wide range of AIV subtypes, including but not limited to H5N1, H6N2, H7N9, H8N4, and H9N2, as well as influenza A viruses isolated from swine, equine, canine, and human hosts. This conservation ensures that the shRNAs retain activity against emerging and evolving strains, thereby providing broad-spectrum and potentially universal protection. The bacterial vectors are further engineered to be diaminopimelic acid (Dap) auxotrophs, rendering them incapable of replication in avian or mammalian hosts, and are non-conjugative, eliminating the risk of horizontal gene transfer or genomic integration. These features collectively ensure the safety of the vectors for use in food-producing animals.

The invention further encompasses methods of administering the anti-AIV tkRNAi vectors to avian populations via non-invasive routes, including oral ingestion, intranasal delivery, or aerosolization, enabling rapid and cost-effective mass application during outbreaks or as a routine prophylactic measure. In contrast to conventional vaccines, the RNAi-based intervention does not elicit an adaptive immune response or produce AIV-specific antibodies, thereby avoiding serological reactivity that impedes international trade. The invention also provides a scalable and stable production process, as the bacterial vectors can be lyophilized or stored frozen for long-term stockpiling without loss of viability or function.

Experimental validation demonstrates that the anti-AIV tkRNAi vectors are efficiently internalized by chicken LMH epithelial cells, with invasion efficiencies exceeding 74%, and that they significantly reduce the shedding of infectious AIV particles in vitro. When administered prior to viral challenge, vectors expressing shRNAs against NP alone, PA alone, or a combination thereof (cocktail) reduced viral titers by 1.6 to 4.0 log10 units, corresponding to 39- to 10,000-fold reductions in infectious virus, depending on the AIV subtype and shRNA construct. The combination of NP and PA shRNAs consistently yielded the greatest antiviral effect, underscoring the advantage of multi-target inhibition in preventing viral escape. Control vectors expressing a scrambled shRNA sequence also exhibited modest antiviral activity, likely due to innate immune stimulation by bacterial components, but this effect was significantly less than that achieved by sequence-specific shRNAs.

Accordingly, the invention provides a safe, effective, rapid, and universally applicable RNAi-based antiviral platform for the control of avian influenza in poultry, with the potential to mitigate economic losses, reduce zoonotic risk, and support global biosecurity and trade objectives. The platform is also adaptable to other mucosal viral pathogens in avian and mammalian species, establishing a new paradigm for RNAi delivery in veterinary and human medicine.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Design of siRNAs and Use of Viral Specific siRNAs to Inhibit Avian Influenza in an Avian Tissue Model

The foundation of the present invention lies in the rational design and selection of small interfering RNA (siRNA) sequences that specifically target essential and highly conserved regions of the avian influenza virus (AIV) genome. Unlike conventional antiviral strategies that rely on immune stimulation or direct-acting inhibitors, RNA interference (RNAi) operates through a sequence-specific post-transcriptional gene silencing mechanism that degrades viral mRNA before it can be translated into functional proteins. For this approach to be effective, the siRNA must be directed against genomic regions that are indispensable for viral replication and that exhibit minimal sequence variability across diverse AIV strains and subtypes. After extensive bioinformatic and experimental evaluation, the nucleoprotein (NP) and polymerase acidic (PA) genes were identified as optimal targets for RNAi-mediated inhibition.

The NP gene encodes the nucleoprotein, a structural component that encapsulates the viral RNA genome and is essential for viral transcription, replication, and assembly. The PA gene encodes the acidic subunit of the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase complex, which is responsible for viral RNA synthesis. Both proteins are highly conserved across influenza A viruses due to strong functional constraints; mutations in these regions often impair viral fitness, making them less prone to antigenic drift compared to surface glycoproteins like hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA). Consequently, siRNAs targeting NP and PA are expected to retain efficacy against a broad spectrum of AIV subtypes, including those with pandemic potential.

Two 19-nucleotide siRNA sequences were selected based on prior validation in mammalian and avian systems: one targeting the NP gene (sense strand: 5′-GGA TCT TAT TTC TTC GGA G-3′) and one targeting the PA gene (sense strand: 5′-GCA ATT GAG GAG TGC CTG A-3′). These sequences were originally described in Ge et al. (2003) and have since been shown to potently inhibit influenza replication in multiple studies. To ensure suitability for use in avian species, the sequences were aligned against the NP and PA genes of two representative AIV strains—A/chicken/Texas/473-2/2010 (H6N2) and A/turkey/Colorado/235497/2003 (H8N4)—using the BLAST heuristic algorithm. Both siRNAs exhibited 100% identity with the corresponding target regions in these strains, confirming their applicability. More importantly, a comprehensive BLAST search against the entire chicken genome revealed no significant sequence homology, particularly in the critical “seed region” (nucleotides 2–8 of the antisense strand), which is responsible for off-target silencing in RNAi. The absence of complementarity to any known chicken mRNA ensures that the siRNAs will not inadvertently silence host genes, thereby minimizing the risk of cytotoxicity or unintended biological effects.

To facilitate sustained intracellular expression, the siRNA sequences were converted into short hairpin RNA (shRNA) constructs by incorporating a 9-nucleotide loop sequence (5′-TTC AAG AGA-3′) between the sense and antisense strands, followed by a poly-T termination signal. These shRNA cassettes were then cloned into the pmbv43 plasmid, a specialized vector designed for use in the transkingdom RNAi (tkRNAi) platform. The pmbv43 plasmid contains a constitutive promoter that drives continuous shRNA transcription, as well as antibiotic resistance markers (kanamycin) for selection and maintenance in bacterial hosts. The resulting plasmids—NP/pmbv43 and PA/pmbv43—were sequence-verified to ensure correct insertion and orientation.

The shRNA-expressing plasmids were then transformed into CEQ221, a nonpathogenic, Dap-auxotrophic strain of Escherichia coli that serves as the delivery vehicle in the tkRNAi system. This bacterial strain is engineered to express two key virulence factors: invasin (Inv), which binds to β(1) integrin receptors on mucosal epithelial cells, and listeriolysin O (HlyA), which lyses the endosomal membrane to release the bacterial payload into the cytoplasm. Once inside the host cell, the shRNA is processed by the endogenous RNAi machinery—Dicer cleaves the hairpin into a functional siRNA duplex, which is then loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). The guide strand of the siRNA directs RISC to complementary viral mRNA, leading to its cleavage and degradation.

To validate the antiviral activity of these constructs in an avian context, chicken primary hepatocellular carcinoma epithelial (LMH) cells were used as an in vitro model. Although LMH cells are derived from liver tissue, they are epithelial in nature, express mucosal characteristics, and are permissive to AIV infection, making them a suitable surrogate for respiratory and intestinal epithelia—the primary sites of AIV replication in birds. Cells were pre-treated with anti-AIV tkRNAi vectors expressing either NP-shRNA, PA-shRNA, a combination of both (cocktail), or a scrambled control shRNA. Twenty-four hours later, cells were challenged with either H6N2 or H8N4 AIV at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI = 0.01) to mimic natural exposure. Supernatants were harvested 48 hours post-infection and analyzed for infectious virus using a TCID50 assay on MDCK cells.

The results demonstrated robust and statistically significant reductions in viral shedding across all treatment groups. The cocktail vector, which simultaneously targets both NP and PA, achieved the greatest inhibition—reducing H8N4 titers by 4.0 log10 (10,000-fold) and H6N2 titers by 1.9 log10 (79-fold). Single-target vectors also showed potent activity, with NP-shRNA reducing H8N4 by 3.8 log10 and PA-shRNA by 2.5 log10. These findings confirm that the selected siRNA sequences retain their antiviral potency in avian cells and that dual targeting enhances efficacy, likely by imposing a higher genetic barrier to viral escape. Importantly, no cytotoxicity was observed at the effective vector concentration (7.8 × 10⁵ CFU/mL), as assessed by crystal violet staining and visual inspection for cytopathic effect (CPE). This establishes the therapeutic window for safe and effective use in avian tissues.

### Inhibiting Avian Influenza Replication in a Chicken Cell Model Using a Unique RNAi Delivery Technology

A critical innovation of the present invention is the adaptation of the transkingdom RNA interference (tkRNAi) platform for use in avian species—a feat previously unachieved and non-obvious to those skilled in the art. While tkRNAi has been extensively validated in mammalian systems, its applicability to birds was uncertain due to fundamental differences in cell biology, receptor expression, and immune function. The successful implementation of tkRNAi in chicken cells required the demonstration that avian mucosal epithelial cells express the necessary molecular machinery for bacterial vector uptake, specifically β(1) integrin receptors, which serve as the entry point for invasin-expressing bacteria.

To address this, LMH cells were analyzed for β(1) integrin mRNA expression under normal growth conditions and following AIV infection. Using reverse transcription PCR with primers specific to chicken β(1) integrin and β-actin (as an internal control), robust expression of the receptor was detected in uninfected cells, as well as at 6 and 24 hours post-infection with H8N4 virus. This confirmed that the receptor is constitutively expressed and not downregulated during viral infection, thereby supporting the feasibility of tkRNAi-mediated delivery throughout the course of AIV infection.

Next, the optimal concentration of tkRNAi vectors for use in LMH cells was determined through a dose-ranging cytotoxicity assay. Serial dilutions of a control vector expressing a scrambled shRNA (anti-AIV/scramble) were applied to confluent LMH monolayers, and cells were monitored for signs of CPE over 48 hours. The highest concentration that did not induce cytotoxicity—7.8 × 10⁵ colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL)—was selected for all subsequent experiments. This concentration represents a balance between sufficient bacterial load for effective shRNA delivery and minimal perturbation of host cell viability.

To directly visualize and quantify vector uptake, the anti-AIV/scramble vector was co-transformed with a red fluorescent protein (RFP) expression plasmid, enabling tracking of intracellular bacteria. Fluorescent microscopy revealed clear RFP signal within LMH cell nuclei (stained with DAPI) as early as 2 hours post-invasion, with signal intensity increasing by 24 hours. Flow cytometry further confirmed intracellular localization, with invasion efficiencies of 74.1% at 20 hours and 75.7% at 36 hours post-invasion. These data provide unequivocal evidence that tkRNAi vectors are efficiently internalized by avian epithelial cells via β(1) integrin-mediated endocytosis, and that the HlyA toxin successfully facilitates endosomal escape, allowing shRNA access to the cytoplasmic RNAi machinery.

With delivery confirmed, the antiviral efficacy of the system was evaluated using the same experimental design described above. The key distinction of this approach is that it measures inhibition of infectious virus—not just viral RNA—thereby providing a clinically relevant endpoint. The TCID50 assay, which quantifies the dilution of virus required to infect 50% of MDCK cell cultures, is the gold standard for assessing infectious titer and correlates directly with transmission potential. The observed log10 reductions in TCID50/mL demonstrate that tkRNAi not only silences viral gene expression but also prevents the production and release of replication-competent virions.

Notably, even the scrambled control vector reduced viral titers by 0.8–2.1 log10, suggesting a secondary, non-sequence-specific antiviral mechanism. This is likely attributable to innate immune activation by bacterial components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which engages Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and triggers an antiviral state through interferon and cytokine production. While this adjuvant-like effect is beneficial, it is substantially weaker than the sequence-specific silencing achieved by NP and PA shRNAs, underscoring the importance of targeted RNAi for maximal efficacy.

### Prophylactic Treatment of Chicken Populations with Anti-AIV Vectors

The ultimate objective of the present invention is the translation of in vitro findings into a practical, field-deployable intervention for the prophylactic control of avian influenza in commercial poultry operations. The anti-AIV tkRNAi vectors are uniquely suited for this purpose due to their compatibility with mass administration, rapid onset of action, and absence of serological interference. Unlike injectable vaccines, which require individual handling of each bird, the bacterial vectors can be delivered via oral, intranasal, or aerosol routes—methods that are easily scalable to thousands or millions of birds using existing farm infrastructure such as spray nozzles, foggers, or water-line dispensers.

For example, during an outbreak or in anticipation of high-risk periods (e.g., migratory bird season), poultry houses can be treated with an aerosolized suspension of the anti-AIV cocktail vector. Birds inhale the bacteria, which then colonize the respiratory mucosa transiently, deliver shRNAs to epithelial cells, and are subsequently cleared due to Dap auxotrophy. Protection is established within 24 hours—far faster than the 2–3 weeks required for vaccine-induced immunity—and lasts for the duration of shRNA expression, which can be extended through repeated dosing if necessary. Because the intervention acts directly on the virus and does not stimulate antibody production, treated flocks remain seronegative in standard AIV diagnostic tests, preserving market access and facilitating surveillance.

The production of tkRNAi vectors is also highly scalable and cost-effective. The CEQ221 strain can be grown in standard fermentation tanks, purified, and formulated into stable liquid or lyophilized preparations that retain viability for months or years when stored at –80°C or in freeze-dried form. This enables strategic stockpiling by governments or industry consortia, ensuring rapid deployment during emergencies. Moreover, because the shRNA targets are conserved, a single vector formulation can protect against multiple AIV subtypes, eliminating the need for strain-matching and reducing inventory complexity.

Future work will focus on in vivo validation in experimentally challenged chickens, optimization of dosing regimens, and assessment of environmental safety. However, the in vitro proof-of-concept provided herein establishes the foundational feasibility, efficacy, and safety of the platform. The invention thus represents a transformative advance in veterinary virology, offering a universal, rapid, and trade-compatible solution to one of the most persistent threats to global poultry health.

## GLOSSARY OF CLAIM TERMS

**Avian Influenza Virus (AIV)**: A type A influenza virus that primarily infects birds, classified by hemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N) subtypes, including but not limited to H5N1, H6N2, H7N9, H8N4, and H9N2.

**Transkingdom RNA Interference (tkRNAi)**: A delivery platform utilizing nonpathogenic, genetically engineered bacteria to produce and deliver short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) into eukaryotic mucosal epithelial cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis and endosomal escape.

**Short Hairpin RNA (shRNA)**: An RNA molecule with a tight hairpin turn that can be processed by the cellular RNAi machinery into a functional small interfering RNA (siRNA) capable of sequence-specific mRNA degradation.

**Nucleoprotein (NP) gene**: A highly conserved influenza A virus gene encoding the nucleoprotein, essential for viral RNA encapsidation, replication, and assembly.

**Polymerase Acidic (PA) gene**: A highly conserved influenza A virus gene encoding the PA subunit of the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase complex, critical for viral transcription and replication.

**β(1) integrin**: A cell surface receptor expressed on mucosal epithelial cells that mediates internalization of invasin-expressing bacteria.

**Invasin (Inv)**: A bacterial surface protein derived from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis that binds to β(1) integrin and triggers receptor-mediated endocytosis.

**Listeriolysin O (HlyA)**: A pore-forming toxin derived from Listeria monocytogenes that facilitates endosomal escape of bacterial payloads into the host cytoplasm.

**Diaminopimelic acid (Dap) auxotroph**: A bacterial strain unable to synthesize diaminopimelic acid, an essential metabolite absent in eukaryotic tissues, rendering it incapable of replication in host organisms.

**Colony-forming unit (CFU)**: A measure of viable bacterial cells, defined as the number of cells that give rise to a single colony on solid agar medium.

**TCID50**: The 50% tissue culture infectious dose, a quantitative measure of infectious virus titer defined as the dilution required to infect 50% of inoculated cell cultures.

**Log10 reduction**: A logarithmic measure of viral titer reduction, where a 1 log10 reduction corresponds to a 10-fold decrease in infectious virus.

**Scrambled shRNA**: A control shRNA sequence with no significant homology to the target viral or host genome, used to assess non-specific effects of vector delivery.

**Mucosal epithelial cells**: Epithelial cells lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal, or urogenital tracts, characterized by the expression of β(1) integrin and accessibility to luminal agents.

**Prophylactic treatment**: Administration of an agent prior to pathogen exposure to prevent or mitigate infection.
