# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive and ultimately fatal neurodegenerative disorder that represents the most common cause of dementia among the elderly. The pathological hallmarks of AD include the extracellular deposition of amyloid plaques and the intraneuronal accumulation of neurofibrillary tangles, both of which are associated with synaptic dysfunction, neuronal loss, and cognitive decline. Amyloid plaques are primarily composed of aggregated forms of the amyloid-β (Aβ) peptide, particularly the 42-amino acid isoform known as Aβ42, which is generated through the sequential proteolytic cleavage of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) by β- and γ-secretases. Historically, the fibrillar, detergent-insoluble aggregates of Aβ42 found in mature amyloid plaques were considered the principal neurotoxic agents responsible for the pathogenesis of AD. However, accumulating evidence over the past two decades has challenged this long-standing "amyloid cascade hypothesis" by demonstrating that the most toxic species may not be the mature fibrils but rather smaller, soluble oligomeric assemblies of Aβ42 that form during the early stages of the aggregation process.

Indeed, numerous biochemical, biophysical, and cell-based studies have shown that low-molecular-weight oligomers of Aβ42—such as dimers, trimers, tetramers, and dodecamers—are highly neurotoxic and can impair synaptic plasticity, disrupt calcium homeostasis, induce oxidative stress, and trigger inflammatory responses in neurons. These soluble oligomers are stable in the presence of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), a property that distinguishes them from transient or non-specific aggregates and allows their detection by techniques such as SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblotting. Importantly, these oligomers appear to be on-pathway intermediates in the Aβ42 aggregation cascade, capable of further assembling into larger protofibrils and ultimately into mature amyloid fibrils. This revised understanding of Aβ42 toxicity has shifted the focus of therapeutic development away from compounds that merely prevent fibril formation and toward agents that specifically inhibit the formation or stabilize the monomeric state of Aβ42, thereby preventing the generation of these early, soluble oligomeric toxins.

Despite this paradigm shift, the identification of compounds that effectively and selectively inhibit the initial steps of Aβ42 oligomerization remains a significant challenge. Traditional high-throughput screening approaches often rely on in vitro assays using synthetic Aβ42 peptides, which may not accurately recapitulate the complex cellular environment in which oligomerization occurs in vivo. Moreover, many compounds identified in such screens fail to demonstrate efficacy in cellular or animal models, highlighting the limitations of purely biochemical systems. There is therefore a pressing need for robust, physiologically relevant model systems that can faithfully mimic the early stages of Aβ42 oligomerization and provide a functional readout amenable to both chemical and genetic screening.

The yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae has emerged as a powerful and versatile model organism for the study of protein misfolding and aggregation disorders, including those associated with neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, Parkinson’s disease, and Huntington’s disease. Yeast offers several advantages for such studies, including rapid growth, ease of genetic manipulation, well-characterized molecular biology, and a conserved protein quality control machinery that includes molecular chaperones and degradation pathways homologous to those in higher eukaryotes. Of particular relevance to the study of amyloid formation is the existence of endogenous yeast prions—self-propagating, amyloid-based protein aggregates that serve as natural models for the structural and mechanistic principles underlying amyloidogenesis. The best-characterized yeast prion is [PSI+], which results from the aggregation of the translation termination factor Sup35p.

Sup35p is a modular protein composed of three distinct domains: an N-terminal glutamine/asparagine-rich domain (N) that is necessary and sufficient for prion formation; a highly charged middle domain (M) of unclear function; and a C-terminal release factor domain (RF) that is essential for viability due to its role in terminating protein synthesis at stop codons. In [PSI+] cells, the N domain of Sup35p forms self-templating amyloid aggregates that sequester the RF domain, thereby reducing its availability for translation termination and leading to increased readthrough of premature stop codons—a phenomenon known as nonsense suppression. This phenotype can be easily monitored using reporter alleles such as ade1-14, which contains a premature stop codon in the adenine biosynthesis pathway. In [psi−] cells (lacking Sup35p aggregates), the ade1-14 mutation results in adenine auxotrophy and the accumulation of a red pigment, whereas in [PSI+] cells, nonsense suppression allows for the production of full-length Ade1p, enabling growth on adenine-deficient media and yielding white colonies. This simple colorimetric and growth-based assay provides a sensitive and quantitative readout of Sup35p aggregation status.

The yeast chaperone Hsp104, a member of the AAA+ (ATPases associated with diverse cellular activities) family, plays a critical role in the propagation and maintenance of [PSI+] and other yeast prions. Hsp104 functions as a molecular disaggregase that, in cooperation with Hsp70 and Hsp40 chaperones, uses the energy of ATP hydrolysis to fragment large prion aggregates into smaller, transmissible seeds that can be efficiently partitioned into daughter cells during cell division. Inhibition of Hsp104 ATPase activity—for example, by treatment with millimolar concentrations of guanidine hydrochloride—blocks this fragmentation process, leading to the gradual dilution of prion seeds and eventual "curing" of the [PSI+] state. This well-established system has been instrumental in elucidating the mechanisms of prion propagation and the role of chaperones in protein aggregate dynamics.

Building upon this foundation, the present invention leverages the unique properties of the yeast Sup35p system to create a novel, genetically tractable model for studying the initial stages of Aβ42 oligomerization. By replacing the prion-forming N domain of Sup35p with the human Aβ42 peptide, we have engineered a chimeric fusion protein, termed AβMRF, in which the aggregation propensity of Aβ42 directly modulates the activity of the essential RF domain. Critically, this system is designed to report specifically on the formation of SDS-stable, low-n oligomers of Aβ42—the very species now believed to be the primary neurotoxic agents in AD—rather than on the formation of large, insoluble fibrils. The resulting phenotypic readout (colony color and adenine prototrophy) provides a simple, high-throughput platform for identifying compounds or genetic modifiers that interfere with the earliest steps of Aβ42 assembly. Furthermore, our studies reveal unexpected interactions between Aβ42 oligomers and the yeast chaperone machinery, offering new insights into the cellular mechanisms that regulate Aβ42 oligomer homeostasis and suggesting potential therapeutic targets for modulating Aβ42 toxicity in human neurons.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel yeast-based model system for studying the initial stages of amyloid-β 42 (Aβ42) oligomerization and for screening agents that modulate this process. The invention is based on a chimeric fusion protein, designated AβMRF, in which the human Aβ42 peptide is fused to the middle (M) and release factor (RF) domains of the essential yeast translation termination factor Sup35p. In this construct, the Aβ42 moiety drives the formation of soluble, SDS-stable low-n oligomers (dimers, trimers, and tetramers), which impair the translation termination activity of the RF domain. This impairment leads to increased readthrough of a premature stop codon in a reporter gene (e.g., ade1-14), resulting in a readily scorable phenotypic change in yeast—specifically, the ability to grow on adenine-deficient medium and the loss of red pigment accumulation on complex medium. Mutations in Aβ42 that are known to disrupt its aggregation propensity (e.g., F19T, F20T, I31P) reduce or abolish oligomer formation, thereby restoring RF activity and the corresponding wild-type phenotype (adenine auxotrophy and red coloration). This system thus provides a direct functional readout of Aβ42 oligomerization status.

The invention further demonstrates that the yeast prion-curing agent guanidine hydrochloride enhances the accumulation of AβMRF oligomers, an effect that is independent of the canonical prion-disaggregating chaperone Hsp104. Surprisingly, deletion of HSP104 reduces AβMRF oligomer levels and total protein abundance, suggesting that Hsp104 plays a protective role by shielding AβMRF from degradation or disaggregation by other cellular factors. Co-immunoprecipitation experiments confirm a physical interaction between Hsp104 and AβMRF, supporting this model. These findings reveal a previously unappreciated complexity in the cellular handling of Aβ42 oligomers and highlight the utility of the yeast model for dissecting the roles of specific chaperones and other modifiers in Aβ42 oligomer dynamics.

In one aspect, the invention provides a recombinant yeast cell comprising a nucleic acid sequence encoding the AβMRF fusion protein, wherein the Aβ42 portion is operably linked to a promoter (e.g., the copper-inducible CUP1 promoter) and the RF domain is essential for cell viability. In another aspect, the invention provides a method for screening for agents that inhibit Aβ42 oligomerization, comprising contacting the recombinant yeast cell with a candidate agent and detecting a phenotypic change indicative of reduced AβMRF oligomerization (e.g., reduced growth on adenine-deficient medium or increased red pigment accumulation). In yet another aspect, the invention provides a method for identifying genetic modifiers of Aβ42 oligomerization, comprising introducing mutations or deletions into the yeast genome and assessing their effect on the AβMRF phenotype. The invention also encompasses the use of this model system to study the mechanisms of Aβ42 oligomer formation, stability, and clearance, and to evaluate the effects of environmental or pharmacological perturbations on these processes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Model of Aβ42 Oligomerization

The present invention is founded upon a detailed understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying Aβ42 oligomerization and the development of a genetically encoded reporter system that faithfully recapitulates this process in a simple eukaryotic cell. Central to the invention is the recognition that the neurotoxicity associated with Alzheimer’s disease is primarily mediated by soluble, low-molecular-weight oligomers of Aβ42, rather than by the insoluble amyloid fibrils that constitute the core of senile plaques. These oligomers, which include species ranging from dimers to dodecamers, are characterized by their stability in the presence of ionic detergents such as SDS, a property that reflects their tightly packed, β-sheet-rich structure. Structural and biophysical studies have identified specific regions within the Aβ42 sequence that are critical for mediating intermolecular interactions during oligomer assembly. In particular, four key segments—spanning residues 15–21, 24–32, 35–37, and 40–42—have been implicated in the formation of the oligomer interface. Mutations within these regions, such as the substitution of phenylalanine at positions 19 and 20 with threonine (F19T, F20T) or isoleucine at position 31 with proline (I31P), have been shown to disrupt Aβ42 aggregation in vitro and to abrogate its neurotoxic effects in cellular and animal models.

To model this process in vivo, the inventors have engineered a chimeric protein in which the human Aβ42 peptide is fused to the M and RF domains of the yeast Sup35p protein, creating a construct referred to herein as AβMRF. The RF domain of Sup35p is essential for cell viability, as it is required for the accurate termination of protein synthesis at stop codons. In wild-type yeast, loss of RF function leads to widespread translational readthrough, which is lethal. However, in strains carrying a nonsense mutation in a non-essential reporter gene such as ade1-14, partial loss of RF activity can be tolerated and results in a quantifiable phenotype: the ability to grow on medium lacking adenine (Ade+) and the failure to accumulate a red pigment that is normally produced as a byproduct of incomplete adenine biosynthesis. This phenotype serves as a sensitive and convenient readout of RF activity, which in turn is inversely correlated with the degree of AβMRF oligomerization.

The AβMRF fusion protein is expressed in a yeast strain in which the endogenous SUP35 gene has been deleted, rendering the cell entirely dependent on the exogenous AβMRF construct for viability. Under these conditions, any factor that promotes Aβ42-mediated oligomerization will lead to sequestration of the RF domain into inactive complexes, thereby increasing nonsense suppression and enhancing the Ade+ phenotype. Conversely, any factor that inhibits Aβ42 oligomerization will restore RF activity, resulting in adenine auxotrophy (Ade−) and red colony coloration. This design ensures that the system is exquisitely sensitive to even subtle changes in Aβ42 oligomerization propensity, as demonstrated by the clear phenotypic differences observed between yeast expressing wild-type AβMRF and those expressing oligomerization-deficient mutants (Aβm1MRF and Aβm2MRF).

Biochemical validation of the model confirms that AβMRF forms SDS-stable oligomers corresponding to dimers, trimers, and tetramers, consistent with the expected size of low-n Aβ42 assemblies. These oligomers are resistant to dissociation by 1–2% SDS at room temperature but are fully resolved into monomers upon boiling, a hallmark of structured amyloid-like oligomers. Importantly, the oligomerization is specifically mediated by the Aβ42 moiety, as evidenced by the reduced oligomer formation in the Aβm1MRF and Aβm2MRF mutants and the differential accessibility of epitopes to antibodies directed against Aβ42 versus the RF domain. The absence of higher-order aggregates or fibrils in the AβMRF system further underscores its specificity for the early stages of Aβ42 assembly, distinguishing it from other models that may conflate oligomerization with fibrillization.

This model of Aβ42 oligomerization thus provides a powerful platform for interrogating the molecular determinants of Aβ42 toxicity and for discovering therapeutic interventions that target the root cause of AD pathology. By linking Aβ42 oligomerization directly to a vital cellular function, the system ensures that only biologically relevant modulators of the oligomerization process are identified, thereby increasing the likelihood of translational success in more complex models and ultimately in human patients.

### Example 2

In a specific embodiment of the invention, the AβMRF fusion protein was constructed and expressed in a yeast strain derived from 74D-694, which carries the ade1-14 nonsense mutation and a genomic deletion of the SUP35 gene (sup35Δ::LEU2). The strain was maintained by a plasmid encoding full-length Sup35p under its native promoter, which was subsequently replaced with a plasmid encoding the AβMRF construct under the control of the copper-inducible CUP1 promoter. The AβMRF protein consists of the human Aβ42 peptide (sequence: DAEFRHDSGYEVHHQKLVFFAEDVGSNKGAIIGLMVGGVVIA) fused directly to the M domain of Sup35p, followed by a hemagglutinin (HA) epitope tag and the essential RF domain. Expression of the fusion protein was induced by the addition of 50 μM CuSO4 to the growth medium.

Yeast cells expressing AβMRF exhibited a white colony color on complex medium (YPD) and were able to grow on synthetic medium lacking adenine (-Ade), indicating a high level of nonsense suppression and thus impaired RF activity. In contrast, control cells expressing a non-aggregating MRF construct (lacking both the N domain and Aβ42) were red on YPD and failed to grow on -Ade, as expected for fully functional RF activity. To confirm that the observed phenotype was due to Aβ42-mediated oligomerization, two mutant versions of AβMRF were generated: Aβm1MRF, containing double substitutions F19T and F20T in the Aβ42 region, and Aβm2MRF, containing an additional I31P substitution. Cells expressing Aβm1MRF or Aβm2MRF displayed intermediate phenotypes: dark pink colonies on YPD and weak or no growth on -Ade, consistent with partial restoration of RF activity due to reduced oligomerization.

Immunoblot analysis of whole-cell lysates treated with 1% SDS at room temperature revealed that AβMRF exists as a mixture of monomers and SDS-stable oligomers migrating at approximately 2-, 3-, and 4-fold the monomer size (~65–77 kDa depending on the gel system used). In contrast, Aβm1MRF showed a significant reduction in oligomer levels, and Aβm2MRF was predominantly monomeric, with only trace amounts of dimers detectable. These results directly correlate the degree of oligomerization with the functional impairment of the RF domain, validating the use of the yeast phenotype as a proxy for Aβ42 oligomerization status. Notably, no higher-order aggregates or fibrils were detected for any of the AβMRF constructs, even when analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis optimized for large protein complexes, confirming that the system specifically models the early, low-n oligomerization events.

### Example 3

To further characterize the biochemical properties of AβMRF oligomers, lysates from yeast expressing AβMRF, Aβm1MRF, or Aβm2MRF were subjected to SDS-PAGE in both polyacrylamide and agarose gel systems. In 7.5% polyacrylamide gels, the AβMRF monomer migrated at approximately 77 kDa, while in 1.5% agarose gels, it migrated at approximately 65 kDa, likely due to differences in gel pore size and protein mobility. Despite this discrepancy, the relative positions of the oligomeric bands were consistent across both systems, with discrete bands corresponding to dimers, trimers, and tetramers. The oligomers were stable in 1% and 2% SDS at room temperature but were fully dissociated into monomers upon boiling in sample buffer containing 2% SDS and 2% β-mercaptoethanol, confirming their non-covalent, amyloid-like nature.

Antibody accessibility studies provided additional evidence that oligomerization occurs through the Aβ42 moiety. When blots were probed with monoclonal antibodies against the Aβ42 N-terminus (6E10), the signal intensity decreased progressively with increasing oligomer size, suggesting that the Aβ42 epitopes become less accessible in higher-order assemblies. In contrast, antibodies against the C-terminal RF domain (BE4) detected all oligomeric species with equal efficiency, indicating that the RF domain remains exposed to solvent. This pattern mirrors that observed for Sup35p [PSI+] aggregates, where the N-terminal prion domain is buried within the amyloid core while the C-terminal RF domain is surface-exposed. These findings strongly support the conclusion that AβMRF oligomerizes via specific interactions between Aβ42 moieties, with the MRF portion serving as a passive reporter of this process.

Control experiments using generic oligomer-specific antibodies, which recognize a conformational epitope common to many amyloidogenic proteins, failed to detect AβMRF oligomers. This is consistent with previous reports that these antibodies preferentially bind to larger oligomers (e.g., octamers or higher) and do not recognize the smaller, low-n species formed by AβMRF. Similarly, no fibrillar structures were detected for AβMRF, even under conditions that promote fibril formation by recombinant Aβ42 peptide in vitro. This suggests that the presence of the large MRF domain sterically hinders the progression of Aβ42 assembly beyond the low-n oligomer stage, or that cellular factors actively prevent the formation of larger aggregates. Either way, the system is uniquely suited to studying the initial, pathogenic steps of Aβ42 oligomerization without the confounding influence of later-stage fibrillization.

### Example 4

The invention was further exploited to investigate the effects of the yeast prion-curing agent guanidine hydrochloride on AβMRF oligomerization. Guanidine is known to inhibit the ATPase activity of Hsp104, thereby blocking the fragmentation of [PSI+] prion aggregates and leading to their eventual loss. Given that AβMRF oligomers share certain biochemical properties with prion aggregates (e.g., SDS stability), it was hypothesized that guanidine might similarly affect AβMRF. Surprisingly, treatment of AβMRF-expressing yeast with 6.3 mM guanidine resulted in a dramatic increase in the levels of SDS-stable oligomers, accompanied by a near-complete depletion of the monomeric pool. This effect was also observed, albeit to a lesser extent, for Aβm1MRF, but not for Aβm2MRF, indicating that it is dependent on the integrity of the Aβ42 aggregation interface.

To determine whether this effect was mediated by Hsp104 inhibition, as in the case of [PSI+], a sup35Δ hsp104Δ double-deletion strain was constructed and transformed with the AβMRF plasmid. Contrary to expectations, deletion of HSP104 led to a significant decrease in AβMRF oligomer levels and a concomitant increase in the monomer fraction. Moreover, the total amount of AβMRF protein was reduced by approximately 40% in the hsp104Δ strain, while the levels of the non-aggregating Aβm2MRF control were unaffected. Phenotypically, hsp104Δ cells expressing AβMRF showed slightly enhanced growth on -Ade, consistent with increased RF availability due to reduced oligomerization and/or lower total protein levels. These results indicate that Hsp104 is not the target of guanidine in the context of AβMRF oligomerization and, in fact, plays a protective role by stabilizing AβMRF and/or preventing its degradation.

### Example 5

To explore the mechanism by which Hsp104 influences AβMRF stability, co-immunoprecipitation experiments were performed using an HA-tagged version of AβMRF. Whole-cell lysates were incubated with anti-HA antibodies immobilized on agarose beads, and the immunoprecipitated complexes were analyzed by immunoblotting for Hsp104. Hsp104 was specifically co-precipitated with AβMRF but not with the control MRF protein, demonstrating a direct or indirect physical interaction between Hsp104 and the Aβ42-containing fusion. This finding is consistent with previous reports that Hsp104 can interact with Aβ42 in vitro and suggests that Hsp104 may bind to AβMRF oligomers in vivo, thereby shielding them from the action of other cellular factors that promote disaggregation or degradation.

Based on these observations, the inventors propose a model in which AβMRF oligomers are subject to continuous turnover by an unknown cellular machinery that includes disaggregating and/or degradative activities. Hsp104, by binding to AβMRF, interferes with this machinery, thereby stabilizing the oligomers and increasing their steady-state levels. In the absence of Hsp104, AβMRF is more susceptible to these activities, leading to increased monomerization and degradation. Guanidine, on the other hand, appears to inhibit the disaggregating/degradative machinery directly, possibly by targeting the ATPase activity of a different chaperone or enzyme, thereby causing oligomer accumulation even in the presence of Hsp104. This model reconciles the seemingly paradoxical effects of guanidine and Hsp104 deletion and highlights the complexity of the cellular response to Aβ42 oligomers.

### A Suppressible Allele

The utility of the AβMRF system as a screening tool is exemplified by its reliance on a suppressible allele—ade1-14—which provides a direct link between Aβ42 oligomerization and a selectable phenotype. The ade1-14 mutation introduces a premature stop codon (UAA) in the ADE1 gene, which encodes an enzyme in the adenine biosynthesis pathway. In a [psi−] background (i.e., with fully functional Sup35p), this mutation results in the production of a truncated, non-functional Ade1p, leading to adenine auxotrophy and the accumulation of a red pigment (phosphoribosylaminoimidazole) due to the buildup of pathway intermediates. However, when Sup35p function is compromised—as in the [PSI+] state or in cells expressing AβMRF—the ribosome occasionally reads through the premature stop codon, producing full-length Ade1p and allowing adenine synthesis to proceed. This readthrough event is stochastic but occurs at a frequency sufficient to support growth on adenine-deficient medium and to prevent red pigment accumulation.

The strength of the nonsense suppression phenotype is directly proportional to the degree of RF domain impairment, which in turn reflects the extent of AβMRF oligomerization. Thus, agents that inhibit Aβ42 oligomerization will shift the equilibrium toward the monomeric, active form of AβMRF, reducing readthrough and restoring the Ade−, red phenotype. Conversely, agents that promote oligomerization will enhance the Ade+, white phenotype. This binary, yet quantifiable, readout enables both qualitative and quantitative screening approaches, from simple visual inspection of colony color to high-throughput growth assays in liquid or solid media. Moreover, because the RF domain is essential for viability, the system is inherently selective for modulators that act specifically on the Aβ42 moiety without causing general toxicity or non-specific protein aggregation.

### Example 7

The AβMRF yeast model was validated as a platform for chemical and genetic screening by testing its responsiveness to known modulators of protein aggregation and by demonstrating its ability to distinguish between structurally similar Aβ42 variants. As shown in the preceding examples, the system is exquisitely sensitive to point mutations in Aβ42 that disrupt key oligomerization interfaces, providing a built-in positive control for screening assays. Additionally, the system responds predictably to pharmacological agents such as guanidine, which, despite its complex mechanism, produces a clear and reproducible enhancement of the oligomerization phenotype.

To illustrate the potential for chemical screening, a hypothetical library of small molecules could be arrayed in microtiter plates, and AβMRF-expressing yeast could be inoculated into each well in the presence of the test compound. After incubation, growth on -Ade medium would be assessed spectrophotometrically or by colony counting, with hits defined as compounds that significantly reduce growth compared to untreated controls. Secondary screens could then be performed to confirm the effect on oligomerization by immunoblotting and to rule out non-specific effects on cell viability or general translation fidelity. Similarly, for genetic screening, a genome-wide deletion library or overexpression library could be crossed with the AβMRF strain, and colonies could be screened for alterations in color or growth phenotype. Modifiers that enhance or suppress the AβMRF phenotype would represent candidate genes involved in Aβ42 oligomerization, stability, or clearance, providing valuable insights into the cellular pathways that govern Aβ42 toxicity.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, versatile, and physiologically relevant model system for studying the initial stages of Aβ42 oligomerization and for discovering therapeutic agents that target this critical step in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis. By integrating the molecular specificity of Aβ42 biochemistry with the genetic tractability and phenotypic simplicity of yeast, the system bridges the gap between in vitro assays and complex animal models, offering a powerful tool for both basic research and drug discovery.
