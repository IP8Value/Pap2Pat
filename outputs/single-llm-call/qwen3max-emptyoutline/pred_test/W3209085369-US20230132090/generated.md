# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of natural language processing, and more specifically to systems and methods for word sense disambiguation using semantic equivalence recognition derived from aligned lexical knowledge across multiple word sense inventories. The invention provides a novel approach to automatically determine the correct meaning of a word in context by leveraging rich semantic information from diverse dictionaries and lexical resources, thereby enabling improved performance on both standard and low-resource word sense disambiguation tasks without dependence on extensive task-specific training data.

## BACKGROUND

Word sense disambiguation (WSD) is a fundamental challenge in natural language processing that involves determining the correct meaning of a word based on its surrounding context. This task is critical for numerous downstream applications including machine translation, information retrieval, question answering, and text summarization. Traditional approaches to WSD have primarily relied on supervised learning methods that require extensive human-annotated training data, where each occurrence of a target word is manually labeled with its corresponding sense from a predefined inventory such as WordNet. However, these supervised approaches face significant limitations when dealing with rare or unseen word senses that lack sufficient training examples, commonly referred to as zero-shot or few-shot scenarios.

Contemporary state-of-the-art WSD systems have increasingly adopted pre-trained language models such as BERT and RoBERTa, which are fine-tuned on task-specific datasets to achieve improved performance. While these approaches have demonstrated remarkable success on standard benchmark datasets, they remain constrained by their dependence on specific word sense inventories and annotated training data. The performance of these models typically degrades substantially when applied to word senses that are infrequently observed or completely absent from the training corpus. Furthermore, existing models are often inventory-dependent, meaning they can only select from word senses defined within a single predefined lexical resource, typically WordNet, which limits their applicability across different linguistic contexts and domains.

Knowledge-based approaches to WSD have attempted to address some of these limitations by incorporating external lexical knowledge bases such as Wikipedia and WordNet to enhance word sense representations. Systems like BabelNet have created integrated resources by mapping encyclopedic knowledge to lexicographic knowledge, providing richer semantic information for disambiguation. However, these approaches still face challenges in effectively leveraging the complementary information available across multiple independent lexical resources, as each dictionary or knowledge base may provide different but semantically equivalent descriptions of the same underlying meanings.

Recent research has explored various architectural innovations to improve WSD performance, including sequence labeling frameworks with self-attention mechanisms, hierarchical co-attention mechanisms for generating gloss and context representations, and bi-encoder models that represent target words and senses in the same embedding space. Gloss-BERT approaches have appended gloss definitions to context sentences to create pseudo-sentences for classification, while other methods have formulated WSD as a relevance ranking task to select the most probable sense definition from candidate options. Despite these advances, the fundamental challenge of effectively utilizing rich lexical knowledge from diverse sources while maintaining robust performance across both common and rare word senses remains largely unaddressed.

The limitations of current approaches stem from their inability to recognize semantic equivalence between different expressions of the same meaning across multiple lexical resources. Professional dictionaries compiled by linguistic experts contain abundant example sentences that illustrate the usage of different word senses in context, along with detailed definitions (glosses) that capture the essential meaning of each sense. Crucially, different dictionaries often provide parallel explanations of the same underlying meanings for a given word, expressed through different linguistic formulations. This observation suggests that if these semantically equivalent glosses could be systematically aligned across different inventories, it would be possible to create a more comprehensive and robust knowledge base for word sense disambiguation that transcends the limitations of any single lexical resource.

However, the technical challenge of automatically aligning glosses across different word sense inventories has not been adequately addressed in prior art. Existing approaches either rely on a single inventory or attempt to integrate multiple resources through simple concatenation or voting mechanisms without establishing meaningful semantic correspondences between equivalent definitions. The lack of a systematic method for identifying and leveraging semantic equivalence relationships across diverse lexical resources represents a significant gap in the current state of WSD technology, limiting the ability of existing systems to generalize effectively to low-resource scenarios and achieve truly robust performance across the full spectrum of word senses.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel system and method for word sense disambiguation that overcomes the limitations of existing approaches by leveraging abundant lexical knowledge from multiple word sense inventories through a systematic gloss alignment algorithm. The invention recognizes that different dictionaries often provide semantically equivalent explanations of the same underlying meanings for a given word, expressed through different linguistic formulations, and provides a technical solution for automatically identifying and utilizing these semantic equivalence relationships.

In one aspect, the invention provides a gloss alignment algorithm that converts the problem of aligning glosses from different word sense inventories into a maximum-weighted bipartite graph matching problem. For each word appearing in multiple inventories, the algorithm retrieves the corresponding sets of glosses from two different inventories and computes textual similarity scores between all pairs of glosses using a pre-trained sentence embedding model such as SBERT. The algorithm then solves the resulting optimization problem to find the optimal matching that maximizes the total similarity score, thereby establishing semantic equivalence relationships between glosses across different inventories.

In another aspect, the invention provides a transfer learning framework that utilizes the aligned lexical knowledge to pretrain a general semantic equivalence recognizer. This recognizer is trained to determine whether a target word in a context sentence and a gloss definition are semantically equivalent, using contrastive learning with positive examples (aligned gloss-context pairs) and negative examples (non-aligned gloss-context pairs). The pretraining process leverages both cross-inventory aligned pairs and within-inventory contrastive pairs to create a rich supervision signal that captures semantic equivalence relationships across diverse lexical resources.

The general semantic equivalence recognizer can be directly applied to downstream word sense disambiguation tasks without any task-specific fine-tuning, demonstrating strong performance particularly on low-shot and zero-shot word senses that are poorly represented in traditional training datasets. Alternatively, the general model can be further fine-tuned on task-specific datasets to produce an expert model that achieves state-of-the-art performance on standard word sense disambiguation benchmarks.

The invention also provides a unified neural architecture for semantic equivalence recognition that utilizes pre-trained transformer models such as BERT or RoBERTa to generate contextual representations of target words in context and sentence-level representations of gloss definitions. The architecture combines these representations through element-wise operations and feeds them through a classification layer to predict semantic equivalence, enabling effective learning of the complex relationships between contextual word usage and definitional meaning.

Experimental results demonstrate that the invention significantly outperforms existing approaches on both standard all-words word sense disambiguation tasks and specialized low-shot evaluation benchmarks. The general model, without any task-specific training data, surpasses supervised baselines by 13.1% on zero-shot word senses. After fine-tuning with built-in training data, the expert model achieves new state-of-the-art performance, outperforming previous best models by 1.2% on all-words WSD tasks and 4.3% on low-shot WSD tasks. The invention also demonstrates superior performance on related tasks such as Word-in-Context (WiC) evaluation, improving upon large language models with significantly fewer parameters.

The technical advantages of the invention include: (1) the ability to leverage complementary lexical knowledge from multiple independent dictionaries and word sense inventories; (2) robust performance on rare and zero-shot word senses through systematic exploitation of semantic equivalence relationships; (3) reduced dependence on extensive task-specific training data; (4) improved generalization across different linguistic contexts and domains; and (5) compatibility with existing pre-trained language models and transfer learning paradigms.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for word sense disambiguation that addresses the fundamental limitations of existing approaches through systematic integration of lexical knowledge from multiple word sense inventories. The core innovation lies in the recognition that different dictionaries and lexical resources often provide semantically equivalent explanations of the same underlying meanings for a given word, expressed through different linguistic formulations, and the development of technical solutions to automatically identify and leverage these semantic equivalence relationships.

The system architecture comprises three main components: a lexical knowledge collection and preprocessing module, a gloss alignment algorithm, and a semantic equivalence recognition model with associated training methodology. Each component is designed to work synergistically with the others to create a robust and scalable word sense disambiguation system that can operate effectively across diverse linguistic contexts and resource availability scenarios.

The lexical knowledge collection and preprocessing module begins by gathering comprehensive word sense inventory data from multiple authoritative sources. In the preferred embodiment, the system collects data from six distinct word sense inventories: WordNet 3.0 and five professional dictionaries designed for advanced English learners, specifically the Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary, Merriam-Webster's Advanced Learner's Dictionary, Collins COBUILD Advanced Dictionary, Cambridge Advanced Learner's Dictionary, and Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English. These advanced learner's dictionaries are particularly valuable because they typically provide abundant example sentences to illustrate the usage of different word senses in context, which serves as crucial training data for the subsequent semantic equivalence recognition model.

The data collection process involves systematically querying each inventory for all available word entries, extracting both the gloss definitions (formal descriptions of word meanings) and the associated example sentences that demonstrate contextual usage. The system maintains careful organization of this data by part-of-speech categories and ensures proper alignment between glosses and their corresponding example sentences within each inventory. In total, the preferred embodiment collects approximately 557,800 glosses and 469,400 example sentences across all six inventories, creating a rich and diverse lexical knowledge base that significantly exceeds the coverage of any single inventory.

The gloss alignment algorithm represents the central technical innovation of the present invention. This algorithm addresses the fundamental challenge of automatically identifying semantic equivalence relationships between gloss definitions from different word sense inventories. The algorithm operates on the principle that for any given word appearing in multiple inventories, the different glosses provided by each inventory represent different linguistic expressions of a relatively small number of underlying semantic meanings. The goal is to establish correspondences between glosses that express the same meaning across different inventories.

The algorithm formulates the gloss alignment problem as a maximum-weighted bipartite graph matching problem. For each word that appears in at least two inventories, the system retrieves the corresponding sets of glosses from two different inventories, denoted as S₁ and S₂. The algorithm then computes a reward function r: S₁ × S₂ → ℝ that measures the textual similarity between each pair of glosses from the two sets. In the preferred embodiment, this reward function is implemented using a pre-trained Sentence-BERT (SBERT) model, which has demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on semantic textual similarity tasks. The SBERT model generates dense vector representations for each gloss sentence, and the cosine similarity between these vectors serves as the reward score.

The optimization problem seeks to find a matching function f: S₁ → S₂ that maximizes the total reward Σᵢ r(aᵢ, f(aᵢ)) across all glosses in S₁. This formulation ensures that the algorithm identifies the globally optimal alignment that maximizes the overall semantic correspondence between the two gloss sets, rather than making locally optimal decisions that might lead to suboptimal global alignments.

The maximum-weighted bipartite matching problem is solved using linear programming techniques. The system introduces binary variables xᵢⱼ for each possible edge between gloss i in S₁ and gloss j in S₂, where xᵢⱼ = 1 indicates that the edge is included in the matching and xᵢⱼ = 0 otherwise. The objective function maximizes the sum Σᵢⱼ wᵢⱼxᵢⱼ, where wᵢⱼ represents the textual similarity score between gloss i and gloss j. The constraints ensure that each gloss in S₁ is matched to exactly one gloss in S₂ and vice versa, creating a perfect matching between the two sets.

The implementation considers all possible combinations of inventory pairs (selecting two from the six available inventories) and applies the gloss alignment solver to all common words shared by each pair of inventories. To maintain linguistic coherence, the alignment is performed separately for each part-of-speech category, ensuring that noun glosses are only aligned with other noun glosses, verb glosses with verb glosses, and so on. This careful attention to grammatical categories prevents spurious alignments that might occur across different parts of speech. The preferred embodiment produces approximately 704,000 gloss alignment links across all inventory pairs and word categories.

The quality of the gloss alignment algorithm is rigorously evaluated through human annotation studies. Random samples of 1,000 aligned gloss pairs are presented to two human annotators who independently judge whether each pair expresses the same meaning. The inter-annotator agreement, measured by Cohen's kappa coefficient, achieves a score of 0.74, indicating substantial agreement. The overall accuracy of the alignment algorithm is measured at 87%, with part-of-speech-specific accuracies of 90% for nouns, 81% for verbs, 88% for adjectives, and 85% for adverbs. To further enhance the quality of training data, the system applies a similarity threshold of 0.6, retaining only those aligned gloss pairs with textual similarity scores above this threshold. This filtering step improves the alignment accuracy to 98% based on human evaluation, ensuring high-quality supervision signals for the subsequent training phase.

The semantic equivalence recognition model constitutes the third major component of the invention. This model is designed to learn the relationship between contextual word usage and definitional meaning by recognizing when a target word in a context sentence and a gloss definition are semantically equivalent. The model architecture is inspired by bi-encoder approaches but incorporates several key innovations that enable effective learning from the aligned lexical knowledge.

The model utilizes a pre-trained transformer architecture, such as BERT or RoBERTa, as its foundation. For each input, the system processes either a context sentence containing a target word or a gloss definition sentence. When processing a context sentence, the system extracts the contextualized embedding corresponding to the target word position. When processing a gloss definition, the system uses the [CLS] token embedding as the sentence-level representation. This dual-processing approach allows the model to generate appropriate representations for both contextual word usage and definitional meaning.

The learning objective combines these representations through a series of element-wise operations designed to capture both similarity and difference information. Specifically, the system concatenates the two representations u and v with their element-wise absolute difference |u - v| and element-wise product u • v, creating a comprehensive feature vector that captures multiple aspects of the relationship between the context and gloss representations. This combined feature vector is then passed through a linear transformation followed by a softmax activation to produce a binary classification decision indicating whether the context and gloss are semantically equivalent.

The training methodology employs a two-stage transfer learning approach that maximizes the utility of the aligned lexical knowledge. In the first stage, the system generates training instances from both cross-inventory aligned pairs and within-inventory contrastive pairs. For cross-inventory pairs, the system creates positive training instances by pairing example sentences from one inventory with aligned glosses from another inventory, and negative instances by pairing example sentences with non-aligned glosses. For within-inventory pairs, the system creates positive instances by pairing glosses with their corresponding example sentences, and negative instances by pairing glosses with example sentences from different glosses within the same inventory.

The preferred embodiment generates approximately 421,000 positive and 538,000 negative cross-inventory training pairs, along with 1,300,000 positive and 418,000 negative within-inventory training pairs. This rich and diverse training set enables the model to learn robust semantic equivalence relationships that generalize across different lexical resources and linguistic contexts.

The first stage of training produces a general semantic equivalence recognizer that can be directly applied to downstream word sense disambiguation tasks without any task-specific fine-tuning. This general model demonstrates remarkable performance, particularly on low-resource scenarios, because it has learned to recognize semantic equivalence relationships from the comprehensive aligned lexical knowledge rather than being limited to patterns observed in specific annotated datasets.

The second stage of training involves fine-tuning the general model on task-specific datasets to produce an expert model optimized for particular evaluation benchmarks. This transfer learning approach allows the model to retain the broad semantic knowledge acquired during pretraining while adapting to the specific characteristics and distributions of the target task.

The system is evaluated on multiple benchmark datasets to demonstrate its effectiveness across different scenarios. For standard all-words word sense disambiguation, the system is evaluated on the framework established by Raganato et al. (2017b), which includes five benchmark datasets from previous Senseval and SemEval competitions. The system uses SemCor as the training set and demonstrates state-of-the-art performance, with the SemEq-Large-Expert model achieving 80.7% F1-score on the ALL category, outperforming the previous best model by 1.2%.

For low-shot and zero-shot scenarios, the system is evaluated on the FEWS dataset, which specifically focuses on annotation-lacking word senses. The results show that the general model, without any task-specific training, surpasses supervised baselines by 13.1% on zero-shot word senses. After fine-tuning, the expert model achieves 82.3% accuracy on few-shot senses and 72.2% on zero-shot senses, significantly outperforming all baseline models.

The system also demonstrates superior performance on the Word-in-Context (WiC) task, which evaluates context-sensitive word meaning recognition without predefined sense inventories. By incorporating semantic equivalence knowledge from aligned inventories, the system improves RoBERTa Large's performance by 6%, surpassing much larger language models with significantly fewer parameters.

The technical implementation details include specific hyperparameter settings that optimize performance. The system uses the AdamW optimizer with initial learning rates of {1e-5, 5e-6, 2e-6}, 0.2 dropout rate, batch size of 64, and 10 training epochs. Two model sizes are considered: SemEq-Base initialized with BERT Base (12 transformer layers, 768 hidden size, 12 attention heads) and SemEq-Large initialized with RoBERTa Large (24 transformer layers, 1024 hidden size, 16 attention heads).

The invention's approach to handling different parts of speech is particularly noteworthy. The system maintains separate processing pipelines for nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs, ensuring that the semantic equivalence relationships are learned within appropriate grammatical categories. This attention to linguistic structure contributes to the system's robust performance across all word types, as evidenced by consistent improvements in F1-scores for each part-of-speech category in the evaluation results.

The system's ability to handle rare and zero-shot word senses represents a significant advancement over existing approaches. Traditional supervised models struggle with these scenarios because they rely on patterns observed in training data, which are necessarily absent for unseen senses. The present invention overcomes this limitation by learning general semantic equivalence relationships from the aligned lexical knowledge, which provides rich definitional information even for rare senses that may not appear frequently in annotated corpora.
