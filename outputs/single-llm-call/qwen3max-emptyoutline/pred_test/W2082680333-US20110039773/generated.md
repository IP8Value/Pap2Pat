# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) and their therapeutic applications in regenerative medicine, particularly in the context of bone formation and repair. More specifically, the invention provides novel modified BMP polypeptides that exhibit enhanced resistance to inhibition by endogenous extracellular antagonists such as noggin, thereby improving their osteogenic potency and efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo settings. The invention further encompasses nucleic acid molecules encoding these modified BMPs, expression vectors containing such nucleic acids, recombinant host cells engineered to produce the modified proteins, and methods for producing and purifying the proteins. Additionally, the invention includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising the modified BMPs and methods for using them in the treatment of bone-related disorders, including non-union fractures, spinal fusion defects, osteoporosis, and other conditions requiring enhanced osteogenesis. The core innovation lies in the identification of a critical amino acid residue—lysine at position 60 of the mature BMP-6 domain—that confers marked resistance to noggin-mediated inhibition, and the strategic engineering of this feature into other BMP family members, such as BMP-7 and BMP-2, to generate variants with superior biological activity. This technological advancement addresses a major limitation of currently approved recombinant BMP therapeutics, which require high doses due to rapid neutralization by natural inhibitors like noggin, thereby offering a path toward more effective, lower-dose, and potentially safer bone regeneration therapies.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) are a subgroup of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) superfamily of secreted signaling molecules that play pivotal roles in embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and postnatal repair processes, most notably in the induction of bone and cartilage formation. Since their initial discovery by Marshall Urist in the 1960s, BMPs have been the subject of intense research aimed at harnessing their potent osteoinductive properties for clinical applications. Among the more than 20 known BMPs, recombinant human BMP-2 (rhBMP-2) and recombinant human BMP-7 (rhBMP-7, also known as OP-1) have received regulatory approval for specific orthopedic and spinal fusion indications. Despite their demonstrated clinical efficacy, the therapeutic utility of these proteins is significantly hampered by several drawbacks, chief among them being the requirement for supraphysiological doses—often in the range of milligrams per application—to achieve a clinically meaningful outcome. This high-dose requirement not only increases the cost of treatment but also correlates with a higher incidence of adverse effects, including ectopic bone formation, inflammatory responses, and potential carcinogenic risks, thereby limiting their broader application.

A primary biological mechanism underlying this inefficiency is the presence of a robust negative feedback loop mediated by endogenous extracellular BMP antagonists. These naturally occurring proteins, including noggin, chordin, gremlin, and sclerostin, bind directly to BMP ligands with high affinity, thereby preventing their interaction with cell surface receptors and effectively neutralizing their signaling activity. Noggin, in particular, is a well-characterized and potent antagonist that is itself transcriptionally upregulated by BMP signaling, creating a self-limiting circuit that tightly controls the spatial and temporal extent of BMP activity in vivo. Following the surgical implantation of a recombinant BMP, the local tissue environment rapidly responds by upregulating noggin expression, which then binds to and inactivates a significant fraction of the administered therapeutic protein before it can engage its cognate receptors on target progenitor cells. This dynamic renders a large portion of the delivered dose pharmacologically inert, necessitating the use of excessive initial quantities to overcome this inhibitory barrier.

The structural and functional relationships among different BMP family members are complex. While BMP-6 and BMP-7 share a high degree of amino acid sequence identity (approximately 73% in their mature domains) and signal through overlapping sets of type I and type II serine/threonine kinase receptors, they exhibit markedly different biological potencies in osteogenic assays. Prior art has documented that BMP-6 is a more potent inducer of osteoblast differentiation in vitro and bone formation in vivo compared to BMP-7. However, the molecular basis for this differential activity has remained elusive. It was previously unknown whether this difference stemmed from variations in receptor binding affinity, downstream signal transduction kinetics, or susceptibility to extracellular antagonists. Furthermore, while the crystal structures of several BMPs, including BMP-7 and BMP-6, have been solved, the precise structural determinants that govern their interaction with noggin and, consequently, their functional resistance to its inhibition had not been identified. Without this knowledge, rational engineering of BMP variants with improved pharmacological profiles was not feasible. The present invention, therefore, addresses a critical gap in the art by elucidating the key molecular feature responsible for the differential noggin sensitivity between BMP-6 and BMP-7 and by demonstrating a generalizable strategy for engineering this resistance into other therapeutically relevant BMPs, thereby providing a foundation for the next generation of more effective and efficient bone regenerative therapeutics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a solution to the long-standing problem of inefficient BMP-based bone regeneration therapies by identifying a specific molecular determinant of resistance to the endogenous BMP antagonist, noggin. The invention is based on the surprising discovery that BMP-6, a member of the BMP family, exhibits a significantly higher intrinsic resistance to noggin-mediated inhibition compared to its close homolog, BMP-7, and other clinically used BMPs like BMP-2 and BMP-4. This resistance is a key factor contributing to the superior osteogenic potency of BMP-6 observed in both cellular and animal models. Through a detailed comparative analysis, the inventors have pinpointed a single amino acid residue at position 60 of the mature BMP-6 polypeptide—a lysine (Lys60)—as the primary structural element responsible for this enhanced resistance. In contrast, the corresponding position in BMP-7 is occupied by a glutamic acid (Glu60), which renders it highly susceptible to noggin inhibition.

Building upon this fundamental insight, the invention provides a series of novel, engineered BMP polypeptides. These include modified forms of BMP-7 in which the native glutamic acid at position 60 is substituted with a lysine (e.g., BMP-7 E60K), resulting in a variant that displays a marked increase in resistance to noggin, comparable to that of native BMP-6. The invention further extends this principle to other BMP family members, such as BMP-2, where the analogous proline residue at position 36 (corresponding to BMP-6 Lys60) is replaced with a lysine (BMP-2 P36K), thereby conferring increased noggin resistance. The invention also encompasses chimeric BMP molecules, such as the 80-1 chimera, which incorporates a specific segment of the BMP-6 mature domain (residues 45-80) into the BMP-7 backbone, and which likewise demonstrates high resistance to noggin.

In addition to the modified proteins themselves, the invention provides the genetic tools necessary for their production. This includes isolated nucleic acid molecules encoding the aforementioned modified BMP polypeptides, as well as recombinant expression vectors capable of directing the expression of these nucleic acids in a suitable host cell. The invention further provides host cells, including mammalian, bacterial, yeast, or insect cells, that have been genetically engineered to contain and express these vectors, thereby serving as factories for the production of the novel BMP variants. A comprehensive set of methods is also provided for the expression and purification of these modified proteins to a high degree of homogeneity, suitable for therapeutic use.

The invention’s utility is demonstrated through a series of rigorous functional assays, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assays in osteosarcoma cells, BMP-responsive element (BRE)-luciferase reporter gene assays in various cell lines, and quantitative real-time PCR analysis of downstream osteogenic genes in primary human mesenchymal stem cells. These assays consistently show that the modified BMPs of the invention maintain or enhance their ability to activate canonical BMP signaling pathways (e.g., Smad1/5/8 phosphorylation) while being significantly less inhibited by exogenous noggin. Crucially, in an in vivo rabbit ulna critical size defect model, the superior bone-healing capacity of BMP-6 over BMP-7 is confirmed, validating the physiological relevance of noggin resistance. The invention thus offers a powerful platform for developing next-generation BMP therapeutics that can achieve equivalent or superior clinical outcomes at substantially lower doses than current products, thereby mitigating the associated risks and costs.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Bone Morphogenetic Proteins

Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs) are a class of secreted, dimeric glycoproteins that belong to the larger TGF-β superfamily. They are synthesized as precursor proteins consisting of an N-terminal signal peptide, a pro-domain, and a C-terminal mature domain. The mature domain, which is proteolytically cleaved from the precursor, is the biologically active moiety responsible for receptor binding and signal transduction. The mature domains of BMPs typically form a characteristic cystine knot structure, stabilized by multiple disulfide bonds, which is essential for their stability and function. BMPs signal by binding to a complex of type I and type II serine/threonine kinase receptors on the cell surface. This binding event leads to the phosphorylation and activation of the type I receptor by the type II receptor, which in turn phosphorylates downstream intracellular effectors, primarily the receptor-regulated Smads (R-Smads: Smad1, Smad5, and Smad8). The phosphorylated R-Smads then form a complex with the common mediator Smad4 and translocate to the nucleus, where they regulate the transcription of a wide array of target genes involved in cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. In the context of skeletal biology, BMP signaling is a master regulator of osteoblast differentiation and bone formation, making it a prime target for therapeutic intervention in bone repair and regeneration. The present invention focuses on specific members of the BMP family, particularly BMP-2, BMP-6, and BMP-7, and their engineered variants, to create proteins with optimized therapeutic properties for these applications.

### Interactions Between BMPs and Their Antagonists

The biological activity of BMPs in vivo is not solely determined by their concentration and receptor affinity but is critically modulated by a network of extracellular antagonists. These antagonists act as natural rheostats to fine-tune BMP signaling gradients during development and to prevent uncontrolled signaling in adult tissues. Among these, noggin is one of the most potent and well-studied. Noggin is a secreted protein that binds directly to the mature domain of specific BMPs with high affinity, physically blocking their ability to interact with their cognate cell surface receptors. The binding interface between noggin and BMPs is extensive and involves key residues on the so-called “wrist” epitope of the BMP, which is the primary site for type I receptor engagement. This direct steric hindrance effectively neutralizes the BMP ligand. Importantly, the expression of the noggin gene itself is a direct transcriptional target of BMP signaling, establishing a classic negative feedback loop. When a BMP ligand activates its pathway, it induces the expression of noggin, which then diffuses and binds to free BMP, thereby dampening the signal. This elegant regulatory mechanism is essential for maintaining tissue homeostasis but poses a significant challenge for therapeutic applications. When a recombinant BMP is administered clinically, it inadvertently triggers this same feedback loop, leading to the rapid upregulation of noggin in the local microenvironment, which then sequesters a large portion of the therapeutic protein, rendering it ineffective. The present invention directly addresses this problem by engineering BMP variants that can evade this inhibitory mechanism, thereby breaking the negative feedback loop and allowing for sustained and more potent signaling.

### Generating Modified BMPs

The core of the present invention is the rational design and generation of modified BMP polypeptides that exhibit enhanced resistance to noggin inhibition. This was achieved through a combination of comparative functional analysis, sequence alignment, and targeted mutagenesis. The process began with the observation that native BMP-6 is significantly more resistant to noggin than BMP-7, despite their high sequence similarity. To map the region responsible for this difference, a series of chimeric proteins were constructed by swapping defined segments of the mature domain of BMP-6 into the corresponding regions of BMP-7. Through systematic testing of these chimeras in functional assays, a specific region encompassing residues 45-80 of the mature BMP-6 domain was identified as being sufficient to confer high noggin resistance to the BMP-7 backbone, as exemplified by the 80-1 chimera. Subsequent fine-mapping within this region, guided by sequence alignments of various BMP family members and their known sensitivities to noggin, led to the identification of a single, critical amino acid at position 60 (using BMP-6 mature domain numbering). The presence of a lysine (Lys) at this position correlated strongly with noggin resistance, as seen in both BMP-6 and BMP-9. In contrast, BMP-7, which has a glutamic acid (Glu) at this position, is highly sensitive. Therefore, the primary method for generating the modified BMPs of the invention involves introducing a point mutation at the codon corresponding to this position in the target BMP gene. For instance, in the case of BMP-7, the codon for Glu60 is mutated to encode a lysine, resulting in the BMP-7 E60K variant. Similarly, in BMP-2, where the analogous residue is a proline (Pro36), a mutation is introduced to change it to a lysine, yielding the BMP-2 P36K variant. These mutations are designed to be conservative in terms of the overall protein fold but transformative in terms of the protein's interaction with the noggin antagonist.

### Preparing Nucleic Acids Encoding Modified BMPs

The preparation of nucleic acids encoding the modified BMPs of the invention is a foundational step in their production and characterization. These nucleic acids are typically DNA molecules, either genomic or, more commonly, cDNA, that have been engineered to contain the specific mutations described above. The process begins with a wild-type nucleic acid template encoding the full-length precursor of the target BMP (e.g., human BMP-7, GenBank accession number NM_001719). Using standard molecular biology techniques, this template is subjected to site-directed mutagenesis. The QuikChange protocol, or any other reliable method for introducing specific point mutations, is employed to alter the nucleotide sequence at the precise location corresponding to the desired amino acid substitution. For example, to create the BMP-7 E60K variant, the nucleotides encoding the glutamic acid codon (e.g., GAA or GAG) are changed to a lysine codon (e.g., AAA or AAG). The resulting mutated DNA is then amplified by PCR, if necessary, and verified by DNA sequencing to ensure that the intended mutation is present and that no unintended secondary mutations have been introduced. The final product is an isolated, purified nucleic acid molecule that is distinct from its natural counterpart and is specifically designed to encode a BMP polypeptide with enhanced noggin resistance. These nucleic acids serve as the starting material for all subsequent steps, including cloning into expression vectors and transformation into host cells.

### Expression Vectors

To enable the production of the modified BMP polypeptides, the engineered nucleic acids must be inserted into a suitable expression vector. An expression vector is a specialized plasmid or viral DNA construct that contains the necessary genetic elements to drive the transcription and translation of a cloned gene in a host cell. The choice of vector is dictated by the intended host system and the need for proper post-translational processing of the BMP protein, which is a secreted glycoprotein. For the purposes of the present invention, mammalian expression vectors are preferred because they provide the appropriate cellular machinery for correct folding, dimerization, and glycosylation of the BMP precursor. The expression vector used in the exemplary embodiments is a proprietary mammalian expression vector, but any commercially available or custom-designed vector with the following features would be suitable. The vector must contain a strong, constitutive or inducible promoter (e.g., CMV, SV40, or EF1α) positioned upstream of a multiple cloning site (MCS). The engineered BMP nucleic acid, typically containing the entire open reading frame from the start codon to the stop codon, is inserted into the MCS downstream of the promoter. The vector should also include a polyadenylation signal sequence to ensure proper mRNA processing and stability. Additionally, for ease of purification, the vector may be engineered to include a tag sequence (e.g., a His6-tag, FLAG-tag, or Fc-fusion) either at the N- or C-terminus of the BMP coding sequence, although the examples provided herein describe the production of untagged, native-sequence proteins. Finally, the vector must contain a selectable marker, such as a gene conferring resistance to an antibiotic like Geneticin (G418), to allow for the selection and maintenance of stably transfected host cell lines.

### Transfection/Transformation

Once the expression vector containing the nucleic acid encoding the modified BMP is constructed, it must be introduced into a host cell to initiate the production of the protein. This process is referred to as transfection when the host is a eukaryotic cell (e.g., mammalian, insect) and transformation when the host is a prokaryotic cell (e.g., bacteria). Given the requirement for complex post-translational modifications, the present invention primarily utilizes transfection of mammalian cells. The exemplary host cell line used is HEK293T, a highly transfectable derivative of human embryonic kidney cells. Transfection is performed using a chemical reagent that facilitates the uptake of the plasmid DNA across the cell membrane. In the described experiments, FuGENE 6 or FuGENE HD reagents from Roche Applied Science were used according to the manufacturer's instructions. The general procedure involves mixing the purified plasmid DNA with the transfection reagent in a serum-free medium, incubating the mixture for a short period to allow complex formation, and then adding the DNA-reagent complexes to the cultured host cells. The cells are then returned to the incubator to allow for the expression of the transfected gene. For stable, long-term production, the transfected cells are subjected to selection with an antibiotic (e.g., Geneticin) to kill any non-transfected cells, resulting in a population of cells that have integrated the expression vector into their genome and continuously produce the modified BMP. For transient, high-yield production, the cells are simply harvested a few days after transfection, as was done in the experimental procedures for small- and medium-scale protein production.

### Hosts for Expression of Modified BMPs

The selection of an appropriate host cell system is critical for the successful production of functional, bioactive modified BMPs. The host must be capable of performing the necessary post-translational modifications, including signal peptide cleavage, pro-domain processing, correct disulfide bond formation, dimerization, and glycosylation. While bacterial systems like *E. coli* are simple and inexpensive, they lack the machinery for these eukaryotic-specific modifications, often resulting in the production of insoluble, inactive inclusion bodies. Yeast and insect cell systems offer some improvements but may still produce proteins with non-human glycosylation patterns that could affect stability or immunogenicity. Therefore, the preferred hosts for the expression of the modified BMPs of the present invention are mammalian cells. The HEK293T cell line, as mentioned, is a workhorse for transient expression due to its high transfection efficiency and robust protein production. For the generation of stable, clonal cell lines for large-scale manufacturing, Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells are the industry standard. CHO cells are well-characterized, can be grown in suspension culture in large bioreactors, and produce proteins with human-like glycosylation. In the experimental procedures, wild-type recombinant human BMP-2, BMP-6, and BMP-7 were produced in CHO cells, highlighting their suitability. Other potential mammalian host cells include COS cells, BHK cells, and NS0 myeloma cells. The choice of host ultimately depends on the scale of production, the required purity and quality of the final product, and regulatory considerations for clinical use.

### Expression Methods

The expression of the modified BMPs in the chosen host cells follows standard cell culture and protein production protocols. The host cells, such as HEK293T, are first maintained in a suitable growth medium, for example, Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and antibiotics, under standard conditions of 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. For transient expression, the cells are seeded in culture vessels (e.g., 6-well plates for small scale, 150-cm² or 225-cm² flasks for medium/large scale) at a density that ensures they are approximately 70-80% confluent at the time of transfection. On the following day, the cells are transfected with the expression vector encoding the modified BMP using a transfection reagent like FuGENE. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the growth medium is replaced with a production medium that is typically low in serum (e.g., 0.5% FBS) to minimize the contamination of the final product with bovine proteins. The cells are then allowed to secrete the recombinant BMP into the conditioned medium over a period of 48 to 72 hours. The conditioned medium is collected, clarified by centrifugation to remove any detached cells and debris, and is then ready for the purification process. For stable cell lines, the process is similar, but the cells are continuously cultured in the presence of a selective agent, and the conditioned medium is harvested on a regular schedule without the need for repeated transfection.

### Purification

The purification of the modified BMPs from the conditioned medium is a multi-step process designed to isolate the protein of interest from a complex mixture of host cell proteins, media components, and other secreted factors. The goal is to obtain a highly pure, homogeneous, and bioactive protein preparation suitable for functional assays and, ultimately, for therapeutic use. The exemplary purification protocol for the 80-1 chimera and the BMP-7 E60K variant, as described in the experimental procedures, illustrates a robust and scalable approach. The first step is a bulk precipitation to concentrate the protein and remove a large fraction of contaminants. In this case, ammonium sulfate precipitation at 50% saturation was used, which is a classic method for precipitating proteins based on their solubility. The precipitated protein pellet is then reconstituted in a buffer compatible with the next purification step. The second step is immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), which exploits the affinity of histidine residues for nickel ions. Although the described proteins were untagged, the native BMP sequence may contain surface-exposed histidines that allow for this purification. The reconstituted protein is loaded onto a nickel-IMAC column, and after washing away non-bound proteins, the bound BMP is eluted using a buffer containing a high concentration of imidazole or, in this specific case, 6 M urea to disrupt the binding. The urea is later removed during subsequent steps. The third and final polishing step is high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), which provides high-resolution separation based on the protein's size, charge, or hydrophobicity. The IMAC-purified fractions are analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blot to identify those containing the correct BMP, which are then pooled and subjected to HPLC. The resulting HPLC fractions are again analyzed by SDS-PAGE and silver staining to confirm purity. The pure fractions are pooled, lyophilized (freeze-dried) to a stable powder, and stored at -80°C until use. This multi-step purification strategy ensures the recovery of a highly active and pure modified BMP protein.

## EXAMPLES

### BMP Induction of Alkaline Phosphatase Activity

The osteogenic potential of various BMPs was initially assessed by their ability to induce alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, a well-established early marker of osteoblast differentiation, in the rat osteosarcoma cell line ROS 17/2.8. In this assay, a panel of recombinant human BMPs, including BMP-2, BMP-4, BMP-5, BMP-6, BMP-7, GDF-5, and GDF-6, were tested across a nine-point dose-response curve. The results revealed a striking hierarchy of potency, with BMP-6 emerging as the most potent inducer of ALP activity, exhibiting an EC50 value of 14 ng/ml. This was followed by BMP-7, BMP-4, BMP-2, and BMP-5, in descending order of potency. Notably, GDF-5 and GDF-6 showed only marginal activity in this assay, confirming their more limited role in osteoblast differentiation compared to the canonical BMPs. The significant difference in potency between the highly homologous BMP-6 and BMP-7 (which share 73% amino acid identity in their mature domains) was a key observation that prompted the subsequent investigation into the underlying mechanisms. This finding was corroborated in other cell models, such as C2C12 myoblasts and C3H10T1/2 mesenchymal progenitor cells, where BMP-6 consistently demonstrated superior activity in inducing both ALP and BRE-luciferase reporter gene expression compared to an equivalent dose of BMP-7. This consistent superiority across multiple in vitro systems established BMP-6 as a uniquely potent osteogenic factor and laid the groundwork for the hypothesis that its resistance to endogenous inhibitors might be a key factor in its enhanced biological activity.

### Noggin Inhibition of a Panel of Exemplary BMPs and Related Proteins

To test the hypothesis that differential sensitivity to the endogenous antagonist noggin underlies the observed differences in BMP potency, a direct comparison of the susceptibility of various BMPs to noggin-mediated inhibition was performed. In this experiment, the ability of BMP-2, BMP-4, BMP-6, and BMP-7 to induce ALP activity in ROS 17/2.8 cells was measured in the presence of increasing concentrations of recombinant human noggin-Fc. The data were fitted to a non-linear regression model to generate dose-response curves for noggin inhibition, from which the IC50 values (the concentration of noggin required to inhibit 50% of the BMP activity) were calculated. The results were unequivocal: BMP-4 was the most sensitive to noggin, with the lowest IC50, followed by BMP-2 and then BMP-7. In stark contrast, BMP-6 demonstrated a remarkable resistance to noggin, with an IC50 value that was 4 to 16 times higher than that of the other BMPs tested. This profound resistance was not an artifact of the specific cell line or assay, as it was confirmed using a BRE-luciferase reporter gene assay in C2C12 cells and in the stably transfected A549-BRE cell line. In these transcriptional reporter assays, BMP-6-induced luciferase activity was largely unaffected by noggin concentrations that completely abolished the activity of BMP-7. This series of experiments provided compelling evidence that the intrinsic resistance of BMP-6 to noggin is a major determinant of its superior osteogenic potency, as it allows its signal to persist in the face of the negative feedback loop that rapidly shuts down the activity of other BMPs.

### BMP-6 Induction of Downstream Genes in Primary Human Bone Marrow-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells is Less Susceptible to Noggin Inhibition

To further validate the physiological relevance of BMP-6's noggin resistance and to extend the findings to a clinically relevant human cell type, the experiment was conducted in primary human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs). These multipotent progenitor cells are the primary targets for BMP-based bone regeneration therapies in humans. hMSCs were stimulated with either BMP-6 or BMP-7, in the presence or absence of noggin, and the expression of key downstream osteogenic target genes—Id-1, Dlx-5, Msx-2, and noggin itself—was quantified by quantitative real-time PCR. The results were strikingly consistent with the previous cell line data. The induction of all four target genes by BMP-7 was significantly and potently inhibited by noggin. In fact, the auto-regulation of noggin by BMP-7 was the most sensitive, being almost completely abrogated by the antagonist. In contrast, the induction of Id-1, Dlx-5, and noggin by BMP-6 was entirely unaffected by noggin. Only the induction of Msx-2 by BMP-6 showed a marginal decrease in the presence of noggin. This experiment is crucial because it demonstrates that the resistance of BMP-6 to noggin is not a peculiarity of immortalized cell lines but is a fundamental property that holds true in primary human stem cells, the very cells that are expected to mediate the therapeutic effect of BMPs in a clinical setting. This finding strongly supports the translational potential of leveraging BMP-6's unique property, or engineering it into other BMPs, for improved human therapies.

### BMP-7 Variant Proteins with Marked Resistance to Noggin Inhibition

The ultimate goal of the present invention is not merely to describe a natural phenomenon but to engineer novel therapeutic proteins based on this knowledge. To this end, the inventors created a series of modified BMP-7 proteins designed to incorporate the noggin-resistance feature of BMP-6. The first approach involved creating chimeric proteins. By aligning the sequences of BMP-6 and BMP-7 and guided by the functional data from the initial chimeras, a specific chimera named 80-1 was generated, which contains the BMP-6 sequence from residues 45-80 grafted into the BMP-7 backbone. When tested in the A549-BRE reporter assay, the purified 80-1 protein exhibited a level of resistance to noggin that was virtually identical to that of native BMP-6, confirming that this segment harbors the key determinants of resistance. The second, more precise approach involved site-directed mutagenesis to introduce a single amino acid substitution. Based on the sequence alignment showing that Lys60 in BMP-6 corresponds to Glu60 in BMP-7, the BMP-7 E60K mutant was created. Functional testing in the BRE-luciferase assay demonstrated that this single point mutation was sufficient to confer a significant increase in noggin resistance to BMP-7. Additional mutants, such as Y65N and a triple mutant R48Q/E60K/Y65N, were also generated, but the E60K single mutant showed the most dramatic and clear effect. This example provides definitive proof-of-concept that the molecular basis of noggin resistance can be rationally engineered into a therapeutically relevant BMP, thereby creating a novel protein with a superior pharmacological profile.

### Example 5

The principles of the invention were further extended to another clinically important BMP, BMP-2. A sequence alignment of the mature domains of BMP-2, -4, -5, -6, -7, and -9 revealed that the position corresponding to BMP-6 Lys60 is occupied by a proline in both BMP-2 and BMP-4. Given that BMP-9, which also has a lysine at this position, was found to be highly resistant to noggin, the inventors hypothesized that introducing a lysine at this position in BMP-2 would increase its resistance. Accordingly, a BMP-2 P36K mutant was generated (where Pro36 is the residue in BMP-2 that aligns with Lys60 in BMP-6). When tested side-by-side with wild-type BMP-2, BMP-6, BMP-7, BMP-9, and the BMP-7 E60K mutant in a noggin inhibition assay, the BMP-2 P36K variant indeed showed a significant increase in resistance to noggin compared to its wild-type counterpart. Although the magnitude of the effect was less pronounced than that observed with the BMP-7 E60K mutation, the result was clear and consistent with the central hypothesis of the invention. This final example demonstrates the generalizability of the discovery. The key molecular feature—the presence of a lysine at the critical position—is not exclusive to BMP-6 but can be successfully transferred to other members of the BMP family to enhance their resistance to noggin-mediated inhibition. This opens the door to a broad platform technology for the engineering of a new generation of BMP therapeutics with improved efficacy, lower required dosing, and a better safety profile, addressing the major limitations of the current standard of care.
