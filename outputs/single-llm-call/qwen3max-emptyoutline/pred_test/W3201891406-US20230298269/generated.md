# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of computer graphics and differentiable rendering, and more specifically to systems, methods, and apparatuses for rendering explicit and implicit surface representations with accurate, smooth derivatives—including at occlusion boundaries—by combining non-differentiable rasterization with differentiable splatting. The invention enables efficient gradient-based optimization of scene parameters for applications in inverse rendering, pose estimation, shape reconstruction, neural rendering acceleration, and parametric surface fitting, while supporting a broad range of geometric representations including triangle meshes, parametric surfaces such as B-spline patches, and isosurfaces derived from implicit functions or volumetric fields such as those produced by Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF). The disclosed technology bridges the gap between traditional rasterization pipelines and modern differentiable rendering requirements by decoupling the computationally intensive but non-differentiable geometry sampling step from the differentiable shading and image formation steps, thereby enabling automatic differentiation without custom gradient implementations and facilitating both forward-mode and reverse-mode differentiation for robust optimization.

## BACKGROUND

Differentiable rendering has emerged as a critical tool in computer vision, graphics, and machine learning, enabling end-to-end optimization of 3D scene parameters from 2D image observations. Traditional rendering pipelines, particularly those based on triangle mesh rasterization, are inherently non-differentiable at occlusion boundaries and topological changes, which poses significant challenges for gradient-based inference and learning. Early approaches to differentiable rendering focused on specialized shape models such as morphable face models or heightfields, where analytical gradients could be derived due to constrained parameterizations. However, these methods lack generality and cannot handle arbitrary 3D geometry or complex lighting and material models.

With the rise of deep learning and 3D reconstruction, there has been increasing demand for differentiable renderers that support general triangle meshes. Several categories of solutions have been proposed. Gradient replacement methods, such as OpenDR, DiRT, and Neural Mesh Renderer (NMR), modify the backward pass using heuristic rules or image-space filtering to define smooth gradients while leaving the forward rendering unchanged. While effective in some scenarios, these approaches often suffer from a mismatch between the forward model and its gradient approximation, leading to convergence instabilities during optimization. Moreover, they typically implement only reverse-mode (backpropagation) gradients, requiring additional engineering effort to support forward-mode differentiation or higher-order derivatives, which are essential for advanced optimizers like Levenberg-Marquardt.

Edge sampling techniques, exemplified by Redner and nvdiffrast, explicitly compute and differentiate through occluding silhouette edges. These methods provide physically motivated gradients but incur computational costs that scale with mesh complexity, as they require identifying and processing all relevant silhouette edges per frame. Although highly optimized GPU kernels can mitigate this overhead—as in nvdiffrast—the resulting implementations are hardware-specific, difficult to extend, and not easily portable across platforms or frameworks.

Reparameterization-based methods, developed primarily in the context of Monte Carlo path tracing, address discontinuities by reformulating the rendering integral to avoid direct differentiation through visibility events. While theoretically sound, these techniques are tied to stochastic ray tracing and do not integrate naturally with rasterization-based pipelines, which remain dominant in real-time and interactive applications due to their efficiency.

Forward-rendering approaches with built-in smoothness, such as Soft Rasterizer and related soft visibility models, modify the forward pass itself to produce inherently differentiable outputs. These methods often rely on costly operations like closest-point queries or mesh-wide distance computations, limiting scalability to high-resolution geometry. Furthermore, they may introduce visual artifacts or blur that degrade rendering fidelity, especially when sharp features are required.

In parallel, volumetric representations—particularly Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF)—have gained prominence for view synthesis and 3D reconstruction. NeRF represents a scene as a continuous 5D function mapping spatial position and viewing direction to color and density, rendered via volume rendering along rays. This formulation is naturally differentiable and handles occlusions smoothly, but at the cost of extreme computational expense: each pixel requires hundreds of network evaluations during ray marching, making real-time rendering infeasible. Attempts to extract explicit surfaces from NeRF (e.g., via Marching Cubes) yield triangle meshes, but the extraction process is non-differentiable due to topological ambiguities and singularities when grid values are nearly equal, preventing end-to-end optimization of the underlying density field through the surface representation.

Implicit surface rendering faces similar challenges. While signed distance functions (SDFs) support topology changes, computing visibility and derivatives at occlusion boundaries remains nontrivial. Some methods assume user-provided occlusion masks or approximate visibility via nearest background hits, but these fail in self-occluding scenes common in indoor or object-centric settings. MeshSDF attempts to combine implicit surface sampling with differentiable visibility but relies on SDF-specific gradient formulations that do not generalize to arbitrary isosurfaces and restricts surface parameterization to neural networks.

Surface splatting—rendering point clouds with disk-like primitives—has also been adapted for differentiable rendering. However, splatting typically treats geometry as an unstructured point set, lacking true surface connectivity, and suffers from sampling inconsistencies during optimization as point distributions evolve. Moreover, fixed splat placements can lead to under- or over-sampling, degrading both visual quality and gradient accuracy.

A fundamental limitation across existing methods is the tight coupling between geometry processing and differentiation. Most require either custom gradient code, specialized hardware acceleration, or modifications to the forward rendering model that compromise performance or visual fidelity. There remains a need for a general, efficient, and framework-agnostic differentiable rendering technique that supports diverse surface representations—explicit and implicit—without sacrificing derivative quality at occlusion boundaries, while remaining compatible with standard automatic differentiation systems and scalable to complex scenes.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel differentiable rendering method termed “rasterize-then-splat” (RtS) that overcomes the limitations of prior art by decoupling non-differentiable geometry sampling from differentiable image formation. The method comprises three sequential stages: (1) non-differentiable rasterization of the surface to produce screen-space parameter buffers; (2) differentiable deferred shading of surface attributes using any programmable shading function; and (3) depth-aware, multi-layer differentiable splatting to generate a final image with smooth derivatives at occlusion boundaries.

In the first stage, a sampling function U(θ) processes scene parameters θ—which may include vertex positions, control points, implicit field coefficients, camera pose, or other geometric descriptors—and outputs non-differentiable per-pixel surface parameters U_k for k = 1 to K layers, where K is the maximum number of depth layers considered. This sampling step leverages conventional, highly optimized rasterization techniques (e.g., Z-buffering with depth peeling for meshes, adaptive subdivision for parametric surfaces, or Marching Cubes for implicit isosurfaces) to resolve visibility and occlusion without requiring gradient computation. Critically, because derivatives are not needed with respect to the sampling pattern itself, U may act as a “black-box oracle,” enabling the use of any existing surface extraction and rasterization algorithm regardless of its differentiability.

In the second stage, an evaluation function G(θ, U_k) interpolates or evaluates surface attributes—such as 3D positions, normals, texture coordinates, or material properties—from the sampled parameters U_k to produce geometry buffers (G-buffers) G_k. This function is expressed entirely within an automatic differentiation framework (e.g., TensorFlow, PyTorch), ensuring that gradients flow correctly from the output image back to the original scene parameters θ. For triangle meshes, G performs barycentric interpolation of vertex attributes; for parametric surfaces like B-splines, it evaluates the basis functions at sampled patch parameters; for implicit surfaces, it reconstructs surface points by linear interpolation along Marching Cubes edges. The key insight is that attribute evaluation is locally smooth and differentiable wherever the surface is well-defined, even if the global sampling process is not.

In the third stage, a deferred shading function C processes the G-buffers to compute shaded color buffers C_k, which may incorporate complex lighting, textures, or neural radiance models (e.g., a NeRF network used as a surface light field). These colors are then passed to a splatting function S, which renders each surface sample as a small Gaussian-shaped splat centered at its projected screen position. To preserve occlusion relationships, S employs a multi-layer accumulation strategy: for each target pixel, incoming splats are classified as occluding (in front), coincident (on the visible surface), or occluded (behind) based on depth comparisons between source and destination layers. Separate accumulation buffers are maintained for each category, normalized independently, and composited in back-to-front order using over-blending. This ensures that derivatives at occlusion boundaries reflect correct visibility changes, avoiding spurious gradients that plague single-layer splatting.

The splat kernel uses a narrow Gaussian (σ = 0.5 pixels) with a small additive constant (ε = 0.05) in the weight normalization to guarantee stable gradient computation even near image boundaries. The entire pipeline—shading, projection, and splatting—is implemented using standard differentiable operations, eliminating the need for custom gradient definitions. Consequently, both forward-mode and reverse-mode automatic differentiation are natively supported, enabling efficient computation of full Jacobians for second-order optimizers.

The invention is applicable to a wide range of surface representations. For triangle meshes, it achieves performance competitive with hand-optimized GPU renderers while offering greater flexibility. For parametric surfaces such as bicubic B-splines or subdivision surfaces, it enables differentiable rendering for the first time by bypassing the need to differentiate through adaptive tessellation. For implicit surfaces, it allows end-to-end optimization of grid-based or neural density fields through their isosurfaces, sidestepping the non-differentiability of Marching Cubes. Notably, the method can convert a pretrained NeRF into a surface light field representation, reducing rendering cost by over 100× (e.g., from 128 to 1 network evaluations per pixel) with minimal loss in visual quality (<0.3 dB PSNR on synthetic scenes).

Experimental results demonstrate superior derivative accuracy compared to baselines, robust convergence in inverse rendering tasks (pose estimation, mesh deformation, parametric surface fitting, implicit shape optimization), and scalability to million-face meshes. The method requires no custom CUDA kernels, integrates seamlessly with existing deep learning frameworks, and maintains compatibility with standard graphics APIs for the non-differentiable rasterization step. By separating concerns between visibility resolution and gradient computation, the invention provides a general, efficient, and practical solution for differentiable surface rendering across diverse domains.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The rasterize-then-splat (RtS) method disclosed herein provides a unified framework for differentiable rendering of both explicit and implicit surface representations. The core innovation lies in the strategic separation of the rendering pipeline into a non-differentiable sampling phase and a fully differentiable evaluation-and-splatting phase. This architecture leverages the efficiency and maturity of conventional rasterization hardware and algorithms for visibility determination while preserving the ability to compute accurate gradients through automatic differentiation for all downstream operations.

As illustrated in Figure 2 of the research paper (herein incorporated by reference in concept though not by figure), the system accepts scene parameters θ as input. These parameters may encode geometry (e.g., vertex coordinates of a mesh, control points of a spline, coefficients of an implicit function), appearance (e.g., texture maps, material properties, neural radiance networks), and camera configuration (e.g., extrinsic and intrinsic parameters). The parameters θ are first processed by a sampling function U, which operates only in the forward pass and produces per-layer screen-space parameter buffers U_k for k = 1 to K, where K ≥ 1 denotes the number of depth layers retained per pixel (typically K = 2 suffices for most occlusion scenarios). The function U is intentionally non-differentiable; it may invoke any standard surface rasterization technique—hardware-accelerated triangle rasterization with depth peeling, adaptive subdivision of parametric patches, or Marching Cubes isosurface extraction followed by software rasterization—without concern for gradient propagation. The output U_k contains sufficient information to reconstruct surface attributes at each pixel, such as triangle indices and barycentric coordinates for meshes, patch identifiers and local parameters for splines, or edge indices and interpolation weights for implicit surfaces.

These parameter buffers U_k are then fed, along with the original scene parameters θ, into an evaluation function G. Unlike U, G is fully differentiable and implemented within an automatic differentiation framework. G reconstructs continuous surface attributes—such as world-space positions x, surface normals n, texture coordinates uv, or other per-vertex data—by interpolating or evaluating the underlying geometric representation using the discrete samples provided in U_k. For instance, in the case of a triangle mesh, G uses the triangle index to fetch the three vertex attributes and applies perspective-correct barycentric interpolation; for a B-spline surface, G evaluates the tensor-product basis functions at the sampled (u,v) parameters within the identified patch; for an implicit isosurface, G linearly interpolates along the Marching Cubes edges to compute the exact surface point corresponding to the isovalue. Because this evaluation occurs only at locations where the surface is well-defined (i.e., away from topological singularities, which are filtered out during sampling), the resulting G-buffers G_k are smooth functions of θ, enabling reliable gradient computation.

Subsequently, a deferred shading function C processes the G-buffers to produce per-layer color buffers C_k. Deferred shading is a well-established graphics technique wherein geometric attributes are first rendered into buffers (G-buffers), and lighting/shading is applied as a post-process. In RtS, this paradigm is extended to the differentiable domain: any shading model that can be expressed as a pixel-wise function of G-buffer contents is admissible. Examples include Phong shading using normals and positions, texture lookup using UV coordinates, or even a neural network such as the color branch of a NeRF, which takes position and view direction as input and outputs radiance. Critically, because shading is decoupled from geometry processing, the computational cost of C scales with image resolution, not geometric complexity, enabling efficient rendering of highly detailed scenes.

Finally, a splatting function S composites the shaded layers C_k into a single output image S. Each pixel in C_k corresponds to a surface sample with an associated screen-space position P_k (computed by projecting the world-space position from G_k through the camera). S converts each sample into a splat—a small, radially symmetric kernel (e.g., Gaussian) centered at P_k and colored by C_k. To handle occlusions correctly, S implements a multi-layer depth-aware accumulation scheme: when applying a splat from position p (source) to a neighboring pixel q (target), the relative depth between the source layer and the target’s visible surface determines whether the splat contributes to an “occluding” buffer (S⁺), a “coincident” buffer (Sᵒ), or an “occluded” buffer (S⁻). After all splats are processed, each buffer is normalized by its accumulated weights and composited in S⁻ → Sᵒ → S⁺ order using alpha blending. This ensures that derivatives at occlusion boundaries reflect the correct visibility transition—e.g., when an object moves behind another, the gradient indicates that pixels should darken, not brighten.

The splat kernel is defined as w(p, q) = exp(−||p − q||² / (2σ²)) + ε, where σ = 0.5 pixels controls blur extent and ε = 0.05 prevents division-by-zero in normalization. The final pixel color s_q is computed as the weighted average of neighboring splat colors, normalized by total weight. The inclusion of ε ensures that interior pixels always undergo proper normalization, which is essential for gradient correctness, while boundary pixels gracefully degrade.

This pipeline—U → G → C → S—enables end-to-end differentiation from image loss back to scene parameters θ, despite the non-differentiable nature of U. Gradients flow through G, C, and S via automatic differentiation, while U acts as a fixed, non-trainable sampler during backpropagation. The method thus combines the best of both worlds: the speed and robustness of traditional rasterization for visibility, and the flexibility and correctness of automatic differentiation for optimization.

### Example Devices and Systems

The rasterize-then-splat method may be implemented on a variety of computing devices and integrated into diverse systems for graphics, vision, and machine learning. A typical implementation environment includes one or more processors (e.g., CPU, GPU, or specialized AI accelerators), memory (RAM, VRAM), and storage, running an operating system (e.g., Linux, Windows, macOS) and a deep learning framework with automatic differentiation support (e.g., TensorFlow, PyTorch, JAX).

In a preferred embodiment, the system comprises a general-purpose computer equipped with a GPU capable of hardware-accelerated rasterization (e.g., NVIDIA RTX series, AMD Radeon Pro). The non-differentiable sampling function U is implemented using standard graphics APIs such as OpenGL, Vulkan, or DirectX for triangle mesh rendering, leveraging depth peeling extensions to capture multiple depth layers per pixel. For parametric or implicit surfaces, U may be implemented in software using optimized CPU or GPU kernels—for example, a CUDA kernel that performs Marching Cubes on a 3D grid and rasterizes the resulting triangles using a scanline algorithm. The output of U—per-pixel parameter buffers U_k—is stored in GPU memory and transferred to the deep learning framework’s tensor format.

The evaluation function G, shading function C, and splatting function S are implemented as differentiable operations within the chosen framework. For instance, in PyTorch, G may consist of tensor indexing and interpolation operations; C may involve neural network modules or custom CUDA kernels wrapped in autograd-compatible functions; S may use convolution-like operations with dynamically computed kernels based on splat positions. The entire differentiable portion of the pipeline runs on the same GPU, minimizing data transfer overhead.

Such a system can be deployed in several application contexts. In an augmented reality (AR) headset, RtS could enable real-time pose tracking of known 3D objects by minimizing reprojection error between rendered silhouettes and camera input, leveraging the method’s speed and robust gradients. In an autonomous vehicle perception stack, RtS might refine 3D bounding box estimates by optimizing mesh deformations to match LiDAR or camera data. In a content creation suite, artists could use RtS for inverse rendering—adjusting material parameters or geometry to match reference photographs—with immediate visual feedback and stable convergence.

Cloud-based rendering services could offer RtS as a differentiable rendering API, allowing users to submit scene parameters and receive rendered images along with gradients for optimization. Mobile implementations are also feasible for lightweight tasks (e.g., single-object pose estimation) by offloading the sampling step to the device’s GPU and running the differentiable components on a neural processing unit (NPU) or CPU.

The modularity of RtS facilitates integration with existing pipelines. For example, a game engine could replace its standard renderer with RtS during development to enable automatic asset optimization (e.g., simplifying meshes while preserving visual fidelity). A NeRF training framework could incorporate RtS as a fast rendering mode after initial volume rendering convergence, drastically reducing inference time for downstream applications like VR walkthroughs.

### Example Model Arrangements

The rasterize-then-splat method supports a wide array of geometric and appearance models, each requiring specific instantiations of the sampling function U and evaluation function G. Below are detailed examples for three major surface representation classes.

**Triangle Meshes**: For a triangle mesh defined by vertex positions V ∈ ℝ^(N×3), vertex attributes A ∈ ℝ^(N×d) (e.g., normals, UVs, colors), and triangle indices F ∈ ℤ^(M×3), the sampling function U performs rasterization with K-layer depth peeling. Using a graphics API, U renders the mesh K times, each time discarding fragments closer than the previous layer’s depth buffer. The output U_k for layer k consists of two buffers: T_k ∈ ℤ^(H×W) storing the triangle index hit at each pixel, and B_k ∈ ℝ^(H×W×3) storing the barycentric coordinates within that triangle. Pixels with no hit are marked with sentinel values (e.g., T_k = −1). The evaluation function G then computes G_k = interpolate(A[T_k], B_k), where interpolate performs perspective-correct barycentric interpolation. If only world positions are needed (e.g., for silhouette rendering), A can be set to V, and G_k yields 3D positions. For pose estimation where only camera extrinsics vary, U can be optimized to output world positions directly (bypassing T_k and B_k), since vertex positions are constant and only projection changes—this “RtS-pose” variant eliminates mesh-dependent computation from the differentiable path.

**Parametric Surfaces**: Consider a bicubic uniform B-spline surface defined by a control grid C ∈ ℝ^(P×Q×3). The surface is composed of (P−3)×(Q−3) rectangular patches, each parameterized by (u,v) ∈ [0,1]². The sampling function U adaptively subdivides each patch until all resulting quads project to less than one pixel, then rasterizes the quads as triangles. U_k stores for each pixel the patch index (i,j) and local parameters (u,v). The evaluation function G computes the surface point as G_k = M_u^T · C[i:i+4, j:j+4] · M_v, where M_u, M_v are cubic B-spline basis matrices evaluated at u and v. Normals can be computed by cross product of partial derivatives, also expressible via basis matrix derivatives. This approach extends to Catmull-Clark subdivision surfaces by using the limit surface evaluation formula, or to Bézier patches by replacing basis matrices with Bernstein polynomials.

**Implicit Surfaces**: Let f_θ: ℝ³ → ℝ be an implicit function (e.g., a neural network or 3D grid of density values). The isosurface is defined as {x | f_θ(x) = τ} for threshold τ. The sampling function U first evaluates f_θ on a regular 3D grid, then applies Marching Cubes to extract a triangle mesh. During extraction, U filters out ambiguous cases where grid cell values are nearly equal (|f_i − f_j| < δ for edge (i,j)), as these cause non-differentiable topology changes. The resulting mesh is rasterized with depth peeling to produce U_k. Each U_k entry is a 9D vector: six integers v₁…v₆ identifying the three grid vertices forming the Marching Cubes cell edges that cross the isosurface, and three floats β₁…β₃ giving barycentric coordinates within the extracted triangle. The evaluation function G first retrieves f-values at v₁…v₆ (via lookup if f_θ is grid-based, or network evaluation if neural), computes edge interpolation coefficients α_m = (τ − f_{v_{2m−1}}) / (f_{v_{2m}} − f_{v_{2m−1}}) for m=1,2,3, constructs triangle vertices x_m = (1−α_m)v_{2m−1} + α_m v_{2m}, then interpolates the final surface point as G_k = β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + β₃x₃. This hides Marching Cubes’ combinatorial complexity in U, leaving G as a smooth composition of linear interpolations.

In all cases, the shading function C can be tailored to the task. For texture mapping, C samples a texture atlas using UVs from G_k. For NeRF-based surface light fields, C invokes the NeRF color network with inputs (G_k.position, view_direction). For silhouette rendering, C outputs binary masks based on depth validity.

### Example Methods

The rasterize-then-splat method is executed through a sequence of computational steps that can be formalized as an algorithm. The following describes the forward pass; the backward pass is handled automatically by the framework.

**Input**: Scene parameters θ, camera parameters Π, image resolution (H,W), number of layers K, splat parameters σ, ε.

**Step 1: Non-Differentiable Sampling**  
Invoke U(θ, Π) to produce K parameter buffers U₁…U_K. For meshes, this involves:  
- Initialize depth buffers D₀ = +∞, D_k = +∞ for k=1..K.  
- For k = 1 to K:  
  - Render mesh with depth test: keep fragments where z < D_{k−1}.  
  - Store triangle indices T_k and barycentrics B_k for kept fragments.  
  - Update D_k with fragment depths.  
For implicit surfaces, this involves:  
- Evaluate f_θ on a 3D grid.  
- Run Marching Cubes with ambiguity filtering.  
- Rasterize resulting mesh with K-layer depth peeling.

**Step 2: Differentiable Evaluation**  
For each layer k = 1 to K:  
- Compute G_k = G(θ, U_k).  
  - For meshes: G_k = barycentric_interpolate(vertex_attributes[θ], T_k, B_k).  
  - For splines: G_k = evaluate_spline_patch(control_points[θ], U_k.patch_id, U_k.uv).  
  - For implicit: G_k = reconstruct_isosurface_point(f_θ, U_k.edge_vertices, U_k.barycentrics).  
- Compute screen positions P_k = project(G_k.positions, Π).

**Step 3: Deferred Shading**  
For each layer k:  
- Compute C_k = C(G_k).  
  - E.g., C_k.rgb = texture(G_k.uv); C_k.a = 1.0.  
  - Or C_k.rgb = NeRF_color_network(G_k.positions, view_dir); C_k.a = 1.0.

**Step 4: Multi-Layer Splatting**  
Initialize accumulation buffers: S⁺ = 0, W⁺ = 0; Sᵒ = 0, Wᵒ = 0; S⁻ = 0, W⁻ = 0 (all H×W×4).  
For each layer k = 1 to K:  
- For each pixel p = (i,j) where C_k is valid:  
  - Let c = C_k[i,j], pos = P_k[i,j].  
  - For each neighbor q ∈ N₃×₃(p):  
    - Compute weight w = exp(−||pos − q||²/(2σ²)) + ε.  
    - Determine depth relation:  
      - Find closest layer l at q such that P_l[q] exists.  
      - If k == l: assign to Sᵒ.  
      - Else if P_k[p].z < P_l[q].z: assign to S⁺.  
      - Else: assign to S⁻.  
    - Accumulate: S^{type}[q] += w·c; W^{type}[q] += w.  
- Normalize each buffer: S^{type} = S^{type} / max(W^{type}, 1.0).  
- Composite: S = over_blend(S⁻, over_blend(Sᵒ, S⁺)).

**Output**: Final image S.

During optimization, a loss L(S, S_target) is computed (e.g., L2, perceptual loss), and gradients ∇_θ L are obtained via automatic differentiation through Steps 2–4. Step 1 is excluded from the gradient graph.

This method enables diverse optimization tasks:
- **Pose Estimation**: θ = camera pose; minimize silhouette error.
- **Mesh Deformation**: θ = vertex positions; minimize RGB error with ARAP regularization.
- **Parametric Fitting**: θ = spline control points; minimize silhouette error.
- **Implicit Optimization**: θ = density grid + NeRF weights; minimize NeRF L2 loss.
- **Texture Synthesis**: θ = texture map; minimize RGB error.

### Additional Disclosure

The rasterize-then-splat method offers several advantages over prior art. First, it eliminates the need for custom gradient implementations, as all differentiable operations are standard tensor computations. This simplifies adoption and ensures compatibility with evolving deep learning frameworks. Second, it supports both forward-mode and reverse-mode differentiation, enabling efficient use of second-order optimizers like Levenberg-Marquardt for problems with few parameters (e.g., pose estimation), where full Jacobian computation is feasible and beneficial for convergence.

Third, the method is representation-agnostic. Any surface that admits a non-differentiable sampler U and a differentiable evaluator G can be rendered. This includes emerging representations like neural implicits, point-based surfaces with local parameterizations, or hybrid models. The Marching Cubes ambiguity filter (discarding cells with near-equal vertex values) ensures that G operates only in regions where the isosurface is a smooth manifold, avoiding gradient singularities.

Fourth, runtime scales with image resolution, not geometric complexity, after the initial sampling step. For static geometry with varying pose (e.g., object tracking), the “RtS-pose” variant reduces differentiable cost to O(HW), independent of mesh size. This enables real-time optimization on large models (e.g., 2M-face meshes) where edge-sampling methods become prohibitive.

Fifth, the multi-layer splatting scheme correctly handles first-order occlusion effects. While it assumes opaque surfaces and does not model transparency or reflections, it accurately captures the dominant visibility changes in most inverse rendering scenarios. Future extensions could incorporate alpha channels or multiple scattering for translucent materials.

The slight blur introduced by splatting (σ = 0.5px) has minimal impact on optimization quality, as demonstrated by texture fitting experiments where PSNR degradation was negligible (<1.5%) with mipmapping, and even improved anti-aliasing without. For applications requiring sharp edges, the blur can be reduced at the cost of slightly noisier gradients, or compensated via deconvolution in post-processing.

In the context of NeRF acceleration, RtS enables a practical trade-off between quality and speed. By converting a volume-rendered NeRF into a surface light field, rendering cost drops from O(N) to O(1) per pixel (N ≈ 128), with PSNR loss under 0.3 dB on surface-dominated scenes like Lego. This makes NeRF viable for interactive applications without retraining.

Finally, the method’s modularity encourages innovation in surface representations. Researchers can focus on designing novel geometric models (e.g., learned parametric shapes) without worrying about rendering differentiability, as long as they provide U and G functions. This lowers the barrier to entry for differentiable graphics and expands the scope of solvable inverse problems.
