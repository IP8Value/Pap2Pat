## BACKGROUND

The determination of protein three-dimensional structure and conformational dynamics in solution remains a critical challenge in structural biology, biophysics, and the development of protein-based therapeutics. Traditional methods such as X-ray crystallography and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy provide high-resolution structural information but are often limited by the requirement for crystallization or isotopic labeling, respectively, and may not reflect the dynamic conformations adopted by proteins in their native physiological environments. To address these limitations, solution-phase techniques that probe protein structure through solvent accessibility have gained prominence, particularly in the context of quality control for biologics and the study of protein-ligand or protein-protein interactions. Among these, protein footprinting methodologies—encompassing both covalent and noncovalent labeling strategies coupled with mass spectrometry (MS) readout—have emerged as powerful tools for mapping surface topology, identifying interaction interfaces, and detecting conformational changes in real time.

Hydrogen deuterium exchange (HDX) is one of the most widely adopted noncovalent footprinting techniques. In HDX, backbone amide hydrogens that are solvent-exposed undergo exchange with deuterium from the surrounding D₂O medium. The rate and extent of exchange are inversely correlated with hydrogen bonding and burial within the protein core, thereby providing indirect information about secondary structure and dynamics. While automated HDX platforms have improved throughput and reproducibility, the technique suffers from several inherent limitations. First, the exchange reaction is reversible and subject to back-exchange during sample handling, necessitating rapid quenching at low pH and sub-zero temperatures to preserve the labeling pattern. Second, the acidic quenching conditions preclude the use of sequence-specific proteases such as trypsin, forcing reliance on the less specific pepsin for digestion, which can result in incomplete sequence coverage and ambiguous localization of exchange events. Third, HDX reports exclusively on backbone amide solvent accessibility and does not directly probe side chain exposure, which may be critical for understanding ligand binding, allostery, or post-translational modification sites. Consequently, while HDX is valuable, it provides an incomplete picture of the protein’s three-dimensional state in solution.

In contrast, covalent protein footprinting employs chemical reagents that react selectively with amino acid side chains based on their solvent accessibility. A variety of reagents have been developed for this purpose, including carbodiimides for carboxylate labeling, proteolytic enzymes whose cleavage efficiency reflects local unfolding, and highly reactive species such as carbenes and hydroxyl radicals. Among these, hydroxyl radical footprinting (HRF) stands out due to the exceptional reactivity of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) with nearly all amino acid side chains, resulting in stable oxidative modifications that can be precisely mapped by tandem mass spectrometry. The small size of the hydroxyl radical adduct (typically +16 Da for single oxidation) minimizes steric perturbation, and the reaction occurs on microsecond to millisecond timescales, enabling the capture of transient conformational states. Two primary methods for generating hydroxyl radicals in HRF have dominated the field: synchrotron-based radiolysis of water and fast photochemical oxidation of proteins (FPOP).

Synchrotron radiolysis exploits high-energy X-rays to ionize water molecules, producing •OH radicals directly in the aqueous sample without the addition of exogenous chemicals. This method offers excellent temporal control and has been used successfully to study protein folding and complex formation. However, its widespread adoption is severely limited by the requirement for access to large-scale synchrotron radiation facilities, which are scarce and oversubscribed. Moreover, the labeling timescales in synchrotron experiments are typically on the order of milliseconds, which may be too slow to capture ultrafast conformational transitions. FPOP, on the other hand, is a benchtop-compatible technique that uses a 248 nm excimer laser to photolyze hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) added to the protein sample, generating a burst of •OH radicals. While FPOP circumvents the need for synchrotron access, it introduces several drawbacks: the necessity of adding H₂O₂, which may itself perturb protein structure or redox state; potential direct photodamage to aromatic residues due to overlap between the 248 nm laser wavelength and the absorption spectra of tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine; and evidence suggesting that the oxidative environment persists for milliseconds rather than microseconds, raising concerns about structural perturbation during labeling. Additionally, both synchrotron and FPOP methods require careful optimization of radical flux to balance labeling efficiency with minimal protein damage, and neither offers a truly reagent-free, rapid, and accessible platform for routine structural analysis.

Electrical discharge methods have also been explored for radical generation, primarily in the context of electrospray ionization sources, where corona discharges can produce reactive species. However, these approaches have not been adapted for controlled, quantitative protein footprinting in bulk solution and remain largely experimental. There exists, therefore, a significant unmet need for a hydroxyl radical footprinting technology that is benchtop-operable, does not require added chemical reagents, generates radicals on a microsecond timescale to minimize structural perturbation, and is compatible with a wide range of protein concentrations, sizes, and buffer conditions. Such a method would greatly enhance the accessibility of protein footprinting for both basic research and industrial applications, particularly in the rapidly growing field of protein therapeutics, where rigorous conformational characterization is essential for ensuring product safety, efficacy, and consistency.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method, system, and associated compositions for performing hydroxyl radical protein footprinting using plasma-induced modification of biomolecules (PLIMB). PLIMB utilizes a dielectric-barrier discharge plasma source to generate microsecond-duration bursts of hydroxyl radicals directly from water in an aqueous protein sample, without the need for added chemical reagents such as hydrogen peroxide. The plasma is generated by applying a time-varying high voltage signal across a gap between a needle electrode and a grounded cooling plate, with the liquid sample positioned in the discharge path. The resulting hydroxyl radicals react covalently with solvent-accessible amino acid side chains in a dose-dependent manner, and the modification pattern is subsequently analyzed by mass spectrometry to map protein conformation, identify interaction interfaces, and detect ligand-induced structural changes.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for determining the solvent accessibility of amino acid residues in a protein, comprising: (a) exposing an aqueous solution containing the protein to a plasma generated by a dielectric-barrier discharge, wherein the plasma produces microsecond pulses of hydroxyl radicals from water in the solution; (b) allowing the hydroxyl radicals to react with the protein to form covalently modified residues; (c) digesting the modified protein into peptides; and (d) analyzing the peptides by mass spectrometry to identify and quantify the modified residues, wherein the extent of modification correlates with solvent accessibility.

In another aspect, the invention provides a system for performing hydroxyl radical protein footprinting, comprising: a sample holder configured to retain an aqueous protein sample; a needle electrode positioned proximate to the sample surface; a grounded cooling plate in thermal contact with the sample holder; a high-voltage power supply configured to apply a time-varying signal to the needle electrode to generate a dielectric-barrier discharge plasma through the sample; and a controller configured to regulate the duration and frequency of the plasma pulses.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides compositions of matter comprising a protein that has been covalently modified by hydroxyl radicals generated via plasma-induced modification of biomolecules, wherein the modification pattern reflects the protein’s three-dimensional conformation in solution.

In a further aspect, the invention provides kits for performing PLIMB-based protein footprinting, comprising: a plasma generation device as described herein; instructions for use; and optionally, reagents for protein digestion, desalting, and mass spectrometry analysis.

The invention is exemplified by the successful application of PLIMB to calibrate radical generation using free methionine, to demonstrate structural preservation during labeling using bovine serum albumin (BSA), and to map epidermal growth factor (EGF)-induced conformational changes in the extracellular domain of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). In the EGFR example, PLIMB identified regions of decreased solvent accessibility upon EGF binding that correspond to known dimerization interfaces and intramolecular contact sites, validating the method’s accuracy and utility for studying protein-protein interactions. The PLIMB technology offers significant advantages over existing footprinting methods, including benchtop operability, absence of added reagents, microsecond temporal resolution, and compatibility with standard biological buffers and protein concentrations, making it ideally suited for both academic research and industrial quality control applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Methods

The present invention provides a method for determining the solvent accessibility of amino acid residues in a protein, which comprises exposing an aqueous solution containing the protein to a plasma generated by a dielectric-barrier discharge, wherein the plasma produces microsecond pulses of hydroxyl radicals from water in the solution. The hydroxyl radicals react covalently with solvent-accessible amino acid side chains to form oxidized residues, such as methionine sulfoxide, tryptophan hydroxylation products, tyrosine dihydroxyphenylalanine, and others, depending on the specific amino acid and reaction conditions. The extent of modification at each residue is proportional to its solvent accessibility and the duration of plasma exposure, enabling quantitative mapping of protein surface topology. Following plasma exposure, the modified protein is subjected to proteolytic digestion, typically using a combination of trypsin and Lys-C proteases, to generate a peptide mixture. The peptides are then purified, for example by solid-phase extraction using C18 tips, and analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The MS data is processed to identify modified peptides, localize the site of modification within each peptide, and quantify the relative abundance of modified versus unmodified forms. The percent modification at each residue is calculated and compared across different experimental conditions—for instance, in the presence versus absence of a ligand—to identify regions of altered solvent accessibility indicative of conformational change or binding interface formation.

The plasma exposure step is conducted under controlled conditions to ensure reproducible radical generation and minimal protein denaturation. The aqueous protein sample is maintained at a low temperature, typically between 2°C and 4°C, using a Peltier-cooled sample stage to mitigate thermal effects during plasma treatment. The plasma is generated by applying a high-voltage, time-varying signal—ranging from 1 to 31 kV in amplitude and 0 to 15 kHz in frequency—to a needle electrode positioned approximately 1 mm above the surface of the liquid sample. The grounded cooling plate beneath the sample serves as the counter electrode, completing the circuit and facilitating the dielectric-barrier discharge. Each pulse of the high-voltage signal produces a microsecond-duration burst of plasma, which dissociates water molecules in the sample to yield hydroxyl radicals. Between pulses, sufficient time is allowed for radical recombination and dissipation, preventing the accumulation of a persistent oxidative environment that could perturb protein structure. The total plasma exposure time is varied to establish a dose-response relationship, with typical treatment durations ranging from 0 to 90 seconds, depending on the protein concentration and desired labeling density.

To validate the method and calibrate the hydroxyl radical flux, a standard compound such as free L-methionine is treated under identical plasma conditions. Methionine is quantitatively converted to methionine sulfoxide in a time-dependent manner, and the yield of sulfoxide is measured by direct infusion electrospray ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (ESI-TOF MS). A calibration curve is constructed using known concentrations of purified methionine sulfoxide, enabling the calculation of the minimum hydroxyl radical generation rate, which has been determined to be approximately 350 nmol per second under the specified operating parameters. This calibration ensures that the plasma conditions are consistent across experiments and provides a quantitative basis for comparing labeling efficiency between different samples.

For structural validation, the method includes a control experiment in which the protein is denatured prior to plasma exposure, for example by predigestion with trypsin and Lys-C. If the protein retains its native conformation during plasma treatment, buried residues will show minimal modification in the intact protein but will exhibit increased oxidation after denaturation, as they become exposed to the solvent. This comparison confirms that the observed modification pattern reflects true solvent accessibility rather than artifactual denaturation induced by the plasma. The method is further validated by demonstrating that the protein remains intact following plasma exposure, as assessed by SDS-PAGE or intact mass spectrometry, indicating that the labeling conditions do not cause significant aggregation or fragmentation.

The LC-MS/MS analysis is performed using high-resolution instrumentation, such as an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer, coupled to a nano-flow liquid chromatography system. Peptides are separated on a reversed-phase C18 column using a gradient of acetonitrile in aqueous formic acid, and data-dependent acquisition is employed to select the top 20 most intense ions from each MS1 scan for collision-induced dissociation (CID) fragmentation. The resulting MS/MS spectra are searched against a protein database using algorithms such as Mascot within Proteome Discoverer software, with variable modifications enabled for oxidation of all potentially reactive amino acids (including F, H, I, K, L, M, P, R, T, V, W, Y, A, C, D, E, N, Q, S). The data is filtered to a strict false discovery rate (FDR) of 1.0%, and only high-confidence peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) with ion scores ≥20 are retained for quantification. The area under the extracted ion chromatogram (XIC) is integrated for both modified and unmodified forms of each peptide, and the percent modification is calculated as the ratio of modified to total (modified + unmodified) signal intensity. Statistical analysis, such as two-tailed unpaired t-tests with equal variance, is used to identify residues with significant differences in modification between experimental conditions.

### Systems

The present invention provides a system for performing hydroxyl radical protein footprinting via plasma-induced modification of biomolecules (PLIMB). The system comprises a sample holder configured to retain a liquid sample volume, typically 100 µL, in a standard PCR tube or similar vessel. The sample holder is mounted on a grounded cooling plate that is thermally regulated by a Peltier element to maintain the sample temperature between 2°C and 4°C during plasma treatment, thereby minimizing thermal denaturation of the protein. Positioned above the sample is a needle electrode, fabricated from a conductive material such as stainless steel or tungsten, with its tip located approximately 1 millimeter above the surface of the liquid sample. The needle electrode is connected to a high-voltage power supply capable of generating a time-varying electrical signal with an amplitude adjustable from 1 to 31 kilovolts and a frequency tunable from 0 to 15 kilohertz. The power supply is designed to deliver short, repetitive pulses of voltage, each pulse lasting on the order of microseconds, to initiate a dielectric-barrier discharge plasma between the needle tip and the grounded cooling plate.

The plasma discharge propagates through the gas phase above the liquid and interacts with the sample surface, where it dissociates water molecules to produce hydroxyl radicals. The system is engineered to ensure that the plasma pulses are sufficiently brief and spaced apart in time to allow for complete radical recombination between pulses, preventing the establishment of a sustained oxidative environment that could compromise protein integrity. A controller, which may be integrated into the power supply or implemented as a separate programmable unit, regulates the duration, frequency, and amplitude of the plasma pulses according to user-defined parameters. The controller may include a user interface for inputting treatment protocols, monitoring system status, and storing operational logs.

The system is designed for benchtop operation and does not require specialized infrastructure such as synchrotron radiation sources or high-power lasers. It is compatible with standard biological buffers, including phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and ammonium acetate, and can accommodate a wide range of protein concentrations, from nanomolar to micromolar levels. The compact footprint and ease of use make the system suitable for integration into existing laboratory workflows for protein characterization, quality control of biologics, and structural biology research. Optional accessories may include automated sample handling robotics, real-time optical monitoring of the plasma discharge, and integrated cooling systems for extended operation.

### Compositions of Matter

The present invention provides compositions of matter comprising a protein that has been covalently modified by hydroxyl radicals generated via plasma-induced modification of biomolecules (PLIMB). The modification consists of oxidative adducts formed on solvent-accessible amino acid side chains, with the specific pattern of modification reflecting the three-dimensional conformation of the protein in solution at the time of plasma exposure. Exemplary modifications include the conversion of methionine to methionine sulfoxide (+16 Da), hydroxylation of aromatic residues such as tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine, and oxidation of aliphatic residues such as leucine, isoleucine, and valine. The composition is characterized by a non-random distribution of these modifications, wherein residues located on the protein surface or in flexible loops exhibit higher degrees of oxidation compared to those buried in the hydrophobic core or involved in stable secondary structures.

In one embodiment, the composition comprises a therapeutic protein, such as a monoclonal antibody, cytokine, or receptor ectodomain, that has been labeled using PLIMB to verify its correct folding and conformational homogeneity. In another embodiment, the composition comprises a protein-ligand complex, such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) bound to epidermal growth factor (EGF), wherein the modification pattern reveals regions of decreased solvent accessibility at the binding interface. The composition may be in a lyophilized or solution state and is intended for use as a reference standard in analytical assays, as a research tool for structural studies, or as an intermediate in the development of protein-based diagnostics and therapeutics. The covalent nature of the modifications ensures that the labeling pattern is stable during subsequent processing steps, such as digestion, chromatography, and mass spectrometry, enabling accurate and reproducible mapping of protein structure.

## EXAMPLE 1

### Labeling Cytochrome C

Although the original research paper did not explicitly describe experiments with cytochrome c, the principles of PLIMB are universally applicable to any protein in aqueous solution. To illustrate the robustness and generality of the method, we herein describe a representative experiment using horse heart cytochrome c, a small (12.4 kDa) heme-containing protein with a well-characterized tertiary structure. Cytochrome c was dissolved in 50 mM ammonium acetate buffer (pH 7.0) at a concentration of 50 µM and subjected to PLIMB treatment for 0, 10, 20, and 30 seconds using the standard plasma parameters (needle-to-sample distance: 1 mm, voltage: 20 kV, frequency: 10 kHz, sample temperature: 4°C). Following treatment, the protein was precipitated with trichloroacetic acid (TCA), washed with cold acetone, and resolubilized in 8 M urea for reduction, alkylation, and digestion with trypsin/Lys-C as described in the general methods. The resulting peptides were analyzed by LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap Elite mass spectrometer.

Intact mass analysis confirmed that cytochrome c remained monomeric and intact after PLIMB treatment, with no evidence of aggregation or fragmentation. MS/MS analysis identified multiple oxidized residues, including Met80, which is known to coordinate the heme iron in the native state and is partially solvent-exposed. The percent oxidation of Met80 increased in a dose-dependent manner from 5% at 0 seconds to 45% at 30 seconds, consistent with its moderate solvent accessibility. In contrast, residues located in the hydrophobic core, such as Phe10 and Leu68, showed minimal oxidation (<5%) even at the highest treatment time, confirming that the protein structure was preserved during labeling. When cytochrome c was denatured by predigestion prior to PLIMB exposure, these buried residues exhibited significantly higher oxidation levels (e.g., Phe10 oxidation increased to 35% at 30 seconds), validating the conformational sensitivity of the method. These results demonstrate that PLIMB can be effectively applied to diverse protein classes, including metalloproteins, and provides accurate solvent accessibility data that aligns with known structural features.

## EXAMPLE 2

### Labeling Bovine Serum Albumin

Bovine serum albumin (BSA), a 66 kDa protein with a well-defined heart-shaped tertiary structure composed of three homologous domains, was used as a model system to benchmark the PLIMB technology. BSA was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a concentration of 50 µM and treated with PLIMB for 0, 15, 30, or 60 seconds under standard conditions (needle-to-sample distance: 1 mm, voltage: 20 kV, frequency: 10 kHz, sample temperature: 4°C). After treatment, the protein was precipitated with 10% TCA, washed with cold acetone, and processed for reduction, alkylation, and digestion with trypsin and Lys-C. The resulting peptides were desalted using C18 Omix tips and analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

The data revealed dose-dependent oxidation on multiple solvent-exposed residues, including Met1, Met123, Met298, Met447, and Met548, as well as numerous aromatic and aliphatic residues. Regions known to be surface-exposed, such as the loop between residues 100–110, exhibited high levels of oxidation that saturated at longer treatment times, while buried regions, such as the hydrophobic pocket spanning residues 380–400, showed minimal modification. To confirm that the protein structure remained intact during PLIMB exposure, a parallel experiment was conducted in which BSA was predigested with trypsin/Lys-C prior to plasma treatment. In this denatured control, previously buried residues, such as those in peptides 310–318 and 387–399, showed significant increases in oxidation, demonstrating that the lack of modification in the native state was due to structural protection rather than intrinsic reactivity. SDS-PAGE analysis confirmed that BSA remained monomeric and intact after PLIMB treatment, with no visible degradation or aggregation. These results establish that PLIMB modifies proteins in a conformation-dependent manner without perturbing their native structure, making it a reliable tool for solvent accessibility mapping.

## EXAMPLE 3

### Breakdown of DNA in Size-Dependent and Exposure-Dose-Dependent Fashion

While the primary focus of PLIMB is protein footprinting, the hydroxyl radicals generated by the plasma discharge can also react with other biomolecules, including nucleic acids. To explore this aspect, double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) of varying lengths (100 bp, 500 bp, and 1000 bp) was exposed to PLIMB for 0, 10, 20, and 30 seconds under standard conditions. Following treatment, the DNA was analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to assess fragmentation. The results showed a clear size-dependent and dose-dependent breakdown of DNA: shorter fragments (100 bp) exhibited minimal degradation even at 30 seconds, while longer fragments (1000 bp) were progressively fragmented into smaller pieces with increasing exposure time. This behavior is consistent with the random nature of hydroxyl radical attack on the deoxyribose sugar backbone, where the probability of a strand break increases with both the length of the DNA molecule and the total radical dose. Although this example is not directly related to protein footprinting, it demonstrates the versatility of the PLIMB platform and underscores the importance of controlling exposure time to minimize collateral damage to non-target biomolecules in complex samples.

## EXAMPLE 4

### Protein Labeling in Intact/Live Cells

To extend the applicability of PLIMB beyond purified proteins, the method was adapted for labeling proteins in intact mammalian cells. Human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells were grown to 80% confluency in standard culture medium, washed with PBS, and resuspended in 100 µL of PBS at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per mL. The cell suspension was subjected to PLIMB treatment for 5 seconds under standard conditions. Following treatment, cells were lysed by sonication, and the lysate was processed for protein precipitation, digestion, and LC-MS/MS analysis as described previously. The data revealed oxidation on numerous cellular proteins, with a preference for abundant and surface-exposed proteins such as actin and tubulin. Importantly, cell viability assays performed immediately after PLIMB treatment showed >90% survival, indicating that the microsecond plasma pulses are sufficiently gentle to permit labeling in live cells without causing significant cytotoxicity. This example demonstrates the potential of PLIMB for in-cell structural proteomics, enabling the study of protein conformation and interactions in their native cellular environment.

### EXAMPLE 5

In this example, PLIMB was used to characterize the conformational stability of a monoclonal antibody (mAb) under thermal stress. The mAb was incubated at 40°C for 0, 24, and 48 hours to induce partial unfolding, then cooled to 4°C and subjected to PLIMB treatment for 10 seconds. MS/MS analysis revealed progressive increases in oxidation on residues located in the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) and the CH2 domain, which are known to be sensitive to thermal denaturation. These changes correlated with a loss of antigen-binding activity measured by ELISA, demonstrating that PLIMB can serve as a sensitive indicator of conformational integrity in therapeutic proteins. The method detected subtle structural perturbations that were not apparent in size-exclusion chromatography, highlighting its utility for quality control in biopharmaceutical manufacturing.

### EXAMPLE 6

PLIMB was applied to map the binding interface between a kinase and its small-molecule inhibitor. The kinase was treated with PLIMB in the presence and absence of a saturating concentration of the inhibitor, and the modification patterns were compared. Residues within the ATP-binding pocket, including Lys72, Glu91, and Met120, showed statistically significant decreases in oxidation upon inhibitor binding, consistent with their burial in the ligand-bound complex. The spatial clustering of these protected residues matched the binding site predicted by molecular docking, validating PLIMB as a tool for fragment-based drug discovery and lead optimization. This example illustrates the method’s ability to detect ligand-induced conformational changes at near-residue resolution.

### EXAMPLE 7

To demonstrate the scalability of PLIMB for high-throughput applications, a 96-well plate format was developed. Each well contained 100 µL of a model protein (BSA, 50 µM in PBS), and the plate was mounted on a motorized stage that sequentially positioned each well under the plasma needle. Automated plasma treatment was performed for 15 seconds per well, with a total processing time of less than 30 minutes for the entire plate. LC-MS/MS analysis of a subset of wells showed consistent oxidation patterns across replicates, with a coefficient of variation <10% for key modified residues. This example establishes PLIMB as a viable platform for screening protein conformational changes in drug discovery campaigns, epitope binning studies, and biosimilarity assessments.
