# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of cellular reprogramming and stem cell biology, particularly to methods for the efficient derivation, identification, isolation, and purification of fully reprogrammed induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from somatic cells. More specifically, the invention provides a novel fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-based strategy that utilizes a combination of positive and negative cell surface markers—namely, SSEA4, TRA-1-60, and CD13—to selectively isolate bona fide iPSCs during the early stages of reprogramming. This approach enables the rapid and robust generation of high-quality, clonally distinct iPSC lines from a wide range of human somatic cell sources, including dermal fibroblasts derived from healthy individuals and patients with diverse genetic and acquired diseases. The invention further encompasses applications in regenerative medicine, disease modeling, drug discovery, and high-throughput screening, where the use of pure, well-characterized, and functionally validated iPSC populations is critical for reproducibility, safety, and therapeutic efficacy. The method is compatible with both integrating (e.g., retroviral) and non-integrating (e.g., Sendai virus) reprogramming vector systems, thereby offering flexibility and scalability for research and clinical-grade cell production. By eliminating contaminating partially reprogrammed, non-reprogrammed, or transformed somatic cells through negative selection against CD13—a marker retained on fibroblasts but lost upon complete reprogramming—the invention significantly improves the fidelity, efficiency, and standardization of iPSC derivation protocols, addressing a major bottleneck in the translation of iPSC technology into clinical and industrial applications.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The advent of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology, pioneered by the forced expression of defined transcription factors such as Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc in somatic cells, has fundamentally transformed the landscape of regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and personalized therapeutics. iPSCs exhibit functional and molecular characteristics nearly indistinguishable from embryonic stem cells (ESCs), including the capacity for self-renewal and differentiation into all three germ layers. This pluripotency, combined with the ability to generate patient-specific cell lines without ethical concerns associated with embryo destruction, positions iPSCs as a powerful platform for studying human development, modeling complex diseases in vitro, and developing autologous cell-based therapies. However, despite these transformative potentials, the practical application of iPSCs has been hindered by significant challenges in the derivation process, particularly concerning the efficiency, purity, and reproducibility of generating fully reprogrammed clones.

Traditional methods for iPSC derivation rely heavily on manual colony picking under light microscopy, typically performed 3–6 weeks after initiating reprogramming. This approach is inherently labor-intensive, subjective, and requires extensive expertise in stem cell culture techniques. Moreover, it suffers from low throughput and poor standardization, as the morphological criteria used to identify putative iPSC colonies are often ambiguous and can include partially reprogrammed or differentiated cells. Consequently, manually picked colonies frequently harbor heterogeneous cell populations, including residual parental fibroblasts or incompletely reprogrammed intermediates, which compromise the quality and utility of the resulting iPSC lines. These contaminants may persist through multiple passages and can confound downstream applications such as directed differentiation, genomic analysis, or drug screening, where cellular homogeneity is essential.

To address these limitations, several alternative strategies have been explored, including the use of reporter genes linked to pluripotency promoters, small molecule enhancers of reprogramming efficiency, and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) based on surface marker expression. Among these, FACS offers a quantitative, objective, and scalable means of isolating reprogrammed cells. Prior studies have demonstrated that during reprogramming, somatic cells undergo dynamic changes in surface antigen expression, including the downregulation of mesenchymal markers such as CD13 and the upregulation of pluripotency-associated markers like SSEA4 and TRA-1-60. Notably, SSEA4 and TRA-1-60 are well-established glycolipid and glycoprotein antigens, respectively, that are highly expressed on human ESCs and fully reprogrammed iPSCs but absent or low in most somatic cells. As a result, dual-positive selection for SSEA4 and TRA-1-60 has been proposed as a means to enrich for pluripotent cells.

However, emerging evidence suggests that reliance on positive selection alone is insufficient to ensure the isolation of fully reprogrammed iPSCs. A significant proportion of cells expressing SSEA4 and TRA-1-60 during the reprogramming process may still retain somatic identity or exhibit incomplete epigenetic resetting, rendering them functionally inadequate. In particular, studies have shown that even late in reprogramming, a subset of SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ cells continues to express CD13, a zinc-dependent metalloprotease abundantly present on the surface of human dermal fibroblasts and other mesenchymal cells. The persistence of CD13 in these double-positive populations indicates that they represent transitional or partially reprogrammed states rather than stable pluripotent cells. Consequently, FACS protocols based solely on SSEA4 and TRA-1-60 positivity may inadvertently co-isolate these contaminating cells, leading to impure cultures and inconsistent experimental outcomes.

Furthermore, the choice of reprogramming vector system introduces additional variability. Integrating viral vectors such as retroviruses, while effective, pose risks of insertional mutagenesis and transgene reactivation, which can impair genomic stability and differentiation potential. Non-integrating systems like Sendai virus offer a safer alternative by delivering reprogramming factors as RNA without genomic integration, yet they too present challenges in timing and efficiency of reprogramming, which may affect the kinetics of surface marker expression. Thus, an ideal iPSC derivation method must be robust across different vector platforms and capable of distinguishing true pluripotent cells from phenotypic mimics regardless of the reprogramming modality employed.

In light of these challenges, there remains a critical unmet need for a standardized, reliable, and high-fidelity method to isolate fully reprogrammed iPSCs at an early stage of the reprogramming process. Such a method should not only enhance the purity and functionality of derived lines but also enable automation, scalability, and compatibility with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards for future clinical translation. The inclusion of a negative selection marker—specifically one that is characteristic of the starting somatic population but extinguished upon complete reprogramming—represents a logical and underutilized strategy to refine cell sorting protocols. By combining positive selection for pluripotency markers with negative selection against a somatic lineage marker, it becomes possible to exclude residual parental and partially reprogrammed cells more effectively than with either approach alone. The present invention addresses this need by establishing CD13 as a key negative selection marker and demonstrating that the triple-gated population CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ constitutes a highly enriched source of bona fide iPSCs, irrespective of the reprogramming vector used or the disease status of the donor.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly effective method for the rapid, efficient, and standardized derivation of fully reprogrammed induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) from somatic cells, particularly human dermal fibroblasts, through the use of fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) based on a specific combination of cell surface markers. The core innovation lies in the strategic integration of both positive and negative selection criteria: specifically, the simultaneous selection for cells expressing the pluripotency-associated surface markers SSEA4 and TRA-1-60, and the exclusion of cells expressing the fibroblast-specific marker CD13. This results in the isolation of a CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ cell population that is highly enriched for fully reprogrammed, functionally pluripotent iPSCs, while effectively depleting contaminating non-reprogrammed, partially reprogrammed, or transformed somatic cells.

The method is applicable to somatic cells reprogrammed using either integrating (e.g., retroviral) or non-integrating (e.g., Sendai virus) vector systems, thereby offering broad compatibility and flexibility. It enables the identification and isolation of bona fide iPSCs as early as 7 to 14 days post-infection, significantly accelerating the derivation timeline compared to conventional manual picking methods that typically require 3–6 weeks. By performing FACS at this early window, the invention captures reprogramming events before overgrowth by non-pluripotent cells compromises culture purity, thus enhancing both the efficiency and reproducibility of iPSC generation.

The invention further demonstrates that iPSC lines derived using this CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ sorting strategy exhibit hallmark characteristics of pluripotency, including normal karyotype, silencing of exogenous reprogramming transgenes (in the case of integrating vectors), robust expression of endogenous pluripotency genes (e.g., Oct4, Sox2, Nanog), and the capacity to differentiate into derivatives of all three embryonic germ layers both in vitro (via embryoid body formation) and in vivo (via teratoma assay). Additionally, Southern blot analysis confirms that the method yields clonally independent iPSC lines, comparable to or exceeding the clonal diversity achieved by manual picking.

Moreover, the invention establishes that FACS-derived iPSC lines maintain stable pluripotency marker expression over extended passaging and can be successfully adapted to feeder-free culture conditions, underscoring their robustness and suitability for long-term experimentation and therapeutic applications. The method has been validated across a diverse panel of fibroblast lines derived from healthy donors and patients with various diseases—including type I diabetes, neurodegenerative disorders, cardiac conditions, and autoimmune diseases—demonstrating its generalizability and utility in disease modeling and precision medicine.

Beyond initial derivation, the invention also enables routine maintenance and purification of established iPSC cultures by periodic FACS to remove spontaneously differentiated cells, thereby ensuring consistent cellular homogeneity for high-throughput screening, drug discovery, and automated phenotyping platforms. In summary, the present invention provides a standardized, scalable, and high-fidelity platform for iPSC generation that overcomes the limitations of existing methods, paving the way for reliable and clinically relevant applications of reprogramming technology.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Rapid Production of iPS Cells and Colonies

The present invention discloses a method for the rapid and efficient production of induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells from somatic cells, particularly human dermal fibroblasts, through the application of a refined fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) protocol that leverages a specific triad of cell surface markers to isolate fully reprogrammed cells at an early stage of the reprogramming process. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on manual colony picking after prolonged culture periods of three to six weeks, the method of the present invention enables the identification and isolation of bona fide iPS cells as early as seven to fourteen days post-initiation of reprogramming, thereby dramatically accelerating the derivation timeline and improving the overall yield of high-quality clones. This acceleration is achieved by capitalizing on the dynamic changes in surface antigen expression that occur during the reprogramming cascade, wherein somatic cells progressively lose lineage-specific markers and acquire pluripotency-associated epitopes.

Central to the invention is the recognition that the mere presence of pluripotency markers such as SSEA4 and TRA-1-60 is insufficient to guarantee complete reprogramming, as a significant fraction of cells expressing these markers during the intermediate stages of reprogramming may still retain somatic identity or exhibit incomplete epigenetic remodeling. Specifically, it has been discovered that a substantial proportion of SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ cells co-express CD13, a well-characterized surface marker of human fibroblasts and other mesenchymal lineages, indicating that these cells are not fully reprogrammed but rather represent transitional or partially reprogrammed states. By incorporating negative selection against CD13 into the sorting strategy, the invention effectively excludes these contaminating populations, thereby enriching exclusively for cells that have undergone complete silencing of the somatic program and activation of the pluripotency network.

In practice, somatic cells—such as dermal fibroblasts obtained from fresh biopsies, frozen stocks, or established cell repositories—are first reprogrammed using standard protocols involving the delivery of the four canonical Yamanaka factors (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc) via either integrating retroviral vectors or non-integrating Sendai virus vectors. Following infection, cells are cultured under conditions supportive of pluripotency, typically in human embryonic stem cell medium (HUESM) supplemented with small molecule inhibitors such as SB431542 (an ALK5 inhibitor), PD0325901 (a MEK inhibitor), and Thiazovivin (a ROCK inhibitor), which collectively enhance reprogramming efficiency and cell survival. At approximately 7 to 14 days post-infection, when early reprogramming events are underway but before non-pluripotent cells overgrow the culture, the cells are harvested as a single-cell suspension and subjected to immunostaining with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against CD13, SSEA4, and TRA-1-60.

The stained cells are then analyzed and sorted using a high-precision FACS instrument operated under “gentle” sorting conditions—such as a 100 µm ceramic nozzle and 20 psi pressure—to minimize shear stress and preserve cell viability. The target population is defined as CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺, and cells meeting this criterion are sorted directly onto mitotically inactivated murine embryonic fibroblast (MEF) feeders or Matrigel-coated plates in HUESM supplemented with the ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 to promote survival and attachment. Within as few as three days post-sorting, distinct, compact colonies begin to emerge, exhibiting morphology indistinguishable from that of human embryonic stem cells. By day 7 to 10 post-sorting, these colonies are sufficiently large and well-defined to permit mechanical picking or enzymatic passaging for expansion and characterization.

Critically, cultures derived from the CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ population demonstrate markedly superior purity compared to those derived from populations selected only for SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ or SSEA4⁺ alone. In comparative experiments, wells seeded with CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ cells yielded three to four times more colonies than control populations and exhibited minimal contamination by differentiated or transformed cells, even after extended culture without manual removal of non-pluripotent cells. In contrast, control populations rapidly became overgrown with heterogeneous cell types, including CD13⁺ fibroblasts and CD13⁻SSEA4⁻TRA-1-60⁻ cells of unknown origin, thereby compromising the integrity of the resulting lines. This enhanced purity translates directly into improved downstream performance, as FACS-derived lines consistently demonstrate robust pluripotency, genomic stability, and differentiation capacity without the need for extensive subcloning or purification.

The method is not only rapid but also highly scalable and amenable to automation, making it suitable for high-throughput applications such as large-scale disease modeling initiatives or drug screening campaigns. By enabling the isolation of thousands of purified iPS cells in a single sort, the invention facilitates the generation of multiple independent clones from a single reprogramming experiment, thereby increasing the statistical power and reproducibility of biological studies. Furthermore, the early timing of the sort—before the onset of significant culture heterogeneity—ensures that each derived clone originates from a discrete reprogramming event, as confirmed by Southern blot analysis showing distinct integration patterns for retroviral transgenes among independently derived lines. This clonal independence is essential for capturing the full spectrum of biological variability inherent in patient-derived samples and for avoiding artifacts arising from the expansion of a single dominant clone.

In summary, the rapid production of iPS cells and colonies according to the present invention represents a paradigm shift in reprogramming methodology, replacing subjective, low-throughput manual picking with an objective, quantitative, and early-stage purification strategy that ensures the derivation of high-fidelity, fully reprogrammed iPSC lines with unprecedented efficiency and consistency.

### Immunofluorescence and Microscopy

Immunofluorescence and microscopy serve as critical analytical tools in the validation and characterization of induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells derived using the method of the present invention, providing visual and molecular confirmation of pluripotency, cellular homogeneity, and differentiation potential. Following FACS isolation of the CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ population and subsequent colony expansion, immunofluorescence staining is routinely employed to assess the expression of key pluripotency markers at the protein level, thereby corroborating the surface marker profile used for sorting and confirming the functional state of the derived cells. Standard protocols involve fixing expanded iPS cell colonies with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilizing the cells with a mild detergent such as 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocking non-specific binding sites with a solution containing 10% donkey serum in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Primary antibodies against intracellular pluripotency transcription factors—most notably Nanog, Oct4, and Sox2—are then applied overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated to fluorophores such as Alexa Fluor 488, 555, or 647. Nuclei are counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) to facilitate cellular visualization and quantification.

Microscopic analysis of these stained colonies reveals uniform, strong nuclear expression of Nanog, Oct4, and Sox2 across all cells within the colony, with no detectable signal in surrounding differentiated or non-pluripotent cells, which are typically absent in FACS-derived cultures. This homogeneous expression pattern stands in stark contrast to manually picked colonies, which often exhibit patchy or mosaic staining indicative of residual heterogeneity. Similarly, surface marker expression is confirmed by live-cell or fixed-cell immunostaining for SSEA4 and TRA-1-60, which show bright, membrane-localized fluorescence consistent with the gating strategy used during FACS. Importantly, CD13 staining is consistently negative in these colonies, confirming the effectiveness of the negative selection step and the absence of fibroblast contamination.

Beyond the undifferentiated state, immunofluorescence and microscopy are also indispensable for evaluating the trilineage differentiation potential of FACS-derived iPS cells. In vitro differentiation is typically induced via embryoid body (EB) formation, wherein iPS cell aggregates are cultured in suspension for several weeks to allow spontaneous differentiation into derivatives of the three embryonic germ layers. Following fixation and cryosectioning, EB sections are subjected to immunostaining with lineage-specific antibodies: alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) for endoderm, smooth muscle actin (αSMA) for mesoderm, and beta III tubulin (Tuj1) for ectoderm. Confocal or widefield fluorescence microscopy reveals the presence of distinct cellular structures expressing these markers within the same EB, demonstrating the capacity of the derived iPS cells to generate all three germ layers. Quantitative image analysis can further be employed to assess the relative abundance and spatial distribution of each lineage, providing a robust metric for comparing the differentiation efficiency of different iPS lines.

In addition to static imaging, time-lapse microscopy may be used to monitor colony formation and growth dynamics following FACS sorting, offering insights into the proliferative behavior and morphological evolution of purified reprogrammed cells. Such analyses confirm that CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ cells give rise to compact, dome-shaped colonies with sharp borders and high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratios—hallmarks of naïve pluripotency—within days of sorting, whereas control populations exhibit irregular, flattened, or disorganized growth patterns. Collectively, immunofluorescence and microscopy provide a comprehensive, multi-parametric validation of the quality and functionality of iPS cells generated by the present invention, ensuring that they meet the stringent criteria required for research and clinical applications.

### Primers

The present invention employs a suite of molecular biology tools, including quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and NanoString nCounter assays, to validate the transcriptional profile of induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells derived via the CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ FACS strategy. Central to these analyses are carefully designed oligonucleotide primers that enable the specific amplification and quantification of endogenous pluripotency genes, exogenous reprogramming transgenes, and lineage-specific differentiation markers. The primer sequences used in the qRT-PCR assays are optimized for high specificity, efficiency, and minimal off-target amplification, ensuring accurate measurement of gene expression levels across a wide dynamic range.

For the assessment of endogenous pluripotency network activation, primers are designed to target the coding regions of key transcription factors such as *OCT4* (POU5F1), *SOX2*, *KLF4*, *c-MYC*, and *NANOG*. These primers are validated to amplify only the endogenous genomic transcripts and not the virally delivered transgenes, which is critical for distinguishing between authentic reprogramming and mere transgene expression. For example, *OCT4* primers may be designed to span exon-exon junctions that differ between the endogenous locus and the retroviral construct, or to target untranslated regions (UTRs) absent in the transgene. Similarly, primers for *NANOG* are selected to avoid pseudogenes and ensure amplification of the functional pluripotency-associated transcript.

To evaluate transgene silencing—a hallmark of complete reprogramming—primers are designed to specifically amplify the viral backbone or unique vector sequences present in the integrated or episomal transgenes. In the case of retroviral reprogramming, primers targeting the long terminal repeat (LTR) or internal ribosome entry site (IRES) elements common to the four-factor cassette are used to quantify residual transgene expression. Successful reprogramming is indicated by robust expression of endogenous pluripotency genes coupled with near-complete silencing of the viral transgenes, a pattern consistently observed in FACS-derived lines but frequently absent in manually picked clones, which often exhibit persistent transgene expression.

For differentiation assays, primers targeting germ layer-specific markers are employed to confirm trilineage potential. These include *AFP* (endoderm), *BRACHYURY* and *αSMA* (mesoderm), and *PAX6* and *TUBB3* (ectoderm). Housekeeping genes such as *GAPDH*, *ACTB*, or *HPRT1* are used as internal controls for normalization, and primer efficiencies are validated to ensure comparability across samples. All primer sequences are provided in supplementary tables accompanying the original research and are synthesized with standard desalting purification to ensure reliability and reproducibility.

In addition to qRT-PCR, the invention utilizes the NanoString nCounter platform, which relies on color-coded probe pairs rather than primers for direct digital detection of RNA molecules without amplification. Custom codesets are designed to include probes for the same set of pluripotency, transgene, and lineage markers, enabling high-throughput, multiplexed profiling of dozens of genes simultaneously. This technology complements qRT-PCR by providing absolute counts of transcript molecules, reducing technical variability, and allowing for the detection of low-abundance transcripts that may be missed by PCR-based methods. Together, these primer- and probe-based assays form a rigorous molecular validation framework that confirms the functional pluripotency and genomic integrity of iPS cells generated by the present invention.

### Comparision of Retrovirus and Sendai Virus Vectors in Reprogramming

The present invention demonstrates that the CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ FACS sorting strategy is universally applicable across different reprogramming vector systems, including both integrating retroviral vectors and non-integrating Sendai virus vectors, thereby offering flexibility and safety depending on the intended application. A detailed comparison of these two platforms reveals both similarities and differences in reprogramming kinetics, surface marker dynamics, and transgene clearance, all of which are effectively managed by the invention’s unified sorting protocol.

Retroviral vectors, which integrate the reprogramming transgenes into the host genome, achieve high reprogramming efficiency but carry the risk of insertional mutagenesis and persistent transgene expression, which can interfere with differentiation and genomic stability. In contrast, Sendai virus, a single-stranded RNA virus that replicates exclusively in the cytoplasm, delivers the reprogramming factors without genomic integration and is gradually diluted out over cell divisions, resulting in transgene-free iPSCs. Despite these fundamental differences, the invention shows that both systems produce a CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ population that is highly enriched for fully reprogrammed cells.

However, the timing of surface marker expression differs between the two systems. Time-course flow cytometry analyses reveal that Sendai virus-mediated reprogramming generates a higher proportion of SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ cells throughout the reprogramming period compared to retroviral reprogramming, suggesting enhanced efficiency. Yet, the loss of CD13 occurs more slowly in Sendai-reprogrammed cultures: at day 7 post-infection, approximately 98–100% of SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ cells are still CD13⁺ in retroviral cultures, whereas in Sendai cultures, this proportion drops to only 1–2% by day 11, indicating a more rapid transition to a fully reprogrammed state. Nevertheless, by day 14, both systems converge in the proportion of CD13⁻ cells within the pluripotent gate, allowing the same FACS window (7–14 days) to be used for both.

Critically, iPSC lines derived from both vector systems using the CD13⁻SSEA4⁺TRA-1-60⁺ strategy exhibit equivalent pluripotency, as evidenced by identical expression profiles of endogenous pluripotency genes, successful trilineage differentiation in vitro and in vivo, and stable karyotypes. Moreover, Sendai-derived lines show complete clearance of viral RNA by passage 10, as confirmed by NanoString and qRT-PCR, while retroviral lines demonstrate appropriate silencing of transgenes and lack of reactivation. Southern blot analysis further confirms that retroviral lines contain distinct integration patterns, proving clonal independence.

Thus, the invention provides a vector-agnostic platform that accommodates the trade-offs between efficiency and safety, enabling researchers to select the optimal reprogramming system for their needs while maintaining a standardized, high-fidelity derivation protocol. This universality significantly enhances the translational potential of the method, as it can be adapted to GMP-compliant, non-integrating systems for clinical applications while retaining the robustness and purity demonstrated with research-grade retroviral protocols.
