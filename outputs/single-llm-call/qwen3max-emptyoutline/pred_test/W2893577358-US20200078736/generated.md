## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of functionalized nanoporous membranes and, more particularly, to ion-selective nanoporous membranes fabricated via layer-by-layer (LbL) deposition of polyelectrolytes on porous polymeric supports, wherein the ionic selectivity and chemical stability of the resulting membrane are independently tunable through controlled cross-linking chemistries. The invention further pertains to methods for manufacturing such membranes using scalable, aqueous-based processing techniques that enable precise modulation of fixed charge density within the polyelectrolyte coating without substantially compromising ionic conductivity. These membranes find utility in a broad range of electrochemical and separation technologies, including but not limited to electrodialysis for water purification, redox flow batteries, fuel cells, and other energy conversion or storage systems requiring robust, selective, and cost-effective ion-conducting barriers.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Nanoporous membranes have long been recognized as critical components in advanced separation and energy technologies due to their ability to regulate the transport of ions and molecules based on size, charge, and chemical affinity. In particular, the surface-to-volume ratio inherent to nanopores provides a unique platform for manipulating ion transport through electrostatic interactions between the pore wall and dissolved electrolytes. When the internal surfaces of such pores are functionalized with charged groups—either inherently present or deliberately introduced—the resulting electric double layer can significantly influence the mobility and selectivity of counter-ions relative to co-ions, thereby enabling phenomena such as ion rectification, enhanced permselectivity, and voltage-gated behavior. These effects are especially pronounced in asymmetric pore geometries, such as conical or hourglass-shaped nanopores, which can exhibit diode-like ionic current characteristics absent in symmetric cylindrical pores.

Despite these promising attributes, the practical deployment of nanoporous membranes in large-scale industrial applications—such as desalination, wastewater treatment, battery separators, or gas separation—has been hindered by challenges related to manufacturability, mechanical robustness, chemical stability, and cost. Traditional fabrication methods for functionalized nanoporous materials often involve complex lithographic processes, high-temperature treatments, or vacuum-based deposition techniques that are incompatible with roll-to-roll production or economically viable at scale. Moreover, many high-performance membranes rely on expensive or environmentally hazardous materials, limiting their sustainability and regulatory acceptance.

In response to these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned to bottom-up self-assembly strategies that leverage molecular-level interactions to construct functional thin films on porous substrates. Among these, layer-by-layer (LbL) deposition of polyelectrolytes has emerged as a particularly versatile and scalable approach. LbL assembly involves the sequential adsorption of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes onto a substrate through electrostatic, hydrogen bonding, or other non-covalent interactions, resulting in the formation of multilayered nanofilms with controllable thickness, composition, and functionality. This technique is notable for its simplicity: it typically requires only aqueous solutions of commercially available polymers, ambient processing conditions, and minimal equipment—often reduced to simple dip-coating or spray-coating protocols. As such, LbL is well-suited for coating large-area or three-dimensional substrates, including commercial track-etched polymeric membranes with uniform nanopore distributions.

A wide variety of substrates have been successfully functionalized via LbL, including anodic aluminum oxide, mesoporous silica, quartz nanopipettes, and polymeric membranes such as polycarbonate (PC) or polyethylene terephthalate (PET). The intrinsic surface charge of many of these materials—often negative due to hydrolysis or oxidation—facilitates the initial adsorption of a cationic polyelectrolyte without the need for extensive surface modification. Subsequent layers are then built up through alternating exposure to anionic and cationic polymers, with each pair constituting a “bilayer” (BL). Depending on the polymer chemistry, molecular weight, ionic strength, pH, and deposition protocol, film growth can proceed linearly or exponentially, allowing fine control over final film thickness at the nanometer scale.

Common polyelectrolyte pairs used in LbL include poly(styrene sulfonate) (PSS) with poly(allylamine hydrochloride) (PAH), or poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) with poly(ethyleneimine) (PEI). While sulfonate- and amine-based systems dominate the literature due to their strong ionic character and commercial availability, carboxylic acid-containing polymers like PAA offer additional advantages, particularly in terms of post-assembly chemical modification. Specifically, the carboxylic acid groups in PAA can participate in covalent cross-linking reactions with amine-functionalized polymers such as PEI, either directly via carbodiimide-mediated amide bond formation or indirectly through bifunctional aldehydes like glutaraldehyde (GA). Such cross-linking not only enhances the mechanical and chemical stability of the polyelectrolyte film—preventing dissolution, delamination, or reorganization during operation—but also enables deliberate tuning of the net fixed charge density within the membrane.

However, prior art has largely treated cross-linking as a means solely to improve durability, with insufficient attention paid to how different cross-linking chemistries differentially affect the electrostatic landscape of the polyelectrolyte network. For instance, GA selectively reacts with primary amines on PEI, converting positively charged –NH₃⁺ groups into neutral Schiff base linkages, thereby reducing cationic fixed charge while leaving anionic carboxylate groups from PAA unaffected. In contrast, carbodiimide reagents such as 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC) catalyze the direct coupling of carboxylic acids to amines, forming amide bonds that simultaneously consume one anionic and one cationic group, preserving the net charge balance. These distinct mechanisms imply that cross-linking is not merely a stabilizing step but a powerful tool for engineering ion selectivity—a parameter of paramount importance in applications like electrodialysis, where high cation or anion transference numbers dictate process efficiency.

Despite this potential, existing literature lacks a systematic framework for decoupling ionic selectivity from ionic conductivity in LbL-coated nanoporous membranes. Most studies report trade-offs wherein increased selectivity comes at the expense of reduced conductivity due to pore narrowing or increased tortuosity. Furthermore, few investigations have examined how film thickness, cross-linking agent choice, and bilayer number interact to govern both performance metrics simultaneously. Critically missing is a demonstration that ionic selectivity can be enhanced independently of conductivity through rational design of the polyelectrolyte architecture and cross-linking strategy—particularly using industrially relevant, large-area membranes (>10 cm²) processed under mild, aqueous conditions.

Accordingly, there remains a significant unmet need for nanoporous membranes that combine high ionic selectivity, adequate ionic conductivity, long-term chemical stability, and scalable manufacturability. The present invention addresses this gap by providing a novel class of LbL-assembled, cross-linked polyelectrolyte membranes wherein the fixed charge density—and thus ion selectivity—is precisely modulated through the selection of cross-linking chemistry, while maintaining sufficient pore accessibility to preserve ionic conduction pathways. This approach enables the creation of tailored ion-selective barriers optimized for specific electrochemical environments without resorting to exotic materials or complex fabrication infrastructure.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a nanoporous membrane comprising a porous polymeric support substrate having a plurality of nanopores extending therethrough, and a polyelectrolyte coating disposed on at least a portion of the internal and external surfaces of said substrate, wherein said polyelectrolyte coating is formed by layer-by-layer (LbL) deposition of alternating layers of a cationic polyelectrolyte and an anionic polyelectrolyte, and wherein said polyelectrolyte coating is covalently cross-linked using a cross-linking agent selected to differentially modify the fixed charge density of the coating, thereby enabling independent control of ionic selectivity and ionic conductivity.

In one embodiment, the cationic polyelectrolyte is poly(ethyleneimine) (PEI) and the anionic polypelectrolyte is poly(acrylic acid) (PAA). The porous polymeric support substrate is preferably a track-etched polycarbonate membrane with nominal pore diameters ranging from 10 nm to 200 nm, though other microporous or nanoporous polymers such as polyethylene, polypropylene, polyethersulfone, or polyvinylidene fluoride may also be employed. The polyelectrolyte coating comprises at least three bilayers (3BL) to ensure complete coverage of the nanopore walls and surface, with preferred embodiments utilizing three to five bilayers.

The cross-linking agent is selected from the group consisting of glutaraldehyde (GA) and carbodiimide compounds such as N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride (EDC). When GA is used, it preferentially reacts with primary amine groups of PEI, reducing the cationic fixed charge density while leaving anionic carboxylate groups of PAA largely intact, thereby increasing the net negative charge of the polyelectrolyte film and enhancing cation selectivity. In contrast, when EDC is used, it catalyzes amide bond formation between carboxylic acid groups of PAA and amine groups of PEI, consuming equal numbers of anionic and cationic functional groups and thus preserving the net charge neutrality of the film, resulting in lower cation selectivity compared to GA-cross-linked counterparts.

The invention further provides a method for manufacturing such membranes, comprising the steps of: (a) providing a nanoporous polymeric support membrane; (b) optionally treating the membrane surface to enhance wettability or introduce reactive groups; (c) sequentially immersing the membrane in aqueous solutions of a cationic polyelectrolyte and an anionic polyelectrolyte to form a multilayered polyelectrolyte coating comprising at least three bilayers; and (d) contacting the coated membrane with a cross-linking agent to covalently stabilize the polyelectrolyte structure and modulate its fixed charge density. The method is conducted entirely in aqueous media at ambient temperature and pressure, making it compatible with continuous, large-scale production.

The resulting membranes exhibit superior performance characteristics compared to uncross-linked or conventionally cross-linked analogues. Specifically, GA-cross-linked membranes with three or more bilayers demonstrate enhanced cation transference numbers (t₊ > 0.65) while maintaining ionic conductivities comparable to or exceeding those of bare support membranes in dilute electrolyte solutions. Cross-linking prevents polymer re-dissolution, surface diffusion, and film delamination during operation, ensuring long-term stability in aqueous environments across a wide pH range. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) confirm uniform pore filling, coherent interfacial adhesion, and effective cross-linking, with film thicknesses ranging from approximately 500 nm to over 1200 nm depending on bilayer number.

Importantly, the invention demonstrates for the first time that ionic selectivity can be decoupled from ionic conductivity through strategic choice of cross-linking chemistry. While both GA and EDC improve mechanical integrity, only GA increases the asymmetry in fixed charge distribution, thereby boosting cation selectivity without proportionally sacrificing conductivity. This represents a paradigm shift from conventional wisdom that higher selectivity necessarily entails lower conductivity due to increased steric or electrostatic barriers.

The membranes of the present invention are particularly suited for applications requiring selective ion transport under low-to-moderate ionic strength conditions, such as electrodialysis stacks for brackish water desalination, anion-exchange or cation-exchange membranes in redox flow batteries, proton-exchange membranes in fuel cells, and lab-on-a-chip devices for biomolecular separation. Their compatibility with inexpensive, commercially available materials and scalable processing makes them economically viable for industrial deployment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Example: LbL Deposition of Bilayer PAA and PEI Polyelectrolytes

The present invention is exemplified by the fabrication and characterization of nanoporous polycarbonate (PC) membranes functionalized with layer-by-layer (LbL) deposited polyelectrolyte coatings composed of poly(acrylic acid) (PAA) and poly(ethyleneimine) (PEI), followed by covalent cross-linking using either glutaraldehyde (GA) or N-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-N′-ethylcarbodimide hydrochloride (EDC). This system serves as a model platform to illustrate the core principles of the invention: namely, that ionic selectivity and chemical stability can be independently engineered through controlled cross-linking chemistry, while maintaining adequate ionic conductivity for practical applications.

The starting material is a commercially available track-etched nanoporous PC membrane with a nominal pore diameter of 50 nm, a thickness of approximately 6 μm, and a porosity of about 1–5%. Prior to LbL deposition, the membrane undergoes a two-step surface activation protocol to remove residual surfactants and enhance surface hydrophilicity. First, the membrane is immersed in a 4.5 M aqueous sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution for 5 minutes to hydrolyze any surface-bound polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) commonly used as a wetting agent during membrane manufacturing. Following thorough rinsing with deionized (DI) water, the membrane is subjected to UV–ozone (UVO) treatment for 10 minutes per side. This step oxidizes the PC surface, generating carboxylic acid and hydroxyl groups that improve wettability and provide anchoring sites for the initial polyelectrolyte layer.

The LbL assembly is initiated by immersing the activated membrane in a 0.1 wt% aqueous solution of branched PEI (average molecular weight ~25,000 g/mol) adjusted to pH 10.4 using NaOH. At this pH, the majority of PEI’s amine groups—primary, secondary, and tertiary—are protonated, rendering the polymer highly cationic. After a 5-minute immersion, the membrane is rinsed extensively with DI water to remove loosely adsorbed chains. It is then transferred to a 0.2 wt% aqueous solution of PAA (average molecular weight ~100,000 g/mol) at pH 3.2, where the carboxylic acid groups are predominantly protonated but capable of forming hydrogen bonds and weak electrostatic interactions with the underlying PEI layer. This sequence constitutes the first bilayer (1BL). Subsequent bilayers are deposited using shorter immersion times of 1 minute per polymer solution to prevent excessive swelling or interdiffusion, which could compromise layer definition.

Membranes are fabricated with 1, 3, or 5 bilayers and are either left uncross-linked or subjected to post-assembly cross-linking. For GA cross-linking, the coated membrane is immersed in a 25% (v/v) aqueous GA solution for 12 hours at room temperature. GA reacts selectively with primary amines on PEI to form Schiff base linkages, effectively neutralizing positive charges and creating covalent bridges between adjacent PEI chains. For EDC cross-linking, the membrane is immersed in a 100 mM aqueous EDC solution for 12 hours. EDC activates carboxylic acid groups on PAA, enabling nucleophilic attack by amine groups on PEI to form stable amide bonds, thereby consuming one anionic and one cationic group per cross-link.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) in attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode is used to verify polyelectrolyte deposition and assess cross-linking efficacy. Spectra of bare PC show characteristic peaks at 1769 cm⁻¹ (carbonate C=O stretch), 1502 cm⁻¹ (C–H bend), and 1216–1157 cm⁻¹ (ether C–O stretches). Upon coating, new peaks emerge at 1714 cm⁻¹ (assigned to protonated carboxylic acid C=O stretch of PAA) and 1550 cm⁻¹ (N–H bending vibration of PEI). In uncross-linked 3BL membranes, the N–H peak exhibits greater absorbance than the C=O peak, indicating an excess of amine groups. After EDC cross-linking, both peaks decrease in intensity by approximately equal amounts, confirming stoichiometric consumption of acid and amine functionalities. In contrast, GA cross-linking results in a dramatic reduction of the N–H peak relative to the C=O peak, demonstrating selective amine depletion and a consequent increase in net negative charge.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) reveals critical morphological differences between cross-linked and uncross-linked films. Plan-view images of uncross-linked 3BL membranes show patchy coverage with exposed nanopores, indicative of polymer desorption or lateral diffusion during handling. In contrast, both GA- and EDC-cross-linked 3BL membranes exhibit smooth, continuous surfaces with no visible pores, confirming that covalent stabilization is essential for film integrity. Cross-sectional SEM of 5BL GA membranes shows a uniform polyelectrolyte layer on both sides of the PC support, with an average total thickness of 1230 ± 193 nm. Freeze-fracture imaging of 3BL GA membranes reveals polyelectrolyte-filled nanopores, often appearing as hollow or solid “wires” extending through the membrane thickness—structures absent in bare PC and consistent with conformal pore wall coating.

Electrochemical characterization is performed to quantify ionic conductivity and selectivity. Conductivity is measured via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) on stacks of 1–4 identical membranes soaked in 10 mM or 100 mM NaCl. The real-axis intercept of the Nyquist plot gives the total resistance, from which the area-specific resistance (ASR) and ionic conductivity are derived. Bare PC membranes exhibit the highest conductivity due to unobstructed pores. Coating with 1BL GA reduces conductivity by partially occluding pores, but further bilayer addition (3BL or 5BL GA) does not cause additional decline; indeed, 5BL GA membranes recover conductivity to near-bare levels, suggesting that thicker, cross-linked films may develop interconnected ion-conducting pathways or reduced tortuosity. Uncross-linked 3BL membranes show the lowest conductivity, likely due to inconsistent pore coverage and polymer swelling that impedes ion flow.

Ionic selectivity is assessed using a potentiometric concentration cell. A transmembrane voltage develops when NaCl solutions of differing concentrations (0.1 mM vs. 0.1 mM to 1 M) are placed on either side of the membrane. The slope of the voltage versus log(activity ratio) plot yields the cation transference number (t₊) via a modified Nernst equation. Bare PC membranes show modest cation selectivity (t₊ ≈ 0.58) due to native negative surface charge. Surprisingly, 1BL GA membranes exhibit lower selectivity (t₊ ≈ 0.52), possibly because incomplete pore coverage creates heterogeneous charge domains. However, 3BL and 5BL GA membranes show progressively higher t₊ values (up to 0.65 for 5BL GA), reflecting uniform, negatively charged pore walls that favor cation transport. In contrast, 3BL EDC membranes display t₊ ≈ 0.54—nearly identical to uncross-linked 3BL—confirming that EDC preserves charge balance.

These results collectively demonstrate that the invention achieves independent control over key membrane properties: GA cross-linking enhances cation selectivity by skewing fixed charge toward negative values, while cross-linking in general—regardless of agent—preserves or even improves ionic conductivity by stabilizing a uniform, pore-spanning polyelectrolyte architecture. The method is scalable, uses benign solvents, and operates under ambient conditions, making it suitable for industrial adoption. The membranes are stable in aqueous electrolytes over extended periods and resist degradation under operational stresses, fulfilling critical requirements for real-world deployment in energy and environmental technologies.
