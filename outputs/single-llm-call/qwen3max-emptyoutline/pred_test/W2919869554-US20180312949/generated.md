# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of metallic materials, and more particularly to gold-based metallic glass matrix composites having controlled optical appearance and enhanced mechanical properties. Specifically, the invention provides a novel class of gold-containing alloys that combine the aesthetic appeal of traditional precious metal alloys with the superior hardness, wear resistance, and processability characteristic of bulk metallic glasses. The invention further encompasses methods for designing and producing such materials with predetermined color characteristics—such as yellow gold, rose gold, or other chromaticities—while simultaneously achieving high hardness values unattainable in conventional gold alloys used in luxury goods. These materials are especially suited for applications in jewelry, timepieces, ornamental articles, and other high-value consumer products where both visual aesthetics and mechanical durability are critical performance criteria. The invention leverages the unique microstructural architecture of metallic glass matrix composites (MGMCs), wherein finely dispersed crystalline dendrites of a gold-rich solid solution are embedded within a continuous amorphous metallic glass matrix, enabling independent control over color through compositional design of the dendritic phase and mechanical performance through the glassy matrix. This approach represents a significant advancement over prior art monolithic metallic glasses and conventional crystalline gold alloys, which are constrained by inherent trade-offs between color tunability, hallmark compliance, and mechanical robustness.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Gold has been prized for millennia not only for its intrinsic value but also for its distinctive luster, chemical inertness, and malleability, making it the material of choice for jewelry, coinage, and decorative arts. However, pure gold (24 karat) is too soft for most practical applications and is therefore almost always alloyed with other metals to enhance its mechanical properties. Traditional gold alloys are formulated to meet specific hallmark standards—such as 18-karat (75 wt% Au), 14-karat (58.3 wt% Au), or 9-karat (37.5 wt% Au)—which legally define the minimum gold content required for commercial sale in many jurisdictions. Beyond hallmark compliance, the aesthetic character of gold alloys is primarily defined by their color, which can range from classic yellow to white, rose, green, or even blue, depending on the nature and concentration of alloying elements. For instance, yellow gold typically contains copper and silver in balanced proportions to preserve the warm golden hue of pure gold; rose or red gold is enriched in copper to impart a reddish tint; white gold incorporates palladium, nickel, or manganese to neutralize color and produce a silvery appearance; and green gold results from higher silver content. Despite centuries of metallurgical refinement, these conventional alloys remain fundamentally limited by the inverse relationship between hardness and ductility: increasing hardness through solid-solution strengthening or precipitation hardening often compromises workability, while maintaining high ductility necessitates softer compositions prone to scratching and deformation.

In parallel, the development of bulk metallic glasses (BMGs)—non-crystalline metallic solids formed by rapid cooling of molten alloys—has introduced a new paradigm in metallic materials science. BMGs exhibit exceptional combinations of properties, including high strength, elastic limit, corrosion resistance, and wear resistance, along with near-net-shape formability in the supercooled liquid region. Gold-based BMGs have attracted particular interest due to their potential to merge the prestige of gold with the engineering advantages of amorphous metals. Early reports identified quaternary and quinary Au–Cu–Ag–Si–Pd systems capable of forming BMGs with critical casting diameters up to 5 mm and gold contents exceeding 85 wt%, thereby satisfying 18-karat or higher hallmark requirements. However, these monolithic Au-based BMGs consistently display a pale, whitish appearance attributed to the "bleaching" effect of silicon and palladium, which suppress the characteristic yellow reflectance of gold. Moreover, some of these glasses exhibit surface tarnishing upon ambient exposure, further detracting from their cosmetic suitability. Critically, the compositional window for glass formation in Au-based systems is extremely narrow, leaving little room to adjust optical properties without sacrificing amorphicity. Consequently, while mechanically superior, existing Au-BMGs lack the rich, customizable chromaticity expected in luxury applications.

An alternative strategy to overcome the limitations of monolithic BMGs involves the creation of metallic glass matrix composites (MGMCs), which consist of a continuous amorphous matrix reinforced with crystalline phases—typically dendritic in morphology. In such composites, the crystalline phase forms first during controlled solidification, followed by vitrification of the residual liquid into a glassy matrix. Pioneering work in Zr–Ti–Be and La–Cu–Ni–Al systems demonstrated that MGMCs can achieve unprecedented combinations of strength and toughness by tailoring dendrite volume fraction, size, and distribution. The success of these systems hinges on a large lattice mismatch between the primary crystalline phase and competing equilibrium intermetallics, which kinetically suppresses the nucleation of brittle secondary phases and promotes glass formation in the interdendritic regions. While this concept has been extensively explored for structural applications, its extension to optical property engineering—particularly in precious metal systems—has remained unexplored until now.

The challenge in applying MGMC design principles to gold alloys lies in identifying a compatible alloy system where (i) a gold-rich crystalline phase can be precipitated with tunable color, (ii) a coexisting metallic glass matrix can be stabilized, and (iii) the resulting microstructure is fine enough to appear visually homogeneous to the naked eye. Conventional wisdom suggests that gold’s face-centered cubic (fcc) structure and low reactivity might preclude sufficient lattice mismatch to enable MGMC formation. However, the inventors have discovered that the Au–Si system—when augmented with Cu, Ag, and Pd—exhibits precisely the requisite thermodynamic and kinetic conditions. Specifically, the primary solidification phase is an fcc Au–Cu–Ag solid solution whose color can be systematically varied across the yellow-to-rose spectrum by adjusting Cu/Ag ratios, while the residual liquid, enriched in Si and Pd, readily forms a stable metallic glass. Crucially, the diamond-cubic structure of elemental Si is highly incompatible with the fcc lattice of the Au-rich dendrites, creating a substantial interfacial energy barrier that inhibits heterogeneous nucleation of Si or silicides, thereby facilitating glass formation in the matrix. Furthermore, the partitioning behavior during solidification ensures that Si is almost entirely excluded from the dendrites, preserving their intended chromaticity.

Prior attempts to engineer color in metallic glasses have focused on surface treatments, thin-film coatings, or nanoscale plasmonic structures, all of which are either temporary, add manufacturing complexity, or fail to provide bulk color uniformity. Similarly, efforts to increase the hardness of colored gold alloys through conventional metallurgy—such as age hardening of Au–Cu systems—yield only modest improvements (typically ≤50% increase over annealed hardness) and often require post-processing heat treatments that may alter color or surface finish. None of these approaches offer a route to simultaneously achieve hallmark-compliant gold content, designer chromaticity, high hardness, and long-term color stability in a single, bulk material. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing a fundamentally new class of gold-based MGMCs wherein color is an intrinsic, bulk property engineered at the microstructural level, and hardness is significantly enhanced through the synergistic combination of a hard glassy matrix and a tailored crystalline reinforcement. This represents a paradigm shift from empirical alloy development to rational microstructure-by-design for cosmetic metals.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel class of gold-based metallic glass matrix composites (MGMCs) that exhibit controlled, uniform color and high hardness, suitable for luxury applications such as jewelry and timepieces. These MGMCs comprise a continuous metallic glass matrix phase and a dispersed crystalline dendritic phase, wherein the dendritic phase consists of a gold-rich face-centered cubic (fcc) solid solution with tunable chromaticity, and the matrix phase is a silicon- and palladium-containing amorphous alloy that appears pale white. The overall color of the composite is visually homogeneous and can be precisely engineered to fall within desired chromatic categories—including yellow gold, rose gold, or intermediate hues—by controlling the composition of the dendritic phase and its volume fraction relative to the matrix. Critically, the microstructural features (including dendrite trunk diameter, arm diameter, and interdendritic spacing) are maintained below approximately 20 micrometers, rendering them unresolved by the naked human eye and ensuring macroscopic color uniformity. The apparent color of the composite follows a rule-of-mixtures relationship, wherein the CIELAB color coordinates are the volume-weighted average of those of the individual phases.

The invention further provides a specific compositional framework based on a thermodynamic tie-line between a quinary eutectic metallic glass-forming alloy (Au₅₀Cu₂₅.₅Ag₃Pd₃Si₁₈.₅) and a ternary gold-rich solid solution (Au₆₅.₂Cu₂₂.₄Ag₁₂.₄). Any alloy whose composition lies along this tie-line, expressed by the general formula Au₆₅.₂₋₁₅.₂ₓCu₂₂.₄₊₃.₁ₓAg₁₂.₄₋₉.₄ₓPd₃ₓSi₁₈.₅ₓ (where x is the molar fraction of the metallic glass phase, ranging from 0 to 1), will form an MGMC with the two equilibrium phases in proportions dictated by the lever rule. By varying x, the volume fraction of the metallic glass matrix can be continuously adjusted from 0% (pure dendritic alloy) to 100% (monolithic metallic glass), thereby enabling fine control over both color and mechanical properties. All compositions within this series maintain a gold weight fraction exceeding 85%, satisfying 18-karat hallmark requirements.

Additionally, the invention discloses methods for producing these MGMCs through rapid solidification of molten alloys, resulting in ultra-fine microstructures with feature sizes typically between 1 and 6 micrometers, independent of sample geometry up to at least 4 mm in thickness. The resulting materials exhibit Vickers hardness values ranging from approximately 120 HV (for the pure dendritic phase) to 350 HV (for the monolithic glass), with MGMC compositions showing linearly interpolated hardness that significantly exceeds that of conventional gold alloys. For example, an MGMC with 65% metallic glass phase achieves a hardness of 295 HV—representing a 145% improvement over the base dendritic alloy—without requiring post-solidification heat treatment. The invention thus enables the concurrent optimization of aesthetic and mechanical performance in a single, bulk gold-containing material, overcoming longstanding trade-offs in precious metal metallurgy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Methods of Processing the Alloy Ingots of the Sample Metallic Glass-Forming Alloys

The production of the gold-based metallic glass matrix composites (MGMCs) according to the present invention begins with the preparation of high-purity alloy ingots, which serve as the precursor material for subsequent casting operations. The ingot fabrication process is meticulously controlled to ensure compositional accuracy, homogeneity, and freedom from contamination, all of which are critical for achieving the desired phase equilibrium and microstructural characteristics in the final product. Elemental constituents—gold (Au), copper (Cu), silver (Ag), palladium (Pd), and silicon (Si)—are weighed according to the target stoichiometry derived from the general formula Au₆₅.₂₋₁₅.₂ₓCu₂₂.₄₊₃.₁ₓAg₁₂.₄₋₉.₄ₓPd₃ₓSi₁₈.₅ₓ, where x represents the molar fraction of the metallic glass phase and ranges from 0 to 1. The purity of each elemental starting material is specified to minimize impurity-induced nucleation or phase instability: gold is employed at a purity of 99.99% (4N), copper at 99.995% (4N5), silver at 99.95% (3N5), palladium at 99.95% (3N5), and silicon at 99.9999% (6N). These high purity levels are essential to prevent the introduction of heterogeneous nucleation sites that could disrupt the delicate balance between dendrite formation and glass vitrification during solidification.

The weighed elemental charges are loaded into a high-purity fused quartz crucible or tube, which is then sealed under vacuum or backfilled with an inert atmosphere to prevent oxidation, particularly of the reactive silicon component. Induction melting is employed as the primary melting technique due to its ability to achieve rapid, uniform heating with minimal contamination. The assembly is placed within an induction coil powered by a radiofrequency generator, and the current is gradually increased to raise the temperature above the liquidus of the alloy, typically to approximately 950°C, which ensures complete melting and homogenization of all constituents. During melting, the quartz container is maintained under a positive pressure of high-purity argon gas (≥99.999%) to exclude atmospheric oxygen and moisture. The molten alloy is held at the melting temperature for a sufficient duration—typically 2 to 5 minutes—to allow for thorough mixing and dissolution of all elements, with particular attention paid to the complete incorporation of silicon, which has a high melting point (1414°C) but dissolves readily in the gold-rich melt at elevated temperatures due to favorable thermodynamics in the presence of copper and palladium.

Following homogenization, the molten alloy is either cast directly into a mold for immediate solidification or allowed to cool slowly to form a master ingot for later remelting. In the latter case, the ingot is removed from the quartz container, cleaned of any adhering oxide or slag, and inspected for surface defects or inhomogeneities. To ensure compositional consistency across multiple processing batches, the master ingot may be subjected to arc melting under inert atmosphere as an alternative or supplementary homogenization step, although induction melting alone is generally sufficient when performed under controlled conditions. The resulting ingot exhibits a uniform metallic luster with no visible segregation, indicating successful alloying. This master ingot serves as the feedstock for the subsequent rapid solidification step, wherein the final MGMC microstructure is developed. The entire ingot preparation process is conducted in a controlled environment—preferably a glovebox or dry room with low humidity—to prevent surface oxidation or moisture absorption, which could introduce hydrogen or oxygen into the melt during subsequent remelting and adversely affect glass formability or color stability.

### Methods of Processing the Sample Metallic Glass Plates

The transformation of the master alloy ingots into finished metallic glass matrix composite (MGMC) plates with controlled microstructure and surface finish involves a precise sequence of thermal and mechanical processing steps designed to achieve rapid solidification while maintaining dimensional accuracy and surface quality. The process begins by sectioning the master ingot into smaller pieces of appropriate mass for the desired plate dimensions—typically 20 mm × 20 mm × 1 mm for optical and mechanical testing, though the method is scalable to larger or more complex geometries. These pieces are then loaded into a custom-fabricated quartz tube with rectangular cross-section, specifically engineered to match the target plate dimensions. The quartz tube has a wall thickness of approximately 0.5 mm to facilitate efficient heat transfer during quenching while maintaining structural integrity under thermal stress. Prior to loading, the interior of the quartz tube is thoroughly cleaned and dried to eliminate any particulate or gaseous contaminants that could interfere with wetting or introduce nucleation sites.

The loaded quartz tube is placed inside a resistance-heated or induction-heated furnace chamber, which is then evacuated and backfilled multiple times with high-purity argon gas (≥99.999%) to establish an oxygen- and moisture-free atmosphere. The furnace is ramped to a holding temperature of 950°C, which is maintained for a dwell time of 2 to 3 minutes to ensure complete melting of the alloy charge and homogenization of the liquid. At this temperature, the alloy exists as a fully molten, low-viscosity liquid that uniformly wets the quartz walls, filling the rectangular cavity without voids or inclusions. The choice of 950°C as the melting temperature is deliberate: it is sufficiently above the liquidus of even the highest-melting composition in the series (the dendritic end-member Au₆₅.₂Cu₂₂.₄Ag₁₂.₄, with Tₗ ≈ 950°C) to guarantee complete liquefaction, yet not so high as to cause excessive reaction with the quartz container or volatilization of lower-boiling constituents such as silver (boiling point 2162°C) or copper (boiling point 2562°C), both of which remain stable under these conditions.

Once homogenization is complete, the quartz tube containing the molten alloy is rapidly extracted from the furnace and plunged into a quenching medium—specifically, a bath of deionized water at ambient temperature (approximately 20–25°C). This water quenching step is executed with high reproducibility using an automated drop mechanism to ensure consistent cooling rates across all samples. The high thermal conductivity of water, combined with the thin wall of the quartz tube, facilitates extremely rapid heat extraction from the melt, achieving cooling rates estimated to exceed 10³ K/s. This rapid solidification is critical for the formation of the desired MGMC microstructure: it allows the primary Au-rich dendrites to nucleate and grow to a limited extent during the initial stages of cooling, but arrests further crystallization before equilibrium intermetallic phases can form, thereby promoting vitrification of the residual liquid into a metallic glass matrix. The resulting as-quenched plate exhibits full density with no porosity or cracks, and retains the precise rectangular geometry of the quartz mold.

Following quenching, the plate is carefully removed from the fractured quartz tube and subjected to a multi-stage polishing protocol to achieve an optically flat, mirror-like surface suitable for color measurement and hardness testing. The polishing sequence begins with coarse grinding using silicon carbide papers of progressively finer grit (e.g., 180, 320, 600, 1200 grit) under continuous water lubrication to remove surface irregularities and machining marks. This is followed by diamond suspension polishing with particle sizes decreasing from 9 μm to 1 μm on woven cloth pads, again with ample lubrication to prevent smearing or embedding of abrasive particles. The final polish is performed using a colloidal silica suspension (0.05 μm particle size) on a napped cloth to eliminate subsurface damage and produce a true mirror finish with surface roughness below 10 nm Ra. Throughout the polishing process, care is taken to avoid overheating or plastic deformation of the surface, which could alter the native microstructure or induce artifacts in subsequent characterization. The finished plate displays a uniform, reflective surface that accurately represents the bulk color and microstructure of the MGMC, with no evidence of oxidation, staining, or preferential etching of either phase.

### Method for Measuring the CIELAB Color Coordinates

The quantification of the optical appearance of the gold-based metallic glass matrix composites (MGMCs) is performed using the internationally standardized CIELAB color space, which provides a perceptually uniform representation of color in terms of three coordinates: L* (lightness), a* (green-red axis), and b* (blue-yellow axis). This method ensures objective, reproducible assessment of color that correlates closely with human visual perception, enabling precise comparison between different alloy compositions and validation of the color-mixing model. Measurements are conducted on the polished 20 mm × 20 mm × 1 mm plate coupons prepared as described above, using a high-precision spectrophotometer specifically calibrated for metallic and reflective surfaces.

The instrument employed is a Konica Minolta CM-700d spectrophotometer, which operates on the principle of diffuse illumination and 8-degree viewing geometry (d/8°), in accordance with ASTM E179 and ISO 7724 standards for color measurement of opaque materials. The spectrophotometer is equipped with a pulsed xenon lamp light source that provides stable, full-spectrum illumination from 360 nm to 740 nm, and a silicon photodiode array detector that captures the reflected spectral radiance factor at 10-nm intervals. Prior to each measurement session, the instrument is calibrated using a certified white calibration tile and a black trap to establish baseline reflectance and zero-point references, respectively. The aperture size is set to 8 mm to ensure that the measurement area is significantly larger than the characteristic microstructural features of the MGMCs (which are <6 μm), thereby capturing a statistically representative average of the composite surface.

Each plate coupon is measured at four distinct locations—specifically, at each of the four corners, approximately 2 mm inward from the edges—to account for any minor spatial variations in composition or microstructure that may arise during casting. At each location, the spectrophotometer probe is placed perpendicular to the polished surface with gentle, consistent pressure to maintain optimal contact without inducing deformation. Three replicate readings are taken at each corner, and the resulting spectral data are converted to CIELAB coordinates using the CIE 1976 standard observer functions and illuminant D65 (representing average daylight). The final reported L*, a*, and b* values for each alloy are calculated as the arithmetic mean of the twelve individual measurements (three replicates × four corners), with standard deviations typically less than ±0.5 units, confirming excellent homogeneity across the sample surface.

The CIELAB coordinates obtained through this method are directly interpretable in terms of visual appearance: higher L* values indicate greater lightness or reflectivity; positive a* values denote a reddish tint, while negative values indicate greenish tones; and positive b* values correspond to yellowish hues, with negative values indicating bluish tones. For the MGMCs of the present invention, the measured coordinates consistently fall within the yellow-to-pale-white region of color space (a* > 0, b* > 0), with systematic variation as a function of the metallic glass phase fraction x. The linearity of L*, a*, and b* with respect to x—demonstrated by correlation coefficients R² > 0.98—validates the assumption that the microstructure is visually unresolved and that the rule of mixtures accurately predicts composite color. This quantitative color measurement protocol is integral to the invention’s color-design methodology, enabling the precise targeting of chromatic specifications for commercial applications.

### Method for Performing Differential Scanning Calorimetry

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) is employed as a key analytical technique to characterize the thermal behavior, phase stability, and transformation temperatures of the gold-based metallic glass matrix composites (MGMCs) and their constituent phases. This method provides critical insights into the glass-forming ability of the matrix, the melting characteristics of the dendritic phase, and the thermodynamic equilibrium between the two phases, all of which underpin the compositional design strategy of the invention. DSC measurements are performed on small disc-shaped specimens sectioned from the central region of 2-mm-diameter rod samples, which are prepared using the same rapid quenching protocol as the plate coupons to ensure microstructural consistency.

The DSC analysis is conducted using a high-sensitivity heat-flux DSC instrument, such as a TA Instruments Q2000 or equivalent, which is capable of accurately resolving subtle thermal events associated with glass transitions, crystallization, and melting. Prior to analysis, the instrument is calibrated for temperature and enthalpy using high-purity indium (melting point 156.6°C, ΔH_fus = 28.4 J/g) and sapphire standards. Each sample disc, weighing between 10 and 15 mg, is hermetically sealed in an aluminum DSC pan with a crimped lid to prevent oxidation or mass loss during heating. An empty aluminum pan serves as the reference. The samples are heated at a constant rate of 20 K/min under a continuous purge of high-purity nitrogen gas (≥99.999%) at a flow rate of 50 mL/min to maintain an inert atmosphere and ensure consistent heat transfer.

During the DSC scan, several characteristic thermal events are observed and recorded. For monolithic metallic glass samples (x = 1.0), a distinct endothermic step change in heat flow is detected at the glass transition temperature (T_g), marking the onset of the supercooled liquid region, followed by an exothermic peak at the crystallization temperature (T_x), where the amorphous structure transforms into crystalline phases. For the pure dendritic alloy (x = 0), a sharp endothermic peak is observed at the liquidus temperature (T_l), corresponding to complete melting, with no glass transition or crystallization events. In the MGMC samples (0 < x < 1), the DSC thermogram exhibits a superposition of these features: a glass transition and crystallization event attributable to the metallic glass matrix, and a melting endotherm corresponding to the dendritic phase. The solidus temperature (T_s)—defined as the onset of melting—is consistently observed at approximately 350°C across all MGMC and glassy compositions, reflecting the eutectic nature of the matrix phase.

The temperatures of these transitions—T_g, T_x, T_s, and T_l—are determined using standard onset and peak analysis protocols provided by the DSC software. Specifically, T_g is identified as the midpoint of the step change in heat capacity; T_x as the peak maximum of the exothermic crystallization event; T_s as the onset of the endothermic melting peak; and T_l as the peak maximum of the melting endotherm. The reproducibility of these measurements is confirmed by triplicate analyses of each composition, with standard deviations less than ±2°C for all transition temperatures. The DSC data reveal that T_g (~115°C) and T_x (~160°C) remain essentially constant across the MGMC series, indicating that the glassy matrix composition is invariant and consistent with the eutectic alloy Au₅₀Cu₂₅.₅Ag₃Pd₃Si₁₈.₅. In contrast, T_l decreases monotonically from ~950°C (x = 0) to ~375°C (x = 1), reflecting the progressive dilution of the high-melting dendritic phase by the low-melting glassy matrix. This systematic variation in T_l, combined with the fixed T_s, enables the construction of a pseudo-binary phase diagram along the compositional tie-line, which validates the lever-rule prediction of phase fractions and confirms the thermodynamic equilibrium between the two phases. Thus, DSC serves as an indispensable tool for verifying the foundational assumptions of the invention’s compositional design framework.
