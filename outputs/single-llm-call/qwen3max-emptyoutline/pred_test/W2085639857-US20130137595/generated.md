# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of diagnostic medicine and, more specifically, to methods and systems for the early detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL) in human subjects who are infected with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). The invention further pertains to the identification and use of a panel of plasma protein biomarkers that are differentially expressed in HIV-infected individuals with HL as compared to HIV-infected individuals without HL. These biomarkers are associated with inflammatory and oncogenic pathways and may be employed in non-invasive screening assays to facilitate the timely diagnosis of HL, thereby enabling earlier and less aggressive therapeutic interventions. The invention also encompasses kits, diagnostic platforms, and analytical protocols configured to detect and quantify one or more of these biomarkers in biological samples, particularly human plasma, using high-sensitivity proteomic methodologies such as accurate mass tag (AMT) liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS). The technical domain of this invention intersects clinical oncology, virology, immunology, and precision diagnostics, and it is particularly relevant to the management of cancer in immunocompromised populations, including those with acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS). The disclosed biomarkers and associated diagnostic methods address an unmet clinical need for routine, non-invasive screening tools capable of detecting HL at its earliest stages in high-risk cohorts, thereby reducing treatment-related morbidity and improving long-term survival outcomes.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL) is a malignant neoplasm of B-cell origin that is distinguished by the presence of rare neoplastic Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg (HRS) cells embedded within a reactive inflammatory microenvironment comprising a heterogeneous infiltrate of non-neoplastic immune cells, including T lymphocytes, macrophages, eosinophils, and plasma cells. This unique histological architecture—where the actual tumor cells constitute less than 2% of the total tumor mass—has led to the conceptualization of HL not merely as a conventional cancer but as a disorder of dysregulated immune signaling and chronic inflammation. The pathogenesis of HL in immunocompromised individuals, particularly those infected with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), is closely linked to co-infection with the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV). In the general population, EBV is carried asymptomatically by over 90% of adults; however, under conditions of impaired cellular immunity, such as in advanced HIV infection, EBV can drive aberrant B-cell proliferation and contribute to the development of HL. Consequently, individuals with HIV/AIDS exhibit a markedly elevated risk—estimated to be between 5.6- and 14.7-fold higher—of developing HL compared to the general population.

The advent of highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) has dramatically improved the life expectancy of people living with HIV, transforming HIV from a terminal illness into a manageable chronic condition. However, this increased longevity has been accompanied by a rising incidence of non-AIDS-defining cancers, among which HL is one of the most prevalent. Despite the effectiveness of current treatment regimens—including combination chemotherapy and radiation therapy—the management of HL in HIV-positive patients remains challenging due to heightened treatment-related toxicities, increased susceptibility to opportunistic infections, and the necessity for concurrent supportive care, such as continued HAART, prophylactic antifungals, and hematopoietic growth factors. Critically, treatment intensity is often dictated by disease stage at diagnosis: early-stage HL typically requires less aggressive therapy, resulting in fewer adverse effects and better quality of life. Therefore, the ability to detect HL at its earliest, asymptomatic phase would represent a significant clinical advancement.

Currently, no routine screening method exists for the early detection of HL in HIV-infected individuals. Diagnosis is typically made only after the onset of clinical symptoms such as persistent lymphadenopathy, fever, night sweats, or weight loss—signs that often indicate more advanced disease. While certain laboratory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP), beta-2-microglobulin (B2M), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), have been reported to be elevated in HL patients in the general population, their utility in the specific context of HIV infection has not been systematically evaluated, and none have been validated as standalone screening tools. Moreover, these markers lack sufficient specificity and sensitivity for population-level screening, particularly in a setting where chronic inflammation and immune activation are already hallmarks of HIV disease itself.

Proteomic technologies offer a promising avenue for the discovery of novel biomarkers that reflect the complex interplay between viral infection, immune dysregulation, and oncogenesis. Among these, the accurate mass tag (AMT) tag approach coupled with high-resolution liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) enables deep, quantitative profiling of the plasma proteome with high throughput and reproducibility. This methodology allows for the simultaneous detection and relative quantification of thousands of peptides across multiple samples, facilitating the identification of subtle but consistent differences in protein expression associated with disease states. Prior applications of AMT-based proteomics have successfully uncovered candidate biomarkers for various cancers and inflammatory conditions, yet its potential for identifying HL-specific signatures in the context of HIV has remained unexplored.

The absence of a validated, non-invasive screening test for HL in HIV-infected populations represents a critical gap in clinical oncology. Existing diagnostic paradigms rely on symptom-driven evaluation, which delays intervention and increases the burden of therapy. There is thus a compelling need for a reliable, objective, and scalable method to detect HL before the manifestation of clinical signs. Such a method would ideally leverage circulating biomarkers that are mechanistically linked to the biology of HL—particularly those involved in inflammation, immune cell recruitment, and tumor microenvironment modulation—and that can be measured in easily accessible biofluids like plasma. The present invention addresses this need by providing a novel panel of 60 plasma proteins that are significantly and consistently altered in HIV-infected individuals with HL compared to matched controls without HL. These proteins, many of which are secreted by leukocytes or hepatocytes and are functionally annotated to pathways in cancer and inflammatory response, form the basis of a new diagnostic strategy for early HL detection in a high-risk population.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for the early detection of Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL) in human subjects infected with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) by measuring the abundance of one or more specific plasma protein biomarkers that are differentially expressed in the presence of HL. The invention is based on the discovery that a defined set of 60 plasma proteins exhibits statistically significant and biologically relevant changes in abundance in HIV-infected individuals diagnosed with HL compared to HIV-infected individuals without HL. These proteins are predominantly associated with inflammatory processes and oncogenic pathways, reflecting the underlying pathophysiology of HL as a malignancy driven by immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation. The method comprises obtaining a plasma sample from an HIV-infected subject, analyzing the sample using high-resolution proteomic techniques—preferably accurate mass tag (AMT) liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS)—to quantify the levels of the biomarker proteins, and comparing the measured abundances to reference values derived from a control population of HIV-infected individuals without HL. A significant deviation in the abundance of one or more of these biomarkers, particularly when exhibiting a fold-change of at least 1.5 and a p-value of 0.05 or less, is indicative of the presence of HL.

In addition to the diagnostic method, the invention encompasses a diagnostic kit comprising reagents, standards, and instructions for performing the assay, including antibodies or affinity reagents specific to one or more of the 60 biomarker proteins, as well as software algorithms for data interpretation and risk stratification. The invention further includes a system for automated analysis of plasma samples, integrating sample preparation, LC-MS instrumentation, and bioinformatic pipelines configured to identify and quantify the biomarker panel. The 60-protein biomarker signature includes both previously reported HL-associated proteins—such as C-reactive protein, beta-2-microglobulin, and lactate dehydrogenase—and 57 novel candidate biomarkers that have not heretofore been linked to HL in any population, let alone in the context of HIV infection. These novel proteins include acute-phase reactants, cytokines, complement factors, and other mediators of immune and inflammatory responses, many of which are secreted by the abundant non-neoplastic leukocytes that constitute the bulk of the HL tumor microenvironment or by the liver as part of the systemic inflammatory response.

The invention is particularly advantageous because it enables non-invasive, pre-symptomatic screening of a high-risk population, thereby facilitating earlier diagnosis and less intensive treatment. Unlike conventional imaging or biopsy-based approaches, which are impractical for routine screening, the plasma-based proteomic assay described herein can be readily integrated into existing clinical monitoring protocols for HIV-infected individuals. Furthermore, the use of a multi-analyte panel enhances diagnostic sensitivity and specificity compared to single-marker approaches, mitigating the confounding effects of chronic immune activation inherent to HIV disease. The biomarker panel was identified through a rigorous, blinded, and randomized proteomic study involving 36 well-characterized plasma samples, with duplicate analyses ensuring data robustness and statistical validation confirming the significance of the observed differences. The biological relevance of the identified proteins was further corroborated using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis, which confirmed enrichment in canonical pathways related to “inflammatory response” and “cancer.” Thus, the present invention provides a scientifically grounded, clinically actionable solution to a long-standing unmet medical need in the care of HIV-infected patients at risk for HL.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

### Materials and Methods

The present invention was developed through a comprehensive proteomic investigation designed to identify plasma protein biomarkers differentially expressed in HIV-infected individuals with and without Hodgkin’s lymphoma (HL). The study protocol received full ethical approval from the George Washington University Medical Center Institutional Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to sample collection. Additional institutional review was conducted by the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) Institutional Review Board, which verified the adequacy of the initial ethical oversight before the transfer of biological specimens. Plasma samples were sourced from the AIDS and Cancer Specimen Resource (San Francisco, CA), a repository dedicated to supporting research on malignancies in HIV-infected populations. The study cohort consisted of 36 subjects, divided into two groups: 21 cases (HIV-infected individuals with histologically confirmed HL) and 15 controls (HIV-infected individuals without HL). The control group was carefully matched to the case group with respect to age and gender to minimize demographic confounding variables. Notably, the majority of HL cases (n = 12) had not received chemotherapy for HL within at least 30 days prior to sample collection, thereby reducing the potential influence of treatment-induced proteomic alterations; for the remaining cases, chemotherapy status was either unknown (n = 9) or known to have occurred within 30 days (n = 1). To ensure analytical rigor, all samples were processed in a blinded manner, with randomization applied within blocks defined by HL status.

Sample preparation began with the depletion of the 12 most abundant plasma proteins using Proteome Purify™ 12 immunodepletion resin (R&D Systems), following the manufacturer’s instructions. This step was essential to enhance the detection of lower-abundance proteins that might otherwise be masked by highly concentrated albumin, immunoglobulins, and other dominant plasma constituents. Following depletion, the remaining proteins were precipitated using ice-cold trichloroacetic acid (TCA) at a final concentration of 10%, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C and centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 5 minutes. The resulting protein pellet was washed with cold acetone to remove residual TCA and then air-dried at room temperature for 5 minutes. The dried pellet was subsequently resuspended in a denaturing buffer containing 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate, 8 M urea, 2 M thiourea, and 5 mM dithiothreitol, and incubated at 60°C for 30 minutes to facilitate protein unfolding and reduction of disulfide bonds. The sample was then diluted four-fold with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate, and calcium chloride was added to a final concentration of 1 mM to support enzymatic activity. Sequencing-grade, methylated trypsin (Promega) was introduced at a substrate-to-enzyme ratio of 50:1 (mass:mass), and digestion was allowed to proceed for 15 hours at 37°C. The resulting peptide mixture was purified using solid-phase extraction (SPE) on a 1 mL C18 column (Supelco), with elution performed using 1 mL of methanol. After concentration via SpeedVac evaporation, the peptides were reconstituted in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate and stored at −20°C until analysis.

Peptide samples were analyzed using a custom-built, automated high-pressure nanocapillary liquid chromatography (HPLC) system coupled online to a LTQ-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The chromatographic column was fabricated by slurry-packing 3-µm Jupiter C18 bonded particles (Phenomenex) into a 65-cm-long, 75-µm-inner-diameter fused silica capillary. The mobile phases consisted of 0.2% acetic acid and 0.05% trifluoroacetic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in 90% acetonitrile/10% water (solvent B). Each sample (2.5 µg of peptides in 5 µL) was loaded onto the column and held at 100% solvent A for 50 minutes to allow for desalting and focusing. Peptide separation was achieved using an exponential gradient, increasing solvent B from 0% to 55% over 115 minutes. Electrospray ionization was performed using an in-house manufactured interface, with the heated capillary maintained at 200°C and the electrospray voltage set to 2.2 kV. Mass spectral data were acquired in the Orbitrap analyzer over a mass range of 400–2000 m/z at a resolution of 100,000, with automatic gain control (AGC) set to 5×10⁵. Data collection commenced 65 minutes after sample injection, corresponding to 15 minutes into the organic gradient, and continued for 100 minutes to capture the majority of eluting peptides.

Peptide identification was performed using the accurate mass tag (AMT) approach, which relies on a pre-existing database of tryptic peptides previously characterized by LC-MS/MS in human plasma. The database used in this study contained 30,573 entries, compiled from multiple prior investigations. Experimental peak lists from each LC-MS run were matched against this database based on accurate mass and normalized elution time, with a false-positive rate estimated at ≤6% using a decoy database search strategy involving sequence-reversed entries. The dataset comprised 76 mass spectrometry runs, including at least two technical replicates per sample, ensuring high reproducibility. Initial processing yielded 6,460 unique peptide identifications, which were filtered to retain only those with sufficient replicate measurements for statistical analysis, resulting in 3,814 peptides. These peptide abundances were normalized using a regression-based approach that accounted for run-specific variations, followed by median absolute deviation normalization across samples to correct for global intensity shifts. Statistical analysis was conducted using two complementary tests: a non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test to assess differences in signal intensity between groups, and a G-test (a likelihood-ratio variant of the chi-square test) to evaluate differences in detection frequency (presence/absence). Peptides with a p-value ≤ 0.05 in either test were considered significantly differentially abundant. Protein-level inference was performed using the RRollup algorithm within the DAnTE software platform, which aggregates peptide-level data into protein abundance estimates by weighting individual peptide contributions. Only proteins represented by at least two unique peptides were included in the final analysis. Redundancy arising from shared peptides across protein isoforms was resolved using Protein Prophet and manual curation favoring well-characterized, canonical proteins over hypothetical or predicted variants. Finally, the list of significantly altered proteins was subjected to functional annotation using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA), which employed the right-tailed Fisher’s Exact Test to determine enrichment in biological pathways and disease associations, with a minimum fold-change threshold of 1.5 applied to ratiometric comparisons (HL+/HL−).

### Results and Discussion

The proteomic analysis of plasma samples from 36 HIV-infected individuals—21 with HL and 15 without—yielded a comprehensive profile of the circulating proteome, leading to the identification of a robust biomarker signature for HL in this high-risk population. Across all 76 LC-MS runs (including duplicates), 3,814 unique peptides were reliably detected and quantified, corresponding to 629 distinct proteins. Of these, 647 peptides exhibited statistically significant differences in abundance or detection frequency between the HL-positive and HL-negative groups, mapping to 152 proteins. Applying a stringent criterion of at least 1.5-fold change in normalized signal intensity in addition to statistical significance (p ≤ 0.05), the list was refined to 60 proteins that constitute the core biomarker panel of the present invention. This panel includes a diverse array of molecules with established roles in inflammation, immune regulation, and cancer biology, thereby aligning with the known pathophysiology of HL as a malignancy characterized by a profound inflammatory microenvironment.

Among the 60 identified proteins, several have been previously implicated in HL in the general population, providing external validation of the current findings. Specifically, C-reactive protein (CRP), beta-2-microglobulin (B2M), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were all found to be significantly elevated in the HL-positive group. CRP, a classic acute-phase reactant produced by the liver in response to interleukin-6, is a well-documented marker of systemic inflammation and has been associated with poor prognosis in various lymphomas. B2M, a component of the major histocompatibility complex class I molecule, is shed by activated lymphocytes and tumor cells and serves as a surrogate marker of tumor burden and immune activation. LDH, a cytoplasmic enzyme released during cell turnover, is a standard prognostic indicator in lymphoma staging. The fact that these three proteins were independently identified in an HIV-infected cohort—despite the confounding background of chronic immune activation—suggests that their elevation in HL is robust and not merely an artifact of HIV disease progression. Indeed, although the HL-positive group exhibited a trend toward lower CD4+ T-cell counts (p = 0.07), indicating slightly more advanced immunosuppression, the consistency of these findings with prior studies in HIV-negative populations supports the specificity of these markers for HL itself.

Beyond these known markers, the invention discloses 57 novel candidate biomarkers that have not previously been associated with HL. These include serum amyloid A proteins, complement components (e.g., C3, C4b), chemokines (e.g., CCL18, CXCL16), adhesion molecules (e.g., ICAM-1), and various protease inhibitors and acute-phase proteins. Many of these are secreted by the abundant non-neoplastic leukocytes that infiltrate HL tumors or by hepatocytes as part of the systemic inflammatory response. Ingenuity Pathway Analysis confirmed that the 60-protein panel is highly enriched for functions related to “inflammatory response” (p = 1.2 × 10⁻⁸) and “cancer” (p = 3.4 × 10⁻⁶), with additional significant associations to “hematological disease,” “immune cell trafficking,” and “cellular movement.” This functional coherence underscores the biological plausibility of the biomarker panel and suggests that the detected proteomic changes reflect the underlying disease process rather than random noise.

Importantly, the biomarker signature was derived from a carefully controlled study design that minimized technical and biological confounders. Sample randomization, duplicate analyses, and rigorous statistical normalization ensured that the observed differences were attributable to HL status rather than batch effects or demographic imbalances. The use of AMT-based proteomics, with its high reproducibility and quantitative accuracy, further enhanced the reliability of the results. While the current study represents a discovery-phase investigation, the identified biomarkers provide a strong foundation for subsequent validation in larger, independent cohorts using targeted assays such as immunoassays or multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mass spectrometry. The ultimate goal is to develop a clinical-grade diagnostic test that can be deployed in routine HIV care settings to screen for HL before symptom onset, thereby enabling earlier intervention and improved outcomes. The present invention thus represents a significant advance in the field of precision oncology for immunocompromised populations, offering a scientifically grounded, non-invasive, and scalable solution to a critical unmet clinical need.
