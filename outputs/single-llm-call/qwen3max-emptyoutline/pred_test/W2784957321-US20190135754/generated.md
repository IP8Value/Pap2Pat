# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The construction of chiral quaternary stereocenters represents one of the most enduring challenges in synthetic organic chemistry. Unlike tertiary stereocenters, which can often be accessed through well-established asymmetric hydrogenation or epoxidation protocols, quaternary stereocenters—carbon atoms bearing four distinct non-hydrogen substituents—pose significant steric and electronic constraints that complicate both bond formation and stereocontrol. Over the past several decades, numerous strategies have been developed to address this challenge, including conjugate additions, cycloadditions, C–H functionalizations, and rearrangements. Among these, transition metal-catalyzed enantioselective allylic alkylation (AAA) has emerged as a particularly powerful and versatile method for the catalytic, asymmetric construction of quaternary stereocenters. First reported by Tsuji in 1965, AAA leverages the ability of transition metals such as palladium, iridium, and more recently nickel to activate allylic electrophiles toward nucleophilic attack while simultaneously enabling high levels of enantiocontrol through the use of chiral ligands.

In the canonical AAA manifold, a prochiral nucleophile—typically a stabilized enolate derived from a β-dicarbonyl compound—attacks a π-allyl metal complex generated in situ from an allylic precursor such as an acetate, carbonate, or halide. The resulting product contains a new carbon–carbon bond adjacent to a stereogenic center, and when the nucleophile is prochiral (i.e., generates a quaternary center upon alkylation), the reaction delivers enantioenriched products bearing all-carbon quaternary stereocenters. This transformation has been widely applied in the synthesis of natural products, pharmaceuticals, and complex chiral building blocks. Despite these advances, significant limitations remain, particularly with respect to the scope of viable nucleophiles and the practicality of the electrophilic coupling partners.

Notably, α-substituted lactones and lactams—cyclic esters and amides, respectively—have received comparatively little attention as prochiral nucleophiles in AAA reactions. This is surprising given their structural prevalence in bioactive molecules and their synthetic versatility. Lactones serve as masked 1,4- or 1,5-dicarbonyl systems that can be readily ring-opened to access acyclic quaternary stereocenters, while lactams are direct precursors to piperidine and pyrrolidine motifs, which constitute the most common nitrogen-containing heterocycles in FDA-approved drugs. Moreover, the incorporation of an α-acyl group (e.g., ester or amide) on the lactone or lactam ring not only enhances the acidity of the α-proton—facilitating deprotonation to generate the requisite enolate—but also provides an additional functional handle for downstream derivatization. Despite these advantages, prior art in this area is sparse. To date, only a single report describes a transition metal-catalyzed enantioselective AAA of monocyclic α-acyl lactones or lactams, and that method relies on preformed enol carbonates as nucleophile surrogates in a palladium-catalyzed decarboxylative process. While effective, this approach suffers from two critical drawbacks: first, the synthesis of enol carbonates is often multistep, low-yielding, and incompatible with certain functional groups; second, the electrophile scope is limited primarily to simple allyl and cinnamyl derivatives, restricting the structural diversity of accessible products.

Furthermore, traditional AAA methodologies typically employ activated allylic electrophiles such as allyl acetates, carbonates, or phosphates, which generate stoichiometric amounts of organic byproducts (e.g., acetic acid, CO₂, phosphate salts) during the catalytic cycle. These byproducts can complicate purification, reduce atom economy, and limit compatibility with sensitive functional groups. In contrast, allylic alcohols represent ideal electrophilic partners due to their commercial availability, low cost, stability, and the fact that water is the sole byproduct of their activation. However, the direct use of unactivated allylic alcohols in AAA reactions is challenging because they are poor leaving groups and require in situ activation—often via redox-active transition metals capable of mediating dehydrogenation or dehydration pathways. Palladium catalysts, which dominate the AAA literature, are generally ineffective with free allylic alcohols unless additional oxidants or activators are employed. Recently, however, nickel catalysis has emerged as a promising alternative for transformations involving allylic alcohols, owing to nickel’s unique redox properties, lower cost relative to palladium, and ability to facilitate unconventional mechanistic pathways.

Indeed, Mashima and colleagues recently demonstrated that nickel complexes, in conjunction with chiral bisphosphine ligands, can catalyze the enantioselective AAA of β-keto esters with allylic alcohols, achieving good yields and enantioselectivities without the need for preactivation of the electrophile. This breakthrough suggested that nickel might also be effective in promoting the AAA of other classes of prochiral nucleophiles, including α-acyl lactones and lactams. However, no such examples had been reported prior to the present invention. The extension of nickel-catalyzed AAA to these cyclic substrates was far from obvious, as the ring strain, conformational rigidity, and electronic properties of lactones and lactams differ significantly from acyclic β-dicarbonyls, potentially leading to altered reactivity, reduced enantiocontrol, or catalyst inhibition. Moreover, the choice of chiral ligand is critical: while phosphinooxazoline (PHOX) ligands and Trost-type C₂-symmetric diphosphines have proven successful in palladium-catalyzed AAA, they may be suboptimal for nickel-mediated processes due to differences in metal coordination geometry, oxidation state preferences, and ligand dissociation kinetics.

Thus, there existed a clear and long-felt need in the art for a general, practical, and enantioselective method to construct quaternary stereocenters via the direct coupling of readily available α-acyl lactones or lactams with unactivated allylic alcohols. Such a method would ideally avoid prefunctionalized nucleophiles or electrophiles, operate under mild conditions, exhibit broad substrate scope, deliver high enantioselectivity, and produce minimal waste. The present invention fulfills this need by providing the first nickel-catalyzed intermolecular enantioselective allylic alkylation of α-substituted lactones and lactams using free allylic alcohols as electrophiles, enabled by a carefully optimized chiral bisphosphine ligand system. This methodology overcomes the limitations of prior art by combining operational simplicity, exceptional functional group tolerance, and high stereoselectivity, thereby enabling the rapid assembly of structurally diverse, enantioenriched building blocks with multiple handles for further synthetic elaboration.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and highly efficient method for the enantioselective construction of chiral quaternary stereocenters through a nickel-catalyzed intermolecular allylic alkylation reaction between prochiral α-substituted lactones or lactams and unactivated allylic alcohols. This transformation represents a significant advancement over existing methodologies by eliminating the need for preactivated nucleophiles (such as enol carbonates) or electrophiles (such as allyl acetates or carbonates), thereby improving atom economy, reducing synthetic steps, and enhancing overall practicality. The reaction is catalyzed by a nickel(0) source in combination with a chiral bisphosphine ligand, preferably (R)-P-phos or structurally related analogs, and proceeds under mild conditions to afford α-quaternary lactones and lactams in high yields (up to 91%) and excellent enantiomeric excess (up to 90%). 

A key feature of the invention is its broad substrate scope. The nucleophilic component may be a monocyclic or bicyclic γ-lactone or δ-lactam bearing an α-ester or α-amide substituent, with various alkyl, aryl, or heteroaryl groups tolerated on the ester moiety. The electrophilic partner encompasses a wide range of allylic alcohols, including those substituted with aryl (para-, meta-, or ortho-substituted), heteroaryl (e.g., thiophene, furan), aliphatic, or alkenyl groups. Notably, both linear and branched allylic alcohols participate efficiently, with exclusive formation of the linear regioisomer observed in cases involving branched substrates, indicating a well-defined π-allyl nickel intermediate that governs both reactivity and selectivity. 

The invention further encompasses the synthetic utility of the resulting α-quaternary products. These compounds serve as versatile chiral building blocks that can be readily transformed into a variety of high-value scaffolds. For instance, α-quaternary lactones can undergo selective reduction to yield enantioenriched diols or react with organometallic reagents (e.g., vinyl Grignard) to furnish enones, which may be further cyclized via ring-closing metathesis to access spirocyclic architectures. Similarly, α-quaternary lactams can be reduced to chiral piperidine derivatives—privileged motifs in medicinal chemistry—or selectively oxidized to aldehydes via Wacker-type conditions, or deprotected to reveal free secondary amides. These downstream transformations demonstrate the modular nature of the products and their applicability in the synthesis of complex molecules, including potential pharmaceutical agents.

In addition to its synthetic advantages, the method of the present invention is operationally simple, scalable, and environmentally favorable. It employs commercially available or easily synthesized starting materials, avoids toxic or expensive reagents, generates water as the sole stoichiometric byproduct, and utilizes a base metal catalyst (nickel) instead of precious metals such as palladium or iridium. The chiral ligands used are either commercially available or accessible via known routes, and the reaction conditions (e.g., solvent, temperature, concentration) are amenable to standard laboratory and industrial practices. Collectively, these attributes make the disclosed methodology a robust and sustainable platform for the asymmetric synthesis of quaternary stereocenters, with immediate relevance to drug discovery, agrochemical development, and materials science.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The present invention is directed to a catalytic enantioselective method for forming carbon–carbon bonds at the α-position of lactones and lactams to generate products containing chiral quaternary stereocenters. Central to this method is the use of a nickel(0) complex coordinated with a chiral bisphosphine ligand to mediate the intermolecular coupling between an α-substituted lactone or lactam and an allylic alcohol. Unlike conventional allylic alkylation protocols that require preactivated electrophiles (e.g., allyl acetates) or nucleophiles (e.g., enol silanes or carbonates), the present method operates directly on free allylic alcohols and unmodified lactone/lactam substrates, thereby streamlining the synthetic sequence and improving overall efficiency. The reaction proceeds under mild thermal conditions in ethereal solvents, typically at temperatures ranging from –10 °C to 10 °C, and achieves high levels of enantioselectivity through precise steric and electronic control imparted by the chiral ligand environment around the nickel center.

The general transformation involves deprotonation of the α-carbon of the lactone or lactam by a mild base (either adventitious or deliberately added, though in many cases the nickel catalyst itself may facilitate enolization), followed by oxidative addition or transmetalation to form a nickel-bound enolate. Concurrently, the allylic alcohol undergoes activation—likely via dehydration or redox-assisted elimination—to generate a π-allyl nickel intermediate. The chiral ligand dictates the facial selectivity of the enolate attack on this π-allyl species, leading to the formation of a new C–C bond with high enantiocontrol. The final product is released upon reductive elimination or protonolysis, regenerating the active nickel(0) catalyst. Critically, the method is intermolecular, allowing for modular assembly of diverse molecular architectures by simply varying either coupling partner. This contrasts with intramolecular variants or decarboxylative approaches that constrain structural diversity.

The invention is applicable to both five-membered (γ-lactones, γ-lactams) and six-membered (δ-lactones, δ-lactams) ring systems, though γ-lactams are particularly emphasized due to their direct relevance to piperidine-based pharmacophores. The α-substituent on the lactone or lactam must be electron-withdrawing to enhance enolate stability; esters (e.g., ethoxycarbonyl, methoxycarbonyl) and amides are preferred, with esters offering greater reactivity in lactone systems and benzoyl-protected amides proving optimal in lactam contexts. The allylic alcohol component may bear a wide array of substituents at the terminal or internal positions of the allyl unit, including aromatic rings (with varied electronic properties), heteroaromatic systems, aliphatic chains, and unsaturated functionalities. The reaction exhibits excellent chemoselectivity, tolerating functional groups such as halides, ethers, and protected amines without interference.

This methodology fills a critical gap in the synthetic toolbox by providing the first general, catalytic, and enantioselective route to α-quaternary lactones and lactams from simple, unactivated precursors. Its development required overcoming significant challenges related to catalyst selection, ligand design, and reaction engineering, none of which were predictable from prior art. The success of this approach underscores the unique capabilities of nickel catalysis in enabling transformations that are inaccessible or inefficient with other transition metals.

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings set forth below.

“Lactone” refers to a cyclic ester formed by intramolecular esterification of a hydroxycarboxylic acid. In the context of the present invention, lactones are typically five- or six-membered rings (γ- or δ-lactones, respectively) bearing an α-substituent that renders the α-proton acidic enough to be deprotonated under the reaction conditions. Representative examples include α-ethoxycarbonyl-γ-butyrolactone and α-methoxycarbonyl-γ-butyrolactone.

“Lactam” refers to a cyclic amide formed by intramolecular amidation of an aminocarboxylic acid. As used herein, lactams are predominantly five- or six-membered (γ- or δ-lactams), with δ-lactams being especially relevant due to their conversion to piperidine derivatives. The nitrogen atom of the lactam may be unsubstituted or protected with an acyl group (e.g., benzoyl, acetyl), with benzoyl protection found to optimize reactivity and enantioselectivity in the disclosed transformations.

“α-Substituted lactone or lactam” denotes a lactone or lactam wherein the carbon atom adjacent to the carbonyl group (the α-carbon) bears a substituent other than hydrogen. In the present invention, this substituent is an electron-withdrawing group, preferably an ester (–CO₂R) or amide (–CONR₂), which stabilizes the enolate formed upon deprotonation and facilitates nucleophilic attack.

“Allylic alcohol” refers to an alcohol in which the hydroxyl group is attached to a carbon atom adjacent to a carbon–carbon double bond (i.e., R¹R²C=CR³–CH(R⁴)OH). The allylic alcohol may be linear (e.g., CH₂=CH–CH₂OH) or branched (e.g., PhCH=CH–CH(OH)CH₃), and may contain additional substituents on the alkene or alkyl portions.

“Unactivated allylic alcohol” means an allylic alcohol that does not contain strongly electron-withdrawing groups or additional activating functionalities (e.g., halides, sulfonates) that would render it inherently reactive toward nucleophiles without metal catalysis. The allylic alcohols used in the present invention are “free” in the sense that they are not preconverted to esters, carbonates, or other leaving group derivatives.

“Quaternary stereocenter” refers to a carbon atom that is bonded to four different non-hydrogen substituents and is chiral due to the absence of a plane of symmetry. In the products of the present invention, this stereocenter is located at the former α-carbon of the lactone or lactam, now bearing the original α-substituent, the lactone/lactam ring, a hydrogen (or deuterium), and the newly introduced allylic group.

“Enantiomeric excess (ee)” is a measure of the optical purity of a chiral compound, defined as |% major enantiomer – % minor enantiomer|. An ee of 90% indicates that the sample contains 95% of one enantiomer and 5% of the other.

“Nickel(0) source” refers to any compound that provides Ni(0) under the reaction conditions, either directly or via in situ reduction. Common examples include bis(1,5-cyclooctadiene)nickel(0) (Ni(COD)₂), tetrakis(triphenylphosphine)nickel(0), or nickel nanoparticles.

“Chiral bisphosphine ligand” denotes a bidentate phosphorus-based ligand possessing chiral elements (e.g., axial chirality, central chirality) that induce asymmetry in the metal coordination sphere. Examples include BINAP, SegPhos, DTBM-SegPhos, and P-phos derivatives.

“Pharmaceutically acceptable salt” refers to a salt form of a compound that is safe for human or veterinary administration and retains the desired biological activity. Such salts may be formed with inorganic acids (e.g., HCl, H₂SO₄) or organic acids (e.g., citric, tartaric).

### Exemplary Methods

The method of the present invention comprises contacting an α-substituted lactone or lactam with an allylic alcohol in the presence of a catalytic amount of a nickel(0) complex and a chiral bisphosphine ligand under inert atmosphere in a suitable solvent at a controlled temperature. The reaction is typically conducted in anhydrous diethyl ether or tetrahydrofuran, although other ethereal solvents may be used with diminished efficiency. The nickel catalyst is employed at loadings ranging from 1 to 10 mol%, with 5–10 mol% being optimal for balancing reaction rate and enantioselectivity. The chiral ligand is used in slight excess relative to nickel (e.g., 1.1–1.5 equivalents per nickel atom) to ensure full coordination and prevent racemic background reactions.

The reaction mixture is stirred at temperatures between –20 °C and 25 °C, though optimal results are obtained between –10 °C and 10 °C. Lower temperatures generally improve enantioselectivity but may slow the reaction, necessitating longer reaction times (up to 48 hours). No additional base is strictly required, as the nickel catalyst may facilitate enolization, but mild bases such as potassium carbonate or triethylamine may be added to accelerate enolate formation if needed. After completion, the reaction is quenched with aqueous ammonium chloride or dilute acid, extracted with organic solvent, dried, and purified by chromatography or crystallization to afford the enantioenriched α-quaternary lactone or lactam.

The method is scalable and has been demonstrated on gram-scale without loss of yield or ee. It is compatible with air- and moisture-sensitive functional groups when conducted under standard Schlenk techniques. The workup is straightforward, and the only stoichiometric byproduct is water, arising from the dehydration of the allylic alcohol during π-allyl formation.

### Transition Metal Catalysts

The transition metal catalyst employed in the present invention is a nickel(0) complex, which serves as the active species for both nucleophile activation and electrophile functionalization. Nickel(0) is uniquely suited for this transformation due to its ability to undergo facile oxidative addition into C–O bonds (albeit indirectly via dehydration pathways), stabilize π-allyl intermediates, and accommodate bidentate phosphine ligands in a geometry conducive to asymmetric induction. Unlike palladium, which typically requires activated electrophiles, nickel can engage free allylic alcohols through redox-neutral or mildly oxidative mechanisms that avoid external oxidants.

Preferred nickel sources include Ni(COD)₂, which is commercially available, air-sensitive, and readily forms active catalysts upon ligand coordination. Alternative sources such as Ni(PPh₃)₄ or Ni(dppe)Cl₂ (reduced in situ) may be used but generally give inferior results. The catalyst loading is optimized to minimize cost and metal residue while maintaining high turnover numbers; 5–10 mol% is typical, though as low as 1 mol% can be effective with prolonged reaction times.

The nickel center operates in a low oxidation state throughout the catalytic cycle, likely cycling between Ni(0) and Ni(II) during enolate formation and π-allyl capture. The exact mechanism remains under investigation, but kinetic and regiochemical data support a pathway involving discrete π-allyl nickel intermediates that are attacked by nickel-bound enolates in a stereodetermining step. The use of nickel instead of palladium also reduces the risk of β-hydride elimination side reactions, which can plague palladium-catalyzed allylations of enolates.

### Chiral Ligands

The enantioselectivity of the present transformation is critically dependent on the structure of the chiral bisphosphine ligand. Extensive screening revealed that ligands with axial chirality and wide bite angles provide superior performance compared to C₂-symmetric or monodentate alternatives. Among the ligands tested, (R)-P-phos (a.k.a. (R)-3,5-xylyl-BINAP or (R)-DTBM-MeO-BIPHEP analogs) consistently delivered the highest ee, attributed to its sterically demanding 3,5-disubstituted aryl phosphine groups that create a well-defined chiral pocket around the nickel center.

Other effective ligands include (R)-BINAP, (S)-SegPhos, and (R)-DM-BINAP, though these generally afford 5–15% lower ee under identical conditions. Ligands with electron-rich phosphines enhance catalyst stability and turnover, while bulky ortho-substituents prevent unselective background reactions. The ligand-to-nickel ratio is maintained at 1.2:1 to ensure complete complexation; substoichiometric ligand leads to racemic product formation due to unligated nickel species.

The ligands are either commercially available or synthesizable via established routes involving resolution or asymmetric synthesis. Their modular nature allows for fine-tuning of steric and electronic properties to accommodate specific substrate combinations, though (R)-P-phos remains the ligand of choice for broad applicability.

### Exemplary Syntheses of Compound Libraries

Using the optimized conditions, a diverse library of α-quaternary lactones and lactams was synthesized to demonstrate the generality of the method. For lactones, variation of the ester group (methyl vs. ethyl), ring size (γ vs. δ), and bicyclic fusion (e.g., decalin-derived) was achieved with consistent yields (70–85%) and ee (80–85%). Electrophile scope included phenyl-substituted allylic alcohols with para-methoxy, meta-chloro, ortho-methyl, and other substituents, all furnishing products in 75–90% ee. Heteroaromatics such as 2-thienyl and 3-furyl were equally effective. Aliphatic allylic alcohols (e.g., prenyl alcohol) gave slightly lower ee (78%) but good yields (80%).

For lactams, N-benzoyl-protected δ-lactams with α-ethoxycarbonyl groups reacted with cinnamyl alcohol derivatives to give products in 74–88% yield and 88–90% ee. N-Acetyl and N-Boc protections were less effective, leading to reduced conversion. Bicyclic lactams and those with α-phenyl substituents also participated successfully. The exclusive formation of linear regioisomers from branched allylic alcohols confirmed the predictability of the method.

These libraries illustrate the method’s utility in generating structurally diverse, enantioenriched scaffolds for high-throughput screening in drug discovery.

### Pharmaceutical Agents and Compositions

The α-quaternary lactones and lactams produced by the present method are valuable intermediates in the synthesis of pharmaceutical agents. Their embedded quaternary stereocenters and multiple functional handles (ester, alkene, lactone/lactam) enable rapid diversification into drug-like molecules. For example, reduction of the lactam to a piperidine provides access to NK₁ receptor antagonists, serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or calcium channel blockers. The lactone-derived diols and enones are precursors to prostaglandin analogs or terpenoid natural products.

Pharmaceutical compositions comprising these compounds may be formulated with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, diluents, or excipients for oral, parenteral, or topical administration. Dosage forms include tablets, capsules, solutions, and injectables. The compounds may be used alone or in combination with other therapeutic agents to treat conditions such as hypertension, depression, pain, or cancer.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Nickel-Catalyzed Enantioselective Allylic Alkylation of Lactones and Lactams with Unactivated Allylic Alcohols

In a representative procedure, Ni(COD)₂ (5.5 mg, 0.02 mmol, 10 mol%) and (R)-P-phos (14.5 mg, 0.024 mmol, 12 mol%) were added to a flame-dried vial under argon. Anhydrous diethyl ether (1.0 mL) was added, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 10 minutes to form the catalyst. A solution of α-ethoxycarbonyl-γ-butyrolactone **1a** (38.4 mg, 0.2 mmol) and allyl alcohol **2a** (17.6 µL, 0.24 mmol) in diethyl ether (0.5 mL) was then added via syringe. The reaction was cooled to –10 °C and stirred for 48 hours. TLC analysis indicated complete consumption of **1a**. The mixture was quenched with saturated NH₄Cl (2 mL), extracted with EtOAc (3 × 5 mL), dried (Na₂SO₄), and concentrated. Purification by flash chromatography (hexanes/EtOAc 9:1) afforded **3aa** (52.1 mg, 80% yield) as a colorless oil. Chiral HPLC analysis (Chiralpak IA, hexanes/iPrOH 90:10, 1.0 mL/min) showed 85% ee (tₘₐⱼₒᵣ = 12.3 min, tₘᵢₙₒᵣ = 14.1 min).

Analogous procedures were used for other substrates. For lactam **4a** (N-benzoyl-α-ethoxycarbonyl-δ-valerolactam), the reaction with (E)-1-phenylprop-2-en-1-ol at 0 °C for 24 h gave **5ab** (74% yield, 90% ee). All products were characterized by ¹H/¹³C NMR, HRMS, and chiral HPLC.

### Example 2

Additional examples include the synthesis of **3ac** from **1a** and 4-methoxycinnamyl alcohol (88% yield, 90% ee), **3ak** from 2-thienyl-substituted allylic alcohol (82% yield, 85% ee), and **5ba** from N-benzoyl-α-methoxycarbonyl lactam (80% yield, 88% ee). Control experiments without ligand gave <5% conversion, confirming the necessity of chiral induction.
