# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of ophthalmology and ocular surface therapeutics, particularly to methods and compositions for the prevention and treatment of ocular surface disorders characterized by epithelial barrier disruption, such as dry eye disease (keratoconjunctivitis sicca), solution-induced corneal staining (SICS), and other conditions involving oxidative stress-mediated damage to the corneal and conjunctival epithelia. More specifically, the invention provides novel therapeutic applications of dynamin GTPase inhibitors, including but not limited to Dynasore and its analogues such as Dyngo-4a, for protecting the ocular surface epithelium from sublethal cellular damage induced by oxidative stress and other environmental insults. The invention further encompasses diagnostic and prognostic methods based on the assessment of vital dye uptake mechanisms in stratified and differentiated ocular surface epithelial models, which more accurately reflect the physiological state of the human ocular surface than conventional monolayer cell cultures. Additionally, the invention includes pharmaceutical formulations comprising dynamin inhibitors for topical ophthalmic administration, designed to preserve the integrity of both the plasma membrane barrier and the apical glycocalyx layer of the ocular surface epithelium, thereby preventing the pathological uptake of vital dyes such as fluorescein and rose bengal that serve as clinical indicators of epithelial compromise. The therapeutic approach disclosed herein represents a paradigm shift from symptomatic management to targeted cytoprotection of the ocular surface, addressing the fundamental mechanisms of epithelial barrier failure rather than merely alleviating secondary inflammatory responses or supplementing tear film deficiency.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The ocular surface, comprising the corneal and conjunctival epithelia overlaid by the tear film, serves as a critical barrier against environmental insults, pathogens, and desiccating conditions. This stratified squamous mucosal epithelium undergoes continuous renewal through a tightly regulated process of basal cell proliferation, upward migration, terminal differentiation, and eventual desquamation of apical cells, with complete turnover occurring within 5–7 days in humans. During differentiation, superficial epithelial cells develop specialized structural and biochemical features essential for barrier function, including the formation of microplicae—membrane folds on the apical surface—and the expression of membrane-associated mucins such as MUC16. These mucins interact with galectin-3 (LGALS3) to form a dense, organized glycocalyx that, together with tight junctions between adjacent cells and the plasma membranes themselves, establishes both transcellular and paracellular barriers that prevent penetration of foreign substances into deeper epithelial layers. Disruption of this sophisticated barrier system is a hallmark of numerous ocular surface diseases, most notably dry eye disease, which affects over 20% of the population in North America, Europe, and Asia, and manifests clinically through increased epithelial permeability, accelerated desquamation, and compromised tear film stability.

For over a century, clinicians have relied on vital dyes—particularly fluorescein and rose bengal—to assess the integrity of the ocular surface epithelium. Fluorescein, first introduced in 1882, and rose bengal, popularized in the 1930s, produce characteristic staining patterns that correlate with epithelial damage: rose bengal typically reveals punctate staining indicative of glycocalyx disruption, while fluorescein hyperstaining suggests plasma membrane compromise. Despite their widespread clinical use, the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying vital dye uptake have remained poorly understood. Early studies demonstrated that undifferentiated corneal epithelial cells in monolayer culture readily take up both dyes, whereas differentiated cells with an intact glycocalyx exclude rose bengal but remain permeable to fluorescein, suggesting distinct barrier mechanisms for each dye. More recent investigations revealed that fluorescein hyperstaining occurs predominantly in living cells exhibiting early apoptotic markers, indicating that dye concentration is an active, energy-dependent process rather than passive diffusion through necrotic membranes. Furthermore, exposure to multipurpose contact lens solutions (MPS) has been shown to induce solution-induced corneal staining (SICS), a phenomenon mechanistically distinct from classical dry eye but similarly characterized by abnormal vital dye uptake.

The prevailing hypothesis regarding fluorescein uptake posited that sublethal cellular stress triggers endocytic membrane remodeling processes, leading to dye internalization via clathrin- or caveolin-mediated endocytosis. This model was supported by observations in monolayer cell cultures where oxidative stress increased both endocytic activity and fluorescein accumulation, with uptake being inhibitable by various endocytosis blockers. However, these findings were derived from relatively undifferentiated cell models that fail to recapitulate the complex barrier properties of the native ocular surface epithelium. Stratified and differentiated epithelial cultures, which better mimic the in vivo condition through the development of functional tight junctions, apical microplicae, and a mature glycocalyx, exhibit fundamentally different responses to stress and dye exposure. Crucially, prior art lacked comprehensive comparative studies between monolayer and stratified models, leading to potential misinterpretations of dye uptake mechanisms relevant to actual clinical conditions.

Dynamin GTPases, originally identified as essential mediators of endocytic vesicle scission, have emerged as multifunctional proteins involved in diverse cellular processes including mitochondrial fission, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and plasma membrane repair. Small-molecule inhibitors such as Dynasore and its more potent derivative Dyngo-4a were developed specifically to probe dynamin-dependent endocytosis, with Dynasore demonstrating rapid cellular penetration and reversible inhibition of dynamin family members DNM1, DNM2, and DRP1. While these compounds have been extensively used as research tools to dissect endocytic pathways, their potential therapeutic applications beyond basic science have remained largely unexplored, particularly in the context of ocular surface protection. Recent studies in non-ocular systems have suggested that dynamin inhibition may confer cytoprotection against oxidative stress by modulating mitochondrial dynamics and inflammatory responses, but whether similar protective effects could be harnessed for ocular surface disorders was unknown.

The current understanding of ocular surface disease pathogenesis suffers from several critical gaps. First, existing therapeutic approaches primarily focus on symptom relief through artificial tear supplementation, anti-inflammatory agents, or immunomodulators, without directly addressing the underlying epithelial barrier dysfunction. Second, diagnostic interpretation of vital dye staining patterns lacks mechanistic foundation, leading to potential misclassification of disease severity and inappropriate treatment selection. Third, preclinical models used for drug development often rely on monolayer cell cultures that inadequately represent the differentiated state of the ocular surface epithelium, resulting in poor translation of in vitro findings to clinical outcomes. Finally, the role of dynamin GTPases in ocular surface homeostasis and stress response has never been systematically investigated, leaving a significant gap in our understanding of potential therapeutic targets for epithelial protection.

These limitations highlight the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that directly target the mechanisms of epithelial barrier preservation, coupled with improved diagnostic frameworks based on physiologically relevant models of the ocular surface. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by revealing the unexpected cytoprotective properties of dynamin inhibitors against oxidative stress-induced damage in stratified ocular surface epithelia, thereby providing both a new class of therapeutic agents and a refined understanding of vital dye uptake mechanisms that can inform more accurate clinical assessment of ocular surface health.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel methods and compositions for protecting the ocular surface epithelium from sublethal damage induced by oxidative stress and other environmental insults, based on the unexpected discovery that dynamin GTPase inhibitors, particularly Dynasore and its analogue Dyngo-4a, exert potent cytoprotective effects that preserve both plasma membrane integrity and glycocalyx barrier function. Contrary to the prevailing hypothesis that vital dye uptake occurs through endocytic vesicles, the invention demonstrates that fluorescein and rose bengal accumulation in stressed ocular surface epithelial cells results from subtle disruptions in barrier integrity that permit passive dye penetration, while the requirement for cellular viability reflects the need for active dye concentration mechanisms rather than endocytic internalization. The invention further establishes that stratified and differentiated epithelial cultures, which accurately model the native ocular surface, respond fundamentally differently to stress and therapeutic intervention compared to conventional monolayer cultures, thereby providing a more clinically relevant platform for both mechanistic studies and therapeutic development.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for preventing or treating ocular surface disorders characterized by epithelial barrier disruption, comprising topically administering to the ocular surface a therapeutically effective amount of a dynamin GTPase inhibitor selected from the group consisting of Dynasore, Dyngo-4a, and pharmaceutically acceptable salts or derivatives thereof. The ocular surface disorders amenable to treatment according to this method include but are not limited to dry eye disease (keratoconjunctivitis sicca), solution-induced corneal staining (SICS), photokeratitis, allergic conjunctivitis, and other conditions involving oxidative stress-mediated epithelial damage. The therapeutic effect is achieved through preservation of plasma membrane integrity, prevention of glycocalyx shedding, and maintenance of epithelial barrier function, rather than through inhibition of endocytic dye uptake as previously hypothesized.

In another aspect, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition for topical ophthalmic administration, comprising a dynamin GTPase inhibitor in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier suitable for ocular delivery. The composition may be formulated as eye drops, ointments, gels, or sustained-release devices, with concentrations optimized to achieve cytoprotective effects without compromising normal epithelial turnover or causing local toxicity. The invention further encompasses methods for preparing such compositions and their use in clinical practice for the prevention and treatment of ocular surface disorders.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a method for assessing the efficacy of potential therapeutic agents for ocular surface protection, comprising exposing stratified and differentiated human corneal epithelial cell cultures to oxidative stress in the presence or absence of the test agent, and measuring preservation of barrier function through vital dye exclusion assays, glycocalyx integrity assessments, and plasma membrane damage markers. This method represents a significant improvement over conventional monolayer-based screening approaches by incorporating the complex barrier properties of the native ocular surface epithelium.

The invention also provides diagnostic methods for distinguishing between different mechanisms of ocular surface damage based on differential responses to dynamin inhibitors. Specifically, conditions characterized by primary glycocalyx disruption (such as certain forms of dry eye) versus those involving direct plasma membrane compromise (such as SICS) can be differentiated by their sensitivity to dynamin inhibitor treatment and their associated patterns of vital dye uptake. This diagnostic capability enables more precise disease classification and personalized therapeutic selection.

Furthermore, the invention reveals that the cytoprotective effects of dynamin inhibitors are independent of their canonical role in endocytosis inhibition, instead involving modulation of plasma membrane cholesterol distribution, lipid raft organization, actin cytoskeleton dynamics, and mitochondrial fragmentation pathways. This mechanistic insight opens new avenues for developing next-generation therapeutics that specifically target these protective pathways without interfering with essential endocytic functions required for normal cellular homeostasis.

The therapeutic approach disclosed herein represents a fundamental shift from reactive symptom management to proactive epithelial protection, addressing the root cause of ocular surface barrier failure rather than merely treating downstream consequences. By preserving the integrity of both the plasma membrane and glycocalyx barriers during periods of environmental stress, dynamin inhibitors enable the natural epithelial renewal process to maintain ocular surface health without interruption, thereby preventing the cycle of damage, inflammation, and further barrier compromise that characterizes chronic ocular surface disorders.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is based on the unexpected discovery that dynamin GTPase inhibitors, particularly Dynasore and its more potent analogue Dyngo-4a, provide remarkable protection against oxidative stress-induced damage to the ocular surface epithelium through mechanisms independent of their canonical role in endocytosis inhibition. This finding fundamentally challenges the prevailing hypothesis that vital dye uptake in stressed ocular surface epithelial cells occurs through endocytic vesicles and instead reveals that dye accumulation results from subtle disruptions in epithelial barrier integrity that permit passive dye penetration, while cellular viability is required for active dye concentration mechanisms that maintain intracellular dye levels.

The invention encompasses several interrelated aspects that collectively address the unmet need for effective therapies targeting the fundamental mechanisms of ocular surface barrier dysfunction. Central to the invention is the recognition that stratified and differentiated epithelial cultures, which accurately model the native ocular surface through the development of functional tight junctions, apical microplicae, and a mature glycocalyx composed of membrane-associated mucins such as MUC16 complexed with galectin-3 (LGALS3), respond fundamentally differently to stress and therapeutic intervention compared to conventional monolayer cultures. This distinction is critical because monolayer cultures, while useful for certain types of basic research, fail to recapitulate the complex barrier properties that define the physiological state of the human ocular surface epithelium.

In stratified and differentiated human corneal epithelial cell cultures, oxidative stress induces vital dye uptake without stimulating endocytic activity, as evidenced by the lack of increased transferrin internalization following stress exposure. Moreover, while endocytosis inhibitors such as chlorpromazine (targeting clathrin-mediated endocytosis) and genistein (targeting caveolin-mediated endocytosis) effectively block stress-stimulated dye uptake in monolayer cultures, they have no effect in stratified cultures. Remarkably, Dynasore and Dyngo-4a remain potent inhibitors of dye uptake in both culture systems, despite the absence of stress-stimulated endocytosis in stratified cultures. This paradoxical finding led to the discovery that these compounds exert their protective effects through alternative mechanisms that preserve epithelial barrier integrity rather than blocking endocytic dye internalization.

The cytoprotective mechanism of dynamin inhibitors involves multiple interconnected pathways that collectively maintain plasma membrane integrity and glycocalyx stability during oxidative stress. First, these compounds prevent plasma membrane damage as demonstrated by their ability to completely abolish trypan blue uptake in stressed monolayer cultures, indicating preservation of membrane barrier function against even large molecular weight dyes. Second, they inhibit the stress-induced shedding of LGALS3 from the apical glycocalyx, as evidenced by reduced levels of soluble LGALS3 in culture supernatants following oxidative stress exposure. This protective effect on glycocalyx integrity occurs without altering the overall expression or surface localization of mucin-type glycoconjugates as detected by jacalin lectin binding, suggesting that dynamin inhibitors specifically prevent the proteolytic or mechanical shedding processes that release LGALS3 ectodomains during stress.

The invention further reveals that the timing of dynamin inhibitor administration is critical for therapeutic efficacy. When applied simultaneously with oxidative stress, Dynasore and Dyngo-4a effectively prevent vital dye uptake in both stratified cultures and ex vivo mouse corneas. However, when administered after stress exposure and barrier disruption have already occurred, these compounds fail to reduce dye uptake, demonstrating that their mechanism involves prevention of damage rather than repair of existing damage or inhibition of dye internalization pathways. This temporal specificity provides important insights into the clinical application of these compounds, suggesting that they are most effective as prophylactic agents or for early intervention before significant barrier compromise has occurred.

The protective effects of dynamin inhibitors appear to involve both dynamin-dependent and dynamin-independent mechanisms. While these compounds were originally developed as specific inhibitors of dynamin family GTPases (including DNM1, DNM2, and DRP1), their cytoprotective actions likely extend beyond canonical dynamin functions. Evidence from non-ocular systems suggests that dynamin inhibitors can modulate plasma membrane cholesterol distribution, disrupt lipid raft organization, and influence actin cytoskeleton dynamics—all processes that could contribute to enhanced membrane stability during oxidative stress. Additionally, inhibition of mitochondrial fission through DRP1 blockade may reduce oxidative stress-induced apoptosis and preserve cellular energy metabolism necessary for maintaining barrier function.

The invention provides specific guidance for the formulation and administration of dynamin inhibitors for ocular surface protection. Pharmaceutical compositions should contain effective concentrations of the active compound (typically 15–80 μM for Dynasore and 15 μM for Dyngo-4a based on in vitro studies) in carriers suitable for topical ophthalmic delivery. Suitable formulations include aqueous solutions, suspensions, gels, or ointments containing appropriate buffers, tonicity adjusters, preservatives, and viscosity modifiers to ensure ocular compatibility and residence time. The frequency of administration should be sufficient to maintain protective concentrations during periods of anticipated stress exposure, with chronic dosing regimens potentially enabling gradual resolution of existing epithelial damage through protection of newly differentiating cells as they migrate to the apical surface.

The therapeutic applications of the invention extend beyond dry eye disease to include any ocular surface condition characterized by oxidative stress-mediated epithelial barrier disruption. This includes solution-induced corneorral staining (SICS) caused by multipurpose contact lens solutions, photokeratitis resulting from ultraviolet radiation exposure, allergic conjunctivitis involving inflammatory mediator release, and post-surgical epithelial healing complications. The invention also provides methods for screening potential therapeutic agents using stratified and differentiated epithelial cultures as a more physiologically relevant model system, thereby improving the predictive value of preclinical testing for clinical outcomes.

Diagnostic applications of the invention include methods for distinguishing between different mechanisms of ocular surface damage based on differential responses to dynamin inhibitors and associated patterns of vital dye uptake. Conditions primarily affecting glycocalyx integrity (such as certain forms of dry eye) may show different sensitivity profiles compared to those involving direct plasma membrane compromise (such as SICS), enabling more precise disease classification and personalized therapeutic selection. Additionally, the invention provides insights into the interpretation of clinical vital dye staining patterns, suggesting that hyperfluorescence represents active dye concentration in viable but stressed cells rather than passive diffusion through necrotic membranes.

The invention represents a paradigm shift in ocular surface therapeutics by moving from reactive symptom management to proactive epithelial protection. Rather than merely supplementing deficient tear components or suppressing downstream inflammatory responses, dynamin inhibitors address the fundamental mechanisms of barrier failure by preserving the structural and functional integrity of both plasma membrane and glycocalyx barriers during periods of environmental stress. This approach leverages the natural epithelial renewal process to maintain ocular surface health without interruption, thereby preventing the vicious cycle of damage, inflammation, and further barrier compromise that characterizes chronic ocular surface disorders.

### EXAMPLE 1

The following example demonstrates the experimental validation of the invention's core principles using both in vitro cell culture models and ex vivo organ culture systems. Human corneal limbal epithelial (HCLE) cells, a telomerase-immortalized cell line that faithfully recapitulates the differentiation capacity of native corneal epithelium, were cultured under two distinct conditions to generate either undifferentiated monolayers or stratified and differentiated epithelial constructs. Monolayer cultures were maintained in standard growth medium until 90% confluence, while stratified cultures were induced by transferring confluent monolayers to differentiation medium containing high calcium ion concentration and bovine serum for seven days, resulting in the formation of multilayered epithelial mounds with apical cell flattening and functional glycocalyx barrier development as confirmed by rose bengal exclusion assays.

Oxidative stress was induced by adding tert-butyl hydroperoxide (tBHP) to culture media at concentrations of 3 mM or 10 mM for monolayer cultures and 10 mM for stratified cultures, based on empirical dose-response assessments that produced substantial vital dye staining without causing significant cell death. Control cultures received vehicle alone under identical conditions. To investigate the role of endocytosis in stress-stimulated vital dye uptake, cultures were co-treated with various endocytosis inhibitors: chlorpromazine hydrochloride (8 μg/mL) to block clathrin-mediated endocytosis, genistein (200 μM) to inhibit caveolin-mediated endocytosis, Dynasore hydrate (40–80 μM) to broadly inhibit dynamin-dependent endocytic processes, or Dyngo-4a (15 μM) as a more potent dynamin inhibitor. All inhibitors were dissolved in DMSO, with matching vehicle controls included in all experiments.

Vital dye uptake was assessed using sodium fluorescein (for cultured cells) or clinical-grade fluorescein solution (for ex vivo tissues), with rose bengal (0.05%) used as a comparative dye to evaluate glycocalyx barrier function. Dye exposure lasted 10 minutes followed by three PBS washes, with immediate imaging to prevent dye diffusion artifacts. Fluorescein uptake was quantified using plate reader measurements (excitation/emission 488/510 nm) and expressed as relative fluorescence units (RFU), while rose bengal uptake was quantified by dye extraction into DMSO followed by absorbance measurement at 562 nm.

Endocytic activity was monitored using Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated transferrin (TF) as a well-established marker of receptor-mediated endocytosis. Cultures were incubated with labeled TF for 20 minutes under stress conditions, followed by cold washes to stop endocytic processes and fluorescence imaging (excitation/emission 532/588 nm). ImageJ analysis was used to quantify TF internalization across experimental conditions.

Cell viability and damage were assessed using multiple complementary assays. Early apoptosis was detected using Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated Annexin V binding to exposed phosphatidylserine, while late apoptosis was evaluated by TUNEL assay detecting DNA fragmentation. Metabolic activity as a measure of cytotoxicity was assessed using both MTT and WST-1 assays, which detect intracellular and extracellular reductase activity respectively. Plasma membrane integrity was directly measured using trypan blue exclusion assays, with quantitative assessment by plate reader absorbance at 590 nm.

Glycocalyx integrity was evaluated through two complementary approaches. Jacalin lectin binding assays detected surface-exposed O-glycans characteristic of mucin-type glycoconjugates, using fluorescein-conjugated jacalin and fluorescence imaging (excitation/emission 488/510 nm). Soluble LGALS3 shedding was quantified by Western blotting of culture supernatants using anti-LGALS3 primary antibody (1:200 dilution) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution), with chemiluminescent detection and photographic film visualization.

Ex vivo validation was performed using wild-type C57Bl/6J mouse eyes obtained immediately following CO2 euthanasia and maintained in Keratinocyte-SFM organ culture medium. Eyes were subjected to oxidative stress (10 mM tBHP) for 2.5 hours with or without concurrent Dynasore treatment (40 μM), followed by vital dye staining as described above. Additional experiments tested the temporal requirements for Dynasore protection by adding the inhibitor either simultaneously with stress or at 15-minute and 30-minute intervals after stress initiation.

Results demonstrated that oxidative stress significantly increased vital dye uptake in both monolayer and stratified HCLE cultures, though the morphological appearance differed between culture systems. Monolayer cultures exhibited scattered hyperfluorescent cells with nuclear dye concentration, while stratified cultures showed more uniform staining without distinct cytoplasmic accumulation patterns. Stress also increased Annexin V binding indicative of early apoptosis, but had minimal effect on TUNEL-positive cells, confirming that observed damage was primarily sublethal.

In monolayer cultures, oxidative stress stimulated both TF internalization and vital dye uptake, with both processes being inhibited by chlorpromazine, genistein, and Dynasore. However, in stratified cultures, oxidative stress failed to stimulate TF uptake and actually appeared to suppress endocytic activity, while vital dye uptake remained sensitive only to Dynasore and Dyngo-4a, not to chlorpromazine or genistein. This dissociation between endocytosis and dye uptake in stratified cultures challenged the endocytic internalization hypothesis.

Ex vivo mouse cornea experiments confirmed that simultaneous Dynasore treatment prevented stress-stimulated fluorescein uptake, but post-stress administration failed to reduce staining even at doubled concentrations (80 μM). This temporal specificity indicated that Dynasore acts preventively rather than therapeutically once damage has occurred.

Cytoprotection assays revealed that Dynasore treatment preserved metabolic activity (particularly in WST-1 assays reflecting plasma membrane electron transport) and completely prevented trypan blue uptake in stressed monolayer cultures. In stratified cultures, Dynasore treatment prevented stress-induced LGALS3 shedding into culture supernatants without affecting jacalin lectin binding, indicating specific protection against glycocalyx disruption rather than general preservation of mucin expression.

These findings collectively demonstrate that dynamin inhibitors protect the ocular surface epithelium through mechanisms independent of endocytosis inhibition, instead preserving both plasma membrane integrity and glycocalyx stability during oxidative stress. The differential responses between monolayer and stratified cultures underscore the importance of using physiologically relevant models for studying ocular surface barrier function and therapeutic intervention. The temporal requirements for effective protection suggest optimal clinical application as prophylactic or early-intervention agents rather than rescue therapies for established damage.
