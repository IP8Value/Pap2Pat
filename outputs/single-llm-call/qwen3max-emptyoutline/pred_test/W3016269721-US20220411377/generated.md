# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), represent a significant and growing burden on global public health. These conditions are characterized by the progressive degeneration and eventual loss of structure and function of neurons in the central and peripheral nervous systems. The clinical manifestations of such disorders encompass a broad spectrum of motor, cognitive, and psychological impairments that severely diminish quality of life and often lead to premature mortality. In addition to chronic neurodegenerative pathologies, acute neurological insults such as ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury, and cerebral palsy also result in substantial neuronal damage through overlapping molecular mechanisms. A critical mediator implicated in the pathogenesis of both chronic and acute neurological disorders is the gaseous signaling molecule nitric oxide (NO). While NO serves essential physiological roles in neurotransmission, vasodilation, and immune regulation under normal conditions, its overproduction in neuronal tissues has been robustly linked to excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately, neuronal cell death.

The biosynthesis of nitric oxide is catalyzed by a family of enzymes known as nitric oxide synthases (NOS). These homodimeric flavoproteins exist in three primary isoforms: neuronal NOS (nNOS), endothelial NOS (eNOS), and inducible NOS (iNOS). Each isoform is encoded by a distinct gene and exhibits unique patterns of expression, subcellular localization, and regulatory mechanisms that correspond to its specialized physiological functions. nNOS is constitutively expressed in specific neuronal populations throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems and is activated in response to calcium influx following neuronal depolarization. eNOS is primarily localized in vascular endothelial cells and plays a crucial role in maintaining vascular homeostasis through the regulation of blood pressure and vascular tone. iNOS, in contrast, is not constitutively expressed but is induced in various cell types, particularly macrophages and other immune cells, in response to inflammatory stimuli, where it produces large quantities of NO as part of the host defense mechanism.

Given the distinct physiological roles of each NOS isoform, the development of isoform-selective inhibitors represents a critical therapeutic strategy for targeting pathological NO overproduction without disrupting essential physiological functions. Specifically, selective inhibition of nNOS holds promise for treating neurological disorders associated with excitotoxic neuronal damage, while preserving eNOS activity is essential to avoid adverse cardiovascular effects such as hypertension. Similarly, maintaining iNOS function may be important for preserving immune competence. The structural basis for achieving isoform selectivity lies in the subtle yet significant differences in the active site architecture among the three NOS isoforms. Although the overall fold and catalytic mechanism are conserved across isoforms, key amino acid substitutions in the substrate-binding pocket and adjacent regions create opportunities for designing inhibitors that preferentially bind to one isoform over others.

Early efforts in NOS inhibitor development focused on compounds that mimic the natural substrate L-arginine, which competes with the substrate for binding in the oxygenase domain of the enzyme. However, these early inhibitors typically suffered from poor pharmacokinetic properties, including low membrane permeability, high polarity, and excessive hydrogen bond donor capacity, which limited their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and reach therapeutic concentrations in the central nervous system. Moreover, many of these compounds exhibited insufficient selectivity among the NOS isoforms, leading to potential off-target effects. Subsequent research has therefore concentrated on developing non-amino acid-based inhibitors that maintain high potency against nNOS while achieving exceptional selectivity over eNOS and iNOS, along with improved drug-like properties suitable for central nervous system penetration.

Recent advances in structural biology, particularly through X-ray crystallography of NOS-inhibitor complexes, have provided detailed insights into the molecular determinants of isoform selectivity. These studies have revealed that specific amino acid residues in the active site, such as Asp597 in rat nNOS (Asp602 in human nNOS) versus Asn366 in human eNOS, and Met336 in rat nNOS (Met341 in human nNOS) versus Val104 in human eNOS, play crucial roles in discriminating between different inhibitor scaffolds. The strategic exploitation of these structural differences has enabled the rational design of novel inhibitor classes with unprecedented levels of isoform selectivity. Among these emerging scaffolds, 2-aminoquinoline derivatives have demonstrated particular promise due to their ability to form key hydrogen bonding interactions with conserved active site residues while simultaneously engaging isoform-specific residues through carefully designed substituent patterns.

The present invention builds upon this foundation of structural knowledge and medicinal chemistry optimization to provide a novel class of 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinoline derivatives that exhibit exceptional potency and selectivity for nNOS inhibition. These compounds are specifically engineered to target the unique structural features of the nNOS active site, particularly through interactions with the nNOS-specific aspartate residue (Asp597/Asp602) and the methionine-containing hydrophobic pocket (Met336/Met341). Furthermore, the compounds of the present invention have been optimized for favorable drug-like properties, including enhanced blood-brain barrier permeability and metabolic stability, making them particularly well-suited for the treatment of central nervous system disorders associated with pathological NO production.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel compounds that are potent and selective inhibitors of neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS), along with pharmaceutical compositions comprising these compounds and methods for their use in treating neurological disorders associated with pathological nitric oxide production. The compounds of the invention are characterized by a 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinoline core structure with specific substitution patterns that confer exceptional selectivity for nNOS over endothelial NOS (eNOS) and inducible NOS (iNOS). This selectivity is achieved through strategic interactions with isoform-specific residues in the nNOS active site, particularly the aspartate residue at position 597 in rat nNOS (position 602 in human nNOS) and the methionine-containing hydrophobic pocket formed by residues Met336 and Leu337 in rat nNOS (Met341 and His342 in human nNOS).

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides novel compounds that function as potent and selective inhibitors of neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS), addressing a critical unmet need in the treatment of neurological disorders associated with pathological nitric oxide production. The compounds are specifically designed to exploit the structural differences between nNOS and the other NOS isoforms, particularly endothelial NOS (eNOS) and inducible NOS (iNOS), to achieve unprecedented levels of selectivity while maintaining excellent drug-like properties suitable for central nervous system applications. The detailed chemical structures, synthetic methodologies, biological activities, and structure-activity relationships of these compounds are described herein.

The core scaffold of the inventive compounds is based on a 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinoline framework, which has been extensively optimized through systematic structure-activity relationship studies to identify the critical structural features responsible for high potency and selectivity. The quinoline moiety serves as a bioisostere for the guanidinium group of L-arginine, the natural substrate of NOS enzymes, and forms essential hydrogen bonding interactions with conserved active site residues including the main chain carbonyl of Trp587/Trp592 and the side chain carboxylate of Glu592/Glu602 in rat and human nNOS, respectively. This binding mode mimics the interactions of the natural substrate while providing a rigid platform for the strategic placement of substituents that engage isoform-specific residues.

The phenyl ring attached at the 7-position of the quinoline core is substituted with an aminomethyl group at the meta-position relative to the quinoline attachment point. This specific substitution pattern has been identified as crucial for achieving high selectivity for nNOS over eNOS, as the meta-positioned aminomethyl group is optimally positioned to interact with the nNOS-specific aspartate residue (Asp597 in rat nNOS, Asp602 in human nNOS) that is replaced by asparagine in eNOS (Asn366). The primary amino group of this substituent forms direct hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions with the carboxylate side chain of the aspartate residue, as well as with surrounding water molecules and polar residues in the active site, including Gln478/Gln483 and Arg481/Arg486. This interaction network is not possible in eNOS due to the presence of asparagine instead of aspartate, resulting in significantly reduced binding affinity for eNOS and thus high selectivity.

Additional optimization of the compounds has been achieved through substitution at the 4-position of the phenyl ring with various functional groups designed to enhance binding affinity through interactions with a hydrophobic pocket formed by Met336 and Leu337 in rat nNOS (Met341 and His342 in human nNOS). This pocket is structurally distinct from the corresponding region in eNOS, where Met336/Val104 and Leu337/Phe105 substitutions create a different steric and electronic environment. The 4-position substituents have been carefully selected to maximize favorable van der Waals interactions with the methionine-containing pocket in nNOS while minimizing interactions with the corresponding region in eNOS.

The synthetic methodology for preparing the compounds of the invention employs a modular approach based on Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reactions, which allows for efficient and versatile construction of the 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinoline scaffold. The synthesis begins with the preparation of a 7-boronate-functionalized quinoline intermediate, which is then coupled with appropriately substituted aryl halides containing the desired aminomethyl and 4-position substituents. The boronate functionality is typically introduced as a pinacol boronate ester or trifluoroborate salt, which provides excellent stability and reactivity in the coupling reaction. The aryl halide coupling partners are prepared through various synthetic routes depending on the nature of the 4-position substituent, including Delépine reactions for primary amines, nucleophilic substitution for ether formation, and protection-deprotection strategies for sensitive functional groups.

A critical aspect of the synthetic methodology is the use of mild reaction conditions that preserve the integrity of the quinoline acetyl protecting group and prevent decomposition of sensitive functional groups. The optimized coupling conditions employ sodium bicarbonate as a mild base in a mixed aqueous-organic solvent system (typically dimethoxyethane/water) under microwave irradiation at 120°C for 20-25 minutes. This protocol provides excellent yields of the coupled products while minimizing side reactions such as deacetylation or protodeborylation. Following the coupling reaction, the products are subjected to a two-step deprotection sequence involving first the removal of the acetyl group from the quinoline nitrogen using potassium carbonate in refluxing methanol, followed by cleavage of the Boc protecting group from the aminomethyl substituent using methanolic hydrochloric acid. This sequential deprotection strategy ensures complete removal of protecting groups while maintaining the stability of the final product.

The biological evaluation of the compounds has been conducted using comprehensive enzymatic assays with purified recombinant NOS isoforms, including rat nNOS, human nNOS, murine iNOS, human iNOS, and human eNOS. The inhibitory potency is determined using the hemoglobin capture assay, which measures the production of nitric oxide through its reaction with oxyhemoglobin to form methemoglobin. The assay provides reliable and reproducible measurements of inhibitory constants (Ki values) that correlate well with cellular and in vivo activity. Selectivity is assessed by calculating the ratio of Ki values between nNOS and the other isoforms, with higher ratios indicating greater selectivity.

X-ray crystallography studies have been instrumental in understanding the molecular basis for the observed potency and selectivity of the compounds. High-resolution crystal structures of representative compounds bound to rat nNOS, human nNOS, and human eNOS have revealed the precise binding modes and key interactions responsible for isoform discrimination. These structures show that the quinoline core maintains a consistent binding orientation across all isoforms, forming the expected hydrogen bonding interactions with conserved active site residues. However, the orientation and interactions of the aminomethyl and 4-position substituents vary significantly between isoforms, reflecting the structural differences in the active site pockets.

The compounds of the invention have also been evaluated for their drug-like properties, including blood-brain barrier permeability and metabolic stability. The parallel artificial membrane permeability assay for the blood-brain barrier (PAMPA-BBB) has been used to assess the potential for central nervous system penetration, with compounds showing effective permeability values greater than 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s classified as having good BBB penetration. Additionally, human liver microsome stability assays have been conducted to evaluate metabolic stability, with half-lives greater than 60 minutes considered favorable for oral bioavailability. The most promising compounds demonstrate excellent performance in both assays, confirming their suitability for development as CNS-active therapeutics.

Off-target profiling has been performed using the National Institute of Mental Health's Psychoactive Drug Screening Program (PDSP), which evaluates binding to a panel of 45 pharmacologically relevant CNS targets and receptors. The results indicate that the compounds of the invention have clean off-target profiles, with minimal binding to unrelated receptors at concentrations significantly higher than their nNOS inhibitory concentrations. This clean profile reduces the risk of adverse side effects and supports the development of these compounds as safe and effective therapeutics.

### ILLUSTRATIVE EMBODIMENTS

**Example 1: Preparation of 7-(3-(2-Aminoethyl)phenyl)-4-methylquinolin-2-amine Dihydrochloride (Compound 12)**

Compound 12 represents a key intermediate in the development of the inventive compounds and demonstrates the importance of the meta-substituted aminomethyl group for achieving high nNOS selectivity. The synthesis begins with the preparation of 4-bromo-N-Boc-phenethylamine (56) from commercially available 3-bromophenethylamine through standard Boc protection procedures. The Suzuki coupling reaction is then performed between 7-(4,4,5,5-tetramethyl-1,3,2-dioxaborolan-2-yl)-4-methylquinolin-2-yl acetate (39) and 4-bromo-N-Boc-phenethylamine (56) using the optimized conditions described previously. The reaction mixture is heated to 120°C under microwave irradiation for 25 minutes, yielding the coupled product 64 as a yellow foam in 90% yield after purification by flash column chromatography.

The protected intermediate 64 is then subjected to the two-step deprotection sequence. First, the acetyl group is removed by heating with potassium carbonate in refluxing methanol for 2.5 hours, followed by standard workup procedures. The resulting free aminoquinoline is then treated with methanolic hydrochloric acid overnight at room temperature to remove the Boc protecting group. After precipitation and washing with diethyl ether, compound 12 is obtained as a cream-colored flocculent solid in 72% yield from the protected intermediate. The compound is characterized by melting point (305.5-306.5°C), ¹H-NMR, and mass spectrometry, confirming its identity and purity.

Biological evaluation of compound 12 reveals excellent inhibitory potency against both rat and human nNOS, with Ki values of 55 nM and 60 nM, respectively. More importantly, the compound demonstrates outstanding selectivity over eNOS, with selectivity ratios of 440-fold (rat nNOS/murine iNOS) and 877-fold (human nNOS/human eNOS). X-ray crystallography studies confirm that the meta-positioned aminomethyl group forms direct hydrogen bonding interactions with Asp597 in rat nNOS and Asp602 in human nNOS, while in human eNOS, the corresponding asparagine residue (Asn366) cannot form equivalent interactions, explaining the observed selectivity.

**Example 2: Preparation of 7-(3-(Aminomethyl)-4-(cyclopropylmethoxy)phenyl)-4-methylquinolin-2-amine Dihydrochloride (Compound 29)**

Compound 29 represents one of the most potent inhibitors in the series and demonstrates the beneficial effects of incorporating a cyclopropylmethoxy substituent at the 4-position of the phenyl ring. The synthesis begins with the preparation of 4-bromo-2-(cyclopropylmethoxy)benzaldehyde, which is then converted to the corresponding amine through a Delépine reaction involving treatment with hexamethylenetetramine followed by acidic hydrolysis. The resulting amine is protected as its Boc derivative (108) and then subjected to Suzuki coupling with the quinoline boronate 39.

The coupling reaction is performed using slightly modified conditions to accommodate the increased steric bulk of the cyclopropylmethoxy group, employing XPhos-Pd-G3 as the catalyst and heating at 80°C for 15 hours. The protected intermediate 127 is obtained as a colorless solid in 78% yield after purification by flash column chromatography. Deprotection is carried out using the standard two-step procedure, yielding compound 29 as a yellow-colored solid in 49% yield from the protected intermediate.

Biological evaluation of compound 29 reveals exceptional inhibitory potency against rat nNOS, with a Ki value of 39 nM, making it the most potent inhibitor in the entire series. The compound also demonstrates good selectivity over iNOS (382-fold) and eNOS (433-fold). X-ray crystallography studies show that the cyclopropyl ring fits perfectly into the hydrophobic pocket formed by Met336, Leu337, and Tyr706 in rat nNOS, forming optimal van der Waals interactions that contribute to the enhanced potency. The compound also demonstrates excellent blood-brain barrier permeability, with an effective permeability value of 15.5 × 10⁻⁶ cm/s, and high metabolic stability in human liver microsome assays, with a half-life greater than 60 minutes.

**Example 3: Preparation of 7-(3-(Aminomethyl)-4-(pyridin-3-ylmethoxy)phenyl)-4-methylquinolin-2-amine Trihydrochloride (Compound 33)**

Compound 33 demonstrates the successful incorporation of a heterocyclic substituent at the 4-position and illustrates the dual functionality of pyridine rings in enhancing both potency and selectivity. The synthesis begins with the preparation of 3-(chloromethyl)pyridine hydrochloride, which is converted to its free base immediately prior to use. The free base is then reacted with 4-hydroxy-3-(aminomethyl)benzaldehyde to form the pyridinylmethyl ether linkage. The resulting alcohol is converted to the corresponding bromide and then subjected to Delépine reaction and Boc protection to yield the coupling partner 112.

The Suzuki coupling reaction between 39 and 112 is performed under the standard conditions, yielding the protected intermediate 131 in 80% yield after purification. Deprotection is carried out using the standard two-step procedure, with careful attention to the solubility characteristics of the intermediate, which requires extensive extraction with ethyl acetate during workup. Compound 33 is obtained as a white solid in 74% yield from the protected intermediate.

Biological evaluation of compound 33 reveals balanced inhibitory potency against both rat and human nNOS, with Ki values of 44 nM and 45 nM, respectively. This balanced activity is particularly valuable for preclinical development, as it allows for meaningful translation between rodent models and human clinical trials. The compound demonstrates moderate selectivity over eNOS (172-fold), which is attributed to the ability of the pyridine nitrogen to form additional hydrogen bonding interactions with water molecules in the eNOS active site. X-ray crystallography studies reveal that the pyridine ring can function as either a hydrophobic moiety in rat nNOS (interacting with Leu337) or a hydrogen bonding acceptor in human nNOS (interacting with His342), explaining the balanced activity across species.

**Example 4: Preparation of 7-(3-(Aminomethyl)-4-((4-(methylthio)-1,3-thiazol-2-yl)methoxy)phenyl)-4-methylquinolin-2-amine Dihydrochloride (Compound 35)**

Compound 35 represents the most potent inhibitor against human nNOS in the series and demonstrates the advantages of incorporating a thiazole heterocycle at the 4-position. The synthesis begins with the preparation of 2-(chloromethyl)-4-(methylthio)-1,3-thiazole, which is reacted with 4-hydroxy-3-(aminomethyl)benzaldehyde to form the thiazolylmethyl ether linkage. The resulting alcohol is converted to the bromide and then subjected to Delépine reaction and Boc protection to yield the coupling partner.

The Suzuki coupling reaction is performed under standard conditions, yielding the protected intermediate in good yield. Deprotection is carried out using the standard two-step procedure, with additional purification by reverse phase chromatography to ensure high purity. Compound 35 is obtained as a tan solid in good yield.

Biological evaluation of compound 35 reveals exceptional inhibitory potency against human nNOS, with a Ki value of 31 nM, making it the most potent human nNOS inhibitor in the series. However, the compound shows reduced selectivity over iNOS and eNOS compared to other compounds in the series, which is attributed to the increased binding affinity for these isoforms resulting from the polar thiazole ring. X-ray crystallography studies reveal that the thiazole sulfur atom faces toward His342 in human nNOS, while the nitrogen atom forms hydrogen bonding interactions with water molecules, contributing to the enhanced potency. Despite the reduced selectivity, the compound's exceptional human nNOS potency makes it a valuable tool compound for studying human-specific nNOS biology.
