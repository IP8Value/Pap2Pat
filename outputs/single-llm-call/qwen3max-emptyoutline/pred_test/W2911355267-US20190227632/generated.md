# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention resides in the field of neuroengineering and human-machine interfaces, particularly in systems and methods for estimating and reproducing fingertip spatial relationships during object manipulation. More specifically, the invention relates to a novel framework for quantifying and modeling the relative contributions of tactile and non-tactile sensorimotor inputs—namely cutaneous mechanoreceptive feedback, proprioceptive signals, and efference copy of motor commands—in the central nervous system’s (CNS) estimation of the vertical distance between the thumb and index finger during precision grip tasks. The invention further encompasses apparatuses, experimental protocols, and computational models that enable the isolation, measurement, and integration of these distinct sensory modalities to elucidate how the brain constructs a dynamic representation of hand configuration in the absence of visual feedback. This technology has direct applications in the development of advanced prosthetic limbs, haptic feedback systems for virtual and augmented reality, robotic teleoperation interfaces, and neurorehabilitation devices aimed at restoring or enhancing dexterous manipulation capabilities in individuals with sensorimotor impairments. By providing a mechanistic understanding of how force-related tactile and non-tactile cues are linearly combined to estimate inter-fingertip distance, the invention establishes a foundational principle for designing next-generation human-centered technologies that align with the natural sensorimotor processing strategies of the human brain.

## BACKGROUND

The ability to accurately perceive and control the relative positions of the fingers during object manipulation is fundamental to dexterous motor behavior. Unlike vision, which provides an external reference frame for limb position, the central nervous system must rely on internal sensorimotor signals to estimate the spatial configuration of the hand when visual feedback is unavailable—a common scenario during everyday tasks such to handling objects in the dark or within occluded environments. Despite the absence of dedicated sensory receptors that directly encode the distance between limb endpoints, such as the thumb and index fingertip, humans can reliably estimate this metric through the integration of multiple sources of information. These include tactile cues arising from mechanical deformation of the skin, proprioceptive inputs from muscle spindles and joint receptors, and an internal copy of the motor commands issued to the muscles, known as efference copy. While each of these signals has been studied in isolation, the precise manner in which they are combined to form a coherent percept of hand configuration remains poorly understood.

Prior research has demonstrated that tactile afferents in the glabrous skin of the fingertips can encode both the magnitude and direction of forces applied to the finger pad, thereby providing exteroceptive information about object properties and contact mechanics. Similarly, proprioceptive signals convey information about joint angles, muscle length, and tension, contributing to the sense of limb position in space. Efference copy, meanwhile, offers a predictive signal about the expected sensory consequences of motor output, allowing the brain to anticipate changes in limb configuration before sensory feedback arrives. However, these signals are not always congruent. For instance, when opposing tangential forces are exerted by the thumb and index finger—such as when the thumb pushes upward while the index finger pulls downward—the resulting patterns of skin deformation and muscle activation may generate conflicting cues about the actual vertical alignment of the fingertips. In such scenarios, the CNS must resolve these discrepancies to maintain accurate motor control.

Previous studies have hinted at the dominance of certain sensory modalities under specific conditions. For example, it has been shown that tactile inputs can override proprioceptive and motor-related signals in the perception of illusory finger movements, suggesting a hierarchical weighting of sensory information. Moreover, work by Shibata et al. (2014) revealed that subjects consistently overestimated the vertical distance between their fingertips when exerting opposing tangential forces, implying that efference copy may dominate over afferent feedback in certain contexts. However, these findings were limited by the inability to isolate the contributions of tactile and non-tactile inputs in a controlled manner. Without the capacity to independently manipulate cutaneous feedback and proprioceptive/efference copy signals, it was impossible to determine their relative weights in the estimation process or to characterize the mathematical form of their integration.

Existing experimental paradigms have also been constrained by methodological limitations. Traditional approaches often rely on passive limb positioning or active force production against fixed handles, which inherently couple tactile and proprioceptive signals, making it difficult to disentangle their individual effects. Furthermore, the use of visual feedback or inconsistent force magnitudes across trials introduces additional confounding variables that obscure the underlying sensorimotor mechanisms. Consequently, there remains a critical gap in knowledge regarding how the brain combines distinct classes of sensorimotor information to estimate inter-fingertip distance—a gap that has hindered the development of biomimetic control systems for prosthetics and robotics, which require accurate models of human sensorimotor integration to function effectively.

The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel experimental framework that enables the independent delivery of tactile and non-tactile force-related inputs during a digit position matching task. Through the use of custom-designed wearable haptic devices and robotic force actuators, the invention allows for the precise control of skin deformation (tactile input) and muscle/joint loading (non-tactile input) without requiring voluntary force production or visual feedback. This approach not only isolates the contribution of each sensory modality but also permits the quantification of their relative influence on perceptual bias. Additionally, the invention provides a computational model based on linear cue combination theory, which accurately predicts the perceptual outcome when both inputs are available simultaneously. This model represents a significant advancement over prior art, as it moves beyond qualitative descriptions of sensory dominance to offer a quantitative, testable framework for understanding multisensory integration in the context of hand representation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for determining the relative contributions of tactile and non-tactile sensorimotor inputs to the central nervous system’s estimation of fingertip vertical distance during precision grip tasks. The invention is based on the discovery that tactile inputs—arising from mechanical deformation of the finger pads—contribute more significantly to perceptual bias in fingertip distance estimation than non-tactile inputs, which include proprioceptive signals and efference copy of motor commands. Furthermore, the invention demonstrates that these two classes of inputs are integrated in a linear fashion, such that the perceptual bias observed when both inputs are available is approximately equal to the sum of the biases elicited by each input in isolation.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a method for estimating fingertip distance comprising the steps of: (a) presenting a subject with a collinear starting position of the thumb and index finger; (b) delivering force-related tactile inputs to the finger pads via wearable haptic devices without requiring voluntary force production, thereby isolating tactile contributions; (c) delivering force-related non-tactile inputs via robotic actuators attached to the digits while preventing skin deformation, thereby isolating non-tactile contributions; (d) measuring the subject’s matching error—the difference between the perceived and reproduced vertical distance—under each condition; and (e) comparing the matching errors to quantify the relative weight of each input type.

In another aspect, the invention includes a computational model for predicting fingertip distance estimation based on a linear combination of tactile and non-tactile inputs. The model defines the total estimation bias as the weighted sum of the individual biases from each modality, where the weights are derived from the observed matching errors in isolation conditions. The model further incorporates reliability-based weighting, wherein the inverse variance of estimation errors serves as a proxy for cue reliability, allowing for the assessment of whether integration is optimal or suboptimal.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides an apparatus for delivering controlled tactile and non-tactile stimuli to the digits. The tactile stimulation apparatus comprises wearable haptic devices configured to apply precise normal and shear forces to the finger pads through compression and skin stretch mechanisms, respectively. The non-tactile stimulation apparatus comprises robotic force actuators (e.g., Phantom Premium 1.5) coupled to the digits via rigid thimbles that transmit forces without inducing skin deformation, thereby engaging only proprioceptive and efference copy pathways.

The invention further discloses a protocol for conducting a digit position matching task that eliminates visual feedback and controls for confounding variables such as muscle fatigue and thixotropy. The protocol includes standardized phases for digit placement, force application, relaxation, reproduction, and holding, ensuring consistent experimental conditions across trials and subjects.

By enabling the dissociation and quantification of tactile and non-tactile contributions to hand representation, the invention provides a foundational tool for advancing neuroprosthetics, haptic interfaces, and sensorimotor rehabilitation. The linear integration model disclosed herein offers a parsimonious yet powerful framework for designing artificial systems that emulate the brain’s natural strategy for combining sensory cues, thereby improving the intuitiveness and performance of human-machine interactions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a novel methodology and associated apparatus for investigating and modeling the neural mechanisms underlying the estimation of fingertip relative position during object manipulation. Central to the invention is the recognition that the central nervous system integrates multiple, partially redundant sources of sensorimotor information—including tactile feedback from cutaneous mechanoreceptors, proprioceptive signals from muscle and joint receptors, and efference copy of motor commands—to construct a dynamic representation of hand configuration. Crucially, the invention reveals that these inputs are not equally weighted; rather, tactile inputs derived from finger pad deformation exert a dominant influence on perceptual judgments of inter-fingertip distance, particularly when tangential forces are applied in opposing directions. Moreover, the invention establishes that the integration of tactile and non-tactile inputs follows a linear summation rule, a finding that has profound implications for the design of biomimetic control systems.

The invention is implemented through a series of controlled experiments that systematically isolate tactile and non-tactile inputs using specialized hardware and rigorous behavioral protocols. In the first experiment (Experiment 1), subjects perform a digit position matching task under conditions where both tactile and non-tactile inputs are naturally available. Specifically, subjects grasp a custom-made sensorized handle equipped with six-axis force/torque sensors that measure normal and tangential forces as well as the three-dimensional center of pressure (CoP) of each fingertip. During the “sense distance” phase, subjects exert predefined combinations of digit forces—including conditions where thumb and index finger tangential forces are directed in the same or opposite vertical directions—while maintaining a collinear starting position (vertical distance dy = 0 ± 3 mm). After a brief relaxation period, subjects reproduce the memorized fingertip distance without visual feedback. The matching error, defined as the difference between the reproduced and original dy, serves as a measure of perceptual bias.

In the second experiment (Experiment 2), the contribution of tactile inputs alone is isolated using wearable haptic devices that deliver controlled mechanical stimuli to the finger pads. These devices apply normal forces via compression and tangential forces via skin stretch, replicating the patterns of cutaneous deformation that would occur during active force production in Experiment 1. Critically, subjects do not exert any voluntary forces; instead, their digits are passively positioned by an experimenter, and the haptic devices are activated to simulate force-related tactile inputs. Because no muscle activation or joint movement occurs beyond minimal postural stabilization, proprioceptive and efference copy signals are effectively eliminated. The resulting matching errors thus reflect the influence of tactile inputs exclusively.

In the third experiment (Experiment 3), non-tactile inputs are isolated using robotic force actuators (e.g., Phantom Premium 1.5) attached to the intermediate and proximal phalanges of the index finger and thumb, respectively. These actuators apply forces directly to the skeletal structure of the digits via rigid thimbles and Velcro straps, bypassing the skin and thereby preventing cutaneous deformation. Subjects actively resist the applied forces, generating efference copy and proprioceptive feedback, but receive no tactile cues related to force direction or magnitude. The matching errors in this condition therefore represent the contribution of non-tactile inputs alone.

Across all three experiments, stringent controls are implemented to ensure data validity. Visual feedback is occluded using opaque goggles or screens. Digit positions are monitored in real time using motion capture or built-in encoders, and auditory alerts are provided if deviations exceed predefined thresholds (±3 mm). Force magnitudes are calibrated to match those used in Experiment 1, and trials are randomized to prevent order effects. Additionally, control conditions involving no tangential forces (“Null”) or only normal forces (“Fn only”) are included to account for baseline perceptual biases unrelated to force direction.

Data analysis proceeds by first verifying compliance with task constraints—specifically, that dy and horizontal distance (dz) remain within tolerance during the sense phase, and that applied forces fall within target ranges. Matching errors are then computed for each trial and normalized by subtracting the mean error observed in the Null condition, thereby controlling for individual idiosyncrasies in distance estimation. Non-parametric statistical tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank, Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA) are employed to assess the significance of biases across conditions and experiments.

The key empirical finding is that significant matching errors occur only when thumb and index finger tangential forces are directed oppositely (e.g., thumb upward, index downward), and that these errors are largest in Experiment 1 (combined inputs), intermediate in Experiment 2 (tactile only), and smallest in Experiment 3 (non-tactile only). For instance, in the TUP-IDN condition, median matching errors are 9.2 mm, 7.9 mm, and 5.7 mm in Experiments 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Importantly, the sum of the biases from Experiments 2 and 3 closely approximates the bias observed in Experiment 1 (10.3 mm vs. 9.2 mm), supporting a linear integration model.

To formalize this observation, the invention introduces a computational framework wherein the total estimation bias \( D_{\text{yercomb}} \) is modeled as:
\[
D_{\text{yercomb}} = \alpha_t D_{\text{yert}} + \alpha_{nt} D_{\text{yernt}}
\]
where \( D_{\text{yert}} \) and \( D_{\text{yernt}} \) are the biases from tactile-only and non-tactile-only conditions, and \( \alpha_t \), \( \alpha_{nt} \) are weighting coefficients. Solving for these weights yields \( \alpha_t \approx 0.58–0.60 \) and \( \alpha_{nt} \approx 0.40–0.42 \), confirming the greater contribution of tactile inputs.

Further validation comes from reliability-based analysis. If integration were optimal, the reliability (inverse variance) of the combined estimate should equal the sum of the individual reliabilities. The data show that combined reliability exceeds that of tactile-only but falls short of non-tactile-only, indicating suboptimal but linear integration.

The invention also quantifies the “strength of bias”—the probability that a given force condition elicits consistent directional errors beyond baseline variability. Tactile inputs produce stronger biases (44% and 33% for positive/negative errors) compared to non-tactile inputs (20% and 17%), reinforcing their dominant role.

These findings have broad technological implications. In prosthetic design, for example, haptic feedback systems should prioritize the delivery of realistic skin deformation cues to enhance users’ sense of hand configuration. In virtual reality, force-rendering algorithms can be optimized by weighting tactile simulations more heavily than joint torque feedback. Moreover, the linear model provides a simple yet effective algorithm for fusing multimodal sensor data in robotic hands, improving their adaptability to unstructured environments.

In summary, the invention provides a complete experimental and theoretical framework for dissecting the sensorimotor basis of hand representation. By isolating, measuring, and modeling the contributions of tactile and non-tactile inputs, it resolves a long-standing question in motor neuroscience and delivers actionable insights for engineering applications that interface with the human sensorimotor system.
