## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel class of non-aqueous electrolytes for use in proton-conducting electrochemical cells, particularly nickel/metal hydride (Ni/MH) batteries, which overcome the intrinsic limitations of conventional aqueous alkaline electrolytes such as 30 wt% potassium hydroxide (KOH). These limitations include a narrow electrochemical stability window of approximately 1.23 volts, which restricts the selection of electrode materials to those with standard potentials within this narrow range, thereby capping the achievable energy density of the battery system. In contrast, the electrolytes of the present invention are formulated as mixtures of ionic liquids (ILs) and non-aqueous protic acids, which together establish a robust hydrogen-bond network capable of facilitating efficient proton transport via the Grotthuss mechanism while simultaneously providing an electrochemical stability window that is at least two to three times wider than that of aqueous systems. This expanded stability window enables the integration of high-potential electrode materials previously incompatible with aqueous environments, thereby unlocking significant gains in energy density, safety, and operational temperature range.

The core innovation resides in the strategic combination of aprotic ionic liquids—specifically those based on imidazolium, alkylammonium, or related heterocyclic cations—with anhydrous organic or inorganic acids such as acetic acid or phosphoric acid. While aprotic ionic liquids alone lack exchangeable protons and exhibit relatively low ionic conductivity due to high viscosity and strong ion pairing, the addition of a non-aqueous acid serves a dual function: it acts as a diluent that reduces viscosity and disrupts ion aggregation, thereby enhancing ionic mobility, and it introduces active proton donors that participate in the formation of a dynamic hydrogen-bond network. This network supports structural diffusion of protons through rapid bond dissociation and reformation, a process known as the Grotthuss mechanism, which results in high proton transference numbers and effective proton conductivity even in the absence of water. The resulting electrolyte exhibits ionic conductivities on the order of 10⁻² S·cm⁻¹—comparable to carbonate-based electrolytes used in lithium-ion batteries—and demonstrates sufficient electrochemical robustness to support reversible charge/discharge cycling in Ni/MH cells.

In one embodiment, the electrolyte comprises 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate ([EMIM][Ac]) combined with 2 M acetic acid, which has been shown to deliver optimal performance in terms of cycle life, overpotential minimization, and discharge capacity retention when paired with AB₅-type metal hydride anodes. The invention further encompasses the use of alternative ionic liquids, including but not limited to 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate ([BMIM][Ac]), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate ([EMIM][TfO]), 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([BMIM][TFSI]), and diethylmethylammonium trifluoromethanesulfonate ([DEMA][TfO]), each of which may be combined with varying concentrations of acetic acid (from 0.5 M to 6 M) to tune electrolyte properties such as conductivity, viscosity, and acidity. Although phosphoric acid was also evaluated as a proton donor, its lower solubility in ionic liquids at room temperature and the resulting instability of electrode interfaces rendered it less suitable for long-term cycling, thereby establishing acetic acid as the preferred acid component for practical applications.

The invention also discloses the unexpected discovery that metal hydride anodes, traditionally operated in strongly alkaline aqueous media, can undergo electrochemical activation and sustained cycling in these weakly acidic, non-aqueous ionic liquid environments. This activation process involves surface modification of the metal hydride alloy, likely through the formation of a solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)-like layer that facilitates proton transfer and protects the bulk material from corrosion—a phenomenon previously thought to be exclusive to aqueous KOH systems. The successful demonstration of reversible hydrogen storage and release in a non-aqueous, non-flammable, and thermally stable electrolyte represents a paradigm shift in Ni/MH battery technology, enabling safer, more energy-dense, and potentially higher-voltage secondary batteries. Furthermore, the principles disclosed herein are extensible to other proton-conducting electrochemical devices, including fuel cells, supercapacitors, and redox flow batteries, where wide electrochemical windows and high proton conductivity are critical.

## DETAILED DISCLOSURE

### x is B or Al and

The present invention relates to advanced electrolyte compositions designed for use in proton-conducting electrochemical cells, with particular emphasis on nickel/metal hydride (Ni/MH) batteries. Central to this innovation is the recognition that the conventional aqueous alkaline electrolyte—typically 30 wt% potassium hydroxide (KOH)—imposes a fundamental thermodynamic limitation on cell voltage due to the narrow electrochemical stability window of water, which is approximately 1.23 volts at standard conditions. This constraint precludes the use of high-capacity or high-potential electrode materials that would otherwise enhance the energy density of the battery. To circumvent this limitation, the inventors have developed a new class of non-aqueous electrolytes based on mixtures of ionic liquids (ILs) and non-aqueous protic acids. These electrolytes leverage the unique physicochemical properties of ionic liquids—including negligible vapor pressure, non-flammability, thermal stability, and a wide intrinsic electrochemical window—while introducing proton conductivity through the incorporation of anhydrous acids that serve as proton donors and viscosity modifiers.

Ionic liquids, by definition, are salts composed entirely of ions that remain liquid at or below 100°C. In the context of this invention, the ionic liquids employed are predominantly aprotic, meaning they do not contain intrinsically exchangeable protons in their chemical structure. Examples include imidazolium-based cations such as 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium ([EMIM]⁺) and 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium ([BMIM]⁺), paired with anions such as acetate ([Ac]⁻), trifluoromethanesulfonate ([TfO]⁻), or bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([TFSI]⁻). While these aprotic ILs offer excellent electrochemical stability and safety, they suffer from relatively low ionic conductivity due to high viscosity and strong Coulombic interactions between cations and anions, which promote ion pairing and reduce the number of free charge carriers. Moreover, in their pure form, they are incapable of supporting proton conduction, which is essential for the operation of Ni/MH batteries where hydrogen is stored and released in the form of protons at the metal hydride anode.

To address these shortcomings, the inventors introduced anhydrous protic acids into the ionic liquid matrix. Acetic acid (CH₃COOH) was selected as the primary acid due to its liquid state at room temperature, high purity availability, and ability to act both as a proton donor and as a diluent. When mixed with an ionic liquid, acetic acid disrupts the extensive ion networks present in pure ILs, thereby reducing viscosity and increasing ionic mobility. Simultaneously, the carboxylic acid group provides a source of labile protons that can participate in hydrogen-bonding interactions with the anions of the ionic liquid (e.g., acetate or [TfO]⁻), forming a dynamic, percolating network through which protons can migrate via the Grotthuss mechanism. This mechanism, distinct from vehicular diffusion, involves the sequential breaking and reforming of hydrogen bonds, allowing protons to “hop” across the electrolyte without requiring the physical displacement of entire molecules. This results in high proton transference numbers and effective proton conductivity, even at moderate acid concentrations.

Experimental results demonstrate that the ionic conductivity of IL/acid mixtures increases monotonically with acid concentration up to 6 M, with the highest conductivity observed for [DEMA][TfO] containing 6 M acetic acid (14.3 mS·cm⁻¹), compared to 9.0 mS·cm⁻¹ for the pure IL. Notably, the [EMIM][Ac]/2 M acetic acid formulation achieved a balance of high conductivity, low overpotential, and long cycle life, making it the preferred embodiment for Ni/MH battery applications. The electrochemical stability window of these electrolytes, as determined by cyclic voltammetry, exceeds 2.0 volts, with a clear separation (~0.6 V) between the hydrogen reduction peak and the onset of electrolyte decomposition, thereby enabling stable cycling without significant parasitic reactions—provided that residual water is minimized.

The successful integration of these electrolytes into Ni/MH cells required overcoming the historical assumption that metal hydride anodes are only functional in strongly alkaline aqueous environments. Surprisingly, AB₅-type metal hydride alloys (e.g., La₁₀.₅Ce₄.₃Pr₀.₅Nd₁.₄Ni₆₀.₀Co₁₂.₇Mn₅.₉Al₄.₇) exhibited reversible charge/discharge behavior in the 2 M acetic acid/[EMIM][Ac] electrolyte after an initial activation period. This activation involved several cycles during which the discharge capacity increased and overpotentials decreased, suggesting the formation of a catalytically active surface layer analogous to the nickel-embedded oxide/hydroxide SEI formed in KOH. Although the exact composition of this interface in the non-aqueous system remains under investigation, its presence is inferred from the stabilization of electrochemical performance after the first 20–30 cycles.

Importantly, the invention is not limited to acetic acid. Other non-aqueous protic acids, such as phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄), were also evaluated. However, phosphoric acid’s solid state at room temperature and limited solubility in ionic liquids led to highly viscous mixtures with poor wettability and rapid electrode degradation, rendering it unsuitable for practical use. Thus, the invention establishes a design principle: the acid must be liquid or highly soluble at operating temperatures, possess multiple proton donor sites, and be chemically compatible with both the ionic liquid and the electrode materials. Acetic acid satisfies these criteria, while also offering low cost, low toxicity, and ease of handling.

The broader implications of this work extend beyond Ni/MH batteries. Any electrochemical system requiring proton conduction—such as certain types of fuel cells, proton-based supercapacitors, or hybrid ion batteries—can benefit from the disclosed electrolytes. The tunability of ionic liquids allows for molecular-level engineering of the electrolyte to match specific electrode chemistries, while the non-aqueous nature eliminates risks associated with water decomposition, gas evolution, and corrosion. Thus, the invention provides a versatile platform for next-generation energy storage devices that demand both high safety and high energy density.

### X is B or Al and

The detailed disclosure of the present invention encompasses the specific compositional parameters and structural features of the ionic liquid components that are critical to achieving optimal electrolyte performance in proton-conducting electrochemical cells. In particular, the cationic moiety of the ionic liquid plays a decisive role in determining the viscosity, ionic conductivity, and compatibility with protic acid additives. The inventors have systematically evaluated a series of imidazolium-based cations, varying the length and branching of the alkyl substituents at the 1- and 3-positions of the imidazole ring. It was found that shorter alkyl chains, such as ethyl (C₂H₅) in 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium ([EMIM]⁺), consistently yield lower viscosities and higher ionic conductivities compared to their longer-chain counterparts, such as 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium ([BMIM]⁺). This trend is attributed to reduced van der Waals interactions between cations with shorter alkyl groups, which diminishes the cohesive energy density of the liquid and facilitates ion mobility. Consequently, [EMIM]⁺-based ionic liquids are preferred over [BMIM]⁺ analogs for electrolyte formulations where high conductivity is paramount.

Furthermore, the choice of anion in the ionic liquid significantly influences the ability of the electrolyte to support proton conduction when blended with a non-aqueous acid. Anions that are themselves conjugate bases of weak acids—such as acetate ([Ac]⁻)—exhibit superior proton-accepting capability and thus participate more effectively in the hydrogen-bond network necessary for Grotthuss-type proton transport. In contrast, anions derived from strong acids, such as [TfO]⁻ or [TFSI]⁻, are weaker proton acceptors and rely more heavily on the added acid to provide the proton-donor functionality. Nevertheless, even with these anions, the addition of acetic acid restores sufficient proton conductivity for battery operation. The data indicate that [EMIM][Ac] outperforms all other tested ionic liquids when combined with 2 M acetic acid, delivering the lowest charge/discharge overpotentials and the most stable cycling behavior in Ni/MH cells. This superiority is likely due to the synergistic effect between the acetate anion and acetic acid, which together create a highly interconnected proton-hopping pathway.

The invention also contemplates the use of non-imidazolium cations, such as the quaternary ammonium-based diethylmethylammonium ([DEMA]⁺). Although [DEMA][TfO] exhibited high conductivity when mixed with 6 M acetic acid, its electrochemical performance in full cells was inferior to that of [EMIM][Ac]-based electrolytes, possibly due to differences in interfacial stability or proton transfer kinetics at the electrode surface. Nonetheless, the inclusion of [DEMA]⁺ demonstrates the versatility of the invention and confirms that the core principle—combining an aprotic ionic liquid with a non-aqueous protic acid—is not restricted to a single chemical class. The scope of acceptable cations includes any organic or heterocyclic cation that is stable under the operating conditions of the electrochemical cell, non-reactive toward the acid additive, and capable of forming a low-viscosity liquid when paired with a suitable anion.

Similarly, the anion is not limited to those explicitly tested. The invention encompasses any anion that is chemically stable in the presence of acetic acid, does not decompose at the operating voltages of the cell, and contributes to the formation of a proton-conducting medium. This includes, but is not limited to, carboxylates (e.g., formate, propionate), sulfonates (e.g., methanesulfonate, p-toluenesulfonate), and imide derivatives beyond [TFSI]⁻, such as bis(perfluoroethylsulfonyl)imide. The key requirement is that the anion must be able to engage in hydrogen bonding with the proton donor, thereby enabling the structural diffusion of protons. Anions that are overly bulky or hydrophobic may hinder this process and are therefore less preferred.

In addition to the ionic liquid components, the concentration of the added acid is a critical parameter. The inventors have established that acid concentrations below 0.5 M are insufficient to generate adequate proton conductivity, while concentrations above 6 M may lead to excessive acidity that corrodes electrode materials or destabilizes the ionic liquid structure. The optimal range is between 1 M and 3 M, with 2 M acetic acid in [EMIM][Ac] representing the best compromise between conductivity, viscosity, and interfacial stability. This concentration provides enough proton donors to sustain the hydrogen-bond network without overwhelming the ionic liquid’s inherent stability.

It is also important to note that the electrolytes must be rigorously dried to minimize water content, as residual water can undergo electrolysis at voltages above 1.5 V, leading to gas evolution, reduced coulombic efficiency, and potential cell failure. The inventors achieved water contents below 1000 ppm by vacuum-drying the ionic liquids at 115°C for 48 hours prior to acid addition. This level of dryness is essential for realizing the full electrochemical stability window of the non-aqueous system.

Finally, the invention is not restricted to Ni/MH chemistry. The same electrolyte platform can be adapted for use with other proton-insertion electrodes, such as tungsten oxide, Prussian blue analogs, or organic carbonyl compounds, thereby expanding the applicability of the technology to a wide range of energy storage and conversion devices. The modular nature of the formulation—where cation, anion, and acid can be independently varied—allows for fine-tuning of the electrolyte to match the specific requirements of any given electrode system.

The present invention further extends to the selection and engineering of metal hydride anode materials that are compatible with the disclosed non-aqueous, ionic liquid-based electrolytes. Traditionally, Ni/MH batteries employ AB₅-type alloys, where A is a rare-earth mixture (e.g., La, Ce, Pr, Nd) and B is primarily nickel, often with partial substitution by cobalt, manganese, and aluminum to improve cycle life and kinetics. In the context of this invention, the AB₅ alloy with the composition La₁₀.₅Ce₄.₃Pr₀.₅Nd₁.₄Ni₆₀.₀Co₁₂.₇Mn₅.₉Al₄.₇ demonstrated the best performance among the tested materials, delivering a discharge capacity of 161.9 mAh·g⁻¹ in 2 M acetic acid/[EMIM][Ac] electrolyte after activation. This alloy contains nickel as the primary B-site element, which is critical for catalyzing the hydrogen adsorption/desorption reactions at the surface. The presence of cobalt enhances structural stability during cycling, while manganese and aluminum contribute to corrosion resistance and plateau voltage tuning.

However, the invention is not limited to AB₅ alloys. The inventors also evaluated two alternative metal hydride systems: an MgNi-based AB-type alloy (AR3, composition Mg₅₂Ni₃₉Co₃Mn₆) and a body-centered cubic (BCC) alloy (BCC-B08, composition Ti₄₀V₃₀Cr₁₅Mn₁₃Mo₂). Both of these materials incorporate transition metals from the group consisting of Sc, Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, Zn, Y, Zr, Nb, Mo, Tc, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, Cd, Lu, Hf, Ta, W, Re, Os, Ir, Pt, or Au. Specifically, AR3 contains Ni, Co, and Mn, while BCC-B08 includes Ti, V, Cr, Mn, and Mo—all of which fall within the enumerated list. These elements are selected for their ability to form intermetallic compounds with high hydrogen storage capacity, favorable thermodynamics, and reasonable kinetics.

Despite their theoretical promise, both AR3 and BCC-B08 exhibited significantly lower capacities (~50 mAh·g⁻¹) and higher overpotentials in the ionic liquid electrolyte compared to AB₅. This inferior performance is attributed to poor stability in the weakly acidic environment and the inability to form a protective, catalytically active surface layer akin to the SEI observed in AB₅. The BCC alloy, in particular, contains vanadium and titanium, which are prone to oxidation in acidic media, leading to rapid degradation. Similarly, the magnesium in AR3 may react with acetic acid to form magnesium acetate and hydrogen gas, thereby depleting the active material.

Nevertheless, the testing of these alternative alloys confirms that the scope of the invention includes any metal hydride composition that incorporates one or more of the listed transition metals, provided that the material can be stabilized against acid-induced corrosion. Future work may involve surface coatings, alloying modifications, or electrolyte additives to enhance the compatibility of these high-capacity materials with the non-aqueous system. For instance, the addition of small amounts of corrosion inhibitors or the use of more robust anion-acid combinations could enable the use of BCC or AB₂ alloys, which typically offer higher gravimetric capacities than AB₅.

The inclusion of noble metals such as Pd, Pt, or Au in the alloy composition is also contemplated, as these elements are excellent catalysts for hydrogen reactions and are highly resistant to acid corrosion. However, their high cost limits practical application, so they are considered optional performance enhancers rather than essential components. More economically viable catalysts include Ni, Co, and Fe, which are already present in commercial AB₅ alloys.

In summary, the invention encompasses metal hydride anodes comprising any combination of the listed transition metals, with the understanding that the specific composition must be optimized for stability and activity in the weakly acidic, non-aqueous ionic liquid environment. The successful demonstration with AB₅ provides a proof-of-concept, while the evaluation of alternative alloys defines the boundaries of material compatibility and guides future development.

The cathode component in the electrochemical cells of the present invention is typically a nickel-based positive electrode, specifically sintered nickel hydroxide (Ni(OH)₂), which is standard in conventional Ni/MH batteries. However, the use of a non-aqueous, weakly acidic electrolyte introduces new challenges for cathode stability that were not present in the traditional alkaline environment. In 30 wt% KOH, Ni(OH)₂ is thermodynamically stable and undergoes reversible oxidation to nickel oxyhydroxide (NiOOH) during charging. In contrast, in the presence of acetic acid, there is a risk of acid-base reaction between the hydroxide and the carboxylic acid, potentially leading to the formation of nickel acetate, water, and degraded electrochemical performance.

The inventors observed that while the sintered Ni(OH)₂ cathode functioned adequately in the short term, long-term cycling revealed signs of instability, including capacity fade and reduced coulombic efficiency. This suggests that the cathode is slowly reacting with the electrolyte, generating water as a byproduct, which then participates in parasitic electrolysis at high voltages. Therefore, the invention implicitly recognizes the need for cathode materials that are chemically inert in weakly acidic, non-aqueous media. Although the current examples use Ni(OH)₂ for direct comparison with conventional systems, the broader scope of the invention includes the development and integration of alternative cathodes that are stable under these conditions.

Potential candidates include metal oxides that do not contain basic hydroxide groups, such as nickel oxide (NiO), cobalt oxide (Co₃O₄), or manganese dioxide (MnO₂), which may be less reactive toward acetic acid. Alternatively, organic cathode materials based on quinones, imides, or conductive polymers could be employed, as many of these are stable in a wide pH range and can undergo reversible proton-coupled redox reactions. The key requirement is that the cathode must operate within the expanded electrochemical window of the ionic liquid electrolyte and must not degrade in the presence of the protic acid additive.

Thus, while the examples focus on Ni(OH)₂, the invention is not limited to this material. The phrase “X is B or Al” in this context refers to the possibility of substituting the cathode active material with other compounds where X represents a metal or metalloid that can form a stable, proton-insertion host structure. Aluminum, for instance, is not typically used in cathodes, but boron-containing compounds such as metal borates might offer interesting electrochemical properties. The essential point is that the cathode must be compatible with the non-aqueous, proton-conducting electrolyte, and the invention provides the electrolyte platform that enables the exploration of such novel cathode materials.

The separator and cell architecture used in the implementation of the present invention are adapted from standard Swagelok-type or coin cell designs commonly employed in laboratory-scale battery testing. A non-woven polyolefin or glass fiber separator, approximately 0.2 mm thick, is used to prevent electrical shorting between the anode and cathode while allowing ionic transport. The separator must be chemically inert to both the ionic liquid and the acid, and must be wettable by the electrolyte to ensure uniform ion distribution. In the examples, a standard non-woven separator was used successfully, indicating that conventional separators are compatible with the disclosed electrolytes.

The cell is assembled in an argon-filled glove box to exclude moisture and oxygen, which could degrade the electrolyte or electrodes. After assembly, 1 mL of the IL/acid electrolyte is injected into the cell, which is then sealed for testing. This procedure ensures that the electrolyte remains anhydrous and that the cell operates under controlled conditions.

## EXAMPLES

### Electrolyte Compositions Comprising Ionic Liquid and Acetic Acid

A series of electrolyte compositions were prepared by mixing anhydrous acetic acid with various ionic liquids to evaluate their suitability for use in nickel/metal hydride (Ni/MH) batteries. The ionic liquids selected for this study included 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate ([EMIM][TfO]), 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium trifluoromethanesulfonate ([BMIM][TfO]), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([EMIM][TFSI]), 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([BMIM][TFSI]), 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate ([EMIM][Ac]), 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate ([BMIM][Ac]), 1-ethylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethylsulfonyl)imide ([EIM][TFSI]), and diethylmethylammonium trifluoromethanesulfonate ([DEMA][TfO]). Each ionic liquid was first dried under vacuum at 115°C for 48 hours to reduce water content to below 1000 ppm, as confirmed by Karl Fischer titration. Anhydrous glacial acetic acid (100%, EMD Millipore) was then added to each ionic liquid to achieve a final concentration of 1 M acetic acid. The resulting mixtures were stirred at room temperature until homogeneous.

The ionic conductivity of each electrolyte composition was measured at 25°C using a YSI Model 3200 conductivity meter. The results showed that the addition of acetic acid significantly increased the ionic conductivity of all ionic liquids compared to their pure forms. For example, the conductivity of [EMIM][Ac] increased from approximately 8 mS·cm⁻¹ (pure) to 12.5 mS·cm⁻¹ with 1 M acetic acid. The highest conductivity was observed for [DEMA][TfO] with 6 M acetic acid (14.3 mS·cm⁻¹), demonstrating that both the ionic liquid structure and acid concentration influence the final conductivity. The increase in conductivity is attributed to the reduction in viscosity and the disruption of ion pairing caused by the introduction of acetic acid, which acts as a diluent and proton donor.

These electrolyte compositions were then tested in half-cell configurations using an AB₅-type metal hydride anode (La₁₀.₅Ce₄.₃Pr₀.₅Nd₁.₄Ni₆₀.₀Co₁₂.₇Mn₅.₉Al₄.₇) and an oversized sintered Ni(OH)₂ cathode. The cells were cycled at a constant current of 2 mA·g⁻¹, with a charge capacity of 20 mAh·g⁻¹ and a discharge cutoff voltage of 0.2 V. After 20 cycles, the [EMIM][Ac]/1 M acetic acid electrolyte exhibited the lowest charge/discharge overpotentials and the most stable voltage plateaus, indicating superior interfacial kinetics and proton transport. In contrast, electrolytes based on [TFSI]⁻ anions showed higher overpotentials, likely due to weaker hydrogen-bonding interactions with acetic acid. These results established that acetate-based ionic liquids, when combined with acetic acid, provide the most favorable environment for proton conduction in Ni/MH batteries.

### Electrolyte Composition Comprising Ionic Liquid and Solvent

In addition to acid-based formulations, the inventors explored the use of molecular solvents to dilute ionic liquids and improve their conductivity. Acetonitrile and butanone were selected as representative polar aprotic solvents due to their ability to dissolve ionic liquids and reduce viscosity. A 1:1 volume ratio of [EMIM][TfO] to acetonitrile was prepared, and its conductivity was measured to be 18.2 mS·cm⁻¹—higher than that of the acetic acid mixtures. However, when tested in a Ni/MH half-cell, this solvent-based electrolyte failed to support reversible hydrogen storage, as no significant discharge capacity was observed. Cyclic voltammetry revealed no discernible redox peaks corresponding to hydrogen adsorption/desorption, indicating that the absence of a proton donor prevented the necessary electrochemical reactions at the metal hydride anode.

This example underscores a critical distinction: while solvents can enhance ionic conductivity, they do not provide the proton-donor functionality required for proton-conducting batteries. The success of the acetic acid formulations lies not only in their ability to reduce viscosity but also in their role as active participants in the proton-transfer mechanism. Therefore, the invention specifically requires the inclusion of a protic acid, not merely a diluent, to enable battery operation. Solvent-based electrolytes may be useful in other contexts, such as lithium-ion batteries, but they are inadequate for proton-conducting systems.

### Electrolyte Compositions Comprising Ionic Liquid and Protic Acid

To further validate the necessity of a protic acid, the inventors prepared electrolytes using phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) as an alternative proton donor. Phosphoric acid is a solid at room temperature (melting point 42°C) and was melted before being added to [EMIM][TfO] to achieve a 1 M solution. The resulting mixture was highly viscous, with a conductivity of only 3.1 mS·cm⁻¹ at 25°C, significantly lower than the acetic acid analogs. When tested in a Ni/MH cell, the phosphoric acid-based electrolyte caused rapid degradation of both the AB₅ anode and the Ni(OH)₂ cathode, with capacity fading to zero within 30 cycles. Post-mortem analysis suggested that the high acidity and low solubility of phosphoric acid led to uneven current distribution and localized corrosion.

This example demonstrates that not all protic acids are suitable. The acid must be liquid or highly soluble at operating temperatures, and its acidity must be balanced to avoid electrode damage. Acetic acid, with its moderate pKa (~4.76) and liquid state, strikes this balance, whereas phosphoric acid (pKa₁ ~2.1) is too strong and poorly soluble. Thus, the invention is specifically enabled by the use of weak organic acids like acetic acid, not strong mineral acids.

### Electrolyte Composition Containing 2 M Acetic Acid in EMIM Ac

The optimized electrolyte composition consists of 2 M acetic acid in 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate ([EMIM][Ac]). This formulation was prepared by adding 11.5 mL of glacial acetic acid to 100 g of dried [EMIM][Ac], resulting in a homogeneous, colorless liquid with a conductivity of 13.8 mS·cm⁻¹ at 25°C. This electrolyte was used in full Ni/MH cells with an AB₅ anode and sintered Ni(OH)₂ cathode, cycled at 4 mA·g⁻¹ between 0.7 V and full charge (40 mAh·g⁻¹).

The cell exhibited an initial capacity of less than 5 mAh·g⁻¹, but after 25 cycles, the capacity stabilized at 28.6 mAh·g⁻¹. Over 100 cycles, the capacity gradually decreased to 23.9 mAh·g⁻¹, representing 83% retention. The charge/discharge curves showed well-defined plateaus at 1.6 V and 1.2 V, respectively, with minimal polarization after activation. Cyclic voltammetry confirmed a hydrogen reduction peak at -1.0 V vs. Ag/AgCl, with a 0.6 V margin before electrolyte decomposition, ensuring high coulombic efficiency in the absence of water.

This example represents the best mode of the invention, demonstrating long cycle life, low overpotential, and high stability.

### Example 5

In a final demonstration, deep charge/discharge tests were conducted using the 2 M acetic acid/[EMIM][Ac] electrolyte with three different metal hydride anodes: AB₅, AR3 (Mg₅₂Ni₃₉Co₃Mn₆), and BCC-B08 (Ti₄₀V₃₀Cr₁₅Mn₁₃Mo₂). Cells were charged to 400 mAh·g⁻¹ at 4 mA·g⁻¹ and discharged to 0.2 V. The AB₅ anode delivered 161.9 mAh·g⁻¹, while AR3 and BCC-B08 yielded only ~50 mAh·g⁻¹, with sloping discharge curves indicative of poor kinetics and instability. This confirms that AB₅ is uniquely suited for the non-aqueous acidic environment, likely due to its nickel-rich surface that resists corrosion and catalyzes proton transfer.

This example highlights the importance of anode selection and validates the practical feasibility of the invention with commercially relevant materials.
