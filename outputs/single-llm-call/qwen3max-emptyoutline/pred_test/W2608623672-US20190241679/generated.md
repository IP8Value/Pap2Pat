# DESCRIPTION

## EXAMPLE 1. DECONSTRUCTION OF BIOMASS AND EXTRACTION OF METALS IN VARIOUS IONIC LIQUIDS

The present invention provides a novel and highly efficient method for the deconstruction of lignocellulosic biomass and the concomitant extraction of valuable components, particularly lignin, using low-cost protic ionic liquids, specifically triethylammonium hydrogen sulfate ([TEA][HSO₄]). This method, herein referred to as the ionoSolv pretreatment process, represents a significant advancement in the field of biorefining by enabling the fractionation of complex biomass feedstocks into high-purity, valorizable streams under mild, industrially relevant conditions. The core of the invention lies in the use of an aqueous solution of [TEA][HSO₄] as a fractionating solvent that selectively dissolves lignin and hemicellulose from the native lignocellulosic matrix while leaving behind a cellulose-rich solid pulp that is highly amenable to enzymatic saccharification. This selective fractionation is achieved at atmospheric pressure and at a relatively low temperature of approximately 120°C, which confers substantial economic and safety advantages over high-pressure pretreatment technologies.

The invention is predicated on the discovery that [TEA][HSO₄], a protic ionic liquid synthesized from the simple and inexpensive combination of triethylamine and sulfuric acid, possesses a unique combination of properties that make it exceptionally well-suited for this purpose. Unlike conventional cellulose-dissolving ionic liquids such as 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium acetate, which are costly, moisture-sensitive, and require the complete dissolution of the biomass composite, the [TEA][HSO₄]-based system operates via a different mechanism. It leverages the inherent acidity of the hydrogen sulfate anion to catalyze the hydrolysis of key linkages within the biomass structure—specifically, the ether bonds in lignin (e.g., β-O-4 linkages), the ester linkages connecting hemicellulose to lignin (e.g., ferulic acid esters), and the glycosidic bonds of hemicellulose itself. The presence of a controlled amount of water, typically in the range of 10 to 40 weight percent and optimally at 20 weight percent, is a critical feature of the invention. This water serves multiple essential functions: it acts as a co-catalyst in the hydrolysis reactions, it moderates the solvent's acidity to prevent excessive degradation of cellulose, and it reduces the viscosity of the ionic liquid mixture, thereby improving mass transfer and processability.

In a preferred embodiment of the invention, a lignocellulosic feedstock, such as Miscanthus x giganteus, wheat straw, corn stover, or sugarcane bagasse, is contacted with an aqueous solution comprising from 60 to 90 weight percent of [TEA][HSO₄] and from 10 to 40 weight percent of water. The solids loading, defined as the ratio of dry biomass to total solvent mass, is maintained at an industrially relevant level of at least 10 weight percent, and more preferably between 10 and 20 weight percent. The mixture is then heated to a temperature between 100°C and 150°C, with 120°C being an optimal balance between reaction rate and product quality. The pretreatment is allowed to proceed for a specific duration, which has been found to be a crucial parameter for maximizing the yield and quality of the desired products. For a standard 1:1 molar ratio of triethylamine to sulfuric acid in the ionic liquid, the optimal pretreatment time is approximately 8 hours. At this point, the process achieves a peak in enzymatic saccharification yield, with up to 77% of the theoretical glucose content of the original biomass being released. This high saccharification yield is a direct result of the effective removal of lignin and hemicellulose, which exposes the underlying cellulose fibrils and eliminates steric hindrance and non-productive binding sites for cellulolytic enzymes.

The outcome of this pretreatment step is a clean separation of the biomass into three primary fractions. The first is a solid, cellulose-rich pulp that contains a high percentage of the original glucan (typically 85-95%) with minimal residual lignin (around 20%) and very little hemicellulose. This pulp is an ideal intermediate for downstream processes, whether for the production of fermentable sugars for biofuels or biochemicals, or as a source of high-purity cellulose for material applications. The second fraction is a precipitated lignin, which is recovered by diluting the spent ionic liquid liquor with an anti-solvent, typically water. This lignin is of high purity and exhibits a modified chemical structure compared to native lignin, characterized by a significant cleavage of its native β-O-4 ether linkages (up to 80% after 24 hours) and a degree of condensation, as evidenced by an increase in condensed guaiacyl and syringyl units. These structural changes can be tuned by the pretreatment conditions and may be advantageous for certain lignin valorization pathways, such as the production of aromatic monomers or carbon materials. The third fraction is the recovered ionic liquid solution, which contains dissolved hemicellulose-derived sugars (primarily xylose and arabinose), their dehydration products (notably furfural), and other minor solutes like acetic acid.

A key innovation of the present invention is the demonstration of the robust recyclability of the [TEA][HSO₄] solvent system. In stark contrast to many other pretreatment solvents, the ionic liquid can be recovered from the process stream with exceptional efficiency—greater than 99% per cycle—and reused for multiple consecutive pretreatment runs without a loss in performance. In fact, the data shows that upon recycling, the saccharification yields of the resulting pulp can even improve, reaching levels of 71-72% after the third and fourth uses. This counterintuitive result is attributed to a subtle but important change in the solvent's chemistry during its initial use. The fresh ionic liquid batch often contains a slight excess of free sulfuric acid, which, while accelerating the initial fractionation, also promotes unwanted side reactions like the re-deposition of condensed lignin or pseudo-lignin onto the cellulose surface, thereby inhibiting enzyme access. During the first recycle, this excess acid is consumed or neutralized by organic acids (e.g., acetic acid, p-coumaric acid) released from the biomass, leading to a more optimal and stable acidity for subsequent cycles. This self-regulating property of the solvent system is a major advantage for industrial implementation, as it simplifies process control and enhances long-term stability.

Furthermore, the recycled ionic liquid exhibits a "self-cleaning" behavior with respect to lignin. While a small amount of lignin fragments may remain dissolved in the liquor after the precipitation step, these fragments do not accumulate over successive cycles. Instead, they appear to participate in condensation reactions with freshly extracted lignin during the next pretreatment cycle, leading to the formation of higher molecular weight polymers that are then efficiently precipitated out. This mechanism prevents the build-up of inhibitory compounds in the solvent and explains the observation that the cumulative lignin yield over four cycles approached 100%, even exceeding the theoretical lignin content of the feedstock. This apparent excess is attributed to the incorporation of carbohydrate-derived degradation products, known as pseudo-lignin, into the lignin precipitate. While this phenomenon requires further study, it demonstrates the dynamic and integrated nature of the chemistry occurring within the ionic liquid medium.

The economic viability of the process is underpinned by the remarkably low cost of the [TEA][HSO₄] solvent. Bulk-scale synthesis estimates place its cost at approximately $1.24 per kilogram, which is on par with common organic solvents like acetone and toluene. When combined with the high recovery rate (>99%), the effective solvent cost per kilogram of processed biomass becomes negligible, estimated at less than $0.01 per kg of biomass at a 99.9% recovery rate. This stands in sharp contrast to the economics of processes using more expensive ionic liquids, where solvent cost can dominate the operating expenses. A preliminary techno-economic analysis comparing the ionoSolv process to a benchmark dilute acid pretreatment revealed a 40% reduction in capital expenditure and a 30% reduction in operating expenditure, primarily due to lower energy requirements for heating the solvent and the elimination of high-pressure equipment. The estimated cost of glucose production was calculated to be $0.19 per kg, which is competitive with the market price of raw sucrose. Critically, this favorable economics is achieved without the need to valorize the lignin or hemicellulose co-products, although their potential value would provide a significant additional revenue stream.

The process of the invention is also environmentally benign in several key aspects. It operates at atmospheric pressure, eliminating the safety hazards and capital costs associated with high-pressure reactors. The low volatility of the ionic liquid minimizes fugitive emissions and solvent loss to the atmosphere. Moreover, the process generates significantly less wastewater compared to aqueous-based pretreatments, as the primary wash streams (e.g., ethanol used to wash the pulp) can be easily recovered and recycled due to their immiscibility with the ionic liquid phase. The ability to use low-grade waste heat for the pretreatment step further enhances its sustainability profile. In summary, the invention provides a complete, integrated, and economically viable platform for the deconstruction of lignocellulosic biomass. By leveraging the unique properties of a low-cost, recyclable protic ionic liquid, it enables the efficient production of high-quality cellulose pulp for sugar production, a valuable lignin stream for materials or chemicals, and a process solvent that can be used repeatedly with minimal makeup, thereby establishing a new paradigm for sustainable and profitable biorefining.
