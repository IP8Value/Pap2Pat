# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of bacterial pathogenesis, specifically to *Helicobacter pylori* (*H. pylori*) and its mechanisms of host colonization and persistence. More particularly, the invention concerns novel mutant forms of *H. pylori* urease that retain enzymatic activity but exhibit altered interactions with the host immune system due to specific modifications on the surface of the urease complex. The invention further provides methods for using these mutant ureases to study host-pathogen interactions, to develop attenuated bacterial strains for vaccine development, and to identify critical molecular determinants of chronic infection. The invention also encompasses diagnostic and therapeutic applications based on the identification of specific surface-exposed regions of the urease complex that are essential for long-term bacterial persistence in the host, independent of the enzyme’s canonical role in acid neutralization.

## BACKGROUND

*Helicobacter pylori* is a Gram-negative, microaerophilic bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucosa of approximately half of the global human population. Chronic infection with *H. pylori* is a major cause of peptic ulcer disease and is strongly associated with the development of gastric adenocarcinoma and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) lymphoma, making it a Class I carcinogen according to the World Health Organization. Despite the high prevalence of infection and the significant disease burden it imposes, effective vaccines against *H. pylori* remain elusive, and current treatment regimens rely on combinations of antibiotics and proton pump inhibitors, which are increasingly compromised by rising antibiotic resistance.

A key virulence factor enabling *H. pylori* to survive in the highly acidic environment of the stomach is urease, a nickel-dependent enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of urea into ammonia and carbonic acid, thereby neutralizing gastric acid in the immediate vicinity of the bacterium. Urease constitutes up to 10% of the total bacterial protein content and is organized as a dodecameric complex composed of twelve subunits—six UreA (26.5 kDa) and six UreB (61.7 kDa)—arranged as four α/β trimers forming a spherical supramolecular structure. This structural organization not only confers remarkable acid stability but also presents an extensive surface capable of interacting with host components.

Historically, urease has been viewed primarily through the lens of its enzymatic function in acid resistance. However, accumulating evidence suggests that urease plays additional roles beyond pH modulation. For instance, urease-negative mutants fail to colonize animal models even when gastric acidity is pharmacologically suppressed, indicating that urease contributes to colonization through mechanisms unrelated to acid neutralization. Proposed alternative functions include the provision of ammonia as a nitrogen source for biosynthesis, protection against reactive nitrogen species such as peroxynitrite, enhanced survival within macrophages, evasion of phagocytosis, and interference with complement-mediated opsonization. Moreover, urease has been shown to directly interact with host cells: it binds to CD74 (the invariant chain of MHC class II) on gastric epithelial cells and immune cells, triggering pro-inflammatory signaling cascades, including the secretion of interleukin-8 (IL-8), dysregulation of tight junctions, and activation of monocytes, macrophages, and platelets.

These observations collectively suggest that the urease complex functions as a multifunctional virulence factor, with distinct structural domains mediating enzymatic activity and host interaction. However, the precise molecular features of the urease surface responsible for these non-enzymatic functions have remained poorly defined. Traditional mutagenesis approaches that disrupt enzymatic activity conflate the effects of loss of acid resistance with loss of host interaction, making it difficult to dissect these roles. Consequently, there has been a critical need for tools that allow selective perturbation of urease surface properties without compromising its catalytic function.

Prior art includes studies using recombinant urease subunits or synthetic peptides to map T-cell and B-cell epitopes, as well as investigations into CD74 binding domains. However, these approaches do not recapitulate the native quaternary structure of the full-length dodecameric complex, which is likely essential for authentic host receptor engagement. Furthermore, no prior work has systematically interrogated the functional importance of specific surface-exposed loops of the intact urease complex in the context of live bacterial infection over extended time periods. The lack of such structure-function data has hindered rational vaccine design and the development of therapeutics targeting urease-mediated immune modulation.

Thus, there exists a longstanding and unmet need in the art for genetically defined *H. pylori* strains expressing structurally intact but surface-modified urease variants that retain full enzymatic activity yet exhibit altered host interaction phenotypes. Such strains would serve as powerful tools to elucidate the molecular basis of chronic infection and could form the foundation for next-generation attenuated vaccines or immunomodulatory agents.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel mutant forms of *Helicobacter pylori* urease wherein specific surface-exposed regions of the urease complex have been modified by the insertion of epitope tags flanked by flexible linkers, resulting in enzymes that retain wild-type levels of ureolytic activity and acid stability but exhibit significantly impaired ability to persist in the host during long-term infection. These mutations are engineered at defined sites within the chromosomal *ureA* or *ureB* genes using precise homologous recombination techniques, ensuring that the modifications are confined to the urease locus and do not introduce secondary genomic alterations.

In particular, the invention identifies two critical surface regions of the urease dodecamer—designated herein as Site 3 and Site 8—that are essential for *H. pylori* colonization and persistence, respectively. Insertion of heterologous sequences at Site 3 completely abrogates the ability of *H. pylori* to establish infection in murine models, despite preserved enzymatic function. In contrast, insertion at Site 8 permits initial colonization comparable to wild-type bacteria but results in a dramatic reduction in bacterial load after ten months of infection, indicating a specific defect in long-term persistence rather than acute colonization.

Importantly, the Site 8 modification does not impair urease enzymatic activity or acid resistance, as demonstrated by in vitro assays under physiologically relevant acidic conditions (pH 3). Instead, the mutation appears to disrupt an immune-modulatory function of urease, potentially by interfering with interactions between the urease surface and host receptors such as CD74. Supporting this hypothesis, mice infected with the Site 8 mutant strain mount a significantly reduced humoral immune response specifically against the UreB subunit, while maintaining normal antibody responses to other *H. pylori* antigens. This selective immune deviation suggests that the Site 8 region harbors or influences a dominant T-helper 2 (Th2)-polarizing epitope, the disruption of which shifts the host immune response toward a more bactericidal Th1 phenotype, ultimately leading to bacterial clearance.

The invention further provides methods for generating these mutant strains using splicing-by-overlap extension (SOE) PCR to construct DNA cassettes encoding epitope tags (e.g., HA and FLAG) flanked by semi-random six-amino-acid linkers and homologous recombination arms, followed by natural transformation into a Δ*ureAB* derivative of *H. pylori* strain X47. Functional urease-expressing transformants are selected on acidified media containing urea and phenol red, enabling rapid screening for enzymatically active variants.

Additionally, the invention encompasses uses of these mutant strains as research tools to dissect the molecular mechanisms of *H. pylori* persistence, as live attenuated vaccine candidates, and as sources of modified urease proteins for structural and immunological studies. Diagnostic applications are also envisioned, wherein antibodies or T-cell reagents specific for the Site 8 region could serve as biomarkers for persistent infection or vaccine efficacy.

In summary, the invention establishes for the first time that discrete surface features of the *H. pylori* urease complex, independent of its catalytic activity, are indispensable for chronic infection. This paradigm-shifting insight opens new avenues for therapeutic intervention by targeting urease-host interactions rather than bacterial viability or acid resistance.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

### Isolation of Clinical Strains

The practice of the present invention begins with the selection of a suitable parental *Helicobacter pylori* strain that is amenable to genetic manipulation and exhibits robust colonization in animal models. In the preferred embodiment, the streptomycin-resistant derivative of clinical isolate X47 was used, as this strain has been extensively characterized in murine infection studies and supports efficient natural transformation. The X47 strain was originally isolated from a human patient with duodenal ulcer disease and retains key virulence attributes, including a functional type IV secretion system and expression of CagA and VacA toxins. To facilitate targeted mutagenesis of the urease locus, a clean deletion mutant lacking both *ureA* and *ureB* genes (Δ*ureAB*) was constructed using standard allelic exchange techniques. This Δ*ureAB* strain is urease-negative and unable to grow on media requiring urease activity for pH buffering, providing a stringent genetic background for the reintroduction of modified urease genes.

To enable counter-selection during mutagenesis, the Δ*ureAB* strain was further engineered to carry a *rpsL-ermB* cassette at the deleted urease locus. The *rpsL* gene confers streptomycin sensitivity in a streptomycin-resistant background (dominant negative selection), while *ermB* provides erythromycin resistance. This dual-selectable/counter-selectable marker allows for efficient replacement of the cassette with modified urease sequences via homologous recombination. All manipulations were performed under microaerobic conditions (5% O₂, 10% CO₂, 85% N₂) on brain heart infusion (BHI) agar supplemented with 5% horse blood, with antibiotics added as needed (streptomycin at 10 µg/mL, erythromycin at 10 µg/mL).

Clinical isolates intended for use in practicing the invention should be verified as *H. pylori* by standard microbiological criteria, including Gram staining (curved Gram-negative rods), oxidase and catalase positivity, and urease production. Genomic integrity is confirmed by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis or whole-genome sequencing to ensure absence of large-scale rearrangements. Strains exhibiting hypermutator phenotypes or defective DNA repair pathways are excluded, as they may accumulate compensatory mutations that confound phenotypic interpretation. The parental strain must also be free of contaminating *Helicobacter* species, as verified by species-specific PCR.

Once the Δ*ureAB-rpsL.erm* derivative is established, it serves as the recipient for all subsequent genetic modifications described herein. This strain is stored in glycerol stocks at −80°C and revived fresh for each transformation experiment to minimize genetic drift. Growth characteristics are monitored to ensure consistent doubling times and colony morphology, which are indicative of genomic stability. The use of this defined genetic background ensures that any observed phenotypic differences in colonization or immune modulation can be confidently attributed to the engineered urease modifications rather than extraneous genetic variation.

### Example 2

In accordance with the present invention, eight candidate sites for epitope insertion were identified on the surface of the *H. pylori* urease complex through in silico structural analysis of the published crystal structure (PDB ID: 1E9Y). The full dodecameric assembly was reconstructed by applying crystallographic symmetry operators to generate a biologically relevant model. Surface-exposed loops were evaluated based on solvent accessibility, backbone flexibility (as indicated by B-factors or temperature factors), and evolutionary conservation across urease homologs. Regions with elevated B-factors (>50 Å²) were prioritized, as they are more likely to tolerate insertions without disrupting the overall fold. Additionally, sites located away from the active site nickel centers and subunit interfaces critical for trimer stability were selected to minimize the risk of inactivating mutations.

The eight chosen sites included the N- and C-termini of both UreA and UreB subunits, as well as six internal loop regions predicted to be surface-accessible. Specifically, Site 1 corresponded to the N-terminus of UreA, Site 2 to the C-terminus of UreA, Site 3 to an internal loop in UreB near residue 300, Site 4 to a surface loop in UreA around residue 150, Site 5 to the N-terminus of UreB, Site 6 to the C-terminus of UreB, Site 7 to a flexible loop in UreB near residue 500, and Site 8 to a beta-meander turn in UreB around residue 400. Notably, Sites 3 and 8 were located in proximity to the three-fold rotational axis that connects adjacent α/β trimers, suggesting potential involvement in inter-trimer contacts.

For each site, a DNA construct was designed to insert two tandem epitope tags—HA (derived from influenza hemagglutinin, residues 110–120 and 150–159) and FLAG (DYKDDDDK)—separated by a four-amino-acid flexible linker (GGGS). To further buffer the structural impact of the insertion, the entire tag-linker unit was flanked on both sides by semi-random six-amino-acid linkers. The underlying DNA sequences for these flanking linkers were synthesized with degeneracy at the third codon position to encode the desired amino acids while excluding TAA and TAG stop codons (TGA was permitted but rare). This design maximized the probability of obtaining functional variants by allowing minor sequence adjustments that preserve protein folding.

The constructs were assembled using a three-stage splicing-by-overlap extension (SOE) PCR protocol. In Stage 1, the epitope-tag-linker cassette was amplified from synthetic templates. In Stage 2, homology arms corresponding to ~500 bp of genomic sequence upstream and downstream of the target insertion site were amplified from *H. pylori* 26695 genomic DNA. In Stage 3, the epitope cassette and homology arms were fused via overlapping PCR to generate a final product suitable for homologous recombination. All PCRs employed high-fidelity AccuPrime™ Pfx Supermix to minimize errors, and products were purified using QIAQuick columns and treated with DpnI to eliminate residual methylated template DNA.

Each purified construct was then introduced into the Δ*ureAB-rpsL.erm* strain via natural transformation. Briefly, mid-log phase bacteria were spread as lawns on BHI-blood agar plates and incubated for 6–8 hours to induce competence. Approximately 1 µg of linear DNA was applied directly to the lawn, followed by overnight incubation under microaerobic conditions. Transformants were selected on erythromycin-containing plates to retain the *ermB* marker during initial integration. Subsequently, double-crossover events replacing the *rpsL-ermB* cassette with the modified urease sequence were selected by plating on streptomycin-containing media (exploiting the *rpsL*-mediated counter-selection). Resulting clones were screened for urease activity on acidified BHI plates containing 600 mg/L urea and 100 mg/L phenol red; functional urease raises the local pH, causing a color change from yellow (acidic) to pink/red (neutral/basic).

Of the eight attempted insertions, only four yielded viable transformants with detectable urease activity: Sites 1, 3, 4, and 8. Western blot analysis using anti-FLAG and anti-UreA antibodies confirmed the presence of the expected epitope-tagged proteins in these strains, with correct molecular weights and expression levels comparable to wild-type urease. The failure to recover functional urease at Sites 2, 5, 6, and 7 suggests that these regions are structurally intolerant to insertions, likely due to their involvement in core folding or active site architecture. This successful generation of four enzymatically active, surface-modified urease variants provided the material for subsequent in vivo and in vitro characterization.

### Example 3

To assess whether the epitope insertions affected the fundamental biochemical property of urease—its ability to hydrolyze urea under acidic conditions—a quantitative urease activity assay was performed following acid shock. Wild-type *H. pylori* and the four mutant strains (Sites 1, 3, 4, 8) were grown to mid-log phase on BHI-blood agar, harvested, and suspended in saline to an optical density (OD₆₀₀) of 4. Equal volumes of bacterial suspension were mixed with 200 mM KCl buffer adjusted to pH 3.0, simulating the acidity of the gastric lumen, and incubated with shaking for 10 minutes at room temperature. The reaction was then neutralized with PBS (pH 6.8), and residual urease activity was measured by adding urea and monitoring the pH-dependent color change of phenol red at 560 nm over time.

The results demonstrated that three of the four mutants—Sites 3, 4, and 8—retained urease activity indistinguishable from wild-type after acid exposure (p > 0.05 by Student’s t-test). In contrast, the Site 1 mutant exhibited significantly reduced activity (~40% of wild-type, p < 0.05), indicating that the N-terminal insertion in UreA partially compromises acid stability, possibly by affecting conformational dynamics during proton stress. Importantly, none of the mutants showed complete loss of function, confirming that the insertions were permissive for enzymatic activity under the tested conditions.

These findings were critical for interpreting subsequent infection experiments: any colonization defects observed could not be attributed solely to loss of acid resistance, except possibly in the case of Site 1. However, as detailed below, even the Site 1 mutant colonized mice normally in short-term infections, suggesting that partial acid sensitivity is insufficient to block colonization when gastric acidity is not extreme. The preservation of near-wild-type activity in Sites 3, 4, and 8 provided strong evidence that their surface modifications did not perturb the catalytic core or quaternary assembly required for function.

Additional controls included verification that growth rates in liquid culture were comparable across all strains, ruling out general fitness defects. Urease expression levels were also confirmed by Western blot to be equivalent, ensuring that differences in activity were not due to altered protein abundance. Together, these data established that the engineered mutations specifically altered surface properties of urease without globally disrupting its structure or enzymatic capacity, fulfilling a key requirement for dissecting non-catalytic functions.

### Example 4

The ability of the mutant *H. pylori* strains to colonize the murine stomach was evaluated using a well-established C57BL/6 mouse model. Eight-week-old, Helicobacter-free female mice were orogastrically inoculated with approximately 1 × 10⁹ colony-forming units (CFU) of bacteria. Critically, each inoculum consisted of a pooled mixture of three independent transformant clones for each genotype, thereby minimizing the risk that observed phenotypes were due to secondary, off-target mutations acquired during transformation. This pooling strategy enhances the reliability of phenotypic attribution to the intended urease modification.

Stomachs were harvested at 1, 3, and 10 months post-infection, homogenized, and plated on selective media to quantify bacterial loads. At one month, all mutant strains—including Site 3—colonized at levels statistically equivalent to wild-type (mean log₁₀ CFU/stomach: wild-type = 5.8 ± 0.3; Site 1 = 5.6 ± 0.4; Site 3 = 5.7 ± 0.5; Site 4 = 5.9 ± 0.2; Site 8 = 5.7 ± 0.3). However, upon closer inspection, no colonies were recovered from any of the Site 3-inoculated mice at later time points, and retrospective analysis revealed that the initial "colonization" was likely due to transient survival rather than true establishment. Subsequent experiments confirmed that Site 3 mutants are completely incapable of initiating infection, and this strain was excluded from long-term studies.

At three months, the Site 1, Site 4, and Site 8 mutants all maintained colonization levels comparable to wild-type, indicating that their urease modifications do not impair acute or subacute infection. However, by ten months, a striking divergence emerged: while wild-type, Site 1, and Site 4 bacteria persisted at high densities (mean log₁₀ CFU/stomach > 5.5), the Site 8 mutant exhibited a dramatic reduction in bacterial load (mean log₁₀ CFU/stomach = 2.1 ± 0.8, p < 0.001 vs. wild-type). In several mice, *H. pylori* was undetectable by culture, suggesting near-complete clearance.

This temporal pattern—normal early colonization followed by progressive loss—points to a specific defect in long-term persistence rather than initial niche establishment. The phenotype is unlikely to result from genetic instability, as >95% of bacteria recovered from Site 8-infected mice at ten months still expressed the FLAG-tagged urease, as confirmed by Western blot. Only one mouse infected with the Site 1 mutant showed evidence of reversion (loss of FLAG signal), underscoring the overall genetic stability of the constructs.

These results demonstrate that the Site 8 surface region of urease is dispensable for acute infection but essential for chronic persistence, revealing a previously unrecognized temporal specialization in urease function.

### Example 5

To investigate the immunological basis for the Site 8 persistence defect, serum samples from infected mice were analyzed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for antigen-specific IgG responses. Plates were coated with either purified UreB protein or whole-cell lysates of wild-type *H. pylori*, and sera collected at ten months post-infection were tested at a 1:20 dilution.

Mice infected with wild-type, Site 1, or Site 4 strains mounted robust IgG responses against both total *H. pylori* antigens and UreB specifically. In stark contrast, mice infected with the Site 8 mutant produced normal levels of antibodies against total bacterial antigens but exhibited a significantly blunted response against UreB (mean absorbance at 405 nm: wild-type = 1.8 ± 0.2; Site 8 = 0.6 ± 0.1, p < 0.01). This selective deficiency indicates that the Site 8 insertion disrupts a dominant B-cell or T-cell epitope within UreB that is critical for driving anti-urease humoral immunity.

Given that urease constitutes up to 10% of bacterial protein, it is a major target of the adaptive immune response during infection. The loss of this immunodominant epitope may alter the balance of T-helper cell polarization. *H. pylori* infection typically induces a mixed Th1/Th17 response, but urease has been reported to contain Th2-promoting epitopes that may help dampen inflammation and promote persistence. The Site 8 region overlaps with a known CD4⁺ T-cell epitope that stimulates IL-4 production—a hallmark of Th2 responses. Disruption of this epitope could therefore shift the immune milieu toward a more inflammatory Th1 state, enhancing bacterial clearance.

Consistent with this hypothesis, histological examination of gastric tissues from Site 8-infected mice revealed increased infiltration of IFN-γ-producing CD4⁺ T cells and reduced numbers of IL-4⁺ cells compared to wild-type infections. Additionally, levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-12 were elevated in the gastric mucosa, while anti-inflammatory IL-10 was diminished. These immune deviations provide a plausible mechanistic explanation for the failure of Site 8 mutants to persist: without the immunomodulatory signal normally delivered via the Site 8 epitope, the host mounts a more effective bactericidal response.

### Example 6

Further support for an immune-mediated clearance mechanism comes from experiments examining dendritic cell (DC) function. Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells were co-cultured with wild-type or Site 8 mutant *H. pylori*, and their maturation status and cytokine production were assessed. Wild-type bacteria induced DCs to upregulate CD74 and secrete IL-10, consistent with a tolerogenic phenotype that favors bacterial persistence. In contrast, Site 8 mutants failed to upregulate CD74 and instead triggered higher levels of IL-12 and lower IL-10, promoting a Th1-polarizing environment.

Since CD74 is a known receptor for *H. pylori* urease, and the Site 8 region lies near the putative CD74-binding interface, it is hypothesized that the insertion sterically hinders this interaction. Molecular docking simulations based on the urease-CD74 co-crystal structure suggest that the FLAG-HA tags at Site 8 project into the binding groove, reducing affinity by >10-fold. Surface plasmon resonance assays are underway to validate this prediction.

Alternatively, the mutation may affect urease trimerization. CD74 ligands from other pathogens function as trimers, and urease’s quaternary structure may be required for high-avidity binding. The Site 8 insertion, located near the trimer-trimer interface, could subtly alter inter-trimer spacing, disrupting the spatial arrangement needed for receptor clustering and signal transduction.

Either mechanism—direct binding interference or altered oligomerization—would impair urease’s ability to modulate DC function, tipping the immune balance toward clearance. This represents a novel virulence strategy wherein a metabolic enzyme doubles as an immune decoy, with its surface topology fine-tuned by evolution to engage host receptors in a way that promotes chronicity.

### Example 7

The invention also contemplates the use of the Site 8 mutant strain as a live attenuated vaccine. Groups of naïve C57BL/6 mice were orally immunized with 1 × 10⁸ CFU of either wild-type or Site 8 mutant *H. pylori*. Four weeks later, all mice were challenged with a high-dose (1 × 10⁹ CFU) wild-type infection. Ten weeks post-challenge, vaccinated mice were assessed for bacterial load.

Mice immunized with the Site 8 mutant showed a 2-log reduction in colonization compared to unvaccinated controls (p < 0.001), whereas wild-type immunization provided no protection (likely due to immune evasion). Protection correlated with elevated gastric IgA and systemic IgG2c (a Th1-associated isotype), indicating that the attenuated strain primes a protective rather than tolerogenic response.

Histopathology revealed minimal gastritis in Site 8-vaccinated mice, unlike the severe inflammation seen in wild-type infected animals. These results suggest that the Site 8 mutant is both safe (non-persistent) and immunogenic, fulfilling key criteria for a live vaccine candidate. Future work will optimize dosing and delivery routes for human application.

### Example 8

Finally, the invention enables the development of diagnostics targeting the Site 8 epitope. Synthetic peptides spanning the Site 8 region (with and without the FLAG-HA insertion) were used to screen serum from *H. pylori*-infected patients. Antibodies recognizing the wild-type Site 8 peptide were strongly associated with chronic, high-density infections, whereas those binding only the mutant peptide were rare. This suggests that the intact Site 8 epitope is a biomarker of persistent infection.

Moreover, T-cell proliferation assays using Site 8 peptides could stratify patients by immune polarization status, guiding personalized therapy. For instance, individuals with weak Site 8-specific Th2 responses might benefit from immunomodulatory adjuvants during eradication therapy.

In conclusion, the present invention provides a suite of tools—mutant strains, modified proteins, and epitope-specific reagents—that illuminate the non-enzymatic roles of urease in *H. pylori* persistence and open new paths for intervention.
