# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to electric machines, and more particularly to high-power-density permanent magnet synchronous machines (PMSMs) designed for use in electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft propulsion systems. Specifically, the invention provides an integrated magnetics, insulation, and cooling architecture—hereinafter referred to as MAGICA—that enhances thermal management, improves electrical insulation integrity, and maintains or minimally compromises electromagnetic performance in slotless PMSM outrunner configurations. The invention is especially suited for applications where transient high-power demands, such as those encountered during takeoff and landing phases of eVTOL flight, impose severe thermal stresses on motor windings due to elevated copper losses. By embedding thermally conductive pathways directly within the magnetic core structure and by incorporating advanced ceramic materials as structural and insulative coil holders, the invention achieves a significant reduction in thermal impedance between the heat-generating windings and the external heatsink, thereby enabling higher sustained power output, improved reliability, and extended operational life under demanding duty cycles.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Electric propulsion systems for aircraft, particularly eVTOL platforms, place extraordinary demands on electric motor design. Unlike conventional ground-based or industrial electric drives that operate near steady-state conditions, eVTOL propulsion motors must deliver peak power levels during critical flight phases—namely takeoff and landing—that can be three to ten times greater than the power required during cruise. This transient overloading results in dramatically increased current densities within the stator windings, which in turn leads to substantial resistive (copper) losses concentrated in relatively small conductor volumes. In slotless permanent magnet synchronous machines—a topology favored in aerospace applications for its high torque density, low cogging torque, and reduced iron losses—the absence of stator teeth means that the armature windings occupy the entire circumferential space between the rotor and the stator yoke. While this configuration maximizes electrical loading and simplifies manufacturing, it also exacerbates thermal challenges because the windings are not embedded within a thermally conductive iron structure that could otherwise serve as a heat-spreading medium. Instead, heat generated in the windings must travel through air gaps or low-conductivity insulation materials before reaching the outer housing or heatsink, creating significant thermal bottlenecks.

Conventional approaches to motor cooling in such architectures often rely on external convection—either natural or forced—applied to the motor housing, or on internal fluid channels routed near the end-windings. However, these methods are inherently limited in their ability to extract heat from the central, radially inner regions of the winding bundle where losses are most intense. Moreover, the dielectric insulation surrounding the conductors, typically composed of polymer films or enamel coatings, possesses very low thermal conductivity (on the order of 0.1–0.3 W/m·K), further impeding heat flow. As temperatures rise during high-power operation, the risk of insulation degradation, partial discharge, and ultimately catastrophic failure increases exponentially. Industry standards for aerospace-grade insulation systems impose strict upper limits on allowable winding temperatures, often capping continuous operation at 180°C or below, with even tighter constraints during transient peaks. Consequently, motor designers are frequently forced to derate the machine’s power capability or incorporate oversized thermal margins, both of which compromise the power-to-weight ratio—a critical metric in aircraft design.

Prior art has attempted to address these limitations through various strategies. Some designs integrate axial or radial cooling channels directly into the stator core, but these require complex machining, introduce magnetic discontinuities, and may compromise structural integrity. Others employ thermally conductive potting compounds to fill voids between windings and housing; however, such compounds often exhibit poor long-term stability under thermal cycling, may outgas in vacuum environments, and can degrade electrical insulation properties. Slotless machines have also been constructed with solid iron yokes, but these suffer from excessive eddy current losses due to the lack of lamination, rendering them impractical for high-frequency operation typical in modern variable-speed drives. Alternative approaches using high-thermal-conductivity polymers or composites as coil formers have shown modest improvements but fall short of the performance required for aerospace applications due to their limited thermal conductivity (typically <1 W/m·K) and insufficient dielectric strength at high voltages.

Furthermore, existing thermal models for electric machines often treat the stator as a homogeneous block with averaged material properties, neglecting the intricate interplay between electromagnetic field distribution, localized loss generation, and anisotropic heat transfer paths. This oversimplification leads to inaccurate predictions of hotspot temperatures and suboptimal cooling system design. In particular, the thermal resistance between the winding center and the ambient environment is dominated by a series of poorly conductive layers—insulation, air gaps, and low-conductivity structural components—that collectively form a high-impedance barrier to heat extraction. Without a deliberate architectural intervention that introduces parallel, low-resistance thermal shunts across these barriers, the fundamental thermal limitations of high-power-density slotless machines remain unresolved.

Thus, there exists a clear and unmet need in the field for an electric machine architecture that simultaneously addresses the intertwined challenges of thermal management, electrical insulation, and electromagnetic performance in a manner that is manufacturable, reliable, and compatible with the stringent weight and volume constraints of eVTOL propulsion systems. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel integrated approach that rethinks the stator assembly not merely as a passive support structure, but as an active participant in heat extraction and electrical isolation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides an integrated magnetics, insulation, and cooling architecture—referred to herein as MAGICA—for high-power-density slotless permanent magnet synchronous machines, particularly suited for electric aircraft propulsion. The invention fundamentally reconfigures the stator assembly to embed multiple parallel thermal conduction pathways that significantly reduce the overall thermal impedance from the heat-generating windings to the external heatsink, while concurrently enhancing electrical insulation integrity and maintaining acceptable electromagnetic performance.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a stator yoke formed from a laminated stack that interleaves thin sheets of high-thermal-conductivity copper with conventional magnetic iron laminations. These copper sheets are strategically inserted at periodic intervals throughout the axial length of the yoke, creating low-resistance thermal shunts that bypass the inherently poor thermal conductivity of the iron laminations (which, despite their magnetic utility, exhibit thermal conductivity of only ~20–30 W/m·K). The copper sheets, with thermal conductivity exceeding 380 W/m·K, provide direct conduction paths from the inner winding region—where heat is generated—to the outer surface of the yoke, which is thermally coupled to a heatsink. This interleaved structure does not substantially disrupt the magnetic flux path when the copper fill factor is maintained below approximately 20%, as demonstrated by electromagnetic finite element analysis, thereby preserving torque production while dramatically improving heat extraction.

In another aspect, the invention incorporates a ceramic coil holder that surrounds and supports the form-wound Litz wire conductors of the stator windings. This coil holder is fabricated from a high-performance engineering ceramic such as aluminum nitride (AlN) or boron nitride (BN), both of which offer exceptional combinations of high thermal conductivity (70–180 W/m·K for AlN), high dielectric strength (>10 kV/mm), and mechanical rigidity. The ceramic holder is shaped to conform to the “C”-shaped cross-section of each phase coil, with thin fins extending between adjacent conductors. This geometry establishes a parallel thermal path along the side walls of the windings, allowing heat to flow laterally through the ceramic material to the stator yoke, in addition to the traditional radial path through insulation and air gaps. Crucially, the ceramic material also serves as a robust electrical barrier between phase conductors and between conductors and ground, significantly reducing the risk of partial discharge and insulation breakdown under high-voltage transients common in wide-bandgap semiconductor drives.

In a further aspect, the invention includes a thermal equivalent circuit model that quantifies the reduction in thermal resistance achieved by the combined effect of the interleaved copper yoke and the ceramic coil holder. First-order calculations based on material properties and geometric dimensions demonstrate that the baseline thermal resistance from winding center to heatsink can be reduced by up to 50%—40% from the copper interleaving alone and an additional 10% from the ceramic holder—when optimized design parameters are employed. This reduction enables higher continuous current densities, lower hotspot temperatures, and extended operational envelopes without exceeding insulation temperature limits.

The invention is embodied in a 300 kW slotless PMSM outrunner configuration featuring an outer rotor with a Halbach permanent magnet array, a shell-mounted stator assembly incorporating the MAGICA architecture, and an additively manufactured heatsink (though the heatsink optimization is considered future work). Prototype hardware has been fabricated using waterjet-cut AlN segments stacked axially to form the ceramic coil holder, and a bonded lamination stack comprising HF-10 iron laminations interleaved with Copper 110 alloy foils at a 20% copper fill factor. Preliminary experimental validation using a comparative thermal test setup confirmed faster heat spreading, more uniform temperature distribution, and lower peak temperatures in the MAGICA-equipped yoke relative to a conventional all-iron baseline.

Accordingly, the invention provides a synergistic solution that transcends incremental improvements by co-designing magnetic, thermal, and electrical subsystems into a unified architecture. It enables electric motors for eVTOL applications to operate reliably at higher power densities, with enhanced safety margins against thermal runaway and electrical failure, while remaining compatible with established manufacturing techniques and material supply chains.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention will now be described in detail with reference to specific embodiments, though it should be understood that these embodiments are provided for illustrative purposes and do not limit the scope of the invention as defined by the appended claims. The preferred embodiment is a 300 kW slotless permanent magnet synchronous machine (PMSM) configured as an outrunner, specifically engineered for electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft propulsion. This machine integrates the MAGICA architecture—comprising an interleaved copper-iron stator yoke and a ceramic coil holder—into a cohesive stator assembly that simultaneously optimizes thermal conduction, electrical insulation, and electromagnetic functionality.

The overall machine topology consists of an outer rotor assembly and an inner stator assembly. The rotor comprises a cylindrical shell supporting a Halbach array of high-energy-product permanent magnets (e.g., neodymium-iron-boron), which is mounted on a rotating shaft or hub. The Halbach configuration is selected for its ability to enhance the air-gap flux density on the stator side while suppressing flux on the back iron side, thereby improving torque density and reducing rotor losses. The stator assembly is fixed to a stationary housing and includes the laminated yoke, the ceramic coil holders, the form-wound three-phase armature windings, and an external heatsink. The air gap between rotor and stator is minimized to maximize magnetic coupling, but is carefully dimensioned to accommodate the thickness of the ceramic coil holder base without unduly increasing magnetic reluctance.

The core innovation of the invention resides in the stator yoke construction. Conventional stator yokes for slotless machines are fabricated from stacks of insulated electrical steel laminations (e.g., M19, M43, or specialized high-frequency grades like HF-10) to suppress eddy currents while providing a low-reluctance path for magnetic flux. However, these laminations possess relatively low thermal conductivity (~25 W/m·K for silicon steel), and the insulating coatings between layers further impede axial heat flow. In the present invention, the yoke is re-engineered as a hybrid lamination stack wherein thin copper sheets are periodically interleaved with the magnetic iron laminations. Specifically, for the prototype embodiment, 0.010-inch (0.254 mm) thick HF-10 C5 insulated iron laminations are alternated with 0.005-inch (0.127 mm) thick Copper 110 alloy foils. To achieve a target copper fill factor of 20%—determined through electromagnetic and thermal trade-off studies—one copper lamination is inserted for every two iron laminations along the axial direction. The laminations are bonded together using a high-temperature epoxy adhesive (e.g., EB-548) that provides mechanical integrity while maintaining electrical isolation between layers. Prior to bonding, the copper surfaces undergo a cleaning and oxidation-prevention treatment to ensure adhesion and long-term reliability.

The interleaved copper sheets serve as embedded thermal shunts. During operation, heat generated in the windings conducts radially outward through the ceramic coil holder and into the inner surface of the yoke. From there, the copper sheets provide low-resistance axial and radial conduction paths to the outer diameter of the yoke, which is in direct thermal contact with the heatsink. Because copper’s thermal conductivity is over an order of magnitude greater than that of electrical steel, even a modest volumetric fraction of copper dramatically reduces the effective thermal resistance of the yoke. Electromagnetic finite element analysis confirms that at a 20% copper fill factor, the impact on torque production is negligible (less than 1% reduction compared to a pure iron yoke), as the magnetic saturation of the iron remains within acceptable limits. Higher copper fill factors (e.g., above 30%) begin to degrade torque due to reduced cross-sectional area of magnetic material, but the 20% threshold represents an optimal balance between thermal benefit and electromagnetic penalty.

Complementing the yoke modification is the ceramic coil holder, which replaces conventional polymer bobbins or bare-wound configurations. The coil holder is fabricated from aluminum nitride (AlN), selected for its superior thermal conductivity (~170 W/m·K in-plane), high dielectric strength (>15 kV/mm), and coefficient of thermal expansion that closely matches copper, reducing thermomechanical stress during cycling. The holder is shaped as a “C”-profile that wraps around each phase coil, with a base section adjacent to the air gap and fin-like protrusions extending between individual conductor bundles. The base thickness is minimized—preferably less than 0.030 inches (0.762 mm)—to avoid excessive increase in the effective air-gap length, which would reduce magnetic coupling and torque. However, it must be thick enough to provide structural support and withstand handling loads during assembly. Similarly, the fin thickness is optimized through thermal finite element modeling to balance two competing effects: thinner fins increase the available cross-sectional area for conductors (reducing current density and copper loss), but too thin a fin fails to establish an effective parallel thermal path; thicker fins improve conduction but crowd the winding space. For the prototype, a fin thickness of 0.030 inches was selected based on simulation results showing minimal maximum temperature rise (ΔT_max) across a range of base thicknesses.

Manufacturing the ceramic coil holder presents unique challenges due to the brittleness of AlN and the complexity of the “C” geometry. The preferred method involves waterjet cutting of flat AlN plates (7 mm thick, the maximum commercially available) to produce 2D cross-sectional profiles, which are then stacked axially and bonded to achieve the full active length of the machine (e.g., 4.5 inches). Waterjet cutting is chosen over laser or mechanical machining because it induces minimal vibration and thermal stress, thereby reducing the risk of microcrack formation. The in-plane thermal conductivity of AlN is significantly higher than its through-plane conductivity, so aligning the cut planes axially maximizes the lateral heat transfer from windings to yoke. Test arrays with fin thicknesses down to 0.015 inches were successfully fabricated, but 0.030 inches was adopted for the final design to provide a safety margin against fracture during handling and thermal cycling.

The combined effect of the interleaved yoke and ceramic holder is best understood through a lumped-element thermal equivalent circuit. In this model, the total thermal resistance from the winding center to the coolant is represented as a series of resistors corresponding to insulation, ceramic, yoke, and interface layers. The baseline machine (without MAGICA) exhibits high resistance in the yoke and insulation paths. With MAGICA, the ceramic holder introduces a parallel branch that bypasses part of the insulation resistance, while the copper sheets create a low-resistance shunt across the yoke resistance. Calculations using material properties from Table II of the research paper show that the equivalent thermal resistance drops from a baseline value R_th,base to approximately 0.5 × R_th,base—a 50% reduction—when both features are implemented at their optimal parameters.

Electromagnetic performance was rigorously evaluated using 3D finite element analysis. A half-pole, half-stack model was simulated to capture axial flux variations induced by the interleaved structure. Results showed that peak flux density in the yoke increases slightly (from 1.06 T to 1.3 T) due to the reduced magnetic cross-section, leading to a moderate rise in iron losses (from 667 W to 1,095 W at rated conditions). However, since copper losses dominate in slotless machines (6,893 W vs. 667 W in the baseline), this increase is acceptable. Eddy current losses in the copper sheets themselves were found to be negligible (<2 W even at 33% fill) because the fringing flux that penetrates the copper generates only small circulating currents, well below material current density limits.

Prototype validation was conducted on a 42.5-degree pole-pair segment of the stator. Two yoke samples were built: one with the MAGICA interleaved stack and one with conventional all-iron laminations. Both were subjected to identical internal heating via a ceramic heater placed at the inner diameter, with surface temperatures monitored by an infrared thermal camera. After 8.5 minutes of heating, the MAGICA sample exhibited significantly lower hotspot temperatures and a more uniform axial temperature profile, confirming the enhanced heat-spreading capability of the embedded copper sheets.

In summary, the preferred embodiment demonstrates that the MAGICA architecture is not only theoretically sound but also practically realizable using existing manufacturing techniques. It delivers transformative thermal performance improvements without sacrificing the electromagnetic efficiency or power density that make slotless PMSMs attractive for eVTOL propulsion. Future iterations may integrate additively manufactured heatsinks with axially varying fin geometries to further optimize air-side convection, but the core innovations—the interleaved thermal shunt yoke and the multifunctional ceramic coil holder—constitute a complete and patentable advance over the prior art.
