# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The detection and diagnosis of ovarian cancer at an early stage remains one of the most significant challenges in clinical oncology. Despite advances in imaging technologies and molecular diagnostics, the majority of ovarian cancer cases are diagnosed at advanced stages (III or IV), where the five-year survival rate drops dramatically compared to early-stage disease. This late diagnosis is largely attributable to the absence of specific symptoms during the initial phases of tumor development and the limitations of currently available biomarkers. Among these, CA-125 (cancer antigen 125) stands as the most widely used serum biomarker for ovarian cancer; however, its utility for early detection is severely constrained. Clinical studies have demonstrated that CA-125 levels are elevated in only approximately 50% of patients with stage I ovarian cancer, rendering it insufficiently sensitive for population-based screening or early intervention strategies. Moreover, CA-125 can be elevated in a variety of benign gynecological and non-gynecological conditions, including endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and even menstruation, thereby compromising its specificity. Consequently, there exists an urgent and unmet medical need for more reliable, sensitive, and specific diagnostic tools capable of identifying ovarian cancer at its earliest and most treatable stages.

Protein glycosylation—the enzymatic attachment of complex carbohydrate structures known as glycans to proteins—represents a critical post-translational modification that profoundly influences protein folding, stability, trafficking, and biological activity. Glycans are particularly abundant on secreted and membrane-bound proteins, which constitute a major fraction of the human serum proteome. Among the various types of glycosylation, N-linked glycosylation, wherein glycans are covalently attached to the amide nitrogen of asparagine residues within the consensus sequence Asn-X-Ser/Thr (where X is any amino acid except proline), is one of the most prevalent and biologically significant forms. The biosynthesis of N-glycans occurs in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus through the sequential action of over 200 glycosyltransferases, resulting in a highly diverse repertoire of glycan structures whose composition and abundance are tightly regulated under physiological conditions. However, this regulation is frequently disrupted in pathological states, particularly in cancer. Tumor cells exhibit profound alterations in their glycosylation machinery, leading to the production of aberrant glycoforms that can be shed into the circulation and detected in bodily fluids such as blood serum. These cancer-associated glycan alterations include increased branching of N-glycan antennae, elevated levels of fucosylation and sialylation, and the appearance of truncated or immature glycan structures. Such changes are not merely epiphenomena but are functionally implicated in key oncogenic processes, including enhanced cell proliferation, evasion of immune surveillance, promotion of angiogenesis, and facilitation of metastasis.

Given the intimate link between glycosylation anomalies and carcinogenesis, the serum glycome—the complete set of glycans present in serum—has emerged as a rich source of potential biomarkers for cancer detection and monitoring. Unlike the proteome, which comprises tens of thousands of distinct proteins, the theoretical diversity of human N-glycans is estimated to be around 1,600 structures, with fewer than 200 commonly observed in human serum. This relative simplicity renders glycomic profiling a more tractable analytical challenge compared to proteomic approaches, while still providing sufficient molecular complexity to capture disease-specific signatures. Furthermore, because glycosylation is a downstream event influenced by the coordinated activity of multiple enzymes, glycan profiles may integrate signals from various dysregulated pathways, offering a systems-level view of the disease state that is less susceptible to the noise inherent in single-protein measurements.

Mass spectrometry (MS), particularly matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) MS, has become a cornerstone technology in glycomics due to its high sensitivity, speed, and ability to analyze complex mixtures without the need for extensive derivatization. When coupled with robust sample preparation protocols, MALDI-TOF MS enables the direct profiling of native glycans released from serum proteins, preserving their natural structural features and quantitative relationships. A key advancement in this field has been the adoption of solid-phase extraction (SPE) using graphitized carbon cartridges (GCC), which effectively isolates glycans from interfering substances such as salts, lipids, and residual proteins following enzymatic release with peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase F). Importantly, the use of stepwise elution with increasing concentrations of acetonitrile in aqueous solution allows for the differential recovery of glycan subpopulations based on their hydrophobicity, thereby enhancing the detection of low-abundance species that might otherwise be masked by dominant glycan signals. This improved dynamic range is critical for biomarker discovery, as disease-relevant glycans are often present at trace levels.

Despite these technological advances, the translation of glycomic findings into clinically actionable diagnostics has been hindered by several factors. First, many prior studies have focused exclusively on annotated glycan structures, potentially overlooking diagnostically valuable signals derived from non-glycan molecules or uncharacterized glycoconjugates that co-purify with N-glycans. Second, the reliance on single biomarkers, whether glycan-based or protein-based, has proven inadequate for complex diseases like ovarian cancer, which exhibit considerable heterogeneity across patients. Third, insufficient attention has been paid to rigorous statistical validation, including the use of independent blind test sets to assess predictive performance and mitigate the risk of overfitting. Finally, existing classification algorithms often fail to account for the varying discriminatory power of individual biomarkers, treating all features equally regardless of their actual contribution to diagnostic accuracy.

In light of these limitations, there is a compelling rationale for developing a multivariate diagnostic platform that leverages the full information content of serum glycomic profiles—including both annotated glycans and reproducible, differentially expressed mass spectral features of unknown identity—to achieve superior sensitivity and specificity in ovarian cancer detection. Such a platform must be grounded in a standardized, reproducible analytical workflow, employ statistically sound methods for biomarker selection, and incorporate a weighted scoring system that reflects the relative importance of each component in the final diagnostic decision. The present invention addresses these needs by providing a novel method for the diagnosis of ovarian cancer based on MALDI-TOF MS analysis of serum-derived N-glycans and associated biomolecules, combined with a sophisticated classification algorithm that significantly outperforms conventional CA-125 testing in both training and independent validation cohorts.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly effective method for the diagnosis and screening of ovarian cancer through the integrated analysis of serum glycomic profiles obtained via MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on single protein biomarkers such as CA-125, the invention utilizes a multibiomarker panel comprising fifteen distinct mass spectral features—derived from both annotated N-linked glycans and reproducibly detected, differentially expressed ion peaks of undetermined molecular identity—that collectively enable robust discrimination between ovarian cancer patients and healthy individuals. The method encompasses a standardized protocol for the isolation and purification of N-glycans from human serum, followed by high-resolution MALDI-TOF MS analysis under controlled conditions to generate quantitative spectral data. Critically, the invention includes a proprietary scoring algorithm that assigns weighted contributions to each biomarker based on its individual diagnostic efficacy, as measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), thereby optimizing overall classification performance. This approach yields significantly higher sensitivity and specificity compared to CA-125 alone, particularly in the detection of early-stage ovarian cancer and recurrent disease, and demonstrates consistent performance across independent validation sets.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for diagnosing ovarian cancer in a human subject, comprising: (a) obtaining a serum sample from the subject; (b) releasing N-linked glycans from glycoproteins in the serum sample by enzymatic treatment with peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase F); (c) purifying the released glycans using solid-phase extraction on graphitized carbon cartridges with sequential elution in 10%, 20%, and optionally 40% acetonitrile/water solutions containing trifluoroacetic acid; (d) analyzing the 10% and 20% acetonitrile fractions by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry in positive-ion reflectron mode over an m/z range of 1000 to 3000 to obtain mass spectra; (e) quantifying the intensities of fifteen predetermined ion peaks at specific m/z values corresponding to the multibiomarker panel; (f) normalizing the absolute peak intensities to generate normalized absolute peak intensities (NAPIs); (g) comparing each NAPI to a predetermined cut-off value derived from a training cohort to assign a binary score (+1 or −1) for each biomarker; (h) weighting each binary score by the AUC of the corresponding biomarker as determined in the training cohort; and (i) summing the weighted scores to produce a total diagnostic score, wherein a total score above a predetermined threshold indicates a diagnosis of ovarian cancer.

In another aspect, the invention provides a diagnostic kit comprising reagents and instructions for performing the aforementioned method, including PNGase F enzyme, graphitized carbon cartridges, MALDI matrix (e.g., 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid), calibration standards, and software for spectral analysis and score calculation. The kit may further include reference spectra and validated cut-off values for the fifteen biomarkers.

The fifteen biomarkers of the panel consist of five ion peaks from the 10% acetonitrile fraction and ten ion peaks from the 20% acetonitrile fraction, selected based on stringent statistical criteria: each exhibits a P-value less than 10⁻⁹ in ANOVA comparing ovarian cancer patients and healthy controls in a training set, is detected in at least 95% of samples, and demonstrates an AUC greater than 0.72 (for 10% fraction peaks) or 0.75 (for 20% fraction peaks). Notably, while ten of the fifteen peaks correspond to known N-glycan compositions (e.g., high-mannose, complex, and hybrid types with varying degrees of fucosylation and sialylation), the remaining five peaks do not match any entries in established glycan databases but are consistently observed across all analyzed samples and show significant differential expression, thereby qualifying as valid diagnostic indicators under the invention’s inclusive biomarker philosophy.

The invention further provides a computer-implemented system for ovarian cancer diagnosis, comprising a processor configured to execute instructions that: receive MALDI-TOF MS spectral data from a subject’s serum glycan analysis; extract intensity values for the fifteen predefined m/z peaks; apply normalization and scoring rules; compute the weighted total score; and output a diagnostic classification. This system may be integrated into laboratory information management systems or deployed as a standalone software application.

The diagnostic method of the invention achieves an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.89 in the training set and maintains high performance in a blind test set of 60 subjects (23 healthy controls, 37 ovarian cancer patients), yielding sensitivity of 81–84% and specificity of 83%—superior to the 78% sensitivity achieved by CA-125 at the standard clinical cut-off of 35 U/mL. Importantly, the method shows enhanced detection of clear-cell and serous subtypes of ovarian cancer and successfully identifies all tested cases of recurrent disease, suggesting utility not only in primary diagnosis but also in post-treatment monitoring. By leveraging the collective power of multiple, independently informative spectral features and a rationally weighted decision algorithm, the invention overcomes the limitations of single-marker approaches and offers a clinically viable tool for improving ovarian cancer outcomes through earlier and more accurate detection.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

### Glycan Analysis from Human Serum

The present invention is predicated upon a meticulously optimized workflow for the extraction, purification, and mass spectrometric analysis of N-linked glycans from human serum, designed to maximize reproducibility, sensitivity, and diagnostic yield. The process begins with the collection of a serum sample from a human subject under standardized clinical conditions. Whole blood is drawn into a clot-activating tube and allowed to stand at room temperature for a period sufficient to permit complete coagulation, typically 30 to 60 minutes. The clotted sample is then centrifuged at approximately 1000 × g for 10 minutes at ambient temperature to separate the cellular components from the liquid phase. The resulting supernatant, constituting the serum, is carefully aspirated to avoid contamination with the buffy coat or red blood cell pellet and transferred into sterile, low-binding polypropylene microcentrifuge tubes. To preserve sample integrity, aliquots of approximately 100 microliters are immediately frozen at −80°C and stored until analysis. Prior to glycan release, the serum sample is thawed on ice and gently vortexed to ensure homogeneity.

The first critical step in the analytical pipeline is the denaturation of serum proteins to expose buried glycosylation sites and facilitate efficient enzymatic deglycosylation. To this end, fifty microliters of serum is mixed with an equal volume of 200 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer containing 10 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), a reducing agent that disrupts disulfide bonds and promotes protein unfolding. The mixture is then subjected to a controlled thermal cycling regimen consisting of four alternating cycles of 15 seconds in a boiling water bath (approximately 100°C) followed by 15 seconds in a room-temperature water bath. This rapid heating and cooling protocol induces moderate yet sufficient denaturation without causing irreversible aggregation or precipitation, thereby maintaining substrate accessibility for the subsequent enzymatic reaction.

Following denaturation, N-linked glycans are enzymatically cleaved from their protein carriers using peptide-N-glycosidase F (PNGase F), a highly specific amidase that hydrolyzes the β-aspartylglycosylamine bond between the innermost N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) residue of the glycan and the asparagine side chain of the protein. One thousand units of recombinant PNGase F are added to the denatured serum mixture, and the reaction is carried out in a microwave-assisted enzyme reaction enhancing system (REDS) at 37°C with a microwave power output of 400 W for a duration of 10 minutes. The use of microwave irradiation significantly accelerates the deglycosylation kinetics compared to conventional incubation methods, reducing reaction time from hours to minutes while maintaining high efficiency and minimizing side reactions. Upon completion of the enzymatic reaction, ice-cold ethanol is added to the mixture to a final concentration of 80% (v/v), and the sample is placed in a −80°C freezer for at least one hour to precipitate residual proteins and other macromolecular contaminants. The precipitated material is then removed by centrifugation at high speed (e.g., 14,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4°C), and the supernatant containing the released glycans is carefully transferred to a new tube.

The glycan-containing supernatant is subsequently dried to completion using a centrifugal evaporator under reduced pressure and mild heating (e.g., 30°C) to prevent thermal degradation of labile glycan structures. The dried glycan residue is then reconstituted in a minimal volume of water and subjected to solid-phase extraction (SPE) using graphitized carbon cartridges (GCC), which offer superior selectivity for carbohydrates over other biomolecules due to their unique retention mechanism based on hydrophobic and polar interactions. Prior to sample loading, the GCC cartridges are preconditioned by sequential washing with methanol, water, and finally with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) in 80% acetonitrile/water to activate the carbon surface and establish appropriate ionic conditions. The reconstituted glycan solution is loaded onto the equilibrated cartridge, and the column is washed extensively with nanopure water to remove salts, residual ethanol, and other hydrophilic impurities that do not bind to the carbon matrix.

Glycans are then eluted in a stepwise manner using three solvent systems of increasing organic strength: 10% acetonitrile (ACN)/water with 0.05% TFA, 20% ACN/water with 0.05% TFA, and 40% ACN/water with 0.05% TFA. This gradient elution strategy exploits the differential hydrophobicity of glycan structures—neutral glycans elute in lower ACN concentrations, while more hydrophobic species such as sialylated or fucosylated glycans require higher ACN percentages for efficient recovery. Each eluate fraction is collected separately and dried in the evaporator. For the purposes of the present invention, only the 10% and 20% ACN fractions are utilized in the initial biomarker discovery and validation phases, as these fractions were found to contain the majority of diagnostically relevant signals. The 40% fraction, which may harbor highly acidic or modified glycans, is reserved for future studies involving chemical derivatization techniques such as permethylation to enhance ionization efficiency.

The dried glycans from the 10% and 20% fractions are each reconstituted in 15 microliters of ultrapure water and prepared for MALDI-TOF MS analysis. One microliter of the glycan solution is spotted onto a pre-coated MALDI target plate containing 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB) as the matrix, and immediately mixed with one microliter of a co-crystallization solution consisting of 70% acetonitrile and 12 mM sodium chloride. The addition of sodium chloride promotes the formation of sodiated adducts ([M+Na]⁺), which yield sharper and more intense peaks in positive-ion mode compared to protonated species. The sample-matrix mixture is allowed to air-dry at room temperature or under gentle vacuum to form homogeneous co-crystals. Mass spectra are acquired using a high-performance MALDI-TOF/TOF instrument operated in positive-ion reflectron mode to achieve high mass accuracy and resolution. The instrument is calibrated using a set of twelve internal calibrants corresponding to known N-glycan masses, ensuring mass measurement errors within ±0.2 m/z units across the entire acquisition range of 1000 to 3000 m/z.

To ensure quantitative reliability, each sample fraction is analyzed in quadruplicate—four independent spots per fraction—resulting in eight mass spectra per serum sample (four from 10% fraction, four from 20% fraction). At each spot, data are collected from forty distinct laser positions, with twenty-five laser shots accumulated per position, yielding a total of one thousand laser shots per spot. This extensive signal averaging minimizes shot-to-shot variability and enhances the signal-to-noise ratio for low-abundance peaks. The raw spectral data are processed using specialized software to extract centroided peak lists containing m/z values, absolute intensities, signal-to-noise ratios, and other relevant parameters. Peaks are considered valid if they exceed a signal-to-noise threshold of 4 and an absolute intensity threshold of 10 counts. The absolute peak intensities (APIs) for all detected peaks in a given spectrum are then normalized to the total ion current of that spectrum to generate normalized absolute peak intensities (NAPIs), calculated according to the formula:

\[
\text{NAPI}_i = \frac{\text{API}_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \text{API}_j} \times 1000
\]

where \( n \) is the total number of peaks in the spectrum, including isotopic variants. This normalization accounts for variations in sample loading, matrix crystallization, and instrument response, enabling meaningful comparisons across different samples and analytical runs. The coefficient of variation (% CV) for peak intensities across the quadruplicate analyses is consistently maintained below 15%, demonstrating excellent analytical reproducibility.

### Example 2

In accordance with the method of the present invention, a training set of serum samples was assembled to develop and optimize the multibiomarker diagnostic panel. The training set comprised eighty subjects: forty histologically confirmed ovarian cancer patients and forty age-matched healthy female controls. All participants were of Korean ethnicity, and written informed consent was obtained under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board of Cheil General Hospital & Women's Healthcare Center. To minimize confounding effects of age-related glycomic changes, the patient and control groups were carefully matched such that the distribution of subjects across age brackets (e.g., 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, ≥60 years) was identical in both groups. The mean age of the patient cohort was 41.8 ± 9.5 years, and that of the control cohort was 41.2 ± 9.7 years, confirming successful age matching. The ovarian cancer patients represented a spectrum of disease stages according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification: nineteen patients were at stage I, three at stage II, fifteen at stage III, and three at stage IV. Histological subtypes included serous, clear-cell, endometrioid, and mucinous carcinomas, reflecting the heterogeneity of the disease.

Serum samples from all eighty subjects were processed in parallel using the standardized glycan isolation and MALDI-TOF MS protocol described above. From each sample, mass spectra were generated for both the 10% and 20% acetonitrile fractions, with quadruplicate analyses per fraction. The resulting spectral data were compiled into a comprehensive dataset containing m/z values and NAPIs for all detected peaks. Initial biomarker discovery focused on identifying ion peaks that exhibited statistically significant differences in intensity between the cancer and control groups. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed on the NAPI values for each m/z peak across the entire training set, yielding a P-value that quantified the likelihood that the observed difference arose by chance. Peaks with P-values less than 10⁻⁹ were considered strong candidates for further evaluation, as this stringent threshold minimized the risk of false positives arising from multiple hypothesis testing.

In addition to statistical significance, candidate biomarkers were required to demonstrate high detection frequency—defined as presence in at least 95% of samples within both the patient and control groups—to ensure robustness and generalizability. Applying these dual criteria, twenty-four ion peaks emerged as potential biomarkers: twelve from the 10% ACN fraction and twelve from the 20% ACN fraction. The m/z values of these peaks ranged from approximately 1200 to 2800, covering the expected mass range for N-glycans and related structures. To assess the diagnostic utility of each candidate, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed using the NAPI data from the training set, and the area under each curve (AUC) was calculated as a measure of classification performance. An AUC of 1.0 represents perfect discrimination, while an AUC of 0.5 indicates no discriminative ability.

A systematic analysis revealed a strong inverse linear correlation between the logarithm of the ANOVA P-value and the AUC for the candidate biomarkers, confirming that statistical significance and diagnostic efficacy are closely linked. Based on this relationship and empirical performance thresholds, the final multibiomarker panel was selected as follows: from the 10% ACN fraction, five peaks with AUC > 0.72 were chosen; from the 20% ACN fraction, ten peaks with both P-value < 10⁻¹⁵ and AUC > 0.75 were selected. This yielded a total of fifteen biomarkers for inclusion in the diagnostic algorithm. Of these fifteen, ten were successfully annotated as known N-glycan compositions using the GlycoMod database with a mass tolerance of ±10 ppm. These annotated glycans included high-mannose structures (e.g., Man5GlcNAc2, Man6GlcNAc2), complex biantennary glycans with zero, one, or two sialic acid residues, and fucosylated variants thereof. The remaining five biomarkers—two from the 10% fraction and three from the 20% fraction—did not correspond to any entries in current glycan databases, suggesting they may represent non-glycan molecules, unusual glycoforms, or glycan derivatives that co-purify with N-glycans under the employed SPE conditions. Despite their unknown identity, these peaks were retained in the panel due to their exceptional reproducibility (detected in >98% of all samples), low % CV (<12%), and high diagnostic performance (AUC ranging from 0.76 to 0.83).

For each of the fifteen biomarkers, an optimal cut-off value for NAPI was determined from its ROC curve in the training set, using a target sensitivity of 70% as the selection criterion. This cut-off defines the threshold above or below which a sample is classified as positive or negative for ovarian cancer based on that individual biomarker. The use of a fixed sensitivity target ensures consistency in the interpretation of individual biomarker contributions across the panel. The fifteen cut-off values thus established serve as the foundation for the subsequent scoring algorithm.

### Example 3

Building upon the biomarker panel defined in Example 2, a proprietary scoring algorithm was developed to integrate the diagnostic information from all fifteen biomarkers into a single, interpretable diagnostic score. The algorithm operates on the principle that not all biomarkers contribute equally to the final classification decision; therefore, each biomarker’s contribution is weighted according to its individual discriminatory power. Specifically, for each serum sample analyzed, the NAPI of each of the fifteen biomarkers is compared to its predetermined cut-off value. If the NAPI exceeds the cut-off, the sample receives a binary score of +1 for that biomarker, indicating a "positive" signal consistent with ovarian cancer; if the NAPI is below the cut-off, the score is −1, indicating a "negative" signal consistent with health. This binary assignment transforms continuous intensity data into a discrete classification signal for each biomarker.

However, to account for the varying reliability of each biomarker, the binary score is then multiplied by a weighting factor equal to the AUC of that biomarker as determined in the training set. For instance, a biomarker with an AUC of 0.85 would contribute +0.85 or −0.85 to the total score, whereas a biomarker with an AUC of 0.76 would contribute ±0.76. This weighting scheme ensures that biomarkers with higher diagnostic accuracy exert a proportionally greater influence on the final outcome, while those with marginal performance have a correspondingly smaller impact. The weighted scores for all fifteen biomarkers are then summed algebraically to produce a total diagnostic score for the sample.

The distribution of total scores in the training set was analyzed to establish a final diagnostic threshold that optimizes the balance between sensitivity and specificity. ROC curve analysis of the total score yielded an AUC of 0.89, substantially higher than the best-performing individual biomarker (AUC = 0.83) and far superior to CA-125 (AUC = 0.79 in the same cohort). At a total score threshold corresponding to 80% sensitivity, the specificity was 83%; at 85% sensitivity, specificity was 78%. These performance metrics demonstrate the synergistic effect of combining multiple biomarkers through the weighted scoring approach. In direct comparison, CA-125 testing at the standard clinical cut-off of 35 U/mL achieved only 74% sensitivity in the training set, with specificity of 85%. Thus, the multibiomarker panel not only improves sensitivity but does so without a substantial loss in specificity.

The scoring algorithm was implemented in custom software that automates the entire analytical pipeline—from raw spectral data import to final diagnostic classification. The software performs peak detection, NAPI calculation, cut-off comparison, weighting, and summation in a fully automated fashion, minimizing operator bias and ensuring consistency across analyses. Importantly, the algorithm is designed to be robust to minor variations in sample preparation or instrument performance, as the normalization and weighting steps inherently compensate for such fluctuations.

### Example 4

To rigorously evaluate the predictive power and clinical applicability of the multibiomarker panel and scoring algorithm, a blind test set of sixty serum samples was assembled and analyzed. This test set was completely independent of the training set and consisted of twenty-three healthy female controls and thirty-seven ovarian cancer patients, all of Korean ethnicity and recruited under the same ethical guidelines. The cancer patients in the blind set spanned a wide age range (30 to 89 years) and included various FIGO stages and histological subtypes, ensuring a realistic representation of clinical diversity. Critically, the identities of the samples (i.e., whether they came from patients or controls) were concealed from the analysts performing the MALDI-TOF MS and scoring calculations, thereby eliminating any possibility of conscious or unconscious bias.

Each sample in the blind test set was processed identically to those in the training set, using the same reagents, instruments, and analytical parameters. The fifteen biomarker peaks were quantified, NAPIs were calculated, and the total diagnostic score was computed using the cut-off values and weighting factors derived exclusively from the training set—no re-optimization or recalibration was performed on the blind set data. This strict separation between training and testing is essential to provide an unbiased estimate of real-world performance and to guard against overfitting.

The results of the blind test were highly encouraging. Across a range of total score thresholds derived from the training set, the multibiomarker panel consistently achieved sensitivity between 81% and 84% while maintaining a constant specificity of 83%. This level of performance represents a clear improvement over CA-125, which yielded only 78% sensitivity at the standard 35 U/mL cut-off in the same blind set, with comparable specificity. Notably, the panel demonstrated particular strength in detecting early-stage disease: among the stage I and II patients in the blind set, sensitivity was 82%, compared to just 65% for CA-125. This enhanced early detection capability is of paramount clinical importance, as it directly addresses the major limitation of current screening strategies.

Subgroup analysis by histological subtype revealed additional insights. For clear-cell ovarian carcinoma—a subtype notoriously difficult to detect with CA-125—the glycan-based panel achieved sensitivities of 90% in both the training and blind sets, outperforming CA-125 by approximately 20 percentage points. Serous carcinomas, the most common subtype, were detected with sensitivities slightly higher than or comparable to CA-125, depending on the score threshold. Although the number of mucinous cases was too small for definitive conclusions, the available data suggested adequate detection performance. These findings indicate that the glycan signature captured by the panel is broadly applicable across ovarian cancer subtypes but may offer particular advantages for certain histologies.

### Example 5

Further validation of the invention’s clinical utility was conducted by applying the diagnostic panel to a small cohort of eight patients with recurrent ovarian cancer. These individuals had undergone initial surgical treatment for ovarian cancer and subsequently experienced disease recurrence, as confirmed by clinical and imaging criteria. Serum samples were collected at the time of recurrence diagnosis and analyzed using the same blinded protocol as the main test set. Remarkably, all eight recurrent cases were correctly classified as positive for ovarian cancer by the multibiomarker panel, achieving 100% sensitivity in this challenging subgroup. In stark contrast, only three of the five recurrent patients for whom CA-125 measurements were available had levels exceeding the 35 U/mL threshold, corresponding to a sensitivity of just 60%. This preliminary finding suggests that the glycan-based approach may be especially valuable for monitoring disease recurrence, a scenario where early detection of relapse can significantly impact treatment decisions and outcomes.

Although the recurrent cohort was limited in size, the perfect classification rate underscores the biological relevance of the glycan alterations captured by the panel, which appear to persist or re-emerge even after initial therapy. This observation aligns with the understanding that glycosylation changes are fundamental to the malignant phenotype and are less likely to be transient or treatment-induced artifacts. Future studies with larger recurrent cohorts will be necessary to confirm these results and to explore the potential of serial glycan profiling for tracking treatment response and predicting relapse.

In summary, the examples presented herein collectively demonstrate that the method of the present invention—combining standardized serum glycan analysis by MALDI-TOF MS with a rationally designed, weighted multibiomarker scoring algorithm—provides a powerful and clinically relevant tool for the diagnosis of ovarian cancer. The approach outperforms the current gold standard (CA-125) in terms of sensitivity, particularly for early-stage and recurrent disease, while maintaining high specificity. By embracing both annotated glycans and reproducible, differentially expressed spectral features of unknown identity, the invention maximizes the information extracted from the serum glycome, paving the way for more accurate and personalized cancer diagnostics.
