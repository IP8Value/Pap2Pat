# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention pertains to the field of molecular oncology and virology, specifically to the therapeutic treatment of human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated neoplasias and cancers. More particularly, the invention relates to the use of Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF), a naturally occurring human cytokine, as a pharmacologically active agent for the suppression of HPV oncogene expression, particularly the E6 and E7 viral oncoproteins, which are under the transcriptional control of the viral long control region (LCR). The invention further encompasses methods for diagnosing and treating HPV-transformed cells, including cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), cervical carcinoma, anal carcinoma, oropharyngeal and laryngeal carcinomas, and other HPV-associated malignancies, by administering LIF or LIF analogs to inhibit LCR-driven transcription and thereby induce cell cycle arrest or differentiation in HPV-positive neoplastic cells. The invention also includes pharmaceutical compositions comprising LIF, methods for manufacturing such compositions, and diagnostic assays based on the modulation of LCR activity by LIF in HPV-infected tissues. The technical scope extends to the molecular mechanisms by which LIF exerts its anti-oncogenic effects through the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, particularly via STAT3 phosphorylation, and the implications of this signaling for selective targeting of HPV-transformed cells without inducing significant cytotoxicity in normal tissues. The invention is grounded in the discovery that LIF, despite its pleiotropic biological roles, functions as a potent and specific repressor of HPV LCR activity, offering a novel, non-surgical, and potentially systemic therapeutic strategy for a broad spectrum of HPV-dependent pathologies.

## BACKGROUND

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a well-established necessary causative agent in the development of cervical cancer, which remains the second most common cancer among women globally and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. Beyond cervical cancer, high-risk HPV genotypes, particularly HPV-16 and HPV-18, have been implicated in the etiology of a growing number of malignancies, including anal cancer, oropharyngeal and laryngeal squamous cell carcinomas, and possibly a subset of breast cancers. Despite advances in screening through Pap smears and HPV DNA testing, and the availability of prophylactic vaccines, millions of individuals remain infected with oncogenic HPV types, and a significant proportion develop precancerous lesions such as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), which can progress to invasive carcinoma if left untreated. Current clinical management of high-grade CIN and early-stage cervical cancer relies heavily on surgical interventions, including loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP), cryotherapy, or conization, which, while effective, carry risks of complications such as cervical stenosis, preterm labor, and infertility. For advanced or metastatic cervical cancer, the standard of care involves concurrent chemoradiation, which is associated with substantial systemic toxicity, and palliative chemotherapy offers limited survival benefit with no curative potential. Critically, there exists no approved, effective, non-invasive pharmacological therapy that directly targets the underlying HPV infection or the viral oncogenes responsible for maintaining the transformed phenotype of cancer cells.

The molecular pathogenesis of HPV-associated cancers hinges on the persistent expression of two viral oncoproteins, E6 and E7, which are encoded by the HPV genome and regulated by the viral long control region (LCR). The E6 protein promotes the degradation of the tumor suppressor p53, thereby disabling a critical cellular checkpoint for DNA damage and apoptosis, while E7 inactivates the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), leading to uncontrolled cell cycle progression. The sustained expression of E6 and E7 is not only necessary for the initiation of transformation but also for the maintenance of the malignant state; experimental downregulation of these oncoproteins—through techniques such as RNA interference, peptide aptamers, or reintroduction of the viral E2 repressor—has consistently resulted in reactivation of p53 and pRb pathways, induction of cell cycle arrest, senescence, or apoptosis, and sensitization of cancer cells to radiation and chemotherapy. Consequently, the HPV LCR represents a highly attractive and virus-specific therapeutic target, as its inhibition would selectively affect only HPV-infected cells, sparing uninfected tissues from off-target effects.

Numerous attempts have been made to modulate HPV LCR activity using cytokines and other signaling molecules, given the known responsiveness of the LCR to host cellular factors. For instance, transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) and interleukin-4 (IL-4) have been shown to repress LCR-driven transcription in certain contexts, but their efficacy is often limited to early-stage or non-malignant HPV-immortalized cells and fails in fully transformed carcinoma lines such as SiHa. Interferons, particularly IFN-α and IFN-γ, have demonstrated anti-HPV activity in vitro and have been explored clinically, yet their effectiveness is inconsistent, especially in advanced disease, and some studies even suggest that interferon-responsive elements within the LCR may paradoxically enhance transcription under certain conditions. Interleukin-6 (IL-6), a member of the same cytokine family as LIF, has been reported to suppress HPV LCR activity, but it simultaneously promotes proliferation in both normal and cancerous cervical epithelial cells, thereby negating any potential therapeutic benefit. These limitations underscore the need for a cytokine or signaling molecule that can potently and selectively repress the HPV LCR without stimulating proliferative or pro-inflammatory pathways that might exacerbate disease progression.

Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) is a pleiotropic cytokine belonging to the IL-6 family, known for its roles in embryonic stem cell pluripotency, neuronal differentiation, bone metabolism, and immune regulation. LIF signals through a heterodimeric receptor complex composed of the LIF receptor (LIFR) and glycoprotein 130 (gp130), leading to activation of the JAK-STAT, MAPK, and PI3K pathways. Notably, LIF has undergone Phase I clinical trials as a therapeutic agent and has demonstrated a favorable safety profile with minimal toxicity. While LIF has been implicated in both pro- and anti-tumorigenic processes depending on the cellular context, its specific role in HPV-associated carcinogenesis has remained unexplored. The present invention arises from the unexpected discovery that LIF acts as a potent and direct repressor of HPV-16 LCR-driven transcription in cervical cancer cells, leading to significant downregulation of E6 and E7 mRNA and a consequent reduction in cellular proliferation. This effect is distinct from other cytokines previously tested and positions LIF as a uniquely promising candidate for the development of a targeted, non-cytotoxic therapy for HPV-dependent neoplasias.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for the treatment of human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated diseases, including precancerous lesions such as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and invasive carcinomas of the cervix, anus, oropharynx, and other anatomical sites, by administering Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) or a biologically active fragment, analog, or derivative thereof. The invention is based on the discovery that LIF specifically and potently inhibits transcription driven by the HPV long control region (LCR), resulting in the downregulation of the viral oncogenes E6 and E7, which are essential for the maintenance of the transformed phenotype in HPV-positive cells. This inhibition leads to a reduction in cellular proliferation, primarily through the induction of cell cycle arrest rather than apoptosis, thereby offering a mechanism to halt or reverse the progression of HPV-associated neoplasia without the cytotoxic side effects associated with conventional chemotherapy or radiation.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method of treating an HPV-associated condition in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of LIF. The HPV-associated condition may include, but is not limited to, cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) grades 1, 2, or 3, cervical carcinoma, anal carcinoma, oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma, laryngeal carcinoma, and other HPV-positive malignancies. The administration may be performed via various routes, including intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, topical, intravaginal, or intralesional, and may be formulated as a monotherapy or in combination with other therapeutic agents such as chemotherapeutics, radiation, or immunomodulators.

In another aspect, the invention encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising LIF in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, optionally in combination with additional active ingredients. These compositions may be tailored for specific routes of administration and may include stabilizers, buffers, and controlled-release formulations to optimize bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy.

In a further aspect, the invention provides a method for diagnosing or monitoring the response to treatment in an HPV-associated condition, comprising measuring the level of LCR-driven transcription or E6/E7 mRNA expression in a biological sample from a subject before and after administration of LIF. A significant reduction in these markers following LIF treatment indicates a positive therapeutic response.

The invention also includes the use of LIF in the manufacture of a medicament for the treatment of HPV-associated diseases. Additionally, the invention provides kits comprising LIF and instructions for its use in the treatment of HPV-transformed cells or tissues.

The therapeutic effect of LIF is mediated, at least in part, through the activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, particularly the phosphorylation and activation of STAT3, which appears to play a role in the transcriptional repression of the HPV LCR. This mechanism is distinct from other cytokines that either fail to repress the LCR in advanced carcinoma cells or exert opposing proliferative effects. The invention thus offers a targeted, virus-specific therapeutic strategy that leverages endogenous cellular signaling pathways to suppress oncogenic viral gene expression, representing a significant advance over existing non-surgical approaches for HPV-associated neoplasia.

## DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “Leukemia Inhibitory Factor” or “LIF” refers to the naturally occurring human cytokine, including full-length recombinant human LIF, as well as biologically active fragments, variants, analogs, or derivatives thereof that retain the ability to bind to the LIF receptor (LIFR) and/or gp130 and elicit a biological response, particularly the inhibition of HPV LCR-driven transcription. This includes LIF proteins with amino acid substitutions, deletions, or insertions that do not substantially impair receptor binding or functional activity, as well as fusion proteins comprising LIF or a functional domain thereof linked to a stabilizing or targeting moiety.

The term “HPV-associated condition” or “HPV-associated disease” refers to any pathological state caused by or associated with infection by human papillomavirus, particularly high-risk oncogenic types such as HPV-16, HPV-18, HPV-31, HPV-33, and others. This includes precancerous lesions such as cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN), anal intraepithelial neoplasia (AIN), and penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PIN), as well as invasive carcinomas of the cervix, anus, oropharynx, larynx, vagina, vulva, and penis, and potentially other malignancies where HPV DNA or oncogene expression has been detected, such as certain breast cancers.

The term “long control region” or “LCR” refers to the non-coding regulatory region of the HPV genome that contains the viral origin of replication and enhancer/promoter elements responsible for the transcription of the E6 and E7 oncogenes. The LCR is responsive to a variety of host cellular transcription factors and signaling pathways, and its activity is a key determinant of viral oncogene expression and disease progression.

The term “therapeutically effective amount” means an amount of LIF that, when administered to a subject suffering from an HPV-associated condition, is sufficient to produce a measurable therapeutic benefit, such as a reduction in lesion size, regression of dysplasia, decrease in E6/E7 mRNA levels, inhibition of cellular proliferation, or prevention of disease progression, without causing unacceptable toxicity.

The term “pharmaceutically acceptable carrier” refers to any excipient, diluent, or vehicle that is compatible with the active ingredient and suitable for administration to a human or animal subject. This includes, but is not limited to, sterile aqueous or non-aqueous solutions, suspensions, emulsions, liposomes, and solid dosage forms such as tablets or capsules.

The term “subject” refers to any human or non-human animal, including mammals such as primates, rodents, and domestic animals, that may be treated with the methods or compositions of the invention. In the context of clinical application, the subject is typically a human patient diagnosed with or at risk for an HPV-associated condition.

The term “cell cycle arrest” refers to a state in which a cell ceases to divide, typically due to the activation of checkpoint mechanisms that prevent progression through the cell cycle phases (G1, S, G2, M). In the context of the present invention, LIF-induced cell cycle arrest in HPV-transformed cells is characterized by reduced metabolic activity and proliferation without the hallmarks of apoptosis, such as loss of membrane asymmetry or DNA fragmentation.

The term “biologically active fragment, analog, or derivative” refers to a modified form of LIF that retains the core functional activity of the native protein, specifically the ability to inhibit HPV LCR-driven transcription and reduce proliferation of HPV-transformed cells. Such modifications may include chemical derivatization, PEGylation, glycosylation variants, or engineered mutations designed to enhance stability, half-life, or receptor specificity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Classification of Papillomaviruses

Papillomaviruses are small, non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses that belong to the Papillomaviridae family. They exhibit a high degree of species and tissue specificity, with over 200 distinct human papillomavirus (HPV) types identified to date, classified primarily based on the nucleotide sequence divergence of the L1 capsid gene, where a difference of more than 10% defines a new type. HPVs are broadly categorized into mucosal and cutaneous types, depending on their preferred site of infection. Among the mucosal types, approximately 15 are classified as high-risk or oncogenic due to their strong association with anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers, with HPV-16 and HPV-18 being the most prevalent and responsible for the majority of HPV-related malignancies. Low-risk HPV types, such as HPV-6 and HPV-11, are primarily associated with benign lesions like genital warts and recurrent respiratory papillomatosis. The genomic organization of all HPVs is highly conserved, consisting of an early (E) region encoding non-structural proteins involved in replication and transformation (E1, E2, E4, E5, E6, E7), a late (L) region encoding the structural capsid proteins (L1, L2), and a non-coding long control region (LCR) that regulates viral transcription and replication. The classification of HPVs is not only important for epidemiological tracking but also for clinical management, as the oncogenic potential of different types dictates the urgency and type of intervention required. The present invention is particularly relevant to high-risk HPV types, especially HPV-16, given its dominance in cervical and other cancers and the demonstrated efficacy of LIF in repressing its LCR-driven oncogene expression.

### Papillomaviruses and Cancer

The causal link between high-risk HPV infection and cancer is well-established, with HPV DNA detectable in nearly all cases of cervical cancer and a significant proportion of anal, oropharyngeal, vaginal, vulvar, and penile cancers. The oncogenic process begins with persistent infection of basal epithelial cells, where the viral genome is maintained as a low-copy episome. In a subset of infections, integration of the HPV genome into the host chromosome occurs, often disrupting the E2 gene, which normally acts as a repressor of the LCR. This disruption leads to constitutive overexpression of the E6 and E7 oncoproteins, which drive cellular transformation by inactivating key tumor suppressor pathways. E6 binds to and promotes the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of p53, thereby abrogating the cellular response to DNA damage and preventing apoptosis. E7, on the other hand, binds to and inactivates the retinoblastoma protein (pRb), releasing E2F transcription factors and driving unscheduled entry into the S phase of the cell cycle. The combined effects of E6 and E7 result in genomic instability, immortalization, and resistance to growth-inhibitory signals, ultimately leading to the development of high-grade dysplasia and invasive carcinoma. The dependence of HPV-transformed cells on continuous E6 and E7 expression for survival and proliferation makes these oncoproteins and their regulatory LCR ideal targets for therapeutic intervention. The present invention exploits this dependency by using LIF to suppress LCR activity, thereby reducing E6 and E7 levels and reversing the transformed phenotype.

### Diagnosis and Treatment of HPV

Current strategies for the diagnosis and management of HPV-associated diseases involve a combination of screening, diagnostic testing, and therapeutic intervention. Primary screening for cervical cancer includes cytology (Pap smear) and/or HPV DNA testing, with co-testing providing the highest sensitivity. Women with abnormal screening results undergo colposcopy and biopsy to confirm the presence and grade of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN). Treatment of high-grade CIN (CIN 2/3) is typically ablative or excisional, using methods such as LEEP, cryotherapy, or laser ablation, which aim to remove or destroy the dysplastic tissue. However, these procedures are invasive, carry risks of complications, and do not address the underlying viral infection, leaving patients susceptible to recurrence. For invasive cervical cancer, treatment depends on stage: early-stage disease is managed with surgery or radiation, while locally advanced disease requires concurrent chemoradiation. Metastatic or recurrent disease has a poor prognosis, with limited options for palliative chemotherapy. Prophylactic HPV vaccines (Gardasil, Cervarix) have significantly reduced the incidence of vaccine-type HPV infections and associated precancers, but they are not therapeutic and do not benefit individuals already infected. There is thus a critical unmet need for non-invasive, therapeutic agents that can eliminate or suppress HPV infection and reverse dysplasia. The present invention addresses this need by providing LIF as a pharmacological agent that targets the root cause of HPV-associated neoplasia—the viral oncogenes—offering a potential alternative or adjunct to current surgical and cytotoxic approaches.

### Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) and its Utility in Treating HPV-Transformed Cells by Inhibiting LCR-Driven Transcription

The present invention is founded upon the novel discovery that Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF), a member of the interleukin-6 (IL-6) cytokine family, functions as a potent and specific repressor of transcription driven by the human papillomavirus (HPV) long control region (LCR). This finding is particularly significant because the LCR governs the expression of the E6 and E7 viral oncogenes, which are indispensable for the initiation and maintenance of the transformed phenotype in HPV-associated cancers. Experimental evidence demonstrates that LIF treatment of HPV-16-transformed cervical cancer cell lines, such as SiHa and CaSki, results in a dose- and time-dependent reduction in LCR activity, as measured by reporter assays utilizing the HPV-16 LCR cloned upstream of a secreted Gaussia luciferase gene. This transcriptional repression translates into a substantial decrease in endogenous E6 and E7 mRNA levels, as confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR using type-specific primers and probes. The magnitude of this inhibition—approximately 60% reduction in LCR activity and corresponding declines in oncogene expression—is comparable to or exceeds that achieved by other experimental approaches such as siRNA-mediated knockdown or E2 repressor reintroduction, both of which have been shown to induce cell cycle arrest or apoptosis in HPV-positive cells.

The mechanism by which LIF exerts this repressive effect appears to involve activation of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. LIF binds to its heterodimeric receptor complex, composed of LIFR and gp130, leading to the phosphorylation and activation of JAK kinases, which in turn phosphorylate signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) on tyrosine residue 705. Phosphorylated STAT3 dimerizes and translocates to the nucleus, where it can act as a transcription factor. In the context of HPV-transformed cells, activated STAT3 may either directly or indirectly interfere with the assembly or function of the transcriptional machinery at the LCR, or it may induce the expression of cellular repressors that bind to the LCR and silence its activity. Importantly, while STAT3 is often associated with pro-survival and proliferative signaling in many cancers, its activation by LIF in HPV-positive cells correlates with growth inhibition, suggesting a context-dependent tumor-suppressive role. This dual functionality of STAT3 underscores the complexity of cytokine signaling in cancer and highlights the unique therapeutic window offered by LIF in HPV-associated disease.

The functional consequence of LIF-mediated LCR repression is a significant reduction in cellular proliferation, as demonstrated by MTT assays showing that LIF-treated CaSki cells reach only about half the population density of untreated controls after 40 hours in culture. Notably, this growth inhibition is not accompanied by markers of apoptosis, such as phosphatidylserine externalization, indicating that LIF induces a cytostatic rather than cytotoxic effect, likely through cell cycle arrest. This is a desirable therapeutic profile, as it minimizes collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissues while effectively halting the expansion of dysplastic or neoplastic clones. Furthermore, LIF’s ability to suppress HPV oncogene expression may sensitize cancer cells to conventional therapies; since E6 and E7 confer resistance to radiation and chemotherapy by disabling p53 and pRb, their downregulation by LIF could restore treatment sensitivity and improve outcomes in combination regimens.

The therapeutic potential of LIF is further enhanced by its favorable safety profile. LIF has already undergone Phase I clinical testing for other indications and has been shown to be well-tolerated with minimal toxicity, a critical advantage over many chemotherapeutic agents. Additionally, LIF’s anti-inflammatory properties, evidenced by its role in promoting regulatory T cell differentiation, may provide ancillary benefits in the context of HPV-associated disease, particularly in HIV-co-infected individuals who are at heightened risk for aggressive CIN and cervical cancer. Although the potential for LIF to promote immune tolerance warrants careful consideration, the dominant effect of oncogene suppression is likely to outweigh any pro-tumorigenic immune modulation, especially in localized or early-stage disease.

In summary, LIF represents a novel, endogenous, and pharmacologically tractable agent for the treatment of HPV-associated neoplasia. Its ability to specifically target the viral LCR, downregulate E6 and E7, and induce cell cycle arrest without significant toxicity positions it as a promising candidate for the development of non-invasive, targeted therapies for cervical dysplasia, carcinoma, and other HPV-dependent malignancies.

### Manipulating Proteins, DNA, and RNA

The present invention contemplates various molecular and genetic strategies to enhance or modulate the therapeutic effects of LIF in the context of HPV-associated diseases. At the protein level, LIF may be engineered to improve its pharmacokinetic properties, such as serum half-life, stability, or receptor affinity. For example, PEGylation of LIF—covalent attachment of polyethylene glycol chains—can reduce renal clearance and proteolytic degradation, thereby prolonging its biological activity. Alternatively, fusion proteins comprising LIF linked to Fc domains of immunoglobulins or albumin can leverage the neonatal Fc receptor recycling pathway to extend circulation time. Site-directed mutagenesis may be employed to generate LIF variants with enhanced specificity for the LIFR/gp130 complex or reduced cross-reactivity with other IL-6 family receptors, minimizing off-target effects.

At the DNA level, gene therapy approaches may be utilized to deliver the LIF gene directly to HPV-infected tissues, enabling sustained local production of the cytokine. Viral vectors such as adeno-associated virus (AAV) or lentivirus, or non-viral vectors like plasmid DNA or nanoparticles, can be engineered to express LIF under the control of tissue-specific or inducible promoters, ensuring targeted expression in cervical or other affected epithelia. Such strategies could be particularly useful for treating localized lesions like CIN, where direct intravaginal or intralesional delivery is feasible.

At the RNA level, the invention encompasses the use of RNA interference or antisense oligonucleotides to modulate the expression of negative regulators of LIF signaling, thereby potentiating its anti-HPV effects. For instance, knockdown of SOCS3 (suppressor of cytokine signaling 3), a known inhibitor of JAK-STAT signaling, could enhance STAT3 activation and amplify LIF-mediated LCR repression. Conversely, RNA-based tools could be used to validate the mechanism of action by silencing LIFR or STAT3 and confirming the dependence of LCR inhibition on this pathway.

Additionally, the invention includes the development of diagnostic RNA assays to monitor treatment response. Quantitative real-time PCR or RNA sequencing of E6 and E7 transcripts in cervical swabs or biopsy samples before and after LIF administration can serve as a biomarker for therapeutic efficacy, allowing for personalized dosing and treatment duration.

### Pharmaceutical Manufacturing

The pharmaceutical manufacturing of LIF for use in the present invention follows established principles of biopharmaceutical production, with adaptations to ensure purity, stability, and biological activity. Recombinant human LIF is typically produced in mammalian cell lines such as Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells or human embryonic kidney (HEK293) cells, which are capable of performing the post-translational modifications—particularly glycosylation—necessary for optimal receptor binding and in vivo stability. The LIF gene is cloned into an expression vector under the control of a strong promoter (e.g., CMV), and stable cell lines are generated through antibiotic selection. Large-scale production is carried out in bioreactors under controlled conditions of pH, temperature, and dissolved oxygen, followed by harvest and clarification of the culture supernatant.

Purification of LIF involves a series of chromatographic steps, including affinity chromatography (e.g., using anti-LIF antibodies or receptor-Fc fusion proteins), ion exchange chromatography, and size exclusion chromatography, to remove host cell proteins, DNA, and other contaminants. The final product is formulated in a buffered solution—typically phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with stabilizers such as human serum albumin or sucrose—to maintain protein integrity during storage. Lyophilization may be employed to produce a stable powder for reconstitution prior to administration.

Quality control is paramount and includes assays for identity (e.g., mass spectrometry, N-terminal sequencing), purity (e.g., SDS-PAGE, HPLC), potency (e.g., cell-based bioassays measuring LCR repression or STAT3 phosphorylation), and sterility (e.g., bacterial endotoxin testing, mycoplasma detection). The manufacturing process must comply with current Good Manufacturing Practices (cGMP) as defined by regulatory agencies such as the FDA or EMA to ensure the safety and efficacy of the final drug product.

Formulation development considers the intended route of administration. For systemic delivery (intravenous or subcutaneous), LIF may be formulated as a liquid or lyophilized powder in vials. For localized treatment of cervical or anal lesions, topical gels, creams, or suppositories containing LIF can be developed using mucoadhesive polymers to enhance residence time at the site of action. Controlled-release formulations, such as biodegradable microspheres or hydrogels, may be employed to provide sustained LIF delivery over days or weeks, reducing the frequency of administration and improving patient compliance.

### Experimental Procedures

The experimental procedures underlying the present invention were designed to rigorously evaluate the effect of LIF on HPV LCR activity, oncogene expression, and cellular proliferation in well-characterized cervical cancer cell lines. Human cervical cancer cell lines SiHa (harboring integrated HPV-16 genomes) and CaSki (containing both integrated and episomal HPV-16) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, penicillin, and streptomycin under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). To minimize confounding effects of serum-derived growth factors, experiments were often conducted in reduced-serum or serum-free medium.

To assess LCR activity, a stable HPV-16 reporter cell line was generated by transfecting SiHa cells with the plasmid pLCRGLuc, which contains the complete HPV-16 LCR cloned upstream of a secreted Gaussia luciferase gene, along with a neomycin resistance gene for selection. Following transfection with SuperFect reagent, cells were selected with 400 μg/mL G418 for seven days, and clonal populations were screened for high LCR-driven luciferase expression. Luciferase assays were performed by seeding cells in 96-well plates, treating with LIF or control cytokines, and measuring luminescence in the supernatant using Gaussia luciferase reagent.

Quantitative real-time PCR was employed to directly quantify E6 and E7 mRNA levels. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy kit, quantified by spectrophotometry, and reverse-transcribed. TaqMan-based qPCR was performed using HPV-16-specific primers and probes: for E6, forward primer 5′-CAAACCGTTGTGTGATTTGTTAATTA-3′, reverse primer 5′-GCTTTTTGTCCAGATGTCTTTGC-3′, and probe 5′[6-FAM]TGTATTAACTGTCAAAAGCCACTGTGTCCTGAAGAA[TAMRA]-3′; for E7, forward primer 5′-GTGACTCTACGCTTCGGTTGT-3′, reverse primer 5′-GCCCATTAACAGGTGTTCCA-3′, and probe 5′[6-FAM]CGTACAAAGCACACACGTAGACATTCGTAC[BHQ1]-3′. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method normalized to β-actin.

Cell proliferation was assessed using the CellTiter 96 MTT assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates, treated with LIF or other cytokines in serum-free medium, and after 40 hours, MTT labeling solution was added. Formazan crystals were solubilized overnight, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm (with 650 nm background subtraction) to quantify metabolic activity as a proxy for cell number.

To investigate signaling pathways, intracellular phosphospecific flow cytometry was performed. Cells were treated with LIF, fixed with paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with methanol, and stained with anti-phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) antibodies. Fluorescence was analyzed on a FC500 flow cytometer, and data were processed using Cytobank software to quantify the percentage of cells with activated STAT3.

Statistical analysis was conducted using unpaired t-tests with GraphPad software, with significance defined as P < 0.05. All experiments were performed in triplicate or higher to ensure reproducibility and statistical power.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the ability of Leukemia Inhibitory Factor (LIF) to inhibit HPV-16 long control region (LCR)-driven transcription was evaluated in the cervical cancer cell line SiHa. SiHa cells, which harbor integrated HPV-16 genomes and exhibit high basal LCR activity, were stably transfected with the reporter plasmid pLCRGLuc, wherein the complete HPV-16 LCR drives the expression of secreted Gaussia luciferase. Following selection with G418, a clonal population demonstrating robust luciferase expression was expanded and used for subsequent experiments. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of approximately 5,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere for six hours. The medium was then replaced with serum-free DMEM containing either 1 ng/mL or 10 ng/mL of recombinant human LIF, or an equal volume of PBS as a control. After 24 hours of treatment, 20 μL of cell culture supernatant was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and Gaussia luciferase activity was measured by adding luciferase reagent and recording luminescence for five seconds. The results demonstrated that LIF treatment led to a significant, dose-dependent reduction in LCR-driven luciferase expression, with 10 ng/mL LIF causing approximately a 60% decrease in luminescence compared to the PBS control (P < 0.05). This inhibition was also time-dependent, with maximal repression observed at 24 to 48 hours post-treatment. To confirm that this effect was not an artifact of the reporter system, parallel experiments were conducted in the CaSki cervical cancer cell line, which contains both integrated and episomal HPV-16 DNA. CaSki cells were treated with 1 ng/mL or 10 ng/mL LIF for one, two, or three days, and total RNA was harvested for quantitative real-time PCR analysis of endogenous E6 and E7 mRNA levels. Using HPV-16-specific TaqMan probes, a significant reduction in E7 mRNA was observed as early as day one, with greater inhibition at the higher LIF dose; by day three, both doses produced similar levels of E7 suppression, approximately 50-70% below baseline. E6 mRNA levels followed a comparable trend, confirming that LIF exerts a direct and potent repressive effect on the transcription of HPV oncogenes controlled by the LCR. These findings establish LIF as a novel and effective modulator of HPV LCR activity, with potential therapeutic implications for HPV-associated neoplasia.

### Example 2

This example investigates the signaling mechanism underlying LIF-mediated repression of HPV LCR activity, focusing on the JAK-STAT pathway. SiHa cells were treated with 10 ng/mL recombinant human LIF for various time points (0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 minutes), and the activation status of STAT3 was assessed using intracellular phosphospecific flow cytometry. Cells were fixed, permeabilized, and stained with a fluorescently labeled antibody specific for STAT3 phosphorylated at tyrosine residue 705 (pY705-STAT3), a hallmark of STAT3 activation. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a transient increase in pY705-STAT3, peaking at 30 minutes post-LIF treatment and returning to baseline by 120 minutes, indicating that LIF rapidly and transiently activates STAT3 in SiHa cells. To determine whether this activation translates into transcriptional activity, SiHa cells were transiently transfected with the reporter plasmid 4xM67 pTATA TK-Luc, which contains four tandem copies of a STAT3-responsive element upstream of a minimal promoter driving firefly luciferase expression. After 18 hours, cells were treated with 10 ng/mL LIF or PBS for six hours, lysed, and luciferase activity was measured. LIF-treated cells exhibited a 1.8-fold increase in luciferase activity compared to controls (P < 0.1), confirming that LIF-induced STAT3 phosphorylation is functionally coupled to transcriptional activation. These results suggest that the JAK-STAT pathway, particularly STAT3, is a key mediator of LIF’s biological effects in HPV-transformed cells. While STAT3 is often associated with oncogenic signaling, its activation in this context correlates with growth inhibition and LCR repression, highlighting a context-dependent tumor-suppressive role. This mechanistic insight supports the rationale for using LIF as a therapeutic agent and provides a basis for future studies aimed at optimizing LIF signaling or identifying downstream effectors responsible for LCR repression.

### Example 3

In this example, the functional consequences of LIF treatment on the proliferation of HPV-transformed cervical cancer cells were examined. CaSki cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well in complete growth medium. After allowing six hours for cell attachment, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM containing 10 ng/mL LIF, 10 ng/mL IL-6 (a known LCR repressor that also promotes proliferation), 10 ng/mL EGF (a potent mitogen), or PBS as a control. After 40 hours of treatment, cell proliferation was assessed using the CellTiter 96 MTT assay, which measures the reduction of MTT to formazan by metabolically active cells. The results showed that EGF treatment significantly increased cellular metabolic activity compared to the PBS control, consistent with its known mitogenic effects. In contrast, LIF treatment resulted in a marked reduction in metabolic activity, with LIF-treated cells reaching only about 50% of the absorbance value of untreated controls (P < 0.05), indicating a substantial inhibition of proliferation. To distinguish between cytostatic and cytotoxic effects, additional experiments were conducted to assess apoptosis using Annexin V staining to detect phosphatidylserine externalization, a hallmark of early apoptosis. No significant increase in Annexin V-positive cells was observed in LIF-treated cultures compared to controls, suggesting that LIF induces cell cycle arrest rather than cell death. This cytostatic profile is advantageous for therapeutic applications, as it minimizes tissue damage while effectively halting the expansion of dysplastic or neoplastic cell populations. The data from this example demonstrate that LIF not only suppresses HPV oncogene expression but also exerts a potent anti-proliferative effect on HPV-transformed cells, reinforcing its potential as a non-cytotoxic therapeutic agent for HPV-associated diseases.
