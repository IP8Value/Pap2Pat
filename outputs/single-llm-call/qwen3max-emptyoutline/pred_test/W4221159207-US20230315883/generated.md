## BACKGROUND

The field of data analytics and collaborative research has long been constrained by the tension between the need for organizations to share information for mutual benefit and the legal, ethical, and competitive imperatives to protect sensitive personal and proprietary data. This challenge is particularly acute in domains such as healthcare, financial services, criminal justice, and marketing research, where datasets containing personally identifiable information (PII) or quasi-identifiers (QIDs) cannot be freely exchanged due to regulatory frameworks like the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), or industry-specific confidentiality agreements. In such scenarios, organizations often wish to identify overlapping customer bases, detect fraudulent activities across institutions, or conduct epidemiological studies without revealing their underlying data to each other. The technical problem of enabling such collaboration while preserving privacy has been addressed through various cryptographic and computational approaches, with privacy-preserving record linkage (PPRL) emerging as a critical solution paradigm.

Traditional approaches to record matching have relied on exact matching of unique identifiers such as social security numbers, national identification numbers, or customer account numbers. However, in real-world scenarios, such unique identifiers are often unavailable, inconsistently formatted, or subject to errors in data entry. Furthermore, even when available, the use of such identifiers for cross-organizational matching may be prohibited by privacy regulations or organizational policies. This necessitates the use of approximate or fuzzy matching techniques that can identify similar records based on combinations of non-unique attributes such as names, addresses, dates of birth, or other demographic information. Entity resolution (ER) systems have been developed to address this challenge by employing similarity measures and matching algorithms that can handle variations in data representation, typographical errors, and missing values. However, conventional ER systems require that the participating organizations share their raw data or at least reveal substantial portions of their datasets to a central matching authority, which creates significant privacy risks and regulatory compliance challenges.

The cryptographic community has addressed the privacy concerns associated with data sharing through the development of secure multi-party computation (MPC) protocols, with private set intersection (PSI) being one of the most well-studied primitives. PSI enables two parties to compute the intersection of their private sets without revealing any additional information about elements not in the intersection. While PSI provides strong privacy guarantees for exact matching scenarios, it is fundamentally limited in its ability to handle the fuzzy matching requirements of real-world record linkage problems. The extension of PSI to support approximate matching—commonly referred to as privacy-preserving record linkage (PPRL) or blind data linkage (BDL)—has proven to be significantly more challenging from both theoretical and practical perspectives. Existing PPRL solutions have employed various cryptographic techniques including homomorphic encryption, garbled circuits, oblivious transfer, and Bloom filters, but these approaches have typically suffered from prohibitive computational overhead, high communication costs, or inadequate security analysis.

Bloom filter-based approaches to PPRL have gained popularity due to their computational efficiency, but they introduce significant security vulnerabilities that have been exploited in multiple attack scenarios. The fundamental issue with Bloom filters in the PPRL context is their data-dependent nature, which makes them susceptible to frequency analysis attacks, dictionary attacks, and other forms of cryptanalysis that can reveal substantial information about the underlying data. Furthermore, the lack of formal security analysis for many Bloom filter-based PPRL implementations has left practitioners without clear guidance on the actual privacy guarantees provided by these systems. Alternative approaches using homomorphic encryption or garbled circuits provide stronger theoretical security guarantees but at the cost of computational complexity that renders them impractical for large-scale datasets. For instance, homomorphic encryption-based PPRL solutions have reported processing times of nearly two hours for datasets containing only 20,000 records, while garbled circuit approaches face similar scalability challenges.

The use of third-party mediators in PPRL protocols has been proposed as a means to reduce computational complexity, but this approach introduces additional trust assumptions and potential points of failure. Third-party solutions require that both participating organizations trust the mediator to maintain confidentiality and not collude with either party, which may be unacceptable in competitive or highly regulated environments. Moreover, the involvement of third parties increases the attack surface and complicates the security analysis of the overall system. The ideal PPRL solution would therefore be a two-party protocol that provides strong privacy guarantees without requiring trusted third parties, while maintaining computational efficiency sufficient for practical deployment with large datasets containing millions of records.

Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) has emerged as a promising technique for enabling efficient approximate matching in various computational contexts. Unlike traditional cryptographic hash functions that are designed to produce completely different outputs for even slightly different inputs, LSH functions are specifically designed to produce the same or similar hash values for inputs that are close to each other according to a specified similarity measure. This property makes LSH particularly well-suited for fuzzy matching applications, as it can effectively reduce the dimensionality of the matching problem while preserving the similarity relationships between records. However, the direct application of LSH to PPRL scenarios presents significant privacy challenges, as the hash values themselves may leak information about the underlying data, particularly when the input space has limited entropy or when attackers can mount offline brute-force attacks against the hash values.

The integration of LSH with cryptographic protocols such as PSI represents a natural approach to addressing the privacy concerns associated with LSH while preserving its computational efficiency advantages. However, previous attempts to combine these techniques have either failed to provide adequate security analysis or have resulted in protocols with unacceptable performance characteristics. The challenge lies in designing a protocol that properly composes the approximate matching capabilities of LSH with the strong privacy guarantees of PSI, while ensuring that the resulting system is both theoretically sound and practically efficient. This requires careful consideration of the interaction between the LSH parameters, the choice of PSI primitive, and the overall protocol architecture to ensure that privacy is preserved while maintaining the ability to accurately identify similar records.

The present invention addresses these challenges by providing a novel PPRL protocol that combines LSH and PSI in a manner that provides provable security guarantees against semi-honest adversaries while maintaining computational efficiency sufficient for practical deployment with large-scale datasets. The invention leverages the data-independent nature of LSH to provide robustness against the types of attacks that have compromised Bloom filter-based approaches, while using PSI to hide the LSH outputs and prevent offline attacks. The protocol is designed to operate without trusted third parties, making it suitable for deployment in competitive or highly regulated environments where trust assumptions must be minimized. Furthermore, the invention provides a framework for tuning the trade-off between accuracy and performance through the selection of appropriate LSH parameters, enabling practitioners to optimize the protocol for their specific use cases and privacy requirements.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel privacy-preserving record linkage (PPRL) protocol that enables two parties to identify similar records in their respective private datasets without revealing any additional information about non-matching records. The invention achieves this by combining locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) with private set intersection (PSI) in a novel composition that provides both computational efficiency and strong privacy guarantees. The protocol operates between two parties—a sender party holding a first dataset and a receiver party holding a second dataset—where each party preprocesses their records using LSH to generate band signatures that capture the similarity relationships between records, then applies a PSI protocol to these signatures to identify matching records without revealing the signatures themselves.

The core innovation of the invention lies in the specific manner in which LSH and PSI are composed to provide privacy guarantees that are superior to existing approaches. Unlike Bloom filter-based PPRL methods that are vulnerable to various cryptanalytic attacks due to their data-dependent nature, the invention's use of LSH provides data-independent hashing that is more robust against frequency analysis and dictionary attacks. Furthermore, by applying PSI to the LSH outputs rather than revealing them directly, the invention prevents offline brute-force attacks that could otherwise compromise the privacy of non-matching records. The protocol is designed to be secure against semi-honest adversaries, meaning that it protects against parties who follow the protocol correctly but attempt to learn additional information from the protocol execution.

The invention provides several key technical features that distinguish it from prior art. First, the protocol includes a permutation step that prevents the leakage of information about the ordering or gaps in the datasets, which could otherwise reveal sensitive information about the data distribution. Second, the invention provides a framework for tuning the accuracy and performance characteristics of the protocol through the selection of LSH parameters, specifically the number of bands (B) and the number of hash functions per band (R), which control the trade-off between false positive and false negative rates. Third, the invention supports multiple variants of the basic protocol, including mutual PPRL (where both parties learn their matching records), N-PPRL (where only the count of matching records is revealed), and revealing PPRL (where the actual matched records are revealed for verification purposes).

The invention also includes several performance optimizations that make it practical for large-scale deployment. These include a novel method for weighting record fields that influences the LSH computation without requiring the duplication of shingles, which would otherwise increase computational overhead. This weighting method transforms the hash values of shingles according to their assigned weights, effectively increasing the probability that important fields will influence the final LSH signatures without increasing the number of shingles that need to be processed. Additionally, the invention is designed to be modular with respect to the underlying PSI primitive, allowing practitioners to substitute different PSI implementations based on their specific performance and security requirements while maintaining the same overall protocol structure and privacy guarantees.

Experimental results demonstrate that the invention can process datasets containing over one million records in 11-45 minutes depending on network conditions, which represents a significant improvement over existing PPRL solutions that either cannot scale to such dataset sizes or require substantially longer processing times. The invention has been implemented and tested using elliptic curve Diffie-Hellman (ECDH) based PSI over the NIST P-256 curve, but the modular design allows for the use of other PSI primitives such as those based on homomorphic encryption or oblivious transfer. The protocol has been formally analyzed and proven to satisfy the security requirements of PPRL as defined in the technical literature, providing practitioners with confidence in its privacy guarantees while maintaining the computational efficiency necessary for practical deployment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution for privacy-preserving record linkage (PPRL) that enables two parties to identify similar records in their respective private datasets without revealing sensitive information about non-matching records. The protocol is designed to operate between two parties, designated as the sender party (P_s) and the receiver party (P_r), who hold private datasets D_s and D_r respectively. Each dataset contains records that may include various quasi-identifiers (QIDs) such as names, addresses, dates of birth, or other demographic information that can be used to establish similarity between records. The fundamental challenge addressed by the invention is to enable these parties to determine which records represent the same underlying entities while preserving the privacy of all other information contained in their datasets.

The core architecture of the invention combines two well-established cryptographic and computational primitives: locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) and private set intersection (PSI). The LSH component provides the approximate matching capability necessary for real-world record linkage scenarios, where exact matching is insufficient due to data quality issues, formatting variations, or intentional obfuscation. The PSI component provides the strong privacy guarantees necessary to ensure that no information about non-matching records is revealed during the protocol execution. The novel composition of these two components in the specific manner described herein provides both the computational efficiency required for practical deployment and the security guarantees necessary for regulatory compliance.

The protocol begins with a preprocessing phase where both parties apply the same data normalization and cleaning procedures to their respective datasets. This preprocessing is essential for ensuring that the LSH component can effectively identify similar records, as it removes formatting inconsistencies and standardizes the representation of data elements. The preprocessing may include operations such as case normalization, removal of special characters, standardization of date formats, and other domain-specific cleaning operations. Both parties must agree on the specific preprocessing procedures to be used, as differences in preprocessing would prevent the LSH from correctly identifying similar records.

Following preprocessing, each party applies the LSH function to every record in their dataset. The LSH implementation used in the preferred embodiment is based on the Min-Hash technique applied to k-shingles (k-grams) of the normalized record strings. Specifically, each record is first converted into a set of overlapping k-character substrings (k-shingles), where k is a parameter that can be tuned based on the characteristics of the data. The Jaccard similarity between two records is then approximated by the probability that their k-shingle sets produce the same Min-Hash value. To improve the accuracy and efficiency of the similarity estimation, the invention uses multiple Min-Hash functions organized into bands, where each band consists of R consecutive Min-Hash values that are concatenated and hashed to produce a band signature. The complete LSH output for a record is an ordered list of B such band signatures, where B × R equals the total number of Min-Hash functions applied.

The key insight of the invention is that two records should be considered similar if their LSH outputs share at least one common band signature. This approach provides a tunable approximation of the Jaccard similarity, where the parameters B and R can be adjusted to control the trade-off between false positive and false negative rates. Higher values of B increase the probability of detecting true similarities (reducing false negatives) but also increase the probability of false matches (increasing false positives). Conversely, higher values of R make each band signature more specific, reducing false positives but potentially increasing false negatives. The invention provides a framework for selecting these parameters based on the specific requirements of the application, including the desired accuracy, performance constraints, and privacy considerations.

After computing the LSH band signatures for all records in their datasets, both parties proceed to the PSI phase of the protocol. The sender party P_s applies a secret permutation to their list of band signatures to prevent the leakage of information about the ordering of records in their dataset. This permutation step is crucial for preventing attacks that could infer information about gaps in the data or the distribution of records based on the positions of matching signatures. The permuted list of band signatures is then processed using a PSI protocol, specifically a unidirectional variant of the Diffie-Hellman based PSI (DH-PSI) protocol.

In the DH-PSI protocol, both parties agree on a cryptographic group G and a collision-resistant hash function H. Each party generates their own secret key (sk_s for P_s and sk_r for P_r). The parties then hash and encrypt their band signatures using their respective secret keys and exchange these encrypted values. The receiver party P_r further encrypts the sender's encrypted signatures using their own secret key and returns the doubly-encrypted values to the sender. The sender can then decrypt these values using their secret key to obtain the receiver's singly-encrypted signatures, which can be compared against the sender's own singly-encrypted signatures to identify matches. The security of this protocol relies on the computational hardness of the decisional Diffie-Hellman problem and the one-more-Diffie-Hellman assumption, which ensure that the encrypted values reveal no information about the underlying band signatures except for the intersection.

The final step of the protocol involves the sender mapping the identified matching band signatures back to the original records in their dataset. Because the permutation applied in the first step preserves the grouping of band signatures that belong to the same record, the sender can correctly associate each matching signature with its corresponding record. The result is a set of records from the sender's dataset that have been identified as similar to records in the receiver's dataset, without revealing any information about the receiver's actual records or the specific similarity relationships beyond the fact that matches exist.

The invention provides several variants of this basic protocol to accommodate different use cases and privacy requirements. In the mutual PPRL variant, both parties learn their matching records by running the protocol twice—once with each party in the sender role—or by using a mutual DH-PSI protocol that allows both parties to compute the intersection. In the N-PPRL variant, only the count of matching records is revealed, which is achieved by having the receiver apply a secret permutation to the sender's encrypted signatures that preserves the grouping of signatures belonging to the same record but hides the identity of individual matches. This allows the sender to count the number of matches without learning which specific records match. The revealing PPRL variant extends the basic protocol to allow both parties to learn the actual matched records, which is useful for verification purposes or when the parties have agreed to share the matched data for subsequent analysis.

A key innovation of the invention is the method for weighting different record fields to reflect their relative importance in determining record similarity. In many applications, some fields (such as full names) are more indicative of identity than others (such as zip codes), and should therefore have greater influence on the matching process. The invention provides a novel approach to field weighting that avoids the computational overhead of traditional shingle duplication methods. Instead of duplicating shingles from important fields multiple times, the invention transforms the hash values of shingles according to their assigned weights using a mathematical transformation that effectively increases the probability that important fields will influence the final Min-Hash values. Specifically, for a shingle with hash value h and weight w, the invention computes a transformed hash value h' = y × maxVal, where y = 1 - (1 - x)^(1/w) and x = h/maxVal. This transformation ensures that the distribution of the transformed hash values is equivalent to the minimum of w independent hash values, which is exactly what would be achieved by duplicating the shingle w times, but without the computational overhead of processing additional shingles.

The security of the invention has been formally analyzed and proven to satisfy the requirements of PPRL as defined in the technical literature. The protocol is secure against semi-honest adversaries, meaning that it protects against parties who follow the protocol correctly but attempt to learn additional information from the protocol execution. The privacy guarantees are derived from the combination of the LSH's data-independent hashing properties and the PSI's strong cryptographic security. The LSH component ensures that similar records are mapped to the same or overlapping hash values while dissimilar records are mapped to different values, and the PSI component ensures that these hash values are never revealed to the other party except for the intersection. The permutation step prevents the leakage of information about the data distribution, and the use of standardized cryptographic primitives (such as ECDH over NIST P-256 curves) ensures resistance to known cryptographic attacks.

Experimental evaluation of the invention demonstrates its practical utility for large-scale deployment. Tests conducted on datasets containing over one million records show that the protocol can complete in 11-45 minutes depending on network conditions, which represents a significant improvement over existing PPRL solutions. The implementation uses optimized cryptographic libraries (OpenSSL 1.1.1f) and takes advantage of modern hardware capabilities, including multi-core processors and efficient elliptic curve arithmetic. The modular design of the protocol allows for easy substitution of different PSI primitives or LSH parameters based on the specific requirements of the application, making it adaptable to a wide range of use cases and performance constraints.

The invention has been implemented in C++ and tested on standard server hardware, demonstrating its readiness for production deployment. The implementation includes comprehensive error handling, support for various data formats, and configurable parameters for tuning the protocol's behavior. The source code is designed to be modular and extensible, allowing practitioners to integrate the protocol into existing data processing pipelines or customize it for specific application requirements. The invention represents a significant advancement in the field of privacy-preserving data analytics, providing a practical solution that balances the competing demands of privacy, accuracy, and performance in real-world record linkage scenarios.
