# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of organ transplantation and, more specifically, to computer-implemented systems and methods for predicting post-transplant survival outcomes in kidney transplant recipients. The invention provides an ensemble predictive modeling framework that dynamically selects and applies distinct survival models tailored to specific patient subpopulations or cohorts, thereby improving the accuracy of post-transplant survival predictions compared to existing clinical risk scoring systems such as the Estimated Post Transplant Survival (EPTS) score. The invention leverages advanced machine learning techniques—including random survival forests built from conditional inference trees and regularized Cox proportional hazards models—in conjunction with data-driven cohort stratification based on clinically relevant variables such as recipient age. By integrating both donor and recipient characteristics, the invention enables more precise estimation of graft and patient survival probabilities over clinically meaningful time horizons, such as five years post-transplantation. The disclosed methodology is particularly suited for application within national organ allocation systems to optimize the matching of available donor kidneys to potential recipients based on predicted long-term survival benefit, thereby enhancing the efficiency, equity, and clinical utility of organ distribution policies. Although primarily developed and validated using kidney transplant data, the underlying architecture of the invention is extensible to transplantation of other solid organs, including liver, heart, and lung, where accurate survival prediction is critical for allocation decisions and clinical management.

## BACKGROUND

Organ transplantation remains a life-saving intervention for patients with end-stage organ failure, yet the persistent scarcity of donor organs necessitates sophisticated mechanisms for equitable and efficient allocation. In the United States, the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN), operated under contract by the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS), governs the national system for organ allocation. A cornerstone of modern kidney allocation policy, implemented in December 2014, is the use of the Estimated Post Transplant Survival (EPTS) score—a statistical model designed to estimate the likelihood that a kidney transplant candidate will survive for a specified period following transplantation, typically five years. The EPTS score, derived from a Cox proportional hazards regression model, incorporates four recipient-specific variables: age, diabetes status, prior solid organ transplant, and time spent on dialysis. This score is used in tandem with the Kidney Donor Risk Index (KDRI), which assesses donor kidney quality, to implement a “longevity matching” strategy wherein the highest-quality kidneys (lowest KDRI) are preferentially allocated to candidates with the highest predicted post-transplant survival (lowest EPTS scores).

Despite its widespread adoption, the EPTS model exhibits notable limitations that constrain its predictive performance and clinical utility. First, the model relies exclusively on recipient factors and excludes any donor characteristics, thereby precluding the assessment of donor-recipient compatibility beyond broad longevity matching. Second, the Cox proportional hazards assumption—that the relative hazard between any two individuals remains constant over time—may not hold across diverse patient populations, particularly when interactions between covariates or non-linear effects are present. Third, the model’s simplicity, while advantageous for interpretability and implementation, sacrifices predictive accuracy; published validation studies report a Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) of approximately 0.69, indicating that the model correctly ranks only about 69% of all possible patient pairs in terms of their actual survival durations. Given that tens of thousands of kidney transplants occur annually in the U.S., even modest improvements in predictive accuracy can translate into substantial gains in life-years saved and reductions in graft failure.

Alternative approaches to post-transplant survival prediction have been proposed in the literature. The Life Years from Transplant (LYFT) model, for instance, also employs a Cox framework but includes additional variables and was reported to achieve a C-index of 0.68. More recently, flexible parametric survival models and Bayesian Belief Networks have been explored, yet none have demonstrably surpassed the EPTS model in large-scale validation or been integrated into national allocation policy. A fundamental challenge across these models is their “one-size-fits-all” architecture: they apply a single statistical model to the entire heterogeneous population of transplant candidates, despite strong clinical evidence that factors influencing survival may differ significantly across subgroups defined by age, comorbidity burden, or etiology of renal disease.

Machine learning offers promising avenues to overcome these limitations. Random survival forests (RSF), an extension of Breiman’s random forests to censored time-to-event data, do not require proportional hazards assumptions and can capture complex interactions and non-linear relationships among predictors. However, RSF models applied uniformly across an entire dataset may still underperform if the underlying data-generating processes vary substantially between subpopulations. Ensemble methods that adaptively select or weight models based on patient characteristics could therefore yield superior performance. Prior art has not successfully integrated such adaptive, cohort-specific modeling into a clinically deployable framework for organ allocation.

Moreover, practical challenges in real-world transplant data complicate model development. Datasets often contain high-dimensional features (hundreds of variables), significant missingness, and categorical variables with numerous sparse levels (e.g., primary kidney diagnosis codes). Naïve handling of these issues—such as listwise deletion of incomplete cases or one-hot encoding of high-cardinality categories—can lead to loss of statistical power, overfitting, or biased estimates. Effective preprocessing strategies, including principled missing data imputation, variable clustering based on hazard similarity, and rigorous variable selection, are essential but have not been systematically combined with adaptive modeling in prior transplant survival prediction systems.

Accordingly, there exists a critical unmet need for a robust, accurate, and clinically actionable predictive model for kidney transplant survival that addresses the heterogeneity of the transplant population, leverages both donor and recipient information, adapts its modeling strategy to distinct patient subgroups, and overcomes the data quality challenges inherent in large observational registries. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel ensemble framework that clusters patients into clinically meaningful cohorts, performs tailored variable selection within each cohort, and deploys the optimal survival model—selected empirically based on cross-validated performance—for each subgroup. This approach not only achieves state-of-the-art predictive accuracy but also provides a flexible architecture that can be extended to other organ systems and adapted as new data and clinical insights emerge.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a computer-implemented method and system for predicting post-transplant survival in organ transplant recipients, with particular emphasis on kidney transplantation. The core innovation lies in an adaptive ensemble modeling framework that recognizes and exploits heterogeneity within the transplant recipient population by partitioning it into distinct cohorts and applying a customized predictive model to each cohort. This approach overcomes the limitations of conventional “one-model-fits-all” systems, such as the EPTS score, which apply identical statistical models and variable sets across all patients regardless of clinical differences.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a method for predicting post-transplant survival comprising the steps of: (a) receiving, by a computing device, a dataset comprising historical transplant records, each record including a plurality of recipient variables, donor variables, and a survival outcome with associated censoring status; (b) preprocessing the dataset to address missing data, reduce dimensionality, and group sparse categorical levels based on their estimated effect on the hazard function; (c) partitioning the preprocessed dataset into at least two cohorts based on a clinically relevant stratification variable, wherein the stratification variable is selected through empirical analysis to maximize inter-cohort differences in survival patterns; (d) for each cohort, performing independent variable selection using a combination of permutation importance from random survival forests and regularization via Lasso-penalized Cox regression to identify a parsimonious set of predictive features; (e) for each cohort, evaluating the predictive performance of multiple candidate survival models—including random survival forests with conditional inference trees and Cox proportional hazards models—using cross-validation and selecting the model that achieves the highest concordance index; (f) training the selected model for each cohort on the corresponding subset of the dataset; and (g) deploying the trained ensemble model to generate personalized post-transplant survival predictions for new transplant candidates by first assigning the candidate to a cohort based on the stratification variable and then applying the cohort-specific model.

In a preferred embodiment, the stratification variable is recipient age, and the dataset is partitioned into a first cohort comprising recipients aged 50 years or younger and a second cohort comprising recipients aged 51 years or older. This age threshold is determined empirically by constructing numerous survival decision trees with a single split on age and averaging the optimal split points identified across trees. The first cohort, characterized by higher baseline survival probability, is modeled using a random survival forest with conditional inference trees as base learners, grown with 800 trees and considering four randomly selected variables at each split, subject to a minimum splitting test statistic threshold to control tree complexity. The second cohort, exhibiting lower baseline survival and potentially different risk factor profiles, is modeled using a Cox proportional hazards model regularized with a Lasso penalty, where the penalty parameter is selected to minimize partial likelihood deviance within one standard error of the minimum to favor model parsimony.

The variable selection process integrates two complementary methodologies. Initially, random survival forests are applied to the full dataset (or cohort-specific subsets) to compute permutation importance scores for all candidate variables, ranking them by the decrease in Harrell’s concordance index when each variable is randomly permuted. Subsequently, a Lasso-regularized Cox model is fitted to each cohort, and the penalty parameter is tuned via 10-fold cross-validation. Variables that are both retained in the Lasso model (i.e., assigned non-zero coefficients) and rank within the top twenty by permutation importance are included in the final predictive model for that cohort. This dual-criteria approach ensures that selected variables are both statistically significant in a traditional survival analysis framework and demonstrate robust predictive power in a non-parametric machine learning context.

The invention further encompasses specific data preprocessing techniques critical to model performance. Missing data in non-categorical variables are handled by complete case analysis, while missing values in categorical variables are recoded as an explicit “unknown” category to preserve sample size and avoid bias. High-cardinality categorical variables, such as primary kidney diagnosis, are collapsed into a smaller number of groups using a hazard-based clustering algorithm that merges categories exhibiting similar effects on the hazard function after adjusting for key covariates. This reduces model variance and mitigates overfitting without discarding clinically relevant information.

The resulting ensemble model demonstrates superior predictive accuracy compared to existing standards. When evaluated on a large, contemporary dataset of U.S. kidney transplants performed between 2002 and 2011, the invention achieves a five-year Harrell’s concordance index of 0.724, representing a statistically and clinically significant improvement over the EPTS model’s reported index of 0.69 and an internally validated index of 0.697 on the same dataset. Even when restricted to recipient-only variables—enabling direct comparison with EPTS—the invention maintains a higher concordance index of 0.717. Additionally, the model exhibits favorable calibration, as evidenced by close alignment between predicted survival curves and observed Kaplan-Meier estimates in out-of-sample validation.

The invention’s flexibility extends beyond kidney transplantation. The modular architecture—comprising cohort definition, tailored variable selection, and model selection per cohort—is readily adaptable to other solid organ transplants by retraining on relevant datasets and potentially redefining stratification variables based on organ-specific clinical knowledge. Furthermore, the system can operate in two modes: a comprehensive mode utilizing both donor and recipient variables for optimal prediction when donor information is available, and a recipient-only mode for scenarios such as waitlist prioritization prior to donor identification.

In another aspect, the invention provides a non-transitory computer-readable medium storing instructions that, when executed by a processor, cause the processor to perform the aforementioned method. In yet another aspect, the invention includes a system comprising a memory storing the preprocessed dataset and trained ensemble model, a processor configured to execute the prediction logic, and a user interface for inputting candidate characteristics and outputting survival predictions and associated uncertainty metrics.

By delivering more accurate and personalized survival estimates, the invention enables organ allocation systems to better match donor organs to recipients who stand to gain the greatest survival benefit, thereby maximizing the societal value of scarce transplantable organs. It also supports clinical decision-making by providing transplant teams with refined prognostic information to guide patient counseling, immunosuppression regimens, and post-transplant monitoring strategies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a sophisticated, adaptive ensemble modeling system for predicting post-transplant survival outcomes, particularly in the context of kidney transplantation. The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, including the data infrastructure, preprocessing pipeline, cohort stratification methodology, variable selection protocol, model training and selection procedures, and deployment architecture. While the exemplary embodiment focuses on kidney transplantation using data from the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS), it will be appreciated by those skilled in the art that the core principles and technical framework are generalizable to other domains involving time-to-event prediction with heterogeneous populations.

The foundation of the invention is a comprehensive dataset derived from the UNOS registry, which captures all solid organ transplants performed in the United States. For the purposes of the exemplary embodiment, the dataset includes 163,199 kidney transplant records spanning the period from January 1, 2002, to December 31, 2011. This temporal window was selected based on rigorous statistical evaluation demonstrating a significant shift in post-transplant survival patterns before and after 2002, as confirmed by log-rank tests and visual inspection of Kaplan-Meier survival curves. The inclusion of transplants up to 2011 ensures that all recipients have a complete five-year post-transplant follow-up window, which aligns with the standard time horizon used in organ allocation policy and clinical research. Each record in the dataset comprises up to 487 variables encompassing recipient demographics (e.g., age, sex, race), medical history (e.g., diabetes status, prior transplants, time on dialysis), donor characteristics (e.g., age, cause of death, cold ischemia time), transplant details (e.g., HLA mismatch, panel reactive antibody level), and post-transplant outcomes, including graft failure, patient death, and censoring indicators. All data are fully anonymized in accordance with regulatory requirements to protect patient privacy.

A critical initial phase in the invention’s workflow is data preprocessing, which addresses common challenges in large-scale observational health data. The first step involves variable reduction to eliminate redundant, irrelevant, or poorly measured features. Variables that are missing in more than 95% of observations are removed outright. However, variables previously identified in the scientific literature as clinically important predictors of transplant outcomes—such as recipient age, diabetes status, or donor age—are retained if they are present in at least 80% of records. Additionally, variables that are recorded multiple times (e.g., duplicate lab values) or that are only known after the transplant event (e.g., biopsy results) are excluded to ensure that the final model uses only information available at the time of allocation decision-making. This filtering process reduces the initial 487 variables to a more manageable set of 73 candidate predictors.

Handling missing data is approached through two complementary strategies to ensure robustness. The primary strategy, used for all model development and selection steps, treats missingness in non-categorical (continuous or ordinal) variables by excluding observations with any missing values in these fields—a technique known as complete case analysis. For categorical variables, however, missing values are not discarded but instead recoded into a distinct “unknown” category. This approach preserves sample size and acknowledges that missingness itself may carry prognostic information (e.g., a missing comorbidity code might indicate absence of documentation rather than absence of disease). An alternative imputation strategy using Predictive Mean Matching (PMM) is employed solely for the purpose of benchmarking against models like EPTS that cannot accommodate “unknown” categories; PMM imputes missing values by drawing from observed values of similar cases, thereby creating a complete dataset without introducing parametric assumptions.

A further refinement addresses high-cardinality categorical variables, which pose a risk of overfitting due to sparse cell counts. The primary example is the variable “kidney diagnosis,” which originally contains 75 distinct diagnostic codes. To mitigate this, the invention employs a hazard-based grouping algorithm. Specifically, for each pair of diagnosis categories, a Cox model is fitted that includes the diagnosis variable along with a set of key adjustment covariates (e.g., age, sex, diabetes). Categories whose estimated hazard ratios are not statistically significantly different from one another are merged iteratively until all remaining groups exhibit distinct hazard profiles. This process reduces the 75 original diagnosis codes to 8 consolidated groups, each representing a cluster of etiologies with similar prognostic implications. Analogous grouping may be applied to other high-cardinality variables as needed.

Following preprocessing, the dataset is partitioned into clinically meaningful cohorts to account for heterogeneity in survival determinants. The invention identifies recipient age as the optimal stratification variable through an empirical procedure. One hundred survival decision trees are constructed, each constrained to make a single binary split based solely on recipient age. The split point in each tree is chosen to maximize the difference in survival distributions between the resulting groups, using statistical criteria recommended by Strobl et al. for unbiased variable selection. The average of the 100 optimal split points is calculated to be 48.7 years, which is rounded to 50 years for clinical interpretability. Thus, the dataset is divided into Cohort 1 (recipients aged ≤50 years) and Cohort 2 (recipients aged ≥51 years). This division is clinically justified: Cohort 1 exhibits a five-year Kaplan-Meier survival probability of 93%, reflecting a generally healthier, lower-risk population, while Cohort 2 has a survival probability of 80%, indicating a higher burden of age-related comorbidities and mortality risk.

For each cohort, an independent variable selection process is executed to identify the most predictive features. This process synergistically combines machine learning and traditional survival analysis. First, a random survival forest (RSF) is trained on the cohort-specific data. The RSF consists of an ensemble of 800 conditional inference trees, which are grown using a non-parametric, permutation-based splitting criterion that minimizes variable selection bias. At each node, four variables are randomly selected from the candidate pool, and the variable yielding the strongest association with survival time (as measured by a test statistic exceeding a threshold of 0.3) is chosen for splitting. The permutation importance of each variable is then computed by measuring the decrease in Harrell’s concordance index when the values of that variable are randomly shuffled across observations. Variables are ranked from most to least important based on this metric.

Concurrently, a Cox proportional hazards model regularized with a Lasso (L1) penalty is fitted to the same cohort data. The Lasso penalty shrinks coefficients of less important variables toward zero, effectively performing automatic variable selection. The optimal penalty strength is determined via 10-fold cross-validation, where the penalty parameter that minimizes the partial likelihood deviance (PLD) is identified. To balance predictive performance with model simplicity, the invention adopts a conservative penalty value—denoted Lσ—which corresponds to the largest penalty within one standard deviation of the minimum PLD. This choice yields a sparser model than the absolute minimum (L0) while maintaining comparable accuracy.

The final variable set for each cohort is defined as the intersection of two criteria: (1) variables that receive non-zero coefficients in the Lasso-regularized Cox model at the Lσ penalty, and (2) variables that rank within the top twenty by permutation importance from the RSF. This dual-filter ensures that selected variables are both statistically significant in a parametric survival framework and demonstrate robust predictive utility in a non-parametric ensemble context. For Cohort 1, this process yields a set of 18 variables, including recipient age, donor age, diabetes status, and region of transplant. For Cohort 2, a partially overlapping but distinct set of 18 variables is selected, notably excluding “region” but including additional comorbidity indicators.

With the cohort-specific variable sets established, the invention proceeds to model selection and training. For each cohort, multiple candidate survival models are evaluated using rigorous cross-validation. The primary candidates are: (a) a random survival forest with conditional inference trees, and (b) a Cox proportional hazards model (with or without regularization). Performance is assessed using two key metrics: Harrell’s concordance index (C-index), which measures the model’s ability to correctly rank pairs of patients by their survival times, and the integrated Brier score, which quantifies overall prediction error across the entire follow-up period.

Empirical evaluation reveals that the optimal model differs by cohort. For Cohort 1 (younger recipients), the RSF achieves a higher C-index (0.724) compared to the Cox model, likely because the younger population exhibits more complex, non-linear interactions among risk factors that the flexible RSF can capture. The RSF for Cohort 1 is thus finalized with 800 trees, four variables considered per split, and a minimum splitting statistic of 0.3 to prevent overfitting. For Cohort 2 (older recipients), the Cox model outperforms the RSF (C-index of 0.664 vs. 0.655), possibly because the dominant effect of age and comorbidities follows a more linear, additive pattern amenable to the Cox framework. The final Cox model for Cohort 2 uses the Lasso-selected variables and is trained on the full cohort dataset.

The complete predictive system is an ensemble that routes new transplant candidates to the appropriate cohort based on their age and applies the corresponding model. When a new candidate’s data are input—comprising values for the selected variables, with missing categorical values coded as “unknown”—the system first checks whether the candidate is ≤50 or ≥51 years old. If ≤50, the RSF model generates a predicted survival curve and associated summary statistics (e.g., five-year survival probability). If ≥51, the Cox model computes the linear predictor and transforms it into a survival probability using the baseline hazard estimated from the training data. The system can operate in two modes: a full mode that utilizes all selected variables (including donor characteristics) when donor information is available, and a recipient-only mode that excludes donor variables for applications such as waitlist prioritization.

Validation of the invention demonstrates its superiority over existing models. In ten-fold cross-validation on the UNOS dataset, the ensemble achieves a mean five-year C-index of 0.724, significantly exceeding the EPTS model’s internal validation score of 0.697 on the same data and its published score of 0.69. Even in recipient-only mode, the invention maintains a C-index of 0.717, confirming that its advantage stems not merely from the inclusion of donor variables but from its adaptive architecture and advanced modeling techniques. Calibration is excellent, with predicted survival curves closely tracking observed Kaplan-Meier estimates across risk strata. Sensitivity analyses using PMM imputation confirm the robustness of these findings.

The invention is implemented as a software system comprising several modules. A data ingestion module receives raw transplant records and executes the preprocessing pipeline. A cohort assignment module routes cases to the appropriate modeling stream. Separate model execution modules host the trained RSF and Cox models, interfacing with prediction libraries such as R’s ‘randomForestSRC’ and ‘glmnet’. A user interface allows clinicians or allocation system operators to input candidate and donor characteristics and receive real-time survival predictions, along with confidence intervals derived from bootstrap resampling. The system is designed for integration into existing organ allocation platforms, such as the OPTN’s DonorNet system, via secure APIs.

In summary, the invention represents a paradigm shift in transplant survival prediction by acknowledging and leveraging population heterogeneity through adaptive, cohort-specific modeling. Its combination of rigorous data preprocessing, dual-criteria variable selection, and empirically driven model choice per cohort delivers unprecedented accuracy, enabling more effective organ allocation and improved patient outcomes.
