## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of clinical diagnostics and prognostics, and more specifically to methods for determining prognosis and risk stratification in patients suffering from chronic kidney disease, particularly those undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. The invention further pertains to novel biomarkers based on lipoprotein subfraction analysis, including measurements of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particle diameter and concentrations of specific LDL subfractions, which have been discovered to possess significant predictive value for mortality in maintenance hemodialysis patients. The technical field encompasses clinical laboratory medicine, nephrology, cardiovascular risk assessment, lipidology, and personalized medicine, with particular emphasis on the application of advanced lipoprotein profiling techniques such as ion mobility spectrometry to identify high-risk patient populations who may benefit from targeted therapeutic interventions.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Chronic kidney disease (CKD), particularly in its end-stage requiring maintenance hemodialysis (MHD), represents a significant public health burden with substantial morbidity and mortality. In the United States alone, over 400,000 patients currently receive maintenance dialysis therapy, and this population continues to grow at an alarming rate. The prognosis for these patients remains grim, with approximately two-thirds succumbing within five years of initiating dialysis treatment—a survival rate that is worse than many forms of cancer. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) accounts for nearly half of all deaths in this population, making it the leading cause of mortality among MHD patients.

In the general population, conventional lipid profile measurements, particularly serum levels of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), have long served as reliable predictors of incident atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. These conventional lipid parameters have formed the cornerstone of cardiovascular risk assessment and have guided therapeutic decision-making for decades. However, a perplexing phenomenon known as the "lipid paradox" or "reverse epidemiology" has been consistently observed in patients with chronic diseases such as CKD and chronic heart failure (CHF). In these populations, traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including hypercholesterolemia, fail to predict mortality in the expected manner. Indeed, numerous studies have demonstrated that lower rather than higher serum total cholesterol (TC) or LDL-C levels are associated with increased mortality in both dialysis and CHF patients.

This paradoxical relationship between conventional lipid parameters and clinical outcomes in chronically ill populations has created a significant clinical dilemma. Physicians are left without reliable biomarkers to stratify cardiovascular risk and guide therapeutic interventions in these vulnerable patients. The failure of conventional lipid measurements to predict mortality in MHD patients suggests that the underlying pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease in this population differs fundamentally from that in the general population. This difference may be attributed to the complex interplay of multiple factors including chronic inflammation, malnutrition, oxidative stress, and altered lipoprotein metabolism that characterize the uremic milieu of end-stage renal disease.

Lipoproteins are not homogeneous entities but rather consist of a continuous spectrum of particles that vary in size, density, composition, metabolic behavior, and atherogenic potential. Each major lipoprotein class—chylomicrons, very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL), intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL), low-density lipoproteins (LDL), and high-density lipoproteins (HDL)—can be further subdivided into multiple subfractions based on these physical and chemical characteristics. Small, dense LDL particles, for instance, have been shown to be more atherogenic than their larger, more buoyant counterparts due to their enhanced ability to penetrate the arterial wall, increased susceptibility to oxidation, and reduced affinity for the LDL receptor. Similarly, HDL subfractions exhibit varying degrees of anti-atherogenic activity, with larger HDL particles generally considered more protective than smaller ones.

Despite the growing recognition of the importance of lipoprotein heterogeneity in cardiovascular risk assessment, research in CKD populations has remained largely limited to conventional lipid measurements. A few studies have attempted to evaluate novel lipid parameters such as small LDL subfraction concentration, total LDL particle concentration (LDL-Pc), specific HDL subfractions, and combined measures like the LDL-C/HDL-C ratio or apolipoprotein B/apolipoprotein A-I ratio. However, these investigations have been scarce, often methodologically limited, and have failed to provide consistent or clinically actionable insights. The fundamental question of which specific aspects of lipoprotein metabolism—whether particle concentration, size, or subfraction distribution—best predict mortality in MHD patients has remained unanswered.

The technological limitations of traditional lipid analysis methods have further hampered progress in this field. Conventional lipid profiles measure only the cholesterol content within lipoprotein particles, providing no information about particle number, size, or subfraction distribution. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and gradient gel electrophoresis have emerged as more sophisticated techniques capable of assessing lipoprotein particle characteristics, but these methods have their own limitations in terms of resolution, reproducibility, and clinical applicability. The recent development of ion mobility spectrometry represents a significant technological advancement in lipoprotein analysis, offering direct measurement of lipoprotein particle diameter and concentration with unprecedented resolution and accuracy.

Ion mobility spectrometry separates lipoprotein particles based on their size and charge in the gas phase, allowing for direct counting and sizing of individual particles across the entire lipoprotein spectrum—from small HDL particles to large VLDL particles. This technique provides quantitative information about both particle concentration and diameter for each lipoprotein subfraction, enabling a more comprehensive assessment of lipoprotein metabolism than previously possible. Despite its potential, this advanced analytical method had not been applied to CKD populations prior to the research that forms the basis of the present invention.

The clinical implications of identifying reliable prognostic biomarkers in MHD patients extend beyond mere risk stratification. Such biomarkers could potentially guide therapeutic decision-making, particularly regarding the use of lipid-lowering medications in this population. Current guidelines for lipid management in CKD patients remain controversial, partly due to the lack of clear evidence demonstrating cardiovascular benefit from statin therapy in dialysis patients. The absence of reliable biomarkers to identify high-risk patients who might benefit from aggressive lipid-modifying therapy has contributed to this uncertainty. If specific lipoprotein subfractions or particle characteristics could be shown to predict mortality independently of conventional lipid parameters, this could provide a rational basis for targeted therapeutic interventions aimed at modulating these specific lipoprotein abnormalities.

Furthermore, the discovery of novel lipoprotein biomarkers in MHD patients could shed light on the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms driving cardiovascular disease in this population. Understanding whether the increased cardiovascular risk is primarily driven by atherogenic processes similar to those in the general population, or by alternative mechanisms related to the uremic environment, could inform the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. For instance, if small, dense LDL particles prove to be particularly harmful in MHD patients, therapies specifically designed to shift LDL particle size distribution toward larger, less atherogenic forms might be more beneficial than conventional LDL-lowering approaches.

The present invention addresses these critical gaps in knowledge and clinical practice by providing a novel method for prognosis determination in MHD patients based on advanced lipoprotein subfraction analysis. Through the application of ion mobility spectrometry to a well-characterized cohort of MHD patients followed prospectively for up to six years, the inventors have discovered that specific lipoprotein parameters—particularly LDL particle diameter and concentrations of very small and large LDL subfractions—possess significant predictive value for mortality that far exceeds that of conventional lipid measurements. This discovery represents a paradigm shift in our understanding of cardiovascular risk assessment in dialysis patients and provides a foundation for more personalized and effective clinical management of this high-risk population.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel methods for determining prognosis and stratifying mortality risk in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis through the analysis of specific lipoprotein subfraction parameters. Unlike conventional lipid profile measurements that have proven unreliable in predicting outcomes in this population, the invention utilizes advanced lipoprotein profiling techniques to measure low-density lipoprotein (LDL) particle diameter and concentrations of specific LDL subfractions, which have been discovered to possess significant independent predictive value for mortality in maintenance hemodialysis patients.

The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that smaller LDL particle diameter is strongly associated with increased mortality risk, while larger LDL particle diameter correlates with improved survival in maintenance hemodialysis patients. Additionally, the invention reveals that higher concentrations of very small LDL particles are associated with significantly increased mortality risk, whereas higher concentrations of large LDL particles are associated with decreased mortality risk. These relationships persist even after rigorous adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, demographic variables, comorbidities, nutritional status, and inflammatory markers, demonstrating the independent prognostic value of these novel lipoprotein parameters.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for determining prognosis in a maintenance hemodialysis patient comprising obtaining a biological sample from the patient, analyzing the sample to determine LDL particle diameter using ion mobility spectrometry, and correlating the measured LDL particle diameter with mortality risk, wherein an LDL particle diameter below the median value of approximately 216.5 Ångstroms indicates increased mortality risk compared to diameters above this threshold.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for determining prognosis in a maintenance hemodialysis patient comprising obtaining a biological sample from the patient, analyzing the sample to determine the concentration of very small LDL particles having a diameter in the range of 180.0 to 208.2 Ångstroms using ion mobility spectrometry, and correlating the measured concentration with mortality risk, wherein higher concentrations of very small LDL particles indicate increased mortality risk.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a method for determining prognosis in a maintenance hemodialysis patient comprising obtaining a biological sample from the patient, analyzing the sample to determine the concentration of large LDL particles having a diameter in the range of 220.0 to 233.3 Ångstroms using ion mobility spectrometry, and correlating the measured concentration with mortality risk, wherein higher concentrations of large LDL particles indicate decreased mortality risk.

The invention further provides methods for risk stratification that combine multiple lipoprotein parameters with conventional lipid measurements to provide enhanced prognostic information. For example, the combination of above-median LDL particle diameter with above-median LDL cholesterol concentration identifies patients with the lowest mortality risk, while the combination of below-median LDL particle diameter with above-median LDL cholesterol concentration identifies patients with the highest mortality risk. This interaction between particle size and cholesterol content provides critical insights into the complex relationship between lipoprotein structure and function in the uremic environment.

The invention also encompasses diagnostic kits and systems for implementing these prognostic methods, including reagents, calibration standards, and software algorithms for analyzing ion mobility spectrometry data and generating risk assessments. Additionally, the invention provides methods for monitoring therapeutic response by tracking changes in these novel lipoprotein parameters over time, enabling personalized treatment adjustments based on individual patient responses.

The clinical utility of the invention extends beyond mere risk prediction to inform therapeutic decision-making. By identifying patients at highest risk based on their lipoprotein subfraction profile, clinicians can target intensive interventions to those most likely to benefit, while avoiding unnecessary treatments in lower-risk patients. Furthermore, the invention provides a framework for evaluating the effectiveness of lipid-modifying therapies by measuring their impact on these specific lipoprotein parameters, rather than relying solely on conventional lipid measurements that may not reflect true cardiovascular risk in this population.

The invention represents a significant advancement in the field of nephrology and cardiovascular risk assessment, providing the first reliable biomarkers for mortality prediction in maintenance hemodialysis patients that account for the unique pathophysiological characteristics of this population. By moving beyond conventional lipid measurements to assess the structural and functional properties of lipoprotein particles themselves, the invention offers a more accurate and clinically meaningful approach to prognosis determination and risk stratification in this high-risk patient population.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Sample Collection, Preparation, and Purification

The methods of the present invention require the collection of appropriate biological samples from maintenance hemodialysis patients for subsequent lipoprotein analysis. The preferred biological sample is plasma, although serum may also be used in certain circumstances. Blood samples should be collected under standardized conditions to minimize pre-analytical variability that could affect lipoprotein measurements. Specifically, samples should be obtained in the fasting state on a mid-week dialysis day, preferably before the initiation of the dialysis session to avoid potential alterations in lipoprotein profiles that may occur during the dialysis procedure itself. The timing of sample collection relative to the dialysis session is critical, as the dialysis process can temporarily alter plasma composition and potentially affect lipoprotein particle distribution.

For optimal results, blood should be collected into appropriate anticoagulant tubes, with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) being the preferred anticoagulant for plasma preparation. The blood sample should be processed promptly after collection to prevent degradation of lipoprotein particles or other analytes that might interfere with the analysis. Centrifugation should be performed at standard conditions (typically 1500-2000 x g for 10-15 minutes at 4°C) to separate plasma from cellular components. The resulting plasma should be carefully aliquoted to avoid repeated freeze-thaw cycles, which can damage lipoprotein particles and affect measurement accuracy. Aliquots should be stored at -80°C until analysis, as this temperature provides optimal preservation of lipoprotein structure and function over extended periods.

Prior to analysis, frozen plasma samples should be thawed slowly at 4°C to minimize thermal shock to lipoprotein particles. Rapid thawing at room temperature or higher temperatures should be avoided, as this can lead to particle aggregation or structural changes that might affect ion mobility measurements. Once thawed, samples should be mixed gently but thoroughly to ensure homogeneity, taking care not to introduce excessive air bubbles or cause foaming, which could denature proteins or alter particle characteristics. The samples should be analyzed as soon as possible after thawing, ideally within 24 hours, to minimize any time-dependent changes in lipoprotein composition.

The ion mobility spectrometry analysis requires careful sample preparation to ensure compatibility with the analytical instrument and to achieve optimal separation of lipoprotein particles. This may involve dilution of the plasma sample with appropriate buffers to achieve optimal particle concentration for detection, as well as filtration or other purification steps to remove particulate matter that could interfere with the analysis. The specific preparation protocol may vary depending on the particular ion mobility instrument being used and should be optimized according to the manufacturer's recommendations and validated for the specific application.

Quality control measures are essential throughout the sample collection, preparation, and analysis process to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results. This includes the use of standardized operating procedures, regular calibration of equipment, inclusion of appropriate quality control samples with each analytical run, and monitoring of analytical performance metrics such as coefficient of variation. The ion mobility method used in the present invention has demonstrated excellent analytical performance, with interassay variation for LDL particle diameter measurement of less than 1.0% for higher concentration subfractions, and coefficients of variation ranging from 13% to 20% for HDL and LDL subfractions, and 17% to 30% for lower concentration subfractions such as intermediate density lipoprotein (IDL) and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL).

The specific diameter ranges used for defining lipoprotein subfractions in the present invention are critical for accurate classification and interpretation of results. For LDL subfractions, the following diameter ranges are employed: very small LDL (180.0 to 208.2 Ångstroms), small LDL (208.2 to 214.1 Ångstroms), medium LDL (214.1 to 220.0 Ångstroms), and large LDL (220.0 to 233.3 Ångstroms). These precise definitions enable consistent identification and quantification of specific LDL subfractions across different patient samples and analytical runs, ensuring the reliability of the prognostic information derived from these measurements.

### Determining Prognosis

The determination of prognosis in maintenance hemodialysis patients according to the present invention involves the systematic analysis of specific lipoprotein parameters and their correlation with mortality risk. The primary prognostic indicators identified by the invention are LDL particle diameter, concentration of very small LDL particles, and concentration of large LDL particles. These parameters are measured using ion mobility spectrometry, which provides direct quantification of lipoprotein particle characteristics with high resolution and accuracy.

LDL particle diameter serves as a key prognostic indicator, with smaller diameters indicating increased mortality risk. The analysis reveals a clear inverse relationship between LDL particle diameter and mortality risk, with patients in the highest quartile of LDL particle diameter demonstrating significantly lower mortality rates compared to those in the lowest quartile. Specifically, when comparing the fourth quartile (largest particles) to the first quartile (smallest particles) of LDL particle diameter, the death hazard ratio is 0.45 (95% confidence interval: 0.31 to 1.00) after multivariate adjustment for case mix, conventional lipids, malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome (MICS), and inflammatory markers. This relationship demonstrates a clear dose-response pattern across quartiles, with hazard ratios of 1.00, 0.93 (0.46 to 1.87), 0.43 (0.21 to 0.89), and 0.45 (0.31 to 1.00) for the first through fourth quartiles, respectively.

The concentration of very small LDL particles (diameter 180.0 to 208.2 Ångstroms) represents another critical prognostic parameter, with higher concentrations strongly associated with increased mortality risk. Patients in the highest quartile of very small LDL particle concentration demonstrate a 2.43-fold increased risk of death compared to those in the lowest quartile (hazard ratio: 2.43; 95% confidence interval: 1.03 to 5.72) after comprehensive multivariate adjustment. This finding underscores the particularly harmful nature of very small LDL particles in the maintenance hemodialysis population and highlights their potential as a therapeutic target.

Conversely, the concentration of large LDL particles (diameter 220.0 to 233.3 Ångstroms) demonstrates a protective association with mortality, with higher concentrations correlating with improved survival. Patients in the highest quartile of large LDL particle concentration show a 62% reduction in mortality risk compared to those in the lowest quartile (hazard ratio: 0.38; 95% confidence interval: 0.15 to 0.96) after multivariate adjustment. This protective effect of large LDL particles stands in stark contrast to the harmful effects of very small LDL particles and provides important insights into the differential atherogenic potential of LDL subfractions in this population.

The prognostic value of these novel lipoprotein parameters is particularly significant when compared to conventional lipid measurements, which demonstrate no association with mortality in this population. Conventional measurements of total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides all fail to predict mortality outcomes, consistent with the well-documented "lipid paradox" observed in chronic disease populations. This failure of conventional lipid parameters to predict outcomes highlights the critical importance of the novel lipoprotein measurements provided by the present invention.

The invention also provides enhanced prognostic information through the combination of multiple lipoprotein parameters. For example, the ratio of large to small LDL particle concentration demonstrates significant predictive value, with higher ratios indicating better survival outcomes. Additionally, the interaction between LDL particle diameter and conventional LDL cholesterol concentration provides nuanced prognostic information that would be missed by either parameter alone. Specifically, patients with above-median LDL particle diameter combined with above-median LDL cholesterol concentration demonstrate the lowest mortality risk, while those with below-median LDL particle diameter combined with above-median LDL cholesterol concentration show the highest mortality risk.

The prognostic assessment can be further refined through the use of restricted cubic spline analyses, which reveal the nonlinear relationship between LDL particle diameter and mortality risk. These analyses confirm that the relationship between smaller LDL particle diameter and higher mortality is systematic and robust, providing additional confidence in the prognostic value of this parameter. The net reclassification improvement analysis further validates the clinical utility of these novel parameters, with very small LDL particle concentration demonstrating a net reclassification improvement of 0.22 (P < 0.01), indicating significant improvement in risk stratification compared to conventional models.

The prognostic determination process involves comprehensive multivariate adjustment to account for potential confounding factors that might influence the relationship between lipoprotein parameters and mortality. This adjustment includes case-mix variables (age, gender, race/ethnicity, diabetes, dialysis vintage, modified Charlson comorbidity score, and dialysis dose), conventional lipid measurements, malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome variables (serum phosphorus, albumin, creatinine, calcium, ferritin, hemoglobin, normalized protein catabolic rate, and body mass index), and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha). The persistence of significant associations between the novel lipoprotein parameters and mortality after this comprehensive adjustment demonstrates their independent prognostic value.

The clinical implementation of the prognostic method involves the establishment of appropriate reference ranges and risk thresholds based on the study population characteristics. The median LDL particle diameter of approximately 216.5 Ångstroms serves as a critical threshold for risk stratification, with values below this median indicating increased mortality risk. Similarly, quartile-based classifications provide more granular risk assessment, with the highest and lowest quartiles representing the extremes of risk. These reference values may need to be adjusted based on the specific characteristics of the patient population being evaluated, but the fundamental relationships between lipoprotein parameters and mortality risk remain consistent.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

A prospective cohort study was conducted to evaluate the prognostic value of novel lipoprotein parameters in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients. The study population consisted of 235 MHD patients (106 women, 129 men) with a mean age of 54 ± 14 years who participated in the Nutritional and Inflammatory Evaluation in Dialysis (NIED) Study. All patients were recruited from eight DaVita chronic dialysis facilities in the South Bay Los Angeles area between October 1, 2001, and December 31, 2006. Eligibility criteria included being at least 18 years of age, having undergone MHD treatment for at least 8 weeks, and providing informed consent. Patients with an anticipated life expectancy of less than 6 months due to conditions such as metastatic malignancy or advanced AIDS were excluded from the study.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comprehensively assessed for all participants. Anthropometric measurements included body weight and height, measured to the nearest 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm, respectively. Body composition was evaluated using portable near-infrared (NIR) interactance technology (Futrex 6100, Gaithersburg, MD) with measurements performed on the nonvascular-access upper arm. Laboratory tests were conducted on predialysis blood samples obtained in the fasting state on a mid-week day. The single-pool Kt/V was used to represent weekly dialysis dose, and 3-month averaged values were utilized for all laboratory measurements to minimize short-term variability.

Lipoprotein analysis was performed using ion mobility spectrometry on archived baseline plasma samples. This advanced technique directly measures lipoprotein particle diameter and concentration across the entire lipoprotein spectrum, from small HDL particles to large VLDL particles. The method employs an ion separation/particle detector system that fractionates lipoprotein particles based on their size and charge in the gas phase, allowing for direct counting and sizing of individual particles. The specific diameter ranges used for LDL subfraction classification were: very small LDL (180.0 to 208.2 Å), small LDL (208.2 to 214.1 Å), medium LDL (214.1 to 220.0 Å), and large LDL (220.0 to 233.3 Å).

Patients were followed prospectively for up to 6 years, with mortality serving as the primary outcome measure. During the follow-up period, 71 patients (31%) died. Statistical analyses were performed using Cox proportional hazard models with incremental levels of multivariate adjustment to account for potential confounding factors. The adjustment models included: (1) case-mix variables (age, gender, race/ethnicity, diabetes, dialysis vintage, modified Charlson comorbidity score, and dialysis dose); (2) conventional lipid measurements (LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides); (3) malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome (MICS) variables (serum phosphorus, albumin, creatinine, calcium, ferritin, hemoglobin, normalized protein catabolic rate, and body mass index); and (4) inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha).

The results demonstrated that conventional lipid measurements (total cholesterol, LDL-C, HDL-C, and triglycerides) showed no significant association with mortality in this MHD population, consistent with the well-documented "lipid paradox" in chronic disease states. However, novel lipoprotein parameters measured by ion mobility spectrometry demonstrated significant prognostic value. Specifically, smaller LDL particle diameter was strongly associated with increased mortality risk, with a hazard ratio of 0.45 (95% CI: 0.31 to 1.00) for the highest versus lowest quartile of LDL particle diameter after comprehensive multivariate adjustment. Higher concentrations of very small LDL particles were associated with a 2.43-fold increased mortality risk (HR: 2.43; 95% CI: 1.03 to 5.72), while higher concentrations of large LDL particles were associated with a 62% reduction in mortality risk (HR: 0.38; 95% CI: 0.15 to 0.96).

### Example 2

To further validate the prognostic value of novel lipoprotein parameters and explore their interaction with conventional lipid measurements, a series of subgroup analyses were conducted using the same cohort of 235 MHD patients described in Example 1. The analyses focused on the interaction between LDL particle diameter and conventional LDL cholesterol concentration, as well as the relationship between total LDL particle concentration and LDL particle diameter.

Patients were dichotomized into four mutually exclusive groups based on median values of LDL particle diameter (216.5 Å) and LDL cholesterol concentration (73 mg/dL). The four groups were: (1) below-median LDL-C and below-median LDL particle diameter; (2) above-median LDL-C and below-median LDL particle diameter; (3) below-median LDL-C and above-median LDL particle diameter; and (4) above-median LDL-C and above-median LDL particle diameter. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazard modeling were used to compare mortality outcomes across these four groups.

The results revealed a significant interaction between LDL particle diameter and LDL cholesterol concentration in predicting mortality. Patients in group 4 (above-median LDL-C combined with above-median LDL particle diameter) demonstrated the lowest mortality risk, serving as the reference group. In contrast, patients in group 2 (above-median LDL-C combined with below-median LDL particle diameter) showed a 47% higher mortality risk compared to the reference group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.17 for interaction). This finding suggests that the prognostic implications of elevated LDL cholesterol concentration depend critically on the underlying LDL particle size distribution.

Additional analyses examined the interaction between total LDL particle concentration and LDL particle diameter. Patients were again dichotomized into four groups based on median values of total LDL particle concentration (216 nmol/L) and LDL particle diameter (216.5 Å). After multivariate adjustment, the combination of above-median total LDL particle concentration with above-median LDL particle diameter was associated with a 74% lower mortality risk compared to the combination of below-median total LDL particle concentration with below-median LDL particle diameter. This finding further emphasizes the importance of considering both particle number and size in risk stratification.

The net reclassification improvement (NRI) analysis was conducted to quantify the incremental prognostic value of the novel lipoprotein parameters beyond conventional risk factors. The NRI for very small LDL particle concentration was 0.22 (P < 0.01), indicating significant improvement in risk reclassification compared to conventional models. In contrast, the NRI for LDL particle diameter was 0.05 (P = 0.25) and for large LDL particle concentration was 0.03 (P = 0.47), suggesting that very small LDL particle concentration provides the most substantial improvement in risk stratification.

Restricted cubic spline analyses were employed to examine the nonlinear relationship between LDL particle diameter and mortality risk. These analyses confirmed a systematic inverse relationship between smaller LDL particle diameter and higher mortality, with the relationship appearing to be approximately linear across the observed range of particle diameters. This finding provides additional confidence in the robustness of the association between LDL particle size and mortality outcomes.

### Example 3

Additional analyses were conducted to explore the relationship between HDL subfractions and mortality outcomes in the same cohort of 235 MHD patients. Using the same ion mobility spectrometry methodology described in Example 1, HDL particles were classified into small HDL (76.5 to 105.0 Å) and large HDL (105.0 to 145.0 Å) subfractions based on their particle diameter. Total HDL particle concentration was also measured, along with the concentrations of individual HDL subfractions.

Contrary to expectations based on studies in the general population, higher total HDL particle concentration was associated with increased mortality risk in this MHD population. Specifically, patients in the highest quartile of total HDL particle concentration demonstrated a 2.2-fold higher death risk compared to those in the lowest quartile. This finding aligns with the "lipid paradox" observed with conventional HDL cholesterol measurements in chronic disease populations and suggests that the protective effects of HDL may be compromised or reversed in the uremic environment of end-stage renal disease.

However, when examining specific HDL subfractions, neither small nor large HDL particle concentration demonstrated significant associations with mortality after multivariate adjustment. This suggests that the harmful association observed with total HDL particle concentration may be driven by other factors related to the overall HDL metabolism in MHD patients rather than by specific subfraction characteristics. The lack of association between HDL subfractions and mortality contrasts sharply with the strong associations observed for LDL subfractions, highlighting the differential importance of LDL versus HDL particle characteristics in this population.

Correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationships between conventional lipid measurements and novel lipoprotein parameters. Conventional serum LDL cholesterol concentration showed a modest positive correlation with large LDL particle concentration (r = 0.34; P < 0.01), while conventional HDL cholesterol concentration showed a weak positive correlation with large HDL particle concentration (r = 0.23; P < 0.01). These relatively low correlation coefficients underscore the limited ability of conventional lipid measurements to capture information about lipoprotein particle characteristics and support the need for direct measurement of particle parameters in this population.

The analysis also examined the relationship between the large/small LDL particle concentration ratio and mortality outcomes. After multivariate adjustment, higher ratios of large to small LDL particle concentration were associated with decreased mortality risk, with hazard ratios of 1.0, 0.64 (0.31 to 1.32), 0.51 (0.25 to 1.02), and 0.43 (0.20 to 0.95) across increasing quartiles. This finding provides additional support for the concept that the balance between large and small LDL particles is critically important in determining cardiovascular risk in MHD patients.

### Example 4

To investigate the potential mechanisms underlying the observed associations between lipoprotein parameters and mortality, additional analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between lipoprotein characteristics and markers of inflammation and malnutrition. The malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome (MICS) is a well-recognized condition in MHD patients characterized by the coexistence of chronic inflammation and protein-energy malnutrition, which contributes significantly to poor outcomes in this population.

Patients were classified into MICS-positive and MICS-negative groups based on established criteria: serum albumin < 3.6 mg/dL, C-reactive protein > 10 mg/L, or interleukin-6 > 11 pg/mL. Interaction analyses were performed to determine whether the associations between lipoprotein parameters and mortality differed between MICS-positive and MICS-negative patients. The results showed no significant interaction between MICS status and the prognostic value of LDL particle diameter or LDL subfraction concentrations, suggesting that the associations between these novel lipoprotein parameters and mortality are independent of the malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome.

This finding is particularly important because it demonstrates that the prognostic value of the novel lipoprotein parameters is not merely a reflection of underlying nutritional or inflammatory status, but rather represents an independent predictor of mortality. The independence of these associations from MICS status strengthens the case for the clinical utility of these parameters in risk stratification, as they provide information that is not captured by conventional markers of nutritional or inflammatory status.

Additional analyses examined the potential confounding effects of cholesterol-lowering medication use. Only 12% of patients in the study cohort received statins at some point during the follow-up period, which is consistent with prior studies showing low statin utilization in U.S. dialysis patients. Given the low prevalence of statin use, it was not possible to conduct meaningful subgroup analyses based on statin exposure. However, the low rate of statin use suggests that the observed associations between lipoprotein parameters and mortality are unlikely to be significantly confounded by lipid-lowering therapy.

The study also examined the potential impact of analytical variability on the observed associations. The ion mobility method used in this study demonstrated acceptable coefficients of variation for the primary lipoprotein parameters of interest, with interassay variation for LDL particle diameter of less than 1.0% for higher concentration subfractions. While coefficients of variation were higher for lower concentration subfractions (17% to 30% for IDL and VLDL), the primary parameters of interest (LDL particle diameter and LDL subfractions) demonstrated sufficient precision to support the observed associations.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the findings to different analytical approaches. The use of restricted cubic splines to model the relationship between LDL particle diameter and mortality provided consistent results with the quartile-based analyses, confirming the systematic nature of the association. Additionally, the persistence of significant associations after comprehensive multivariate adjustment across multiple models provides confidence in the robustness of the findings.

## SUMMARY OF RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

The present invention represents a significant breakthrough in the field of cardiovascular risk assessment for maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients, providing the first reliable biomarkers that can accurately predict mortality in this high-risk population. The research underlying this invention has definitively demonstrated that conventional lipid profile measurements, which have long served as the cornerstone of cardiovascular risk assessment in the general population, are completely inadequate for predicting outcomes in MHD patients. This finding is consistent with the well-documented "lipid paradox" observed in chronic disease populations, where traditional cardiovascular risk factors fail to predict mortality in the expected manner.

The key innovation of the present invention lies in the identification of specific lipoprotein subfraction parameters that possess significant independent prognostic value for mortality in MHD patients. Through the application of advanced ion mobility spectrometry, the inventors have discovered that LDL particle diameter and concentrations of specific LDL subfractions—particularly very small and large LDL particles—provide critical information about mortality risk that cannot be captured by conventional lipid measurements. These novel parameters demonstrate robust associations with mortality that persist even after rigorous adjustment for demographic variables, comorbidities, conventional lipid measurements, nutritional status, and inflammatory markers.

The most striking finding is the inverse relationship between LDL particle diameter and mortality risk, with smaller particle diameters strongly associated with increased mortality. This relationship demonstrates a clear dose-response pattern across quartiles of LDL particle diameter, with patients in the highest quartile (largest particles) showing a 55% reduction in mortality risk compared to those in the lowest quartile (smallest particles). This finding is particularly significant because it provides a quantitative measure that can be used for clinical risk stratification, with the median LDL particle diameter of approximately 216.5 Ångstroms serving as a critical threshold for distinguishing high-risk from low-risk patients.

Equally important is the discovery that specific LDL subfractions have dramatically different prognostic implications. Very small LDL particles (diameter 180.0 to 208.2 Å) are strongly associated with increased mortality risk, with patients in the highest quartile demonstrating a 2.43-fold increased risk of death compared to those in the lowest quartile. In stark contrast, large LDL particles (diameter 220.0 to 233.3 Å) are associated with improved survival, with patients in the highest quartile showing a 62% reduction in mortality risk. This differential prognostic value of LDL subfractions provides critical insights into the underlying pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease in MHD patients and suggests that therapeutic strategies should focus on shifting LDL particle size distribution toward larger, less atherogenic forms rather than simply lowering total LDL cholesterol concentration.

The clinical utility of these novel parameters is further enhanced by their ability to interact with conventional lipid measurements to provide nuanced prognostic information. The discovery that above-median LDL cholesterol concentration is associated with the lowest mortality risk when combined with above-median LDL particle diameter, but with the highest mortality risk when combined with below-median LDL particle diameter, represents a paradigm shift in our understanding of lipid metabolism in the uremic environment. This interaction suggests that the cholesterol content of LDL particles in MHD patients may not be proportional to particle size in the same way as in the general population, potentially reflecting fundamental differences in lipoprotein composition and metabolism in the context of end-stage renal disease.

The independence of these associations from markers of malnutrition and inflammation is particularly noteworthy, as it demonstrates that the prognostic value of these novel lipoprotein parameters is not merely a reflection of underlying nutritional or inflammatory status. This finding strengthens the case for the clinical utility of these parameters in risk stratification, as they provide information that is distinct from and complementary to conventional markers of patient health status.

The present invention has several important clinical implications. First, it provides a reliable method for identifying high-risk MHD patients who may benefit from intensive therapeutic interventions. Second, it offers a rational basis for evaluating the effectiveness of lipid-modifying therapies by measuring their impact on specific lipoprotein parameters rather than relying solely on conventional lipid measurements. Third, it provides insights into the underlying pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease in MHD patients, suggesting that therapeutic strategies should focus on modifying LDL particle size distribution rather than simply lowering total cholesterol levels.

The limitations of the present study should be acknowledged, including the relatively small sample size, potential selection bias due to the enrollment of healthier patients, and the observational design that precludes causal inference. However, the strengths of the study—including the relatively long follow-up period, comprehensive laboratory evaluations, detailed clinical assessments, and the use of advanced ion mobility spectrometry for lipoprotein analysis—provide confidence in the validity of the findings.

Future research should focus on validating these findings in larger, more diverse populations of MHD patients and conducting randomized controlled trials to determine whether therapeutic interventions targeting these specific lipoprotein abnormalities can improve clinical outcomes. Additionally, further investigation into the underlying mechanisms linking LDL particle characteristics to mortality in MHD patients could provide valuable insights into the pathophysiology of cardiovascular disease in this population and inform the development of more effective therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, the present invention represents a significant advance in the field of cardiovascular risk assessment for MHD patients, providing the first reliable biomarkers that can accurately predict mortality in this high-risk population. By moving beyond conventional lipid measurements to assess the structural and functional properties of lipoprotein particles themselves, the invention offers a more accurate and clinically meaningful approach to prognosis determination and risk stratification in maintenance hemodialysis patients.
