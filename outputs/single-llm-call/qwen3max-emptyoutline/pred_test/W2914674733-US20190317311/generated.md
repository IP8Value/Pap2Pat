## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to high-throughput optical imaging systems for biological and biomedical applications, and more particularly to a parallelized multi-well plate imaging system that integrates Fourier ptychographic microscopy (FPM) with fluorescence imaging capabilities. The invention further pertains to methods and apparatus for simultaneously acquiring phase and fluorescence images from all wells of a standard multi-well plate—such as a 96-well plate—without mechanical scanning, thereby achieving imaging throughput comparable to conventional plate readers while retaining the rich morphological and molecular information provided by microscopy. The disclosed system overcomes significant optical and engineering challenges associated with parallel imaging through the use of custom-designed miniature objectives, shared illumination architecture, and advanced computational reconstruction algorithms capable of correcting spatially varying aberrations and extending the effective depth of field beyond the native limits of the optical hardware.

## BACKGROUND

Multi-well plate readers have long served as indispensable tools in high-throughput screening for drug discovery, toxicology, genomics, and cell biology. These instruments typically operate on standardized formats such as 96-well or 384-well plates and provide rapid measurements of bulk optical properties—including absorbance, luminescence, and fluorescence—across all wells in seconds. While highly efficient, such readers offer only population-averaged data and lack the spatial resolution necessary to resolve individual cells or subcellular structures. To address this limitation, automated microscopy systems have been developed that scan each well sequentially using a single high-magnification objective mounted on a motorized stage. These well plate imagers enable detailed morphological analysis, single-cell tracking, and multiplexed fluorescence detection, thereby vastly expanding the scope of experimental interrogation. However, this enhanced capability comes at a steep cost in throughput: even state-of-the-art commercial systems require several minutes to image an entire 96-well plate at moderate resolution (e.g., 1.2 μm per pixel), representing a throughput penalty of approximately fifty-fold compared to non-imaging plate readers.

Efforts to bridge this throughput gap have included strategies such as faster stage mechanics, higher-speed cameras, and reduced sampling density. Yet fundamental physical constraints—namely the finite data rate of image sensors and the inertia of mechanical components—impose hard limits on sequential scanning approaches. An alternative paradigm involves parallel imaging, wherein multiple microscope channels operate simultaneously to capture data from all wells at once. In principle, a 96-channel imager could match the speed of a plate reader while delivering full microscopic detail. However, practical implementation of such a system faces formidable obstacles. First, standard multi-well plates exhibit significant geometric warping due to manufacturing tolerances and material deformation, resulting in well-to-well variations in focal plane position that can exceed ±50 μm—far beyond the depth of field (typically <10 μm) of high-numerical-aperture (NA) objectives. Second, the dense packing required for 96 parallel channels (with center-to-center spacing of ~9 mm) necessitates extremely compact objectives, often less than 6 mm in diameter, which are difficult to fabricate with consistent optical quality using conventional glass polishing techniques. Third, even if such miniaturized objectives could be manufactured, inevitable variations in lens alignment, surface figure errors, and material inhomogeneities would introduce spatially heterogeneous aberrations across the array, degrading image fidelity in a non-uniform manner that cannot be corrected by global post-processing.

Conventional adaptive optics or hardware-based focus correction schemes are ill-suited to this context. Wavefront sensors add complexity and cost, while mechanical refocusing of individual channels defeats the purpose of parallel acquisition. Moreover, the presence of liquid media within the wells—typically 100–200 μL of aqueous buffer—introduces additional optical distortions. The curved meniscus formed at the air-liquid interface acts as a dynamic lens, altering the angle and position of incident illumination rays in a spatially dependent manner. This “parallax effect” corrupts the precise angular control required for coherent imaging modalities such as Fourier ptychography, leading to reconstruction artifacts if unaccounted for.

Fourier ptychographic microscopy (FPM) has emerged as a powerful computational imaging technique that addresses many of these challenges. By capturing a sequence of low-resolution intensity images under varying illumination angles and computationally synthesizing a high-resolution complex field via iterative phase retrieval, FPM effectively extends both the numerical aperture and the depth of field of the underlying optical system. Critically, FPM algorithms can simultaneously recover the sample’s complex transmission function and the system’s pupil function—including defocus and higher-order aberrations—enabling on-the-fly correction without additional hardware. Prior work has demonstrated FPM’s ability to correct up to thirty Zernike modes of wavefront error and extend the effective depth of field by threefold or more relative to the native optical sectioning capability.

Despite these advantages, scaling FPM to a massively parallel 96-well configuration presents new challenges not encountered in single-channel implementations. The need for identical illumination conditions across all wells must be reconciled with the physical constraints of LED array design and crosstalk avoidance. Data acquisition rates become prohibitive when multiplied by 96 channels, demanding novel strategies for synchronized capture, transfer, and processing. Furthermore, the use of low-cost, consumer-grade CMOS sensors—necessary for economic feasibility at scale—introduces noise and dynamic range limitations that must be mitigated through algorithmic means.

Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for a high-throughput, multi-modal imaging platform that combines the speed of plate readers with the resolution and contrast mechanisms of advanced microscopy, while robustly accommodating real-world variations in plate geometry, lens quality, and sample preparation. The present invention fulfills this need through an integrated hardware-software co-design that leverages the unique capabilities of Fourier ptychography to overcome the inherent limitations of parallel optical systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a high-throughput, parallelized imaging system and associated methods for simultaneously acquiring phase and fluorescence images from all wells of a standard multi-well plate—such as a 96-well plate—without mechanical scanning. The system, referred to herein as the “96 Eyes” system, comprises an array of ninety-six miniature microscope objectives, each aligned to a corresponding well of the plate, and paired with a dedicated CMOS image sensor. These objectives and sensors are densely packed onto a single custom-designed printed circuit board (PCB) in a 9 mm × 9 mm pitch grid, forming ninety-six independent but synchronized imaging channels. A shared illumination subsystem, consisting of a large-area light-emitting diode (LED) array positioned above the plate, provides spatially programmable oblique illumination for Fourier ptychographic microscopy (FPM). The same optical path is also used for epifluorescence excitation and emission collection, enabling multimodal imaging with intrinsic pixel-level registration between phase and fluorescence channels.

A key innovation of the invention lies in its use of FPM not merely as a super-resolution technique, but as a comprehensive computational framework for correcting the diverse optical imperfections inherent in a massively parallel imaging architecture. Specifically, the FPM reconstruction algorithm is configured to perform embedded pupil recovery, wherein the complex pupil function—including defocus, astigmatism, spherical aberration, and other Zernike modes—is jointly estimated with the sample’s complex field during iterative phase retrieval. This allows the system to compensate for (i) inter-objective aberration variations arising from manufacturing tolerances in plastic-molded lenses, (ii) intra-well focal plane deviations caused by plate warping and meniscus-induced optical path differences, and (iii) illumination angle distortions due to refraction through the curved liquid-air interface within each well. As a result, the system achieves an effective depth of field of ±15 μm for phase imaging—three times greater than the native optical depth of field—thereby accommodating the majority of commercially available 96-well plates without per-well mechanical focusing.

The illumination architecture is optimized for parallel data acquisition. The LED array is designed with a pitch of 3 mm—exactly one-quarter the well-to-well spacing—allowing a single LED to illuminate up to nine adjacent wells simultaneously. By carefully selecting the vertical distance between the LED array and the sample plane (33 mm), and by enforcing a minimum angular separation between active LEDs based on the objective’s numerical aperture, the system ensures that each well receives illumination from only one LED at any given time, thereby avoiding crosstalk while maximizing hardware utilization. This “parallel illumination scheme” reduces the total number of required LEDs by 75% compared to a naive per-well design and minimizes camera idle time during acquisition.

For fluorescence imaging, the system employs side-mounted excitation sources that deliver homogenized blue light (centered at 465 nm) through the plate walls at extreme angles, ensuring that direct excitation light is blocked by the internal apertures of the objectives. Emitted green fluorescence (centered at 535 nm) is collected through the same objectives and filtered before reaching the sensors. Due to the limited depth of field in fluorescence mode, the system performs a rapid z-stack scan over a 100 μm range (±50 μm about the nominal focal plane) with 25 μm steps, selecting the sharpest frame per well for final output. Digital frame averaging is applied to suppress temporal noise, enabling fluorescence acquisition across the entire plate in under 30 seconds.

Data handling is managed through a high-bandwidth pipeline that captures raw intensity frames at 340 MB/s, transfers them via four frame grabber boards to a workstation equipped with a GPU array (e.g., four NVIDIA Tesla K80 cards), and reconstructs phase images using a parallelized FPM algorithm. The reconstruction process partitions each well’s field of view into overlapping tiles, groups corresponding tiles across all 96 wells, and processes them in batch on the GPUs to maximize throughput. Intermediate complex-valued images are stitched using feather blending to produce seamless, high-resolution mosaics.

In operation, the system acquires a full set of 64 FPM illumination patterns across all 96 wells in 90 seconds, yielding phase images with 1.2 μm lateral resolution over a 1.1 mm × 0.85 mm field of view per well. Fluorescence images are acquired in 12 seconds (assuming 10-frame averaging per z-step and five z-steps). The combined multimodal dataset enables label-free morphological analysis via phase contrast alongside specific molecular localization via fluorescence, all with perfect spatial correspondence and without user intervention for focus or alignment.

The invention thus provides a transformative solution for high-content screening, combining unprecedented speed with microscopic detail, while robustly handling real-world variability in consumables and sample preparation through intelligent computational optics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### I. Introduction

The present invention addresses a longstanding bottleneck in high-throughput cell biology: the trade-off between imaging speed and information content. Traditional multi-well plate readers sacrifice spatial resolution for throughput, while automated microscopes preserve resolution at the cost of speed. The disclosed system breaks this dichotomy by integrating parallel hardware with computational imaging, specifically Fourier ptychographic microscopy (FPM), to deliver both speed and detail. Unlike prior art that treats optical imperfections as nuisances to be minimized through precision engineering, the present invention embraces variability as an expected condition and corrects it algorithmically. This philosophy enables the use of low-cost, mass-producible components—such as plastic-molded objectives and consumer CMOS sensors—while maintaining scientific-grade image quality. The following sections describe the system architecture, optical design, illumination strategy, aberration characterization, and image reconstruction pipeline in detail, demonstrating how the invention achieves its performance objectives.

### II. Imaging System for Fourier Ptychographic (FP) Imaging and Fluorescent Imaging

The imaging system of the present invention comprises three principal subsystems: (1) a 96-channel miniature microscope array, (2) a shared programmable illumination module, and (3) a multimodal detection and processing unit. The microscope array is implemented as a single custom PCB measuring approximately 90 mm × 90 mm, populated with ninety-six identical imaging units arranged in an 8 × 12 grid matching the standard 96-well plate footprint. Each unit consists of a custom-designed finite-conjugate objective lens and a monolithic CMOS image sensor. The objectives are fabricated via precision injection molding of optical-grade plastic, chosen for its cost-effectiveness and suitability for high-volume production. Each objective provides 4× magnification, a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.23, and a working distance of 4 mm, with a fixed object-to-image distance of 48 mm. This unusual combination of low magnification and relatively high NA is enabled by a shortened tube length of 34 mm, which enhances compactness without sacrificing light-gathering ability. The CMOS sensors are selected from consumer-grade models due to their small die size (enabling tight packing) and low unit cost; each sensor features a pixel pitch of 1.6 μm, which, after 4× magnification, corresponds to an effective object-space sampling of 0.4 μm per pixel.

Surrounding each objective-sensor pair is a wafer-shaped lens hood array fabricated from black anodized aluminum or opaque polymer. These hoods serve a dual purpose: they prevent stray light from adjacent channels from reaching the sensor (critical for low-signal fluorescence detection), and they provide mechanical rigidity to the densely packed assembly. The entire sensor board is mounted rigidly within the instrument chassis, with no moving parts during acquisition, ensuring long-term alignment stability.

The illumination module is positioned above the sample plane and consists of a planar array of 1120 surface-mount LEDs, each emitting at a peak wavelength of 530 nm with a spectral bandwidth of ±10 nm. The LEDs are arranged in a rectangular grid with 3 mm pitch, which is precisely one-quarter the 12 mm well-to-well spacing of a standard 96-well plate. This geometric relationship allows the illumination pattern to be tiled efficiently across the plate. The LED array is elevated 33 mm above the nominal sample plane, a distance chosen to satisfy the coherence redundancy condition for ptychographic imaging: specifically, the transverse coherence length at the sample plane, given by \( x_d \approx 2\lambda h / w \) (where \( \lambda = 530 \) nm, \( h = 33 \) mm, and \( w = 250 \) μm is the LED emitter size), is calculated to be approximately 140 μm, ensuring sufficient overlap between adjacent illumination pupils for stable phase retrieval.

For fluorescence excitation, two high-power laser diodes (e.g., Nichia NUBM07E) are coupled into liquid light guides and directed toward the sides of the well plate at grazing incidence. The excitation light (465 nm ± 2.5 nm) enters the plate through the polystyrene or cyclic olefin copolymer (COC) walls and illuminates the sample from the periphery. Because the angle of incidence exceeds the acceptance cone of the objectives (NA = 0.23 corresponds to a half-angle of ~13°), direct excitation light is blocked by the objective’s internal aperture stop. Only fluorescence emitted isotropically from the sample is collected by the objectives. An emission filter (e.g., Chroma 59022m, 535 nm center, 50 nm bandwidth) is placed in front of each sensor to reject residual excitation light and ambient background.

The detection and processing unit includes four high-speed frame grabber boards connected via Camera Link or CoaXPress interfaces to the sensor array, providing aggregate data throughput of 340 MB/s. Raw image data is streamed directly to a workstation equipped with multiple GPUs (e.g., NVIDIA Tesla K80, each containing two GK210 processors) and large solid-state storage (≥15 TB). Custom software controls the synchronized activation of LEDs (for FPM) or lasers (for fluorescence), manages z-stage motion via a piezoelectric actuator (range: 300 μm, repeatability: 2.5 μm), and orchestrates the parallel reconstruction pipeline described below.

### III. Variable-Illumination Fourier Ptychographic Imaging Methods

The core imaging methodology of the invention is a variable-illumination Fourier ptychographic protocol adapted for massively parallel operation. In a standard FPM experiment, a sequence of low-resolution intensity images is captured while shifting the illumination angle in a raster pattern across the back focal plane of the objective. The present invention modifies this protocol to accommodate the shared illumination architecture and liquid-filled wells.

First, the system calibrates the effective illumination angles for each well, accounting for refraction through the liquid medium and meniscus curvature. Using a ray-tracing model based on Snell’s law, the system computes the mapping from LED physical positions to incident angles at the sample plane, considering the 3 mm thickness of aqueous buffer and the concave meniscus profile (typically with a radius of curvature of ~2 mm). This calibration is performed once per plate type and stored in a lookup table.

During acquisition, the system activates LEDs in a sparse pattern such that no two LEDs whose illumination cones overlap at any well are turned on simultaneously. Given the objective NA of 0.23, the maximum allowable angular separation between active LEDs is determined by ensuring that the edge of one LED’s illumination cone does not intersect the center of another’s within the same well. This results in a rectangular activation grid with a period of m = 4 LEDs in both x and y directions. Thus, in each acquisition cycle, 7 × 7 = 49 LEDs are activated in a staggered fashion across the array, illuminating all 96 wells without crosstalk. Sixty-four such cycles are performed to collect a complete ptychographic dataset.

For each well, the 64 raw intensity frames are processed using a GPU-accelerated FPM algorithm that implements the extended ptychographic iterative engine with embedded pupil recovery (EPIE-EPRY). The algorithm initializes the sample estimate as a uniform field and the pupil as a circular aperture. In each iteration, it propagates the current sample estimate to the pupil plane, multiplies by the current pupil estimate, propagates back to the image plane, and enforces consistency with the measured intensity. Simultaneously, it updates the pupil estimate using gradient descent on the reconstruction residual. Crucially, the algorithm incorporates the calibrated illumination angles and allows the pupil function to vary spatially across the field of view to account for off-axis aberrations.

To handle the large data volume, the reconstruction is parallelized across wells and across field segments. Each well’s 1.1 mm × 0.85 mm field is divided into 80 overlapping tiles of 110 μm × 110 μm (5% overlap). All tiles occupying the same relative position across the 96 wells are grouped into a single batch and processed together on one GPU. This “position-wise batching” exploits the fact that all wells experience identical illumination sequences, enabling vectorized operations and memory coalescing. After reconstruction, the complex-valued tiles are stitched using feather blending to suppress boundary artifacts, yielding a full-field phase and amplitude image per well.

For samples that are significantly out of focus (e.g., due to plate warping), a two-stage refocusing procedure is employed. First, a coarse estimate of the defocus distance is obtained by performing a linear search over a range of ±150 μm and selecting the offset that minimizes the FPM reconstruction residual. This estimate is then used to initialize the pupil function with a quadratic phase term, which is subsequently refined during joint pupil-sample recovery. This approach ensures convergence even when the initial defocus exceeds the native depth of field by an order of magnitude.

Fluorescence images are acquired separately by activating the side-mounted lasers and capturing a z-stack of 5 frames spaced 25 μm apart over a 100 μm range. At each z-position, 10 video frames are averaged to reduce temporal noise. The frame with the highest sharpness metric (e.g., variance of Laplacian) is selected as the final fluorescence image for that well. Because both FPM and fluorescence use the same optical path, the resulting images are intrinsically registered, requiring no post-hoc alignment.

Through this integrated hardware-software approach, the invention achieves high-throughput, aberration-corrected, multimodal imaging of entire 96-well plates in under two minutes, setting a new standard for high-content screening platforms.
