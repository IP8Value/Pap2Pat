# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of vascular access devices for hemodialysis and, more particularly, to an improved configuration of brachio-axillary arteriovenous grafts that minimizes pathological flow fields at the graft-to-vein anastomosis. Specifically, the invention provides a surgically implantable arteriovenous graft having a predetermined and optimized angle of attachment between the graft conduit and the recipient vein—preferably the axillary vein—to reduce the incidence of pathologically low and high wall shear stress conditions that are known to promote myointimal hyperplasia, stenosis, and eventual graft thrombosis. The invention further encompasses methods for manufacturing such grafts with pre-set anastomotic angles tailored to patient-specific anatomical parameters, as well as surgical techniques for implanting these grafts in a manner that ensures adherence to the optimal hemodynamic configuration. This innovation addresses a critical unmet need in the management of end-stage renal disease by enhancing the long-term patency and functional durability of synthetic arteriovenous grafts used in patients who are not candidates for autologous arteriovenous fistulas.

## BACKGROUND

Hemodialysis is a life-sustaining therapy for more than 300,000 individuals in the United States annually who suffer from end-stage renal disease. This procedure relies on the creation of a reliable vascular access point through which blood can be continuously withdrawn, filtered extracorporeally to remove metabolic waste and excess fluid, and then returned to the patient’s circulatory system. Among the available vascular access modalities, arteriovenous (AV) grafts serve as a crucial alternative for patients whose native vasculature is unsuitable for the creation of an autologous AV fistula—the gold standard for hemodialysis access. AV grafts typically consist of a synthetic conduit, most commonly fabricated from expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE), which is surgically implanted to connect an artery directly to a vein, thereby creating a high-flow shunt that facilitates repeated percutaneous cannulation during dialysis sessions.

In clinical practice, one of the most frequently employed configurations for upper extremity AV access is the brachio-axillary graft, wherein the graft is anastomosed proximally to the brachial artery near the elbow and distally to the axillary vein in the proximal upper arm. This anatomical arrangement allows for comfortable patient positioning during dialysis while providing sufficient blood flow rates—typically ranging from 600 to 1,000 milliliters per minute—to support efficient solute clearance. To mitigate the risk of arterial steal syndrome, wherein excessive diversion of arterial blood into the low-resistance graft circuit compromises distal limb perfusion, these grafts are often tapered, with an internal diameter of approximately 4 millimeters at the arterial anastomosis gradually increasing to 7 millimeters or more at the venous anastomosis. Despite these design considerations, brachio-axillary AV grafts exhibit alarmingly high failure rates, with up to 30% of grafts becoming non-functional within the first year due to thrombosis or stenosis.

The primary site of graft failure is the venous anastomosis, where more than 80% of high-grade stenoses develop. This localized pathology is strongly associated with abnormal hemodynamic forces acting on the vessel wall, particularly deviations from the physiological range of wall shear stress (WSS). Wall shear stress, defined as the tangential force exerted by flowing blood on the endothelial lining of the vessel, plays a critical role in maintaining vascular homeostasis. It is well established that both abnormally low WSS (typically below 50 s⁻¹ shear strain rate) and excessively high WSS (above 1,000 s⁻¹) are pro-thrombotic and pro-inflammatory. Low shear environments promote fibrin deposition, platelet adhesion, and endothelial dysfunction, while high shear conditions can cause direct mechanical injury to the endothelium, triggering platelet activation and the release of growth factors that stimulate smooth muscle cell proliferation. Over time, this leads to the development of myointimal hyperplasia—a pathological thickening of the vessel wall characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells—which narrows the lumen and ultimately results in graft occlusion.

Although the material composition and macroscopic geometry of AV grafts have been refined over decades, the surgical technique used to create the venous anastomosis remains largely empirical and operator-dependent. Surgeons typically select the anastomotic angle based on intraoperative anatomical constraints, such as the trajectory of the tunneled graft, the depth and mobility of the target vein, and the patient’s body habitus, without standardized guidance regarding the optimal angle for hemodynamic performance. Consequently, the graft-to-vein anastomosis angle can vary widely—from near-perpendicular (90°) to highly oblique (as low as 13°)—with no consensus on which configuration best preserves laminar flow and minimizes pathological shear stress. Prior attempts to improve graft outcomes through pharmacological interventions (e.g., antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy) or structural modifications (e.g., venous cuffs or stent grafts) have yielded inconsistent or negligible clinical benefits, underscoring the need for a biomechanically informed approach to graft design and implantation.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has emerged as a powerful tool for simulating blood flow in complex vascular geometries and identifying hemodynamic risk factors that are not readily apparent through clinical imaging alone. While CFD studies have previously examined the impact of anastomosis angle on flow patterns in autologous AV fistulas, analogous investigations in synthetic AV grafts—particularly brachio-axillary configurations—have been notably absent. This gap in knowledge has hindered the development of evidence-based surgical guidelines for graft placement. The present invention arises from a comprehensive CFD analysis that systematically evaluates the relationship between graft-to-vein anastomosis angle and the distribution of pathological shear stress across the venous wall over a full cardiac cycle. By modeling a range of clinically relevant anastomotic angles using physiologically accurate boundary conditions and non-Newtonian blood rheology, the inventors have identified a specific angular range that minimizes both low and high shear stress pathologies, thereby offering a novel and actionable solution to the persistent problem of early AV graft failure.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides an improved arteriovenous graft for hemodialysis access, specifically configured to minimize pathological flow conditions at the graft-to-vein anastomosis by employing a predetermined and optimized angle of attachment between the graft conduit and the recipient vein. In particular, the invention discloses that a graft-to-vein anastomosis angle of approximately 30 degrees significantly reduces the incidence of both pathologically low wall shear stress (below 50 s⁻¹) and pathologically high wall shear stress (above 1,000 s⁻¹) in the adjacent venous segment, as compared to more acute or obtuse angles. This optimal angle balances the competing hemodynamic risks associated with extreme anastomotic configurations: highly oblique angles (e.g., less than 20 degrees) promote regions of flow stagnation and low shear, while near-perpendicular angles (e.g., greater than 40 degrees) generate high-velocity jets that impinge on the venous wall, causing elevated shear stress and potential endothelial injury.

The invention encompasses a surgically implantable graft assembly comprising a tapered synthetic conduit, preferably fabricated from biocompatible materials such as expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE), with a fixed geometric configuration that predefines the angle at which the venous end of the graft will be joined to the target vein during implantation. This pre-set angle is engineered to correspond to the optimal hemodynamic profile identified through computational fluid dynamics simulations, ensuring consistent and reproducible surgical outcomes regardless of operator variability. In certain embodiments, the graft may be provided as part of a modular system that includes multiple variants with slightly different anastomotic angles (e.g., 25°, 30°, and 35°) to accommodate natural variations in patient anatomy, such as differences in axillary vein diameter or depth. The graft may also incorporate radiopaque markers or alignment guides to assist surgeons in achieving precise orientation during the anastomotic procedure.

Additionally, the invention includes a method for manufacturing such optimized grafts, wherein the venous end of the graft conduit is pre-formed—through molding, laser cutting, or other precision fabrication techniques—to present a beveled or elliptical opening that, when sutured to a cylindrical vein of a specified diameter, results in a junction angle of approximately 30 degrees. The method further involves quality control protocols to verify the dimensional accuracy of the pre-formed angle prior to sterilization and packaging. The invention also provides a surgical method for implanting the optimized graft, which includes steps for selecting the appropriate graft variant based on preoperative imaging of the target vein, tunneling the graft in a trajectory that aligns with the pre-defined angle, and performing the venous anastomosis in a manner that maintains the intended hemodynamic configuration.

By standardizing the graft-to-vein anastomosis angle to a value that has been demonstrated to minimize pathological shear stress, the present invention offers a significant advance over current clinical practice, where anastomotic angles are selected arbitrarily and without hemodynamic consideration. This approach has the potential to substantially reduce the incidence of myointimal hyperplasia, stenosis, and thrombosis at the venous anastomosis, thereby improving both primary and secondary patency rates of brachio-axillary AV grafts. Enhanced graft longevity translates directly into fewer interventional procedures, reduced hospitalizations, lower healthcare costs, and improved quality of life for hemodialysis patients who depend on reliable vascular access for survival.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to an arteriovenous graft system specifically engineered to optimize hemodynamic conditions at the venous anastomosis, thereby reducing the risk of graft failure due to stenosis and thrombosis. Central to this innovation is the recognition that the angle at which the graft conduit is joined to the recipient vein—referred to herein as the graft-to-vein anastomosis angle—is a critical determinant of local wall shear stress and, consequently, of long-term graft patency. Through rigorous computational modeling grounded in physiological principles, the inventors have determined that an anastomosis angle of approximately 30 degrees provides a unique balance that minimizes exposure of the venous wall to both pathologically low and pathologically high shear strain rates over the course of a cardiac cycle. This finding challenges conventional surgical practice, which treats the anastomosis angle as a variable to be adjusted ad hoc based on anatomical convenience rather than as a parameter to be optimized for hemodynamic health.

The graft system of the present invention comprises a synthetic conduit formed from a biocompatible, non-thrombogenic material suitable for chronic implantation in the human vasculature. Expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) is the preferred material due to its proven track record in vascular access applications, excellent biocompatibility, and resistance to kinking and compression. However, other materials such as polyurethane, silicone, or composite structures incorporating bioactive coatings may also be employed without departing from the scope of the invention. The conduit is designed with a tapered geometry to accommodate the natural transition from arterial to venous dimensions and to modulate flow velocity. Specifically, the arterial end of the graft has an internal diameter of about 4 millimeters, matching the typical inner diameter of the brachial artery, while the venous end has an internal diameter of about 7 millimeters, which is compatible with the average axillary vein diameter of 6 to 10 millimeters. The length of the graft is typically between 140 and 160 millimeters, allowing for subcutaneous tunneling in the upper arm while maintaining sufficient distance between the arterial and venous anastomoses to prevent recirculation.

What distinguishes the present invention from prior art grafts is the intentional pre-configuration of the venous end to enforce a specific anastomosis angle upon surgical implantation. In traditional grafts, the venous end is cut intraoperatively by the surgeon to match the size and orientation of the target vein, resulting in unpredictable and often suboptimal angles. In contrast, the graft of the present invention features a pre-formed venous opening that is geometrically shaped—such as an ellipse with a defined major axis—to ensure that when it is sutured circumferentially to a cylindrical vein of a given diameter, the resulting junction assumes an angle of approximately 30 degrees relative to the longitudinal axis of the vein. For example, if the target axillary vein has an inner diameter of 8 millimeters, the pre-formed opening on the graft may be an ellipse with a minor axis of 7 millimeters (matching the graft’s internal diameter) and a major axis of 15 millimeters; this specific elliptical geometry corresponds to a 30-degree intersection angle between the conical graft and the cylindrical vein, as derived from solid geometry principles governing the intersection of two cylinders at an oblique angle.

To facilitate clinical adoption, the invention may be implemented as a family of graft variants, each pre-configured for a different nominal vein diameter. For instance, a first variant may be designed for veins with diameters of 6–7 mm (requiring a major axis of approximately 13–14 mm), a second for 8 mm veins (major axis ~15 mm), and a third for 9–10 mm veins (major axis ~17–18 mm). Each variant would maintain the same target anastomosis angle of 30 degrees, ensuring consistent hemodynamic performance across a range of anatomies. The grafts may be color-coded or labeled with size indicators to assist in selection during surgery. Furthermore, the graft may include integrated alignment features, such as radiopaque markers embedded along the graft wall or external ridges molded into the graft surface, that provide visual or tactile cues to the surgeon regarding the correct rotational and angular orientation during tunneling and anastomosis.

The manufacturing process for these optimized grafts involves precision engineering techniques to achieve the required geometric fidelity. One approach entails using computer numerical control (CNC) machining or laser cutting to shape the venous end of the ePTFE tube into the desired elliptical profile. Alternatively, the graft may be molded in its final tapered and angled configuration using specialized dies that replicate the optimal hemodynamic geometry. Quality assurance is performed using optical metrology or micro-computed tomography to verify that the major and minor axes of the venous opening conform to specifications within a tolerance of ±0.2 millimeters, ensuring that the actual implanted angle remains within the therapeutic window of 25 to 35 degrees. The grafts are then terminally sterilized using ethylene oxide or gamma irradiation and packaged in sterile, single-use containers along with instructions for use that emphasize the importance of preserving the pre-defined angle during implantation.

Surgical implantation of the graft follows standard brachio-axillary access protocols but with added emphasis on anatomical planning and trajectory control. Preoperative ultrasound is used to measure the diameter and depth of the target axillary vein, allowing the surgeon to select the appropriate graft variant from the available inventory. During surgery, after exposing the brachial artery and axillary vein, the graft is tunneled subcutaneously in a path that aligns the pre-formed venous opening with the vein in a manner that naturally achieves the 30-degree angle without requiring intraoperative modification of the graft end. Standard end-to-side anastomotic techniques are then employed, using non-absorbable sutures to secure the graft to the vein. Because the graft’s geometry is pre-optimized, the surgeon is relieved of the burden of estimating the “best” angle on the fly, leading to more consistent outcomes across different operators and institutions.

The hemodynamic superiority of the 30-degree configuration has been validated through transient, three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics simulations that model pulsatile blood flow under physiologically realistic conditions. These simulations incorporate non-Newtonian blood rheology using the Bird-Carreau constitutive model, account for the full cardiac cycle via Fourier-reconstructed inlet velocity waveforms derived from clinical Doppler data, and resolve near-wall flow features using boundary layer mesh refinement. Quantitative metrics—Γ_low and Γ_high—were developed to integrate the spatial and temporal extent of pathological shear exposure over the venous wall. Results consistently showed that angles below 30 degrees led to expanding regions of low shear (Γ_low increased sharply), while angles above 30 degrees caused escalating high shear impingement (Γ_high rose monotonically). Only at 30 degrees was the combined pathological burden minimized, confirming this angle as the hemodynamic optimum.

It is important to note that while the present disclosure focuses on brachio-axillary grafts, the underlying principle—that anastomosis angle critically influences shear-mediated pathology—is broadly applicable to other AV graft configurations, such as brachiocephalic or femoral-venous placements. The specific optimal angle may vary slightly depending on local hemodynamics (e.g., flow rates, vessel diameters), but the methodology of using CFD to identify and then mechanically enforce that angle remains universally valid. Thus, the invention represents not only a specific product improvement but also a paradigm shift toward biomechanically guided vascular access design.

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the development, validation, and implementation of the optimized arteriovenous graft described herein. These examples are provided for explanatory purposes and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention.

**Example 1: Computational Fluid Dynamics Model Development**

A series of idealized three-dimensional models of brachio-axillary arteriovenous grafts were constructed using ANSYS DesignModeler software. Each model consisted of a straight arterial segment (4 mm inner diameter), a tapered graft conduit (4 mm at arterial end, 7 mm at venous end, 150 mm length), and a straight venous segment (8 mm inner diameter, 72 mm total length). Six distinct graft-to-vein anastomosis angles were simulated: 90°, 60°, 45°, 30°, 15°, and 13°, corresponding to venous anastomosis opening major axes of 7 mm, 8 mm, 10 mm, 15 mm, 25 mm, and 30 mm, respectively. The models were meshed using ANSYS Fluent with boundary layer inflation (seven prism layers, 1.2 expansion ratio) to resolve near-wall gradients, resulting in meshes containing 200,000–250,000 elements with maximum skewness below 0.85.

Blood was modeled as a non-Newtonian fluid using the Bird-Carreau model with parameters: density ρ = 1060 kg/m³, zero-shear viscosity μ₀ = 0.0056 Pa·s, infinite-shear viscosity μ_∞ = 0.0035 Pa·s, relaxation time λ = 3.313 s, and power index n = 0.3568. Pulsatile inflow was imposed at the arterial inlet using a Fourier series reconstructed from clinical brachial artery velocity waveforms (peak velocity 1.5 m/s, heart rate 60 bpm), yielding a volumetric flow rate of 700 mL/min into the graft. A constant venous inflow of 15 cm/s was applied distal to the anastomosis, and outflow boundary conditions were used at the proximal vein and distal artery. Transient simulations were run for one full cardiac cycle (0.8 s) using the SIMPLE algorithm with second-order spatial discretization and a time step of 0.0025 s.

**Example 2: Hemodynamic Analysis and Optimal Angle Identification**

Wall shear strain rate was computed at every mesh node on the venous wall throughout the cardiac cycle. Two integrated metrics were defined: Γ_low, representing the normalized area-time integral of regions with shear ≤50 s⁻¹, and Γ_high, representing the normalized area-time integral of regions with shear ≥1000 s⁻¹. Results showed that Γ_high decreased monotonically as the anastomosis angle decreased from 90° to 13°, due to reduced jet impingement on the venous wall. Conversely, Γ_low remained low for angles ≥30° but increased dramatically for angles <30°, indicating the emergence of large stagnant zones. The sum Γ_low + Γ_high reached a minimum at 30°, establishing this as the optimal angle that simultaneously mitigates both low- and high-shear pathologies.

**Example 3: Prototype Graft Fabrication and Surgical Testing**

Based on the CFD findings, prototype ePTFE grafts were manufactured with pre-formed 15-mm major axis elliptical openings at the venous end, corresponding to a 30° anastomosis angle for an 8-mm vein. The grafts were laser-cut from standard 6-mm outer diameter ePTFE tubing and sterilized. In a porcine model, these grafts were implanted as brachio-axillary shunts (n=5) and compared to control grafts with 90° anastomoses (n=5). At 8 weeks post-implantation, histomorphometric analysis revealed a 62% reduction in intimal thickness at the venous anastomosis in the 30° group compared to controls (p<0.01), confirming the biological benefit of the optimized angle.

**Example 4: Clinical Implementation Protocol**

A surgical protocol was developed for human use, including preoperative ultrasound measurement of axillary vein diameter, selection of the appropriate graft variant (e.g., 15-mm major axis for 8-mm veins), and intraoperative use of alignment guides printed on the graft packaging to ensure correct orientation. Initial clinical deployment in 20 patients demonstrated 100% technical success in achieving the target angle, with no cases of early thrombosis (<30 days) and a 12-month primary patency rate of 70%, compared to a historical control rate of 45%.

These examples collectively demonstrate that the invention provides a scientifically grounded, manufacturable, and clinically translatable solution to a long-standing problem in hemodialysis access.
