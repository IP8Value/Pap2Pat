# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of medical image processing and, more specifically, to systems and methods for interactive three-dimensional (3D) segmentation of anatomical structures in volumetric medical imaging data. The invention provides a novel deep learning-based framework that enables accurate, context-aware segmentation of arbitrary anatomical structures—including those not included in the training dataset—by leveraging user-provided manual delineation on a single image slice as contextual prior information. This approach is particularly advantageous in clinical applications such as radiotherapy planning, surgical navigation, and diagnostic imaging, where precise contouring of both standard and highly variable pathological structures (e.g., tumors) is essential but often hindered by the limitations of fully automatic segmentation algorithms. The invention bridges the gap between manual contouring and fully automated machine learning models by introducing a semi-automatic, interactive segmentation system that dynamically interprets user input within the spatial and visual context of adjacent image slices, thereby enabling robust generalization across diverse anatomical targets without requiring structure-specific model retraining.

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

### Image Segmentation

Image segmentation in medical imaging refers to the process of partitioning a digital image into multiple segments or regions, each corresponding to a distinct anatomical structure or pathological entity of interest. In clinical practice, accurate segmentation is foundational to numerous diagnostic and therapeutic workflows. For instance, in radiation oncology, precise delineation of tumor volumes and surrounding organs-at-risk is critical for treatment planning, as it directly influences the calculation of radiation dose distribution. Errors in segmentation can lead to under-dosing of malignant tissue or excessive irradiation of healthy organs, potentially compromising treatment efficacy and patient safety. Similarly, in neurosurgery, segmentation of brain lesions or functional areas guides operative strategy and intraoperative navigation. In cardiology, segmentation of the myocardium or chambers from cardiac MRI supports quantitative assessment of ejection fraction and wall motion abnormalities. Despite its importance, segmentation remains a labor-intensive task when performed manually, often requiring expert radiologists or radiation oncologists to spend hours contouring a single patient’s dataset. This manual process is not only time-consuming but also susceptible to significant inter- and intra-observer variability, which can undermine the reproducibility and reliability of clinical decisions derived from the segmented data. Consequently, there has been sustained interest in developing automated or semi-automated segmentation techniques that can reduce human effort while maintaining or improving accuracy and consistency.

### The Need for Interactive Segmentation

While fully automatic segmentation methods have made substantial progress—particularly with the advent of deep learning—they remain constrained by inherent limitations that necessitate the development of interactive alternatives. Machine learning models, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), excel when trained on large, representative datasets of the target anatomy. However, in real-world clinical settings, anatomical structures exhibit considerable variability in shape, size, location, and appearance, particularly in the case of pathological entities such as tumors. This variability poses a fundamental challenge: no finite training dataset can encompass all possible manifestations of every conceivable structure, especially rare or patient-specific pathologies. As a result, fully automatic models often fail when confronted with out-of-distribution examples—i.e., structures that differ significantly from those seen during training. Moreover, even when trained on extensive datasets, automatic models may produce anatomically implausible or clinically unacceptable segmentations that require manual correction, thereby negating much of the anticipated time savings. Interactive segmentation addresses these shortcomings by incorporating human expertise into the segmentation loop. Rather than relying solely on pre-learned statistical priors, interactive methods allow clinicians to provide sparse, high-level guidance—such as a few clicks, scribbles, or a single contour—which the algorithm then uses to infer the full segmentation. This hybrid approach combines the speed and consistency of automation with the adaptability and contextual understanding of human experts, yielding results that are both efficient and clinically trustworthy. Crucially, interactive segmentation is particularly valuable for de-novo contouring tasks, where no prior model exists for a given structure, or when existing models perform poorly due to domain shift or data scarcity.

### Application Examples of Segmentation:

The utility of accurate and efficient segmentation spans a wide array of medical specialties and clinical scenarios. In radiation therapy, segmentation of gross tumor volume (GTV), clinical target volume (CTV), and organs-at-risk (OARs) such as the spinal cord, lungs, heart, and kidneys is mandatory for treatment planning. Errors in OAR delineation can lead to severe toxicities; for example, inaccurate lung segmentation may result in radiation pneumonitis. In neuro-oncology, segmentation of gliomas from MRI enables assessment of tumor burden, response to therapy, and surgical planning. In abdominal imaging, segmentation of the liver, spleen, pancreas, and kidneys supports diagnosis of focal lesions, quantification of organ volume, and guidance for interventions such as biopsy or ablation. Cardiovascular applications include segmentation of the left and right ventricles from cine MRI for functional analysis, as well as coronary artery segmentation from CT angiography for stenosis detection. In musculoskeletal radiology, segmentation of bones and cartilage aids in the evaluation of osteoarthritis and fracture healing. Beyond diagnosis and therapy, segmentation also plays a key role in longitudinal studies, where consistent delineation across time points is essential for tracking disease progression or treatment response. Given the diversity and complexity of these applications, a flexible segmentation system that can adapt to any structure—whether common or rare, benign or malignant—is of immense clinical value. The present invention fulfills this need by providing an interactive 3D segmentation framework that generalizes beyond the training data through contextual learning, thereby supporting a broad spectrum of clinical use cases without requiring bespoke model development for each new anatomical target.

## PRIOR ART

### Fully Automated Segmentation Using ML

Fully automated segmentation using machine learning, particularly deep learning, has become a dominant paradigm in medical image analysis over the past decade. Architectures such as U-Net and its variants have demonstrated remarkable success in segmenting a wide range of anatomical structures from CT, MRI, and other imaging modalities. These models are typically trained on large annotated datasets, where each voxel is labeled with a class identifier (e.g., “tumor,” “liver,” “background”). Once trained, they can process new images in seconds, producing dense segmentation masks with high accuracy—often rivaling or exceeding human performance on in-distribution data. However, this success is contingent upon the assumption that the test data closely resembles the training data in terms of anatomy, pathology, imaging protocol, and acquisition parameters. When this assumption is violated—as is frequently the case in heterogeneous clinical environments—the performance of these models degrades significantly. For example, a model trained exclusively on lung cancer patients may fail to segment pancreatic tumors due to differences in texture, shape, and surrounding anatomy. Moreover, fully automatic models are inherently static: once deployed, they cannot incorporate new knowledge or adapt to user feedback without retraining. This rigidity limits their utility in dynamic clinical workflows where unexpected findings or rare anatomies are common. Additionally, because these models operate as black boxes, their outputs may lack interpretability, making it difficult for clinicians to trust or correct erroneous segmentations. While post-processing techniques such as morphological operations or shape priors can mitigate some errors, they cannot compensate for fundamental mismatches between the model’s learned representations and the actual image content. Consequently, despite their speed and scalability, fully automated approaches remain insufficient for many real-world segmentation tasks, particularly those involving novel or highly variable structures.

### Interactive 2D Image Segmentation Approaches

Interactive segmentation in two dimensions has a long history in computer vision and medical imaging, with early methods such as intelligent scissors, graph cuts, and random walks allowing users to guide segmentation through minimal input like seed points or bounding boxes. More recent approaches integrate deep learning with user interaction, enabling more sophisticated inference from sparse annotations. For instance, some systems use a user-drawn contour or scribble on a single 2D slice to condition a neural network that predicts the full segmentation of that slice. These methods are effective for 2D tasks but do not naturally extend to 3D volumetric data, where anatomical structures span multiple slices and exhibit complex spatial relationships. While one could apply 2D interactive segmentation slice-by-slice, this approach is inefficient and fails to leverage the rich contextual information available in adjacent slices. Furthermore, it introduces discontinuities between slices, resulting in jagged or non-anatomical 3D surfaces. To address this, some researchers have proposed propagating 2D segmentations across slices using optical flow or registration, but these methods are sensitive to motion artifacts, intensity variations, and topological changes, and often require manual correction at slice boundaries. Thus, while 2D interactive segmentation offers a useful tool for single-slice analysis, it is inadequate for comprehensive 3D contouring in clinical practice, where volumetric consistency and anatomical plausibility are paramount.

### Interactive 3D Segmentation in Medical Images

Efforts to develop interactive 3D segmentation methods have focused on extending 2D paradigms into the third dimension or designing inherently volumetric models. One common strategy is to train a 3D CNN on sparsely annotated volumes, where only a subset of slices is manually labeled, and the network learns to infer the full 3D segmentation from these partial annotations. Another approach involves using user input on one or more slices as conditioning information for a 3D segmentation model. For example, a user might draw a contour on a central slice, and the model uses this as a prior to segment the entire volume. However, most existing methods are structure-specific: they are trained to segment a predefined set of anatomies (e.g., the heart or prostate) and do not generalize to unseen structures. This limitation arises because the models learn structure-specific features rather than abstract contextual relationships between user input and image content. Consequently, deploying such systems in clinical practice requires maintaining a library of specialized models, each trained for a particular anatomy, which is impractical given the vast number of potential targets. Moreover, retraining or fine-tuning a model for a new structure demands annotated data and computational resources, creating a barrier to adoption in resource-constrained settings. A few recent works have attempted to build more generalizable interactive systems, but they often rely on handcrafted features or shallow architectures that lack the representational power of deep learning. Thus, there remains a critical unmet need for an interactive 3D segmentation framework that is both deep learning-based and capable of generalizing to arbitrary anatomical structures based solely on contextual cues from user input.

### Interactive Contour Propagation Methods

Contour propagation methods represent another class of interactive segmentation techniques, wherein a user-drawn contour on one slice is propagated to neighboring slices using image similarity, deformation fields, or learned models. Early approaches used rigid or affine transformations, but these failed to account for non-rigid anatomical changes. Later methods employed deformable registration to align slices and warp contours accordingly, but registration errors—especially in regions of low contrast or pathology—often led to inaccurate propagations. More recently, machine learning has been used to predict how a contour should evolve from one slice to the next. For example, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or 3D CNNs have been trained to take a contour on slice *n* and the image on slice *n+1* as input and output the contour for slice *n+1*. While promising, these methods typically assume that the structure of interest is known and consistent across the training set, limiting their applicability to novel structures. Additionally, most propagation methods operate in a sequential manner, either superiorly or inferiorly from a seed slice, which can accumulate errors over distance and lacks the ability to refine predictions using global context. Some systems allow bidirectional propagation or user correction at intermediate slices, but this increases interaction time and disrupts workflow efficiency. Crucially, none of the existing contour propagation methods decouple the segmentation task from the identity of the anatomical structure; instead, they implicitly encode structural priors during training, making them brittle when applied to unseen targets. The present invention overcomes these limitations by explicitly designing a model that learns to segment based on the *context* provided by the user’s input—namely, the relationship between a reference image slice and its corresponding binary mask—without encoding any assumptions about the underlying anatomy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention discloses a novel system and method for interactive three-dimensional segmentation of anatomical structures in medical images, based on a contextual deep learning framework that enables generalization to arbitrary, previously unseen structures. The core innovation lies in the formulation of the segmentation problem as a context-conditioned prediction task, wherein the model is trained not to recognize specific anatomies but to infer what to segment on a target image slice based on the visual and spatial relationship between a user-provided reference slice and its corresponding manual contour. This is achieved through a multi-input neural network architecture that simultaneously processes three distinct channels of information: (1) the target image slice to be segmented, (2) a reference image slice containing a user-drawn contour, and (3) a binary mask representing the user’s contour on the reference slice. By deliberately omitting any structural labels or semantic information from the training data—such as whether the contour corresponds to a heart, tumor, or spleen—the model is forced to learn abstract, context-dependent features that capture the morphological and textural correspondence between the reference and target slices, irrespective of the underlying anatomy.

The neural network architecture employed in the preferred embodiment is a modified U-Net, specifically a residual recurrent U-Net augmented with attention gates. This architecture was selected for its proven efficacy in medical image segmentation, its ability to capture both local and global context through skip connections, and its capacity for iterative refinement via recurrent modules. The input layer of the network is expanded from the conventional single channel to three channels to accommodate the three aforementioned inputs. The first channel receives the target image slice, typically a 256×256 pixel CT image after preprocessing. The second channel receives the reference image slice, which is the same size and modality as the target slice. The third channel receives the binary mask of the user’s contour on the reference slice, where foreground pixels are set to 1 and background to 0. These three inputs are concatenated along the channel dimension and fed into the encoder portion of the U-Net, which consists of multiple downsampling blocks that extract hierarchical feature representations. Each block incorporates residual connections to mitigate vanishing gradients and improve training stability. The decoder portion employs upsampling blocks with skip connections from the encoder to recover spatial resolution, and attention gates are integrated at each skip connection to modulate the feature maps based on their relevance to the current segmentation task. These attention gates implement soft self-attention, dynamically highlighting salient regions in the feature maps that are most informative for predicting the contour on the target slice, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to focus on contextually relevant structures.

The network is trained using a pixelwise binary cross-entropy loss function, which penalizes discrepancies between the predicted probability map and the ground truth binary mask for the target slice. Optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1×10⁻⁵ and a batch size of 4. To enhance robustness and generalization, on-the-fly data augmentation is applied during training, including random affine transformations comprising translation (±10 mm), rotation (±10 degrees), and scaling (±5%). Importantly, the training strategy emphasizes diversity in the anatomical structures represented in the dataset. The training set comprises 3D CT volumes and corresponding manual delineations of 19 different anatomical structures sourced from multiple publicly available datasets, including but not limited to the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, pancreas, spinal cord, and various tumor types. The dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test sets in a 60:20:20 ratio, with care taken to ensure that each structure is equally represented in the training set by randomly selecting up to 2000 image slices per structure. Structures that are delineated on fewer than 10 slices or across very few patients are excluded to maintain data quality and training efficiency.

A critical aspect of the training methodology is the selection of reference and target slice pairs. To prevent the model from merely copying contours from adjacent slices—a behavior that would impede learning of true contextual relationships—the interslice distance between the reference and target slices is carefully sampled from a range of distances relative to the size of the anatomical structure. This ensures that the model encounters both small and large deformations during training, thereby learning to interpolate and extrapolate contours across varying spatial offsets. Moreover, the reference slice is always chosen to lie within the volume of the structure being segmented, ensuring that a valid contour exists to provide context, whereas the target slice may lie outside the structure’s volume, enabling the model to learn not only where to draw a contour but also when to terminate segmentation. This design choice is crucial for handling structures with irregular or discontinuous appearances, such as infiltrative tumors.

During inference, the system operates in an interactive mode. A user selects a single image slice from a 3D volume and manually delineates the structure of interest on that slice. This user-provided contour, together with the reference slice image, serves as the contextual input to the model. The model then segments the remaining slices in the volume using one of two prediction strategies: direct prediction or iterative prediction. In direct prediction, the same reference slice and contour are used as input for every target slice in the volume, regardless of distance. In iterative prediction, the model first segments the slice adjacent to the reference slice, and the resulting prediction is then used as the new reference contour for the next slice, propagating the segmentation sequentially through the volume. Experimental results demonstrate that iterative prediction yields superior performance for structures not included in the training set, as it allows the model to incrementally adapt to local image characteristics and minimize cumulative error.

The system is evaluated using multiple metrics, including the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), Hausdorff Distance (HD), and Relative Added Path Length (APL). While DSC and HD assess geometric accuracy, APL is particularly relevant in clinical practice as it quantifies the amount of manual editing required to correct the predicted contour, thereby serving as a proxy for contouring time. Results show that as the diversity of the training set increases—i.e., as more anatomical structures are added—the model’s performance improves not only for the newly included structures but also for those excluded from training, demonstrating genuine generalization through contextual learning. For example, a model trained on heart, lung, and tumor data achieves a relative APL of 0.35 for the spleen—a structure never seen during training—indicating that only 35% of the ground truth contour length needs to be manually edited, a significant reduction compared to manual contouring. This capability enables rapid de-novo contouring of rare or novel structures without the need for structure-specific model retraining, addressing a major bottleneck in clinical deployment of AI segmentation tools.

In summary, the present invention provides a robust, generalizable, and clinically practical solution for interactive 3D medical image segmentation. By decoupling segmentation from anatomical identity and instead framing it as a context-conditioned prediction task, the system overcomes the limitations of prior art and enables accurate contouring of any structure for which a user can provide a single-slice annotation. This represents a significant advancement in the field, with broad applicability across radiology, radiation oncology, surgery, and other medical disciplines.
