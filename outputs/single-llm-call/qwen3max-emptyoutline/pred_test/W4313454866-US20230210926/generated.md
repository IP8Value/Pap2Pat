## BACKGROUND

### Field

The present invention relates generally to the field of molecular imaging and, more specifically, to genetically encoded acoustic reporter genes that enable non-invasive, non-destructive ultrasound imaging of gene expression in living organisms. The invention provides novel bacterial and mammalian acoustic reporter gene constructs derived from gas vesicle (GV)-encoding gene clusters that produce robust non-linear ultrasound contrast when expressed heterologously in bacterial or mammalian cells, respectively. These reporter genes facilitate high-resolution, deep-tissue visualization of cellular localization, colonization dynamics, and transcriptional activity in vivo without requiring destructive imaging protocols or clonal selection of engineered cell lines.

### Description of the Related Art

Ultrasound imaging is a widely used clinical and preclinical modality due to its real-time capabilities, deep tissue penetration, safety, portability, and relatively low cost compared to other imaging technologies such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or positron emission tomography (PET). However, conventional ultrasound lacks molecular specificity because it relies on endogenous acoustic impedance differences between tissues, which do not directly report on gene expression or cellular function. To overcome this limitation, researchers have developed exogenous contrast agents, including microbubbles and gas-filled protein nanostructures known as gas vesicles (GVs).

Gas vesicles are naturally occurring, hollow protein nanostructures produced by certain aquatic bacteria and archaea to regulate buoyancy. They consist of a rigid, gas-permeable protein shell that excludes water while allowing gases to diffuse freely, thereby creating an air-filled cavity that strongly scatters ultrasound waves. Because GVs are composed entirely of proteins encoded by genetic operons, they represent a unique class of genetically encodable contrast agents—termed acoustic reporter genes (ARGs)—that can be introduced into heterologous hosts to render them visible by ultrasound.

Early efforts to develop ARGs focused on expressing native GV gene clusters in model organisms such as *Escherichia coli* and mammalian cells. For example, the *Bacillus megaterium* GV operon was successfully expressed in *E. coli*, producing detectable ultrasound contrast. Similarly, a mammalian-adapted version of this operon (mARG^Mega) enabled ultrasound detection of transduced human cells. However, these first-generation ARGs suffered from significant limitations that hindered their practical utility. In bacterial systems, many native GV operons failed to produce functional GVs when expressed heterologously due to incompatibilities in host physiology, including differences in growth temperature, turgor pressure, codon usage, and chaperone availability. Even when GVs were produced, they often generated only linear ultrasound scattering, making them difficult to distinguish from background tissue signals without using destructive imaging sequences that collapse the GVs irreversibly.

In mammalian cells, the challenges were even more pronounced. The mARG^Mega construct required monoclonal selection of transduced cells and treatment with histone deacetylase inhibitors to maintain expression, likely due to epigenetic silencing of the transgene. Moreover, it produced minimal non-linear contrast, necessitating destructive B-mode imaging for detection and precluding longitudinal studies or real-time procedural guidance. These shortcomings severely limited the applicability of early ARGs in dynamic biological contexts such as tracking therapeutic bacteria in tumors or monitoring gene expression patterns during tumor development.

Non-linear ultrasound imaging techniques, such as cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM), have been developed to selectively enhance signals from non-linear scatterers like GVs while suppressing linear background echoes from tissues. Unlike destructive methods that rely on the irreversible collapse of GVs to generate contrast, xAM preserves the integrity of the GVs, enabling repeated imaging over time. However, the effectiveness of xAM depends critically on the ability of the expressed GVs to exhibit strong non-linear acoustic behavior—a property that varies significantly across different GV types and is influenced by structural features such as shell composition and mechanical rigidity.

Prior art also lacked systematic approaches for identifying and optimizing GV gene clusters with superior imaging properties. Most studies tested only one or two native operons without exploring the vast natural diversity of GV-producing organisms. Given that over 280 species across multiple phyla are known to produce GVs, there exists a rich reservoir of genetic variation that could be mined to discover ARGs with enhanced brightness, stability, temperature compatibility, and non-linear response. Furthermore, rational engineering strategies—such as deleting non-essential genes, tuning stoichiometries of structural versus assembly proteins, or modifying regulatory elements—had not been systematically applied to improve ARG performance in heterologous hosts.

Thus, there remains a critical need for improved acoustic reporter genes that: (1) produce bright, non-linear ultrasound contrast when expressed in physiologically relevant hosts such as probiotic bacteria or human cancer cells; (2) function robustly at mammalian body temperatures (e.g., 37 °C); (3) impose minimal metabolic burden on host cells; (4) remain genetically stable over multiple generations; and (5) enable non-destructive, longitudinal imaging of gene expression in vivo. The present invention addresses these unmet needs through a combination of genomic mining, heterologous expression screening, and targeted genetic engineering to develop next-generation bacterial and mammalian ARGs with unprecedented sensitivity and specificity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel acoustic reporter gene (ARG) constructs that enable non-invasive, non-destructive ultrasound imaging of gene expression in both bacterial and mammalian cells in vivo. Specifically, the invention discloses a bacterial ARG derived from *Serratia* sp. 39006, designated bARG^Ser, and a mammalian ARG adapted from *Anabaena flos-aquae*, designated mARG^Ana. These ARGs encode gas vesicle (GV) protein nanostructures that produce strong non-linear ultrasound contrast when expressed heterologously, allowing high-resolution visualization of cellular localization, colonization dynamics, and transcriptional activity without destroying the contrast agent or requiring clonal selection of engineered cell lines.

The bARG^Ser construct comprises a genetically optimized polycistronic operon from *Serratia* sp. 39006, wherein a non-essential hypothetical gene (Ser39006_001280) has been deleted to enhance GV expression and ultrasound signal intensity. When expressed in the probiotic bacterium *Escherichia coli* Nissle 1917 (EcN) under the control of an L-arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter and stabilized by an Axe-Txe toxin-antitoxin cassette, bARG^Ser enables robust, non-linear ultrasound detection of tumor-colonizing bacteria at depths exceeding 1 cm. The construct exhibits minimal metabolic burden, high genetic stability over >35 generations, and supports dynamic imaging through repeated induction and re-expression cycles after acoustic collapse.

The mARG^Ana construct consists of two plasmids: one encoding the major structural protein GvpA (with WPRE-hGH polyA stabilization elements), and another encoding a polycistronic assembly factor module (gvpNJKFGWV) linked via P2A co-translational cleavage sites and fused to a fluorescent reporter (GFP). Critically, the mARG^Ana system omits the minor structural protein GvpC, which enhances non-linear ultrasound scattering by increasing GV shell deformability. When stably integrated into human breast cancer cells (MDA-MB-231) under a doxycycline-inducible promoter, mARG^Ana produces 38.8-fold stronger non-linear xAM signal than prior mammalian ARGs and enables sensitive, non-destructive detection down to 30,000 cells per milliliter. This allows real-time, three-dimensional mapping of gene expression patterns within orthotopic tumors and facilitates ultrasound-guided biopsy of genetically defined cell subpopulations.

Methods of use include introducing the disclosed ARG constructs into target cells (bacterial or mammalian), inducing expression under controlled conditions, and imaging with non-linear ultrasound modalities such as xAM or parabolic amplitude modulation (pAM). The invention further encompasses kits comprising the ARG plasmids, host cells engineered to express them, and instructions for ultrasound imaging. Applications span cancer therapy monitoring, microbiome tracking, immune cell trafficking, developmental biology, and image-guided interventions, offering a powerful platform for longitudinal, deep-tissue molecular imaging with clinical translatability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Definitions

As used herein, the term “acoustic reporter gene” or “ARG” refers to a nucleic acid sequence encoding one or more proteins that self-assemble into a gas-filled protein nanostructure capable of scattering ultrasound waves, thereby rendering cells expressing the gene detectable by ultrasound imaging. The primary example of such a structure is the gas vesicle (GV), a hollow, cylindrical or spindle-shaped organelle composed of a protein shell that excludes liquid water but permits gas diffusion.

The term “gas vesicle” or “GV” denotes a naturally occurring or engineered protein nanostructure that forms an air-filled cavity within a cell, providing acoustic contrast due to the large difference in density and compressibility between the gas phase inside the vesicle and the surrounding aqueous cytoplasm or extracellular matrix. GVs are typically encoded by polycistronic gene clusters containing a major structural gene (*gvpA*) and multiple accessory genes involved in assembly, regulation, or structural reinforcement.

“Non-linear ultrasound contrast” refers to the generation of harmonic or subharmonic frequencies in response to incident ultrasound waves, resulting from the non-linear oscillation of gas-filled structures under acoustic pressure. This contrasts with linear scattering, where the backscattered signal occurs only at the fundamental frequency of the transmitted wave. Non-linear contrast enables selective detection of GVs against tissue background using pulse sequences such as cross-propagating amplitude modulation (xAM).

“Heterologous expression” means the expression of a gene or gene cluster in a host organism that does not naturally produce the encoded product. In the context of this invention, it refers to the expression of GV gene clusters from environmental microbes in model bacterial strains (e.g., *E. coli*) or mammalian cell lines (e.g., HEK293T, MDA-MB-231).

“Polycistronic operon” describes a single transcriptional unit containing multiple coding sequences under the control of a common promoter, which are translated into individual proteins either through internal ribosome entry sites (IRES), proteolytic cleavage signals (e.g., P2A peptides), or separate ribosome binding sites in prokaryotes.

“Cross-propagating amplitude modulation” or “xAM” is a non-destructive ultrasound imaging technique that applies two amplitude-modulated pulses propagating in orthogonal directions to enhance signals from non-linear scatterers while canceling linear background echoes. It preserves the integrity of GVs, enabling repeated imaging over time.

“Burstable ultrasound response through signal tracking” or “BURST” is a destructive ultrasound imaging method that detects GVs by applying high-pressure pulses sufficient to collapse them irreversibly, generating a transient signal spike that distinguishes them from static tissue scatterers.

“Inducible promoter” refers to a genetic regulatory element that drives transcription of downstream genes only in the presence of a specific chemical inducer (e.g., L-arabinose for pBAD, doxycycline for TRE promoters), allowing temporal control over ARG expression.

“Genetic stability” indicates the maintenance of functional ARG expression over multiple cell divisions without loss-of-function mutations, assessed by colony opacity, microscopy, or ultrasound signal retention in the absence of antibiotic selection.

### Acoustic Reporter Genes for Nondestructive In Vivo Imaging

The present invention provides two novel classes of acoustic reporter genes—bacterial (bARG^Ser) and mammalian (mARG^Ana)—engineered to overcome the limitations of prior art ARGs by delivering bright, non-linear ultrasound contrast under physiological conditions. These constructs were developed through a systematic genomic mining approach that screened 15 distinct GV gene clusters from phylogenetically diverse organisms for their ability to produce functional GVs when expressed heterologously in *E. coli*. Of these, only five clusters yielded detectable ultrasound signals at 30 °C, and three at 37 °C, highlighting the stringent host compatibility requirements for GV biogenesis. Among the top performers, the *Serratia* sp. 39006 cluster produced the strongest non-linear xAM signal—19.2 dB (9-fold) higher than the next best candidate—when expressed in *E. coli* patches normalized to equal cell density.

To optimize this lead candidate, targeted deletions of individual genes within the *Serratia* operon were evaluated. Deletion of a non-annotated hypothetical protein (Ser39006_001280) increased both xAM signal intensity and optical opacity of bacterial colonies, whereas deletions of known GV-associated genes (*gvpC, gvpH, gvpW, gvpY, gvpZ*) reduced or abolished contrast. The resulting minimized operon, designated bARG^Ser, was cloned into a pBAD-inducible vector and introduced into *E. coli* Nissle 1917 (EcN), a clinically relevant probiotic strain known for its ability to colonize solid tumors. To ensure plasmid maintenance in the absence of antibiotics—a necessity for in vivo applications—an Axe-Txe toxin-antitoxin stability cassette was incorporated, enabling retention of the 23.2-kb plasmid for at least five days of serial passaging without selection pressure.

In parallel, efforts to develop a superior mammalian ARG focused on the *Anabaena flos-aquae* GV cluster, which demonstrated detectable BURST signal upon transient co-transfection of monocistronic plasmids into HEK293T cells. Hypothesizing that the native operon’s multiple *gvpA* copies might benefit from stoichiometric tuning, titration experiments revealed that increasing the molar ratio of *gvpA* relative to other GV genes enhanced ultrasound contrast up to an eight-fold excess. Further optimization included transcript stabilization via WPRE-hGH polyA elements and, critically, omission of the minor structural protein *gvpC*. Since GvpC reinforces the GV shell and restricts its deformation under acoustic pressure, its removal significantly amplified non-linear scattering, yielding a 38.8-fold increase in xAM signal compared to the *B. megaterium*-based mARG^Mega.

The final mARG^Ana system comprises two plasmids: (1) a GvpA-expressing vector with IRES-driven EBFP2 for fluorescence tracking, and (2) a polycistronic assembly factor construct (gvpNJKFGWV) linked by P2A peptides and fused to Emerald GFP. Both are driven by CMV promoters for transient expression or integrated into the genome via PiggyBac transposition under a doxycycline-inducible TRE3G promoter for stable cell line generation. This modular design allows precise control over GvpA stoichiometry while ensuring coordinated expression of essential assembly factors.

Both bARG^Ser and mARG^Ana exhibit exceptional performance characteristics that address key shortcomings of earlier ARGs. They function robustly at 37 °C, produce strong non-linear contrast detectable by xAM without GV destruction, impose metabolic burdens comparable to fluorescent proteins, and maintain genetic stability over extended culture periods. These attributes collectively enable previously unattainable applications in deep-tissue molecular imaging, including longitudinal monitoring of bacterial tumor colonization and real-time ultrasound-guided biopsy of genetically defined cell populations.

### Methods of Imaging and Treatment

The disclosed ARG constructs are employed in methods that combine genetic engineering with advanced ultrasound imaging to visualize and manipulate biological processes in vivo. In bacterial applications, EcN cells harboring the pBAD-bARG^Ser-AxeTxe plasmid are administered systemically (e.g., via intravenous injection) to tumor-bearing subjects. After allowing time for bacterial homing and colonization (typically 3–5 days), GV expression is induced by administering L-arabinose (e.g., 120 mg intraperitoneally in mice). Non-linear ultrasound imaging using xAM or pAM sequences is then performed to map the spatial distribution of colonizing bacteria within tumors, with signals localized to the necrotic core where EcN preferentially accumulates due to immune privilege and nutrient availability.

Importantly, because xAM is non-destructive, the same tumor can be imaged repeatedly over days to monitor changes in bacterial load or distribution. If desired, GVs can be collapsed by applying high-pressure pulses (e.g., 3 MPa), followed by re-induction of ARG expression to assess dynamic transcriptional responses or confirm signal specificity. Tumor colonization levels can be quantified post-imaging by homogenizing excised tumors and plating for colony-forming units (CFUs), with negligible mutational silencing observed even after prolonged in vivo residence.

For mammalian applications, cells stably expressing mARG^Ana (e.g., MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells) are implanted orthotopically or subcutaneously to form xenograft tumors. Doxycycline administration induces GV production, and xAM imaging reveals microscale patterns of gene expression within viable tumor regions, excluding necrotic cores. Three-dimensional tomographic reconstruction enables volumetric assessment of transgene activity over time. The high specificity of non-linear contrast allows differentiation between adjacent cell populations—for example, in chimeric tumors composed of mARG^Ana-positive and Antares-fluorescent cells—facilitating ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration biopsies targeted to genetically defined regions.

These imaging methods are compatible with standard clinical ultrasound platforms equipped with research interfaces for custom pulse sequencing. Imaging parameters are optimized to balance signal strength and GV stability: for bARG^Ser, xAM is typically performed at 1.29 MPa, while mARG^Ana imaging uses 0.42 MPa to minimize partial collapse. Detection limits are remarkably sensitive—10⁷ cells/mL for bARG^Ser and 3×10⁴ cells/mL for mARG^Ana using non-destructive xAM, with even greater sensitivity achievable via destructive BURST imaging when longitudinal tracking is not required.

Therapeutically, the ARG-expressing bacteria or cells can be co-engineered to deliver anticancer agents, enabling image-guided therapy where ultrasound both monitors delivery and potentially enhances drug release through acoustic activation. Alternatively, the ARG itself serves as a surrogate marker for therapeutic gene expression, allowing non-invasive pharmacodynamic assessment. In diagnostic contexts, mARG^Ana-expressing cells could be used to tag rare circulating tumor cells or immune subsets for detection in deep tissues, overcoming the depth limitations of optical imaging.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

**Genomic Mining and Optimization of Bacterial and Mammalian Acoustic Reporter Genes**

Fifteen GV gene clusters were selected from a phylogenetically diverse set of 288 known GV-producing organisms and cloned into expression vectors for testing in *E. coli* BL21(DE3). Each operon was expressed as confluent patches on dual-layer agar plates containing varying concentrations of IPTG inducer and incubated at 30 °C or 37 °C. Patches were scanned using a home-built robotic ultrasound imager applying the xAM pulse sequence to detect non-linear contrast. Only three clusters—*Anabaena flos-aquae*, *Bacillus megaterium* Δ*gvpA-Q*, and bARG1—produced substantial xAM signals at 37 °C, while five—including *Serratia* sp. 39006 and *Desulfobacterium vacuolatum*—were active at 30 °C. Phase-contrast microscopy confirmed GV formation in positive clones, while transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed abundant intracellular GVs in *Serratia*-expressing cells.

To enhance *Serratia* GV production, individual gene deletions were constructed. Deletion of Ser39006_001280 (a hypothetical protein) increased xAM signal by 2.1 dB and colony opacity, whereas deletions of *gvpC*, *gvpH*, *gvpW*, *gvpY*, or *gvpZ* abolished contrast. The optimized bARG^Ser operon (ΔSer39006_001280) was transferred to EcN under pBAD control with an Axe-Txe stability cassette. Plasmid retention exceeded 99% over five days of subculturing without antibiotics, and <0.3% of colonies exhibited loss-of-function mutations after ~35 generations.

For mammalian ARG development, monocistronic plasmids encoding *A. flos-aquae* GV genes were co-transfected into HEK293T cells at varying *gvpA*:chaperone ratios. BURST signal peaked at eight-fold *gvpA* excess. Omission of *gvpC* enhanced xAM signal 38.8-fold versus mARG^Mega. A polycistronic mARG^Ana construct (gvpNJKFGWV-P2A-GFP) was paired with gvpA-IRES-BFP for stoichiometric control. Stable MDA-MB-231-mARG^Ana cells were generated via PiggyBac integration and sorted for triple fluorescence (Antares, GFP, BFP). Doxycycline induction yielded xAM-detectable GVs within 24 hours, with signal saturating at 1 µg/mL doxycycline.

In vivo, intravenous injection of pBAD-bARG^Ser-AxeTxe EcN into MC26 tumor-bearing mice followed by L-arabinose induction enabled xAM visualization of bacterial colonization in tumor cores at >1 cm depth. Signals were absent in uninduced controls and re-expressible after acoustic collapse. Orthotopic MDA-MB-231-mARG^Ana tumors showed doxycycline-dependent xAM contrast restricted to viable regions, enabling 3D mapping and successful ultrasound-guided biopsy of mARG^Ana+ versus mARG^Ana− zones in chimeric tumors.

## Materials and Methods

### Genomic Mining of ARG Clusters

A comprehensive literature search identified 288 microbial strains reported to produce gas vesicles, with 16S rRNA sequences available for 270. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using Clustal Omega and rendered with Evolview to guide selection of 15 representative clusters spanning halophiles, thermophiles, and mesophiles. GV operons were identified in genomes via annotation and BLAST against known *gvp* genes, prioritizing contiguous clusters with minimal non-*gvp* content to reduce metabolic burden. Clusters were PCR-amplified from source organisms (obtained from culture collections) and cloned into pET28a(+) via Gibson assembly.

### Bacterial Plasmid Construction and Molecular Biology

All constructs were assembled using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly (NEB) and transformed into NEB Stable *E. coli*. The *Serratia* sp. 39006 operon (NCBI: txid104623) was synthesized as a 16.8-kb fragment and subcloned into pBAD33 with chloramphenicol resistance. Ser39006_001280 was deleted via overlap extension PCR. The Axe-Txe toxin-antitoxin cassette was inserted downstream of the ARG operon to enable antibiotic-free maintenance. Constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing across all junctions.

### In Vitro Bacterial Expression of ARGs

BL21(DE3) or EcN electrocompetent cells were transformed with ARG plasmids and grown on dual-layer LB agar: bottom layer contained inducer (IPTG, aTc, or L-arabinose) and antibiotic; top layer contained 1% glucose for catabolite repression. Patches were incubated at 30 °C or 37 °C for 24–48 hours. For liquid cultures, overnight starters were diluted 1:100 into LB + 0.4% glucose + antibiotic, grown to OD₆₀₀ 0.1–0.3, induced, and harvested at specified timepoints. Cells were normalized to 10⁹ cells/mL in 1% agarose phantoms for ultrasound imaging.

### In Vitro Ultrasound Imaging of Bacteria Expressing ARGs on Solid Media

Patches on square agar plates were scanned using a Verasonics Vantage system with an L22-14vX transducer (14 MHz center frequency). The xAM sequence applied two amplitude-modulated pulses (modulation frequency 5 kHz, duty cycle 50%) along orthogonal axes, with differential processing to isolate non-linear signals. Images were acquired at 1.74 MPa peak negative pressure, and signal-to-background ratios (SBRs) were quantified using custom MATLAB scripts.

### In Vitro Ultrasound Imaging of Bacteria Expressing ARGs Suspended in Agarose Phantoms

Cells from liquid cultures were pelleted, washed, and resuspended in molten 1% low-melt agarose (40 °C) at defined densities. Aliquots were loaded into wells of pre-formed 1% agarose slabs and allowed to solidify. xAM and BURST imaging were performed across acoustic pressures (0.31–3.0 MPa) to assess signal intensity and GV stability. Parabolic B-mode (pAM) used chirp pulses for deeper penetration.

### Microscopy of Bacteria

Phase-contrast microscopy (Zeiss Axiocam, ×40 Ph2 objective) visualized GVs as bright refractile bodies in live cells. For TEM, cells were adsorbed to glow-discharged carbon-coated copper grids, washed in HEPES buffer, air-dried, and imaged unstained on a Tecnai T12 microscope (120 kV).

### In Vivo Bacterial ARG Expression and Ultrasound Imaging

Female Balb/cJ mice bearing subcutaneous MC26 tumors (200–300 mm³) received 10⁸ EcN cells via tail vein injection. Three days later, L-arabinose (120 mg IP) induced bARG^Ser expression. Ultrasound imaging (xAM, pAM, BURST) was performed 24 hours post-induction using a Vevo LAZR system with a 21-MHz transducer. GVs were collapsed by applying 3 MPa pressure throughout the tumor volume, followed by re-induction and repeat imaging 24 hours later. Tumors were harvested on day 20 for CFU quantification.

### Histology of Tumors Colonized by Bacteria

Excised tumors were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining assessed necrosis and morphology. Immunohistochemistry used rabbit anti-*E. coli* antibody (ViroStat 1001) with Opal 520 tyramide amplification and DAPI counterstain. Sections were scanned on a Leica Aperio VERSA at 40-µm resolution.

### Mammalian Plasmid Construction

*A. flos-aquae* GV genes were codon-optimized and synthesized (Twist Bioscience). The gvpNJKFGWV-P2A-GFP cassette was cloned into pCMVSport with WPRE-hGH polyA. gvpA-IRES-EBFP2-WPRE-hGH was assembled via Gibson cloning. PiggyBac integrants placed both cassettes under TRE3G promoters. Lentiviral rtTA-Antares vectors used P2A linkers for bicistronic expression. All plasmids were sequence-verified.
