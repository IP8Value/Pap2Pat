# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular biology and gene regulation, particularly to the development and use of synthetic nucleic acid libraries encoding short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) for the identification and optimization of RNA molecules capable of modulating cellular phenotypes through RNA interference (RNAi) or non-canonical mechanisms. More specifically, the invention provides a novel method for constructing, screening, and optimizing a random, unbiased shRNA-encoding library wherein each shRNA possesses a stem of approximately 29 nucleotides in length, a non-complementary loop, and the capacity to form mismatches within the stem duplex upon mutagenesis. The invention further encompasses the use of such libraries in functional selection assays to identify shRNA sequences that confer a desired biological phenotype—such as resistance to apoptosis, enhanced cell survival, or altered differentiation—without prior knowledge of target genes or mechanistic pathways. The identified shRNA sequences may be used directly as therapeutic agents, research tools, or leads for further optimization via random mutagenesis and re-screening. The invention is particularly advantageous in contexts where multi-gene targeting, non-canonical RNA-mediated regulation, or phenotypic outcomes not predictable by rational design are desired, and it overcomes significant limitations of existing siRNA- and shRNA-based libraries that rely on single-gene targeting or canonical RNAi assumptions.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

RNA interference (RNAi) is a conserved eukaryotic mechanism by which small non-coding RNAs regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. In the canonical RNAi pathway, endogenous microRNAs (miRNAs) are transcribed as primary transcripts that form hairpin structures, which are sequentially processed by the ribonucleases Drosha and Dicer into mature ∼22-nucleotide small interfering RNAs (siRNAs). These siRNAs are loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), where they guide sequence-specific cleavage or translational repression of complementary messenger RNAs (mRNAs). The specificity of this interaction is largely determined by a “seed” region comprising nucleotides 2–7 of the miRNA, with supplementary contributions from nucleotide 1 (preferably a uracil) and nucleotide 8. Notably, even partial complementarity—sometimes as short as six to seven nucleotides—can elicit significant phenotypic effects, primarily through translational repression rather than mRNA degradation. When complementarity extends beyond the 10th nucleotide, however, endonucleolytic cleavage of the target mRNA is typically observed.

The discovery of RNAi has led to the widespread adoption of synthetic siRNAs and expressed short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) as tools for targeted gene knockdown in both research and therapeutic contexts. Rational-design algorithms have been developed to select siRNA or shRNA sequences that maximize on-target silencing while minimizing off-target effects, and large-scale RNAi libraries have been constructed for genome-wide functional screens. These libraries typically encode shRNAs designed to target individual genes based on known genomic sequences, and many are embedded within miRNA scaffolds to enhance processing efficiency and stability. While such libraries have proven invaluable for identifying genes involved in specific biological processes, they are inherently limited by their reliance on pre-existing knowledge of gene function and sequence, and they assume that phenotypic modulation arises primarily from the suppression of single, well-defined targets.

Moreover, emerging evidence indicates that small RNAs can exert effects beyond canonical RNAi. For instance, certain siRNAs directed to promoter regions have been shown to activate, rather than repress, gene expression—a phenomenon termed RNA activation (RNAa). Similarly, miRNAs that repress translation in proliferating cells can switch to activating translation in quiescent or differentiated cells, depending on the cellular context and the presence of specific co-factors such to FXR1 and AGO2. These non-canonical functions underscore the complexity of RNA-mediated regulation and suggest that the full therapeutic potential of small RNAs may not be captured by libraries designed solely around the principles of single-gene knockdown.

In response to these limitations, several groups have attempted to develop unbiased, random RNAi libraries that do not presuppose target identity or mechanism of action. One such approach involved the use of two opposing promoters to transcribe sense and antisense strands from a shared 19-base-pair random DNA sequence, thereby generating siRNA duplexes. However, this design suffers from several critical drawbacks. First, siRNAs are generally less potent than shRNAs due to differences in intracellular stability and RISC loading efficiency. Second, because the siRNA strands are encoded on opposite DNA strands, the resulting RNA duplexes are perfectly complementary, precluding the mismatches that are common in endogenous miRNA:target interactions and that may contribute to regulatory specificity and potency. Third, the 19-bp stem length used in this system is suboptimal; studies have demonstrated that shRNAs with 27–29 bp stems are significantly more effective at inducing RNAi, likely due to enhanced Dicer processing and RISC incorporation. However, siRNAs longer than 23 bp risk triggering non-specific interferon responses, complicating their use in mammalian systems.

These challenges highlight the need for a random RNAi library that combines the unbiased nature of random sequence design with the structural and functional advantages of shRNAs. Specifically, an ideal library would encode shRNAs with stems of optimal length (e.g., 29 nucleotides), include a non-complementary loop to facilitate proper folding, allow for the introduction of mismatches between the two arms of the stem, and be compatible with robust expression from a polymerase III promoter. Additionally, the library should enable straightforward retrieval of “hit” sequences following functional selection, and support iterative optimization through random mutagenesis. Prior to the present invention, no such library existed that satisfied all these criteria while also being synthetically feasible at scale.

The synthetic construction of a random shRNA library presents formidable technical obstacles. Chief among these is the requirement to encode, on a single DNA strand, a random 29-nucleotide sequence followed by its reverse complement, separated by a fixed loop sequence. This arrangement is necessary to produce a hairpin RNA upon transcription, but it is difficult to achieve using conventional oligonucleotide synthesis due to the repetitive and self-complementary nature of the sequence. Furthermore, the DNA must be double-stranded for cloning into an expression vector, and the final construct must include appropriate regulatory elements—such as a poly-pyrimidine tract upstream of the transcription start site and a polymerase III termination signal (e.g., a run of T residues)—to ensure high-level, precise expression. Previous attempts to address these challenges have either resulted in biased sequence representation, inefficient cloning, or constructs that lack the structural features necessary for optimal RNAi activity.

In light of these unmet needs, the present invention provides a novel solution: a synthetically engineered, random shRNA-encoding library that overcomes the limitations of both rational-design and prior random RNAi libraries. By enabling the functional selection of shRNA sequences based solely on their ability to confer a desired phenotype—without requiring knowledge of their molecular targets or mechanisms of action—the invention opens new avenues for the discovery of RNA-based therapeutics and research tools that operate through canonical, non-canonical, or hybrid modes of action.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for the design, synthesis, and functional screening of a random, unbiased short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-encoding library, wherein each shRNA comprises a stem of approximately 29 nucleotides in length, a non-complementary loop, and the capacity to form mismatches between the two arms of the stem. The invention further provides a method for hit optimization through random mutagenesis and iterative re-screening, enabling the identification of highly potent shRNA sequences that confer a desired cellular phenotype—such as resistance to apoptosis, enhanced proliferation, or altered differentiation—without prior knowledge of target genes or regulatory mechanisms. The library is constructed using a novel enzymatic and chemical synthesis strategy that ensures high fidelity, minimal sequence bias, and compatibility with standard retroviral expression vectors. Functional selection is performed in a mammalian cell system subjected to a selective pressure (e.g., cytokine withdrawal), and enriched shRNA sequences are retrieved by PCR amplification from genomic DNA and re-cloned for validation and optimization. The invention demonstrates that initial hits from the random library, though modest in effect, can be significantly enhanced through random mutagenesis, and that the improved activity often depends on both specific nucleotide identities and the presence of mismatches within the shRNA stem—features that are inaccessible to perfectly complementary siRNA libraries. The shRNA sequences identified by this method may be used directly as therapeutic agents, delivered as synthetic siRNAs, or employed as research tools to probe complex biological networks. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift from target-centric RNAi screening to phenotype-driven discovery of functional RNA molecules.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### EXPERIMENTAL DETAILS SECTION

#### Materials and Experimental Methods

The present invention was developed through a series of carefully orchestrated synthetic, molecular, and cellular biology experiments designed to construct a functional random shRNA library, screen it under selective conditions, and optimize initial hits through iterative mutagenesis. The foundational step involved the chemical synthesis of a double-stranded DNA cassette encoding a random 29-nucleotide shRNA stem, a fixed non-complementary loop, and flanking regulatory elements necessary for robust expression in mammalian cells. All oligonucleotides were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT Inc.), and all enzymatic reactions were performed using reagents from New England Biolabs unless otherwise specified.

The library synthesis began with the annealing of a short primer (Primer 1) to a 97-nucleotide oligonucleotide (Oligo 2) containing a 29-nucleotide random sequence (denoted N29) at its 5′ end. To facilitate subsequent enzymatic manipulations, Oligo 2 was designed with multiple restriction enzyme recognition sites and five phosphorothioate linkages positioned between the last three adenine residues of a poly-A tail and the first three random nucleotides. These phosphorothioate bonds are resistant to nuclease digestion and were critical for enabling asymmetric cleavage in later steps. In the initial implementation, the 29th position of the random sequence was fixed as a guanine (G), resulting in an “N28+G” library that represents one-quarter of the theoretical sequence space for a fully random 29-mer. This design choice was justified by the fact that Dicer processing removes the 29th nucleotide during canonical RNAi maturation, rendering its identity functionally neutral in the final siRNA product.

The annealed primer-template complex was extended using the Klenow fragment of DNA polymerase I at 37°C for 30 minutes, generating a partially double-stranded intermediate. This product was then ligated to a synthetic hairpin-loop linker (Linker 3) using T4 DNA ligase at 15°C overnight. The loop sequence, CTAAAC, was selected for its non-complementarity to the random stem and its compatibility with RNA polymerase III transcription. Following ligation, the construct was treated with the nicking endonuclease Nb.BbvCI, which cleaves only one strand of the DNA duplex, creating a single-stranded “flap” that served as a binding site for a second primer (Primer 4). The nicked structure was then resolved using the strand-displacing polymerase Bst at 65°C for 30 minutes in the presence of a 10-fold molar excess of Primer 4, resulting in a fully extended, double-stranded molecule containing the complete shRNA-encoding cassette.

To prepare the cassette for cloning, it was digested with BtgZI, a type IIS restriction enzyme that cuts downstream of its recognition site. Due to the phosphorothioate linkages, this digestion was asymmetric, producing a recessed 5′ end on one strand and a blunt end on the other. The recessed end was filled in using Klenow polymerase to generate a blunt terminus, while the opposite end was cleaved with NotI to create a compatible sticky end. The resulting cassette was ligated into a modified pSuper retroviral expression vector (OligoEngine) that had been linearized with NotI and dephosphorylated to prevent self-ligation. The pSuper vector was chosen for its strong U6 promoter, which drives high-level shRNA expression, and its inclusion of a puromycin resistance marker and green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter for selection and tracking of transduced cells.

After initial cloning, the loop sequence was refined by dual digestion with PmeI and AarI. These enzymes were selected because their recognition sites were preserved in the original design through intentional mismatches between Primer 1 and Oligo 2. PmeI cleaved on one side of the loop, while AarI cleaved on the other, excising the original loop and allowing its replacement with the desired CTAAAC sequence. The recessed end generated by AarI digestion was filled in with Klenow, and the vector was recircularized via blunt-end ligation using T4 DNA ligase at 15°C overnight. The final library, referred to as the “300K library” due to its derivation from approximately 300,000 E. coli colonies, was subsequently transferred en bloc to the pSiren retroviral vector (Clontech) to improve GFP expression and transduction efficiency.

For random mutagenesis of hit sequences, a PCR-MATE EP 391 DNA synthesizer (Applied Biosystems) was used to synthesize variant oligonucleotides. The manufacturer’s protocol was modified by “spiking” each phosphoramidite bottle (A, C, G, T) with small amounts of the other three nucleotides, adjusted for differential incorporation efficiencies (A = 20%, G = 26%, C = 24%, T = 30%). This approach yielded an average of 1.5 mutations per 87-mer oligonucleotide, which included the shRNA stem-loop and flanking cloning sequences. The synthesized oligos were purified using oligonucleotide purification cartridges and annealed in a stepwise thermal denaturation-renaturation cycle (95°C for 30 sec, 72°C for 2 min, 37°C for 2 min, 25°C for 2 min, followed by 4°C hold) to promote correct hairpin formation. The annealed duplexes were phosphorylated with T4 polynucleotide kinase and cloned into pSiren vector prepared with BglII and NotI and dephosphorylated with Shrimp Alkaline Phosphatase.

Cell culture and retroviral transduction were performed using the murine pro-B cell line FL5.12, which is dependent on interleukin-3 (IL3) for survival. Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 10 mM HEPES, antibiotics, β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.3 ng/ml IL3. Retroviral supernatants were generated by co-transfecting 293T cells with the shRNA library and the ecotropic packaging plasmid pCL-Eco using Effectene reagent. Viral particles were concentrated by three rounds of centrifugation (1000 × g, 1 hr each) and used to infect FL5.12 cells in the presence of 5 µg/ml polybrene. Infection efficiency was monitored by GFP fluorescence using a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer, with a target transduction rate of ≤33% to ensure single integrant per cell by Poisson distribution.

Functional selection was carried out by withdrawing IL3 from the culture medium, thereby inducing apoptosis in non-protected cells. After 3 days of IL3 starvation, surviving cells were returned to IL3-containing medium for 3 days of recovery. This “starvation-recovery” cycle was repeated four times to enrich for protective shRNA sequences. Genomic DNA was extracted from enriched populations using a Qiagen kit, and the integrated shRNA cassettes were amplified by PCR using pSiren-specific primers flanking the insert. Due to the stable secondary structure of the hairpin template, a long extension time (10 min) was used in the PCR protocol. To sequence the hairpin regions, a “half-shRNA” PCR strategy was employed, using primers that spanned the loop to separately amplify each arm of the stem.

Apoptosis assays were performed by staining cells with propidium iodide (PI) in the presence of RNase A and analyzing them by flow cytometry. The percentage of GFP-positive (infected), PI-negative (live) cells was calculated relative to the pre-starvation baseline. Caspase-3 activity was measured using a colorimetric assay kit (BIOMOL) based on the cleavage of Ac-DEVD-pNA. Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t-test, with error bars representing standard deviations from 3–6 independent experiments.

#### Results

The successful synthesis of the 300K random shRNA library was confirmed by sequencing 40 individual clones, which revealed an AT composition of 52.8% and GC composition of 47.2% across the 28 variable positions, indicating minimal sequence bias introduced during synthesis. The library was then used to infect FL5.12 cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) yielding ~30% GFP positivity, ensuring that most infected cells received a single shRNA construct. Following four rounds of IL3 withdrawal and recovery, the proportion of GFP-positive cells in the library-infected population increased from ~30% to 60%, whereas control-infected populations showed no such enrichment, suggesting the presence of shRNA sequences that conferred a survival advantage.

Genomic DNA from enriched cells was used to retrieve shRNA sequences by PCR, and ten clones were sequenced. Two identical sequences (designated 1p and 7p) were identified, indicating selective enrichment. Three putative hits (1p, 3p, and 8p) were individually tested for their ability to protect FL5.12 cells from IL3 withdrawal-induced apoptosis. In flow cytometry assays, clones 1p and 3p significantly increased the fraction of live, infected cells compared to control shRNA or empty vector (p < 0.0001), while clone 8p showed delayed but comparable protection after extended culture. None of the clones affected baseline growth rates. Caspase-3 activity was markedly reduced in cells expressing 3p and 8p (p < 0.001), and modestly reduced in 1p-expressing cells (p = 0.031), confirming that protection occurred via inhibition of the apoptotic cascade. Notably, standard miRNA target prediction algorithms (BLAST, miRanda, TargetScan, etc.) failed to identify convincing mRNA targets for any of the three shRNAs, suggesting non-canonical or multi-target mechanisms.

To optimize the activity of clone 3p, a sublibrary of random variants (Sublibrary 9.1) was generated using spiked oligonucleotide synthesis, yielding ~1.5 mutations per construct. Approximately 10,000 bacterial colonies were pooled to ensure coverage. This sublibrary, along with parental 3p and a control shRNA, was subjected to increasingly stringent IL3 starvation cycles (up to 5 days). Only cells infected with Sublibrary 9.1 survived the fifth cycle, with GFP positivity exceeding 85%. Sequencing of ten recovered clones revealed that eight were identical (designated 3p05p), demonstrating strong selective enrichment.

Functional testing showed that 3p05p conferred significantly greater protection than 3p (p = 0.014), which itself was twice as protective as control. Sequence comparison revealed three nucleotide differences between 3p and 3p05p, introducing mismatches in the shRNA stem. To dissect the contribution of each mutation, single-variant clones (MCp, MTp, MAp) were synthesized. Only MCp (bearing an A-to-C change) enhanced protection (p < 0.0001), nearly matching 3p05p, while the others were less active than 3p. Further analysis showed that both the specific identity of the cytosine and the mismatch it created were essential: when the mismatch was eliminated by introducing a complementary guanine (clone G-C), or when cytosine was replaced by thymine while preserving the mismatch (clone C-T), the protective effect was abolished (p < 0.001 for both).

Additional variants of 3p05p were tested: NoMMp, in which all three mismatches were eliminated by complementary base changes, and 3Mp, in which the new nucleotides were substituted with different bases of the same chemical class (purine for purine, pyrimidine for pyrimidine). Both variants lost activity, with NoMMp showing near-complete ablation, reinforcing the importance of stem mismatches—likely for proper Dicer processing, RISC loading, or target engagement.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the random shRNA library enables the discovery of functional RNA sequences through unbiased phenotypic selection, and that hit optimization via random mutagenesis can dramatically enhance potency through mechanisms involving both sequence specificity and structural features such as stem mismatches—elements that are inaccessible to conventional siRNA libraries or rational design approaches.
