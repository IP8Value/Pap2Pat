# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of semiconductor materials and their fabrication, and more specifically to a method for the direct growth of single-crystalline III–V semiconductor structures on amorphous or non-epitaxial substrates. The invention further encompasses semiconductor devices fabricated from such materials, including but not limited to metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs), photoconductive detectors, and integrated photonic components. The disclosed methodology enables the deterministic patterning of high-quality single-crystalline III–V semiconductors—such as indium phosphide (InP), gallium phosphide (GaP), and indium antimonide (InSb)—in arbitrary two-dimensional and three-dimensional geometries without requiring lattice-matched crystalline substrates. This approach overcomes longstanding limitations in conventional epitaxial growth techniques, which are constrained by substrate compatibility, high processing temperatures, and limited scalability. The invention is particularly advantageous for applications in flexible electronics, large-area optoelectronics, monolithic three-dimensional integration, and heterogeneous system-on-chip platforms where the integration of high-performance III–V semiconductors onto silicon, glass, plastic, or other non-native substrates is essential. By leveraging a templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth mechanism combined with vapor-phase delivery of only the group V element, the invention provides a scalable, low-temperature, and self-limiting route to single-crystalline semiconductor formation with precise geometric control, tunable doping, and excellent electronic and optoelectronic properties.

## BACKGROUND

The integration of III–V compound semiconductors—such as InP, GaAs, and InSb—into modern electronic and photonic systems has long been pursued due to their superior electron mobility, direct bandgaps, and high saturation velocities compared to silicon. These materials are foundational to high-speed transistors, laser diodes, photodetectors, and solar cells. However, their widespread adoption in mainstream integrated circuits has been hindered by fundamental challenges associated with conventional epitaxial growth methods, such as molecular beam epitaxy (MBE) and metal–organic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD). These techniques typically require single-crystal substrates with closely matched lattice constants and thermal expansion coefficients to minimize defect formation during growth. For example, high-quality InP is traditionally grown on InP wafers, which are expensive, brittle, and incompatible with standard silicon complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) processing lines. Attempts to grow III–V materials directly on silicon or amorphous substrates via epitaxy have resulted in high densities of threading dislocations, anti-phase domains, and stacking faults due to lattice mismatch and interfacial reactions, severely degrading device performance.

Alternative approaches, such as wafer bonding and transfer printing, have been developed to circumvent these limitations by fabricating III–V devices on native substrates and then transferring them onto target platforms. While effective in some cases, these methods are complex, costly, and often suffer from yield, alignment, and scalability issues, particularly for large-area or high-density integration. Moreover, they do not enable true monolithic integration, where active semiconductor layers are formed directly on the final substrate. Consequently, there remains a critical need for a growth technique that can produce high-quality, single-crystalline III–V semiconductors directly on arbitrary substrates—including silicon dioxide, glass, and flexible polymers—without relying on epitaxial relationships.

Liquid-phase epitaxy (LPE) and related solution-based methods have been explored as lower-temperature alternatives, but they typically lack the spatial control required for microelectronic patterning and often result in polycrystalline or randomly oriented films. Vapor–liquid–solid (VLS) growth, commonly used for nanowires, offers good crystallinity but is limited to one-dimensional structures and requires catalytic metal nanoparticles that can introduce impurities. Recent advances in templated growth strategies have shown promise, but none have achieved deterministic, single-crystal formation in user-defined planar geometries across wafer-scale areas with in situ doping control and compatibility with non-epitaxial substrates.

A key scientific challenge in non-epitaxial single-crystal growth is the suppression of multiple nucleation events within a confined region. In conventional solidification processes, rapid nucleation leads to polycrystalline grains with random orientations. To achieve single-crystal formation, it is necessary to ensure that only one nucleus forms per patterned feature and that it grows to consume the entire available material before secondary nucleation can occur. This requires precise control over supersaturation, diffusion kinetics, and interfacial energetics—parameters that are difficult to manage in standard vapor-phase or solution-based systems.

The present invention addresses these longstanding challenges through a novel templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth mechanism that exploits the unique thermodynamics and kinetics of group III–V reactions under mechanical confinement. By pre-patterning a group III metal (e.g., indium) within a mechanically rigid, vapor-permeable template (e.g., SiOx) on an amorphous substrate, and then exposing the structure to a vapor-phase group V precursor (e.g., phosphine), the invention enables the formation of stoichiometric III–V compounds with single-crystal quality. Critically, the growth process is self-limiting due to the formation of a phosphorus depletion zone around each growing nucleus, which suppresses additional nucleation within the same template. This effect, combined with geometric confinement, allows for the deterministic growth of single-crystalline features in arbitrary shapes—from simple circles and squares to complex alphanumeric characters and logos—with lateral dimensions ranging from sub-micron to tens of microns. Furthermore, the process operates at relatively low temperatures (500–535 °C), enabling compatibility with temperature-sensitive substrates such as glass and plastics, and can be scaled to full-wafer processing using simple cold-wall furnace designs. The ability to incorporate in situ doping via co-introduction of dopant gases (e.g., GeH4 for n-type InP) further enhances the utility of the method for functional device fabrication. Prior art does not disclose or suggest a method that simultaneously achieves single-crystal quality, arbitrary geometric patterning, wafer-scale scalability, low-temperature processing, and in situ doping control for III–V semiconductors on amorphous substrates, making the present invention a significant advancement in semiconductor manufacturing technology.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for the direct growth of single-crystalline III–V semiconductor structures on amorphous or non-epitaxial substrates through a templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth process. The method comprises the steps of: (a) providing a substrate comprising an amorphous surface layer, such as silicon dioxide, borosilicate glass, or a polymer; (b) depositing a thin nucleation-promoting layer, such as molybdenum oxide (MoOx), onto the substrate; (c) patterning a group III metal layer, such as indium, gallium, or aluminum, into a desired two- or three-dimensional geometry on the nucleation layer; (d) encapsulating the patterned group III metal with a mechanically rigid, vapor-permeable capping layer, such as silicon oxide (SiOx), to form a confined template; and (e) heating the encapsulated structure in a controlled atmosphere containing a vapor-phase group V precursor, such as phosphine (PH3), arsine (AsH3), or stibine (SbH3), at a temperature sufficient to melt the group III metal but below the decomposition temperature of the resulting III–V compound, thereby inducing reaction between the group III and group V species to form a single-crystalline III–V semiconductor that replicates the original patterned geometry.

In a preferred embodiment, the group III metal is indium, the group V precursor is phosphine, and the resulting semiconductor is indium phosphide (InP). The nucleation layer is typically 1 to 10 nanometers thick and serves to promote heterogeneous nucleation of the III–V compound while preventing dewetting or agglomeration of the liquid metal. The capping layer is deposited to a thickness of 10 to 100 nanometers and may be conformally coated on the top and sidewalls of the patterned metal to ensure complete mechanical confinement during the liquid phase. The growth temperature is maintained between 500 °C and 535 °C, and the partial pressure of the group V precursor is controlled to modulate the nucleation rate and grain size, with lower partial pressures favoring single-crystal formation even in larger features.

The invention further includes the capability for in situ doping by introducing a dopant gas, such as germane (GeH4) for n-type doping or diethylzinc (DEZn) for p-type doping, into the growth chamber during the reaction step. This enables precise control over carrier concentration, as demonstrated by photoluminescence shifts consistent with the Burstein–Moss effect and Urbach tail parameters comparable to those of bulk single-crystal wafers.

Additionally, the invention encompasses semiconductor devices fabricated from the TLP-grown materials, including long-channel Schottky n-type MOSFETs with top gates exhibiting high ON-currents (>100 μA/μm), ON/OFF ratios exceeding 10⁵, and effective electron mobilities of approximately 675 cm²/V·s. Photo-MOSFETs incorporating transparent indium tin oxide (ITO) gates demonstrate high responsivity (~700 A/W) and specific detectivity (~8.4 × 10¹¹ Jones), rivaling state-of-the-art epitaxial detectors.

The method is scalable to full 4-inch wafer processing and compatible with a variety of substrates, including silicon/silicon dioxide, borosilicate glass, and flexible polyethylene terephthalate (PET) after transfer. Three-dimensional architectures, such as vertically stacked InP/SiOx multilayers, can be realized by starting with multilayered In/SiOx templates, enabling monolithic 3D integration. The invention is not limited to InP; proof-of-concept demonstrations of GaP and InSb growth confirm the general applicability of the TLP approach to other III–V systems.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, scalable, and versatile platform for the direct writing of optoelectronic-quality single-crystalline III–V semiconductors in user-defined geometries on arbitrary substrates, overcoming the limitations of conventional epitaxial methods and enabling new paradigms in heterogeneous integration, flexible electronics, and large-area photonics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for the direct growth of single-crystalline III–V semiconductor structures on amorphous or non-epitaxial substrates using a templated liquid-phase (TLP) crystal growth technique. The method enables the deterministic formation of high-quality, stoichiometric III–V compounds—such as indium phosphide (InP), gallium phosphide (GaP), and indium antimonide (InSb)—in arbitrary two-dimensional and three-dimensional geometries without reliance on lattice-matched crystalline substrates. The following detailed description sets forth the preferred embodiments of the invention, including materials, processing conditions, structural configurations, and device implementations, all of which are intended to be illustrative and not limiting.

The method begins with the selection of a suitable substrate. The substrate may be any material that is stable at the growth temperatures employed (typically 500–535 °C) and possesses an amorphous or non-single-crystal surface. Preferred substrates include silicon wafers with a thermal oxide layer (e.g., 50 nm SiO₂ on Si), borosilicate glass slides, quartz, sapphire (with amorphous interfacial layers), or even flexible polymers such as polyimide or polyethylene terephthalate (PET), provided appropriate thermal management is employed. The key requirement is that the substrate surface lacks long-range crystalline order, thereby eliminating epitaxial constraints and enabling truly heterogeneous integration.

Once the substrate is selected, a thin nucleation-promoting layer is deposited onto its surface. This layer, typically composed of a transition metal oxide such as molybdenum oxide (MoOx), serves to enhance the wetting and nucleation of the subsequently deposited group III metal and the resulting III–V compound. The MoOx layer is preferably deposited by thermal evaporation or sputtering to a thickness of 1 to 10 nanometers. Thinner layers may be insufficient to prevent dewetting, while thicker layers could introduce excessive impurities or interfere with charge transport in eventual devices. Alternative nucleation layers may include tungsten oxide (WOx), titanium oxide (TiOx), or chromium oxide (CrOx), though MoOx has been found to provide optimal results for InP growth due to its favorable interfacial energy and reactivity with indium and phosphorus.

Following deposition of the nucleation layer, a group III metal—such as indium (In), gallium (Ga), or aluminum (Al)—is patterned onto the substrate in a desired geometry. Patterning is achieved using standard lithographic techniques, including photolithography or electron-beam lithography, followed by physical vapor deposition (e.g., thermal evaporation) and lift-off. The thickness of the group III metal layer is selected based on the desired final semiconductor thickness and typically ranges from 50 to 200 nanometers. For InP, a thickness of approximately 125 nanometers has been found to yield optimal device performance. To ensure smooth film morphology and prevent agglomeration during subsequent processing, the substrate is cooled to below 150 K (e.g., using liquid nitrogen) during metal deposition. This cryogenic deposition minimizes surface diffusion and promotes uniform, continuous films even at nanoscale thicknesses.

After patterning the group III metal, the structure is encapsulated with a mechanically rigid, vapor-permeable capping layer. This layer, preferably composed of silicon oxide (SiOx), is deposited by thermal evaporation or plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) to a thickness of 10 to 100 nanometers. The capping layer serves two critical functions: (1) it mechanically confines the group III metal during the liquid phase, preventing flow or deformation that would distort the patterned geometry, and (2) it permits diffusion of the group V precursor vapor (e.g., PH₃) into the liquid metal while blocking convective mixing or contamination. To ensure complete encapsulation, especially for high-aspect-ratio features, angled evaporation may be employed to coat the sidewalls of the patterned metal with an additional 4 to 50 nanometers of SiOx. This conformal coating is essential for maintaining geometric fidelity during growth.

The encapsulated structure is then subjected to a thermal treatment in a controlled atmosphere containing a vapor-phase group V precursor. For InP, phosphine (PH₃) diluted in hydrogen (H₂) is used, with typical concentrations of 10% PH₃ in H₂ further diluted to achieve partial pressures between 0.1 and 10 Torr. The growth is carried out in a low-pressure furnace—either hot-wall or cold-wall—at temperatures between 500 °C and 535 °C for durations of 10 to 20 minutes. At these temperatures, the group III metal (e.g., indium) melts into a liquid phase but remains confined within the SiOx template. Phosphorus atoms from the PH₃ gas diffuse through the SiOx cap and dissolve into the liquid indium, creating a supersaturated solution. Once the solubility limit is exceeded, InP nuclei precipitate out of the liquid. A key feature of this process is the formation of a phosphorus depletion zone around each growing nucleus, which locally reduces the phosphorus concentration and suppresses the formation of additional nuclei within the same template. By designing the patterned feature size to be smaller than or comparable to the depletion zone radius—which can be tuned via PH₃ partial pressure—single-crystal growth is achieved.

The resulting InP structures replicate the original indium template geometry with high fidelity, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) verifies stoichiometric 1:1 In:P composition, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirms the exclusive presence of zincblende-phase InP with no residual indium or secondary phases. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) mapping demonstrates that individual features—ranging from 3 to 20 micrometers in diameter—are predominantly single crystals, with occasional twin boundaries that do not significantly degrade electronic properties. A preferential crystallographic orientation along the (1 0 n) direction (where n = 1–2) is observed, likely due to anisotropic surface energies influenced by the MoOx nucleation layer.

The number of grains per feature follows a quadratic dependence on feature diameter, described by the equation N_grains = βd², where d is the diameter and β is a proportionality factor dependent on growth conditions. By reducing the PH₃ partial pressure from 1 Torr to 0.1 Torr, β decreases from 3 × 10⁻³ to 4 × 10⁻⁴, enabling near-single-crystal growth even in 20-micrometer features. This tunability allows for optimization based on application requirements.

In situ doping is achieved by introducing a dopant gas—such as germane (GeH₄) for n-type doping—into the growth chamber during the reaction step. Photoluminescence spectroscopy reveals a systematic blue shift in the emission peak with increasing GeH₄ partial pressure, consistent with the Burstein–Moss effect due to increased electron concentration. Carrier concentrations up to 1.3 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ are achieved, approaching the degenerate doping limit for InP. Urbach tail analysis indicates minimal band-tail broadening, confirming high crystal quality comparable to bulk single-crystal wafers.

Electronic devices fabricated from TLP-grown InP microwires demonstrate excellent performance. Long-channel Schottky n-type MOSFETs with 3-micrometer gate lengths and 125-nanometer body thickness exhibit ON-currents of 120 μA/μm at V_GS = V_DS = 2 V, ON/OFF ratios >10⁵, and peak extrinsic transconductance of 100 S/μm. Effective electron mobility, extracted via Sentaurus drift-diffusion simulations, is 675 cm²/V·s, surpassing many reported unpassivated InP nanowire devices. Top-gated photo-MOSFETs with transparent ITO gates show strong photoresponse under illumination, with peak responsivity of ~700 A/W and specific detectivity of ~8.4 × 10¹¹ Jones at room temperature—performance metrics competitive with state-of-the-art epitaxial InGaAs detectors.

The process is highly scalable. Full 4-inch Si/SiO₂ wafers have been processed in a simple cold-wall furnace, yielding uniform arrays of InP circles and bars with consistent photoluminescence intensity across the wafer. Growth on borosilicate glass and subsequent transfer to PET substrates using polyamic acid demonstrate compatibility with flexible electronics. Moreover, three-dimensional architectures—such as vertically stacked InP/SiOx multilayers—are achievable by starting with multilayered In/SiOx templates, enabling monolithic 3D integration for future high-density circuits.

The invention is not limited to InP. Proof-of-concept demonstrations of GaP and InSb growth confirm the general applicability of the TLP method to other III–V systems. Furthermore, the single-crystalline TLP-grown layers may serve as virtual substrates for subsequent epitaxial growth of heterostructures, thereby extending the utility of the method to complex device architectures.

In conclusion, the templated liquid-phase crystal growth method of the present invention provides a transformative approach to III–V semiconductor integration, combining single-crystal quality, arbitrary geometric patterning, wafer-scale scalability, low-temperature processing, and in situ doping control—all on amorphous substrates. This enables a wide range of applications in flexible electronics, large-area photonics, monolithic 3D integration, and heterogeneous system-on-chip platforms that were previously inaccessible with conventional epitaxial techniques.
