# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

In the field of clinical decision-making, predictive models have become increasingly integral to risk assessment, diagnosis, and treatment planning. These models, often derived from statistical or machine learning techniques, aim to estimate the likelihood of adverse health outcomes—such as myocardial infarction, stroke, or mortality—based on a patient’s clinical characteristics, laboratory results, and historical data. A cornerstone of model validation in this domain is the evaluation of performance metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and calibration. While these metrics provide valuable insights into the average behavior of a model across a population, they offer limited guidance regarding the reliability of individual predictions, particularly for specific patient subgroups that may deviate from the normative patterns observed in the training data.

The clinical utility of a predictive model hinges not only on its overall performance but also on the clinician’s ability to discern when a given prediction is trustworthy. In high-stakes medical contexts, where misclassification can lead to life-threatening consequences, it is insufficient to rely solely on aggregate performance statistics. For instance, a model may exhibit excellent discrimination and calibration across a large cohort yet perform poorly for certain demographic groups, patients with rare comorbidities, or those presenting atypical symptom profiles. The Framingham Risk Score, a widely used tool for estimating cardiovascular risk, exemplifies this limitation: while generally accurate, it has been shown to systematically underestimate risk in certain subpopulations, such as women with subclinical atherosclerosis. Such discrepancies underscore the need for a mechanism that can flag individual predictions as potentially unreliable, thereby enabling clinicians to exercise caution or seek corroborating evidence before acting on the model’s output.

Existing approaches to quantifying prediction reliability fall broadly into two categories: model-dependent and model-independent methods. Model-dependent techniques typically involve internal measures of uncertainty, such as confidence intervals derived from parameter estimation (e.g., in logistic regression) or Bayesian posterior distributions in probabilistic models. Neural networks may employ dropout-based uncertainty estimation or ensemble methods to gauge prediction confidence. However, these approaches are inherently tied to the architecture and training methodology of the specific model, limiting their applicability across diverse clinical tools. Moreover, many widely used clinical risk scores—such as the GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events) score—are not probabilistic in nature and do not natively support uncertainty quantification, rendering model-dependent reliability metrics inapplicable.

Model-independent approaches, by contrast, aim to assess reliability without requiring access to the internal mechanics of the predictive model. One prominent strategy involves retraining the model on an augmented dataset that includes the new test instance, with its label inferred from the original model’s prediction. The degree of change in model behavior before and after retraining is then used as a proxy for reliability. Another approach, known as the “trust score,” evaluates the geometric proximity of a test instance to training examples of the predicted class versus the alternative class, using nearest-neighbor distances. While conceptually appealing, these methods suffer from significant practical limitations. Most critically, they require access to the original training dataset—a condition rarely met in real-world clinical settings due to stringent privacy regulations, data governance policies, and proprietary restrictions. Even when such data are available, retraining complex models is computationally prohibitive for routine clinical use, especially in time-sensitive scenarios such as acute coronary syndrome management.

Furthermore, both classes of existing methods have been inadequately evaluated in the context of class imbalance, a pervasive feature of clinical datasets. In cardiovascular risk prediction, for example, the incidence of adverse events such as death or stroke is often low (e.g., <1% to 10%), resulting in highly skewed class distributions. Under such conditions, models may achieve high accuracy by predominantly predicting the negative class, while failing to reliably identify high-risk individuals. Traditional reliability metrics often fail to account for this imbalance, leading to misleading assessments of prediction trustworthiness. There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a reliability metric that is: (1) capable of identifying patient subgroups where model performance is demonstrably degraded; (2) applicable to any predictive model regardless of its underlying algorithm; (3) operable without access to the original training data, relying instead on summary statistics; and (4) robust in the presence of severe class imbalance.

This patent addresses these challenges by introducing a novel, model-independent method for estimating the unreliability of individual predictions generated by clinical risk models. The method leverages generative modeling principles to construct an alternate risk estimate based on the same underlying data distribution that informed the original model, without requiring direct access to individual patient records. By comparing the original model’s prediction with this alternate estimate, the method identifies discrepancies that signal insufficient support in the training data for confident prediction. This approach enables clinicians to recognize when a model’s output may be misleading, particularly in high-risk scenarios where erroneous predictions carry grave consequences. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in the safe and responsible deployment of predictive analytics in clinical practice.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system, method, and computer-readable medium for determining the unreliability of a prediction generated by a clinical risk model for a given patient. The invention is designed to identify patient-specific predictions that are likely to be inaccurate or misleading due to insufficient representativeness of the patient’s clinical profile within the training data used to develop the risk model. Unlike conventional approaches, the invention does not require access to the original training dataset or retraining of the predictive model, making it broadly applicable across diverse clinical settings and compatible with proprietary or privacy-restricted risk scoring systems.

At the core of the invention is an unreliability metric, denoted as \( U(\vec{x}) \), which quantifies the discrepancy between the prediction produced by a clinical risk model \( f(\vec{x}) \) and an alternate risk estimate \( P^G(y = 1 \mid \hat{y}) \) derived from a generative model trained on summary statistics of the original training data. The generative model approximates the joint distribution of patient features and outcomes using parametric or non-parametric techniques, such as multivariate normal distributions, and enables the computation of the probability that a patient belongs to the positive class (e.g., experiencing an adverse event) given the risk score assigned by the original model. The unreliability metric is defined as the absolute difference between these two estimates:  
\[
U(\vec{x}) = \left| P^G(y = 1 \mid \hat{y}) - \hat{y} \right|,
\]  
where \( \hat{y} = f(\vec{x}) \) is the risk score output by the clinical model for a patient with feature vector \( \vec{x} \). Higher values of \( U(\vec{x}) \) indicate greater disagreement between the two risk estimates and, consequently, lower confidence in the reliability of the original prediction.

The invention is model-independent, meaning it can be applied to any clinical risk model—whether based on logistic regression, machine learning algorithms, or empirical scoring rules—provided that the model outputs a risk score interpretable as a probability or convertible to one via a nomogram or calibration function. The method requires only summary statistics from the original training data, such as the prevalence of the outcome, and the mean and covariance matrices of the feature distributions for the positive and negative outcome classes. These statistics can be published alongside the risk model or made available through secure data-sharing agreements without compromising patient privacy.

In operation, the system receives a patient’s clinical feature vector \( \vec{x} \), computes the risk score \( \hat{y} \) using the clinical risk model, and then calculates the unreliability metric \( U(\vec{x}) \) using the generative model and the aforementioned summary statistics. If \( U(\vec{x}) \) exceeds a predefined threshold—such as the 99th percentile of unreliability scores observed in a reference population—the system flags the prediction as unreliable. Clinicians are thereby alerted to exercise caution in interpreting the risk score and may consider additional diagnostic tests, alternative risk models, or expert consultation before making clinical decisions.

Empirical validation using the GRACE risk score for 6-month mortality and a custom Ridge Logistic Regression model for in-hospital stroke demonstrates that patients with high unreliability scores form subgroups where the original risk model exhibits significantly reduced calibration, increased prediction error (as measured by normalized Brier score), and diminished discriminatory ability (as measured by AUC). Notably, these effects are most pronounced in the presence of class imbalance, where high-risk predictions are disproportionately associated with high unreliability. Comparative analysis with the trust score—a leading alternative reliability metric—reveals that the present invention more consistently identifies subgroups with degraded model performance, whereas the trust score may misclassify reliable predictions as untrustworthy or vice versa.

The invention thus fulfills a critical need in precision medicine by enabling real-time, patient-specific assessment of prediction reliability without compromising data privacy or computational efficiency. It enhances clinical decision-making by providing actionable insights into the limitations of predictive models, thereby reducing the risk of harm from overreliance on potentially inaccurate risk estimates.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for evaluating the reliability of individual predictions generated by clinical risk models. The system operates by comparing the output of a primary clinical risk model with an alternate risk estimate derived from a generative model that captures the statistical properties of the original training data. This comparison yields an unreliability metric that serves as a quantitative indicator of prediction trustworthiness. Below, we describe the components, operational workflow, mathematical formulation, and practical implementation of the invention in detail.

The invention comprises three principal components: (1) a clinical risk model \( f(\vec{x}) \), (2) a generative model \( P^G(y = 1 \mid \vec{x}) \), and (3) an unreliability computation module. The clinical risk model may be any existing or future model used in clinical practice to predict the probability of an adverse health outcome, such as mortality, stroke, or heart failure, based on a set of patient features \( \vec{x} \). These features may include demographic variables (e.g., age, sex), clinical measurements (e.g., blood pressure, heart rate), laboratory results (e.g., troponin levels, creatinine), and medical history (e.g., prior myocardial infarction, diabetes). The output of the model, \( \hat{y} = f(\vec{x}) \), is a scalar value between 0 and 1 representing the estimated risk of the adverse outcome.

The generative model is constructed using summary statistics from the same dataset that was used to develop or validate the clinical risk model. Critically, the invention does not require access to individual patient records from this dataset. Instead, it utilizes aggregate statistics such as the overall prevalence of the outcome \( P(y = 1) \), and the first- and second-order moments (mean vectors and covariance matrices) of the feature distributions conditioned on the outcome class. Specifically, let \( \mu_1 \) and \( \Sigma_1 \) denote the mean and covariance of features among patients who experienced the adverse outcome (positive class), and \( \mu_0 \) and \( \Sigma_0 \) denote the corresponding statistics for patients who did not (negative class). These parameters are sufficient to define a pair of multivariate normal (MVN) distributions that approximate the conditional feature distributions \( P(\vec{x} \mid y = 1) \) and \( P(\vec{x} \mid y = 0) \).

Using these MVN distributions, the generative model simulates synthetic patient populations for both outcome classes. For each simulated patient, the clinical risk model \( f(\cdot) \) is applied to generate a risk score \( \hat{y} \). By aggregating these simulated scores, the system constructs empirical probability density functions (PDFs) \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y = 1) \) and \( P^G(\hat{y} \mid y = 0) \), representing the distribution of risk scores that would be assigned by the clinical model to patients in each outcome class, according to the generative assumptions. These PDFs are estimated using histogram-based methods with adaptive binning and convergence criteria based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to ensure numerical stability.

Once the conditional PDFs are obtained, Bayes’ theorem is applied to compute the posterior probability that a patient belongs to the positive class given their risk score \( \hat{y} \):  
\[
P^G(y = 1 \mid \hat{y}) = \frac{P^G(\hat{y} \mid y = 1) P(y = 1)}{P^G(\hat{y} \mid y = 1) P(y = 1) + P^G(\hat{y} \mid y = 0) P(y = 0)}.
\]  
This quantity represents the alternate risk estimate derived from the generative perspective. The unreliability metric is then computed as the absolute deviation between this estimate and the original model’s prediction:  
\[
U(\vec{x}) = \left| P^G(y = 1 \mid \hat{y}) - \hat{y} \right|.
\]

The system operates as follows. Upon receiving a new patient’s feature vector \( \vec{x} \), it first computes \( \hat{y} = f(\vec{x}) \) using the clinical risk model. It then retrieves the precomputed generative model parameters (means, covariances, prevalence) and uses them to evaluate \( P^G(y = 1 \mid \hat{y}) \) via the PDFs described above. The unreliability score \( U(\vec{x}) \) is calculated and compared against a reliability threshold. This threshold may be fixed (e.g., 0.1) or dynamically determined based on the empirical distribution of unreliability scores in a reference population (e.g., the 99th percentile). If \( U(\vec{x}) \) exceeds the threshold, the system generates an alert indicating that the prediction is unreliable.

The invention is particularly effective in settings of class imbalance. As demonstrated in validation studies using the GRACE dataset, when the positive class is rare (e.g., 7.15% mortality), high-risk predictions are more likely to exhibit high unreliability because the model has limited exposure to true positive cases during training. The generative model, by explicitly modeling the scarcity of positive examples, captures this uncertainty and flags such predictions accordingly. Calibration analyses confirm that patients in the top 1% of unreliability scores exhibit significant miscalibration, with the clinical model systematically overestimating or underestimating risk relative to observed outcomes. Moreover, these subgroups show statistically significant increases in normalized Brier score and decreases in AUC, confirming degraded predictive performance.

The method is agnostic to the choice of generative model. While the preferred embodiment uses MVN distributions for computational efficiency and analytical tractability, alternative generative approaches—such as Gaussian mixture models, kernel density estimators, or deep generative networks—may be employed to better capture complex feature dependencies. The core principle remains unchanged: construct an alternate risk estimate from summary statistics and compare it to the original prediction.

Implementation of the invention may occur within electronic health record (EHR) systems, clinical decision support platforms, or standalone software applications. The required summary statistics can be disseminated by model developers alongside the risk scoring algorithm, ensuring compatibility without exposing sensitive patient data. Computational requirements are modest, involving only matrix operations and histogram evaluations, making the method suitable for real-time use in clinical workflows.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, privacy-preserving, and model-agnostic solution to the critical problem of prediction reliability in clinical risk modeling. By enabling clinicians to identify when a model’s output is likely to be misleading, it enhances patient safety and supports more informed, personalized care.
