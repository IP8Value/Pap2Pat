# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of agricultural diagnostics and plant health monitoring, specifically to a non-invasive method and system for the early detection of pathogen infection in crop plants. More particularly, the invention provides a hydrogel-based extraction technique coupled with fluorescence spectroscopy for the selective detection of blue fluorescent phytoanticipins—such as chlorogenic acid and salicylic acid—that accumulate in plant tissues in response to microbial infection. The invention further encompasses a sensor device comprising a biocompatible hydrogel film, preferably composed of agarose, configured to extract water-soluble phenolic compounds from the apoplastic fluid of living plant leaves through natural cuticular defects without causing physical damage to the plant tissue. This method enables real-time, in situ monitoring of plant immune responses prior to the manifestation of visible disease symptoms, thereby facilitating timely intervention strategies such as targeted pesticide application or localized removal of infected zones. The technical domain spans plant pathology, analytical chemistry, biosensor engineering, and precision agriculture, with direct applicability to sustainable farming practices aimed at minimizing crop loss and reducing chemical input through proactive disease management.

## BACKGROUND ART

Conventional approaches to detecting plant pathogen infections have historically relied on post-symptomatic diagnostic techniques that require destructive sampling and complex laboratory infrastructure. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based molecular diagnostics remain the gold standards for pathogen identification in agricultural settings; however, these methods necessitate the homogenization of leaf tissue—typically via mortar-and-pestle grinding—to release intracellular pathogens or their biomarkers, rendering them incompatible with live-plant monitoring. Furthermore, both ELISA and PCR are resource-intensive, requiring trained personnel, controlled environments, and significant time investment, which collectively impede their deployment in field conditions where rapid decision-making is critical. Although advances in machine learning have spurred interest in image-based disease detection using visible or hyperspectral imaging, these computational models suffer from limited generalizability due to insufficient training data that captures the full variability of environmental stressors, lighting conditions, and plant phenotypes encountered in real-world agricultural landscapes. Consequently, such systems often fail outside controlled greenhouse or laboratory environments, undermining their practical utility for early warning systems.

Parallel research has explored the use of intrinsic plant fluorescence as a non-invasive indicator of physiological stress. Under ultraviolet (UV) excitation, healthy plant leaves emit a characteristic multispectral fluorescence profile comprising blue-green fluorescence (BGF) peaking near 440 nm (F440) and red/far-red chlorophyll fluorescence at approximately 680 nm (F680) and 740 nm (F740). The BGF component arises primarily from phenolic compounds—such as ferulic acid, p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, scopoletin, and chlorogenic acid—that are covalently bound to epidermal cell walls or present as soluble metabolites in the apoplast. Upon pathogen challenge, plants upregulate the biosynthesis of these phenylpropanoid-derived phytoanticipins as part of their innate immune response, leading to measurable increases in BGF intensity. Multispectral fluorescence imaging has thus been proposed as a tool for stress detection; however, its quantitative accuracy is severely compromised by chlorophyll-mediated fluorescence quenching. Because chlorophyll strongly absorbs blue light in mesophyll cells, the observed BGF signal from intact leaves represents a convoluted mixture of true phenolic emission and quenching artifacts, making it difficult to correlate fluorescence intensity with actual metabolite concentration. This limitation renders conventional fluorescence imaging unsuitable for precise, early-stage quantification of defense-related compounds.

In contrast, hydrogel-based extraction technologies have emerged in biomedical contexts as a means to non-invasively sample biofluids such as sweat. These systems typically employ a thin film of agarose or similar hydrophilic polymer placed in contact with human skin, where capillary forces and diffusion drive the passive uptake of water-soluble analytes into the gel matrix. Integrated electrochemical sensors then detect target molecules like lactate, glucose, cortisol, or chloride ions directly within the hydrogel. Agarose is particularly favored due to its biocompatibility, mechanical stability, and porous microstructure (pore diameters of 0.1–1 µm), which permits rapid molecular transport while excluding large proteins or cellular debris. Inspired by this paradigm, recent studies have hypothesized that analogous hydrogel interfaces could interface with plant surfaces to extract apoplastic solutes. However, the plant cuticle—a waxy, hydrophobic barrier composed of long-chain aliphatic compounds (C20–C40)—poses a formidable obstacle to aqueous exchange. While natural micropores, stomata, trichome bases, and micro-damaged sites permit limited leaching of internal compounds under certain conditions, the efficiency of passive diffusion across this barrier remains poorly characterized, and no prior art has successfully demonstrated the integration of hydrogel extraction with fluorescence-based detection of pathogen-induced metabolites in living crops.

Moreover, existing attempts to quantify salicylic acid—a key phytohormone mediating systemic acquired resistance—have been hampered by methodological constraints. Destructive methanolic extraction followed by fluorescence measurement suffers from severe chlorophyll interference unless extreme dilution is employed, which reduces sensitivity and complicates calibration. Even then, the resulting data reflects total tissue content rather than dynamic apoplastic concentrations relevant to intercellular signaling. No known technique enables repeated, spatially resolved sampling of defense metabolites from the same leaf over time, a capability essential for tracking infection progression before symptom onset. Thus, despite decades of research into plant-pathogen interactions, there remains an unmet need for a simple, field-deployable, non-destructive diagnostic platform that can reliably detect the biochemical signatures of incipient infection with high specificity and minimal operator expertise.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

### Technical Problem

The primary technical problem addressed by the present invention is the lack of a practical, non-invasive method for the early detection of pathogen infection in crop plants prior to the appearance of visible disease symptoms. Existing diagnostic tools either require destructive tissue sampling, are too slow or expensive for routine field use, or suffer from poor reliability due to confounding factors such as chlorophyll fluorescence quenching in optical measurements. Specifically, while it is well established that plants accumulate specific blue-fluorescent phenolic compounds—including salicylic acid and chlorogenic acid—as part of their defense response to microbial attack, current analytical approaches cannot accurately quantify these metabolites in living plants without disrupting tissue integrity or introducing significant measurement artifacts. The hydrophobic nature of the plant cuticle further impedes direct access to apoplastic fluids, where many of these signaling molecules reside, thereby preventing the development of surface-contact biosensors analogous to those used in human wearable diagnostics. Consequently, farmers and agronomists are forced to rely on reactive disease management strategies that are less effective and more environmentally damaging than proactive interventions enabled by presymptomatic detection.

### Solution to Problem

The present invention solves this problem by introducing a novel hydrogel extraction and fluorescence detection system specifically engineered for plant health monitoring. The core innovation lies in the use of a thin, biocompatible hydrogel film—preferably composed of agarose—that is temporarily adhered to the surface of a living plant leaf to selectively extract water-soluble, blue-fluorescent defense metabolites from the apoplast through naturally occurring cuticular defects such as micropores, stomatal openings, trichome bases, and micro-damaged regions. Unlike conventional solvent-based extraction methods, this hydrogel film does not penetrate or disrupt plant cells, thereby preserving tissue viability and enabling repeated measurements on the same leaf over time. Critically, because the hydrogel excludes chlorophyll and other hydrophobic cellular components, the extracted sample is free from fluorescence quenching effects that plague direct leaf fluorescence measurements. After a brief incubation period (typically 1–4 hours), the hydrogel film is illuminated with UV light (e.g., 310 nm excitation), and the resulting blue fluorescence emission (peaking between 410–440 nm) is measured using a portable spectrofluorometer or even a simple UV lamp with visual or photographic assessment. The intensity of this fluorescence correlates directly with the concentration of pathogen-induced phenolic compounds—particularly chlorogenic acid and salicylic acid—in the leaf apoplast, providing a quantitative readout of the plant’s immune activation status. To enhance adhesion to the hydrophobic leaf surface, the invention optionally includes a pretreatment step involving brief ethanol application to partially dissolve cuticular waxes, thereby improving hydrogel-leaf contact and boosting extraction efficiency by up to two-fold. This entire process requires no specialized training, generates no hazardous waste, and can be performed directly in the field using low-cost, reusable equipment.

### Advantageous Effect of Invention

The invention offers several significant advantages over existing plant disease detection methods. First, it is entirely non-invasive and non-destructive, allowing continuous monitoring of individual plants throughout the growing season without compromising yield or health. Second, it eliminates chlorophyll interference by physically separating fluorescent metabolites from photosynthetic pigments during extraction, thereby enabling accurate, linear quantification of defense compounds without the need for extensive sample dilution or complex correction algorithms. Third, the method is rapid, with results obtainable within hours of hydrogel application, compared to days required for laboratory-based ELISA or PCR. Fourth, it is highly cost-effective, utilizing inexpensive, plant-derived agarose and standard UV light sources rather than proprietary reagents or expensive instrumentation. Fifth, the system is adaptable to a wide range of crop species, as demonstrated by successful application in both cherry tomato and tobacco, suggesting broad agronomic relevance. Sixth, the technique detects biochemical changes associated with pathogen recognition—such as the accumulation of chlorogenic acid within 14 days of Ralstonia solanacearum infection—well before macroscopic symptoms appear, thereby enabling truly preventive disease management. Seventh, the modular design allows integration with digital imaging or smartphone-based fluorescence readers for automated data collection and geospatial mapping of infection hotspots across large fields. Finally, by focusing on endogenous plant defense markers rather than pathogen-specific antigens or DNA, the method is inherently capable of detecting a wide spectrum of biotic stresses (bacterial, fungal, viral) through a common physiological response pathway, reducing the need for multiple pathogen-specific assays.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS

### <Fluorescence Emission Phenomenon>

The fluorescence emission phenomenon central to the present invention arises from the intrinsic photophysical properties of certain phenolic secondary metabolites produced by plants in response to biotic stress. When exposed to ultraviolet (UV) radiation in the range of approximately 300–350 nm, these compounds absorb photons and undergo electronic excitation to higher energy states, subsequently relaxing to their ground state through the emission of longer-wavelength visible light, predominantly in the blue to green region (400–520 nm). This blue-green fluorescence (BGF) is distinct from the red and far-red chlorophyll fluorescence (680–740 nm) and originates primarily from phenylpropanoid derivatives such as chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid, scopoletin, and salicylic acid, all of which contain conjugated aromatic ring systems capable of efficient fluorescence. In healthy plants, baseline levels of these compounds are relatively low, resulting in weak BGF signals. However, upon recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), plants activate defense signaling cascades that upregulate the phenylpropanoid pathway, leading to a marked increase in the biosynthesis and accumulation of these fluorescent phytoanticipins in the apoplastic space—the aqueous compartment surrounding plant cells. The present invention exploits this natural amplification mechanism by capturing these elevated metabolite levels non-invasively. Crucially, when these compounds are extracted into an aqueous hydrogel matrix, they are removed from the quenching influence of chlorophyll, which in intact leaves absorbs much of the emitted blue light due to spectral overlap. As a result, the fluorescence quantum yield of the extracted phenolics increases dramatically, producing a strong, quantifiable signal that directly reflects their concentration in the leaf apoplast. The peak emission wavelength varies slightly depending on the specific compound: salicylic acid exhibits maximum fluorescence near 410 nm, while chlorogenic acid peaks around 428–435 nm. This spectral signature not only confirms the presence of defense-related metabolites but also allows for semi-quantitative differentiation between compound classes based on emission maxima, enhancing diagnostic specificity.

### <Electrochemical Behavior>

Although the primary detection modality of the present invention relies on optical fluorescence rather than electrochemical transduction, the underlying principles of molecular transport and interfacial behavior are closely aligned with those of electrochemical biosensors. The hydrogel film functions as a passive diffusion medium that mimics the role of an electrolyte in traditional electrochemical cells, facilitating the movement of charged or polar analytes from the plant apoplast into the gel matrix. Water-soluble phenolic acids such as salicylic acid (pKa ≈ 2.97) and chlorogenic acid (pKa ≈ 3.5–4.5) exist predominantly in their deprotonated, anionic forms at physiological pH (5.5–6.5 in the apoplast), enhancing their solubility in the aqueous pores of the agarose hydrogel. The diffusion process is driven by concentration gradients established between the high metabolite levels in infected leaf tissue and the initially analyte-free hydrogel. Agarose, a linear polysaccharide composed of alternating D-galactose and 3,6-anhydro-L-galactose units, forms a three-dimensional network with pore sizes ranging from 0.1 to 1 µm when hydrated, which is sufficiently large to accommodate small organic molecules (<1 kDa) while excluding macromolecules like proteins, chlorophyll-protein complexes, and cellular organelles. This size-exclusion property ensures that only relevant defense metabolites are extracted, minimizing background interference. While the current embodiment does not incorporate electrodes or redox reactions, the system is readily adaptable to electrochemical detection by embedding working, reference, and counter electrodes within the hydrogel film. For instance, salicylic acid can be oxidized at carbon-based electrodes at potentials around +0.8 V vs. Ag/AgCl, generating a measurable current proportional to concentration. Such an integrated electrochemical-fluorescence dual-mode sensor could provide redundant validation and enhanced sensitivity. However, the purely optical approach described herein was selected for its simplicity, robustness, and compatibility with low-resource settings where electrical instrumentation may be unavailable.

### <Methyl Salicylate Sensor>

While the exemplary embodiments focus on salicylic acid and chlorogenic acid as key biomarkers, the invention is equally applicable to the detection of methyl salicylate (MeSA), a volatile derivative of salicylic acid that plays a critical role in systemic signaling during plant immune responses. MeSA is synthesized from salicylic acid by the enzyme salicylic acid carboxyl methyltransferase (SAMT) and can diffuse through air spaces or be transported via the phloem to distal tissues, where it is hydrolyzed back to salicylic acid to activate defense genes. Although MeSA itself is not fluorescent, it can be hydrolyzed within the hydrogel matrix under mild alkaline conditions (e.g., by incorporating a trace amount of NaOH or using a buffered agarose formulation at pH 8–9) to regenerate fluorescent salicylic acid. Alternatively, the hydrogel may be functionalized with immobilized SAMT inhibitors or esterases to control the conversion kinetics. In practice, a hydrogel film placed on a leaf would capture both free salicylic acid and MeSA that has condensed or dissolved at the leaf surface from the headspace. Upon hydrolysis, the total salicylate pool becomes detectable via fluorescence, providing a more comprehensive measure of salicylic acid pathway activation. This extension significantly broadens the diagnostic scope of the invention, as MeSA emission is a well-documented early event in pathogen response, often preceding visible symptoms by days. Moreover, because MeSA is volatile, its detection via hydrogel extraction offers a unique advantage over destructive methods that lose gaseous components during sample preparation. Thus, the methyl salicylate sensor embodiment represents a powerful refinement of the core technology, enabling the monitoring of both local and systemic immune signaling with a single, non-invasive assay.

### <Method for Early Detection of Pathogen Infection in Crop>

The method for early detection of pathogen infection in crop plants according to the present invention comprises the following sequential steps: (a) selecting a target plant exhibiting no visible disease symptoms; (b) optionally applying a small volume of ethanol (e.g., 10–50 µL) to the leaf surface using a pipette and gently wiping it with absorbent paper to reduce cuticular hydrophobicity; (c) placing a pre-formed hydrogel film—comprising 2–5 wt% agarose in water, cast into a 1–2 mm thick sheet of 1–4 cm² area—onto the treated or untreated leaf surface; (d) maintaining contact between the hydrogel and leaf for a predetermined extraction period of 1 to 4 hours under ambient conditions; (e) removing the hydrogel film and exposing it to UV light at an excitation wavelength of 300–350 nm; and (f) measuring the intensity of blue fluorescence emission in the 400–500 nm range using a spectrofluorometer, digital camera with appropriate filters, or visual comparison against calibrated standards. The fluorescence intensity is then correlated with the concentration of pathogen-induced phenolic compounds using a pre-established calibration curve, wherein higher intensities indicate active defense responses suggestive of incipient infection. The method is particularly effective for detecting bacterial pathogens such as Ralstonia solanacearum, which trigger rapid accumulation of chlorogenic acid within days of root exposure, but is equally applicable to fungal and viral infections that elicit similar phenylpropanoid responses. By performing this assay weekly or biweekly during high-risk growing periods, farmers can identify infection foci before symptoms develop, allowing for precise, localized interventions that minimize chemical usage and prevent epidemic spread. The method’s non-destructive nature permits longitudinal tracking of individual plants, enabling the assessment of treatment efficacy and disease progression dynamics in real time.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

In this example, the hydrogel extraction technique was validated using salicylic acid as a model analyte in cherry tomato plants (Solanum lycopersicum cv. Yellow-mimi). A rooted branch was prepared by cutting a 50 cm shoot from a 4-month-old plant and submerging its base in water for 7 days to induce adventitious rooting. The rooted branch was then transferred to a container holding 200 mL of an aqueous solution containing 7 mmol/L salicylic acid and allowed to absorb the compound through its roots for 24 hours under indoor lighting near a south-facing window. Following absorption, a 2 cm × 2 cm × 1 mm hydrogel film composed of 4 wt% agarose was placed on the adaxial surface of a mature leaf and left in contact for 3 hours. The hydrogel film was fabricated by dissolving 0.8 g of agar powder in 19.2 mL of deionized water, heating the mixture in a microwave until fully dissolved, pouring it into a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) frame sandwiched between glass slides, and allowing it to cool to room temperature to form a solid gel. After extraction, the hydrogel film exhibited strong blue fluorescence when illuminated with a xenon lamp at 310 nm excitation. Fluorescence spectroscopy revealed an emission peak at 410 nm with an intensity corresponding to a salicylic acid concentration of 0.55 mmol/L in the hydrogel, as determined by comparison with a calibration curve generated from hydrogel films spiked with known concentrations of salicylic acid (0–1 mmol/L). The linear correlation coefficient (R²) of the calibration curve was 0.999, with a limit of detection (LOD) of 1.0 µmol/L (3.3 ng/cm²). Based on the hydrogel volume (0.4 cm³) and water content (96%), the total salicylic acid extracted was calculated as 7.2 µg/cm² of leaf area. In parallel, a destructive methanolic extraction of 0.5 g of the same leaf yielded a salicylic acid content of 33 µg/cm² after accounting for dilution factors necessary to mitigate chlorophyll quenching. This indicated an extraction efficiency of approximately 23%, demonstrating that the hydrogel method captures a significant fraction of the apoplastic salicylic acid pool without tissue damage.

### Comparative Example 1

For comparison, a conventional methanolic extraction was performed on the same salicylic acid-fed cherry tomato leaf used in Example 1. The leaf sample (0.5 g) was ground in a mortar with 10 mL of methanol, centrifuged at 10,000×g for 5 minutes, and the supernatant collected. The residue was re-extracted with an additional 10 mL of methanol, and the combined supernatants were diluted to a final volume of either 50 mL or 2000 mL with methanol. Fluorescence spectra of both dilutions were recorded at 310 nm excitation. The 50 mL dilution showed negligible fluorescence below 450 nm due to overwhelming quenching by co-extracted chlorophyll, which absorbs strongly in the blue region. Only after extreme dilution to 2000 mL did a discernible salicylic acid emission peak emerge at 410 nm, allowing quantification via the calibration curve. This requirement for massive dilution not only reduced analytical sensitivity but also introduced significant error due to volumetric inaccuracies and potential analyte loss during handling. Moreover, the process destroyed the leaf tissue, precluding any follow-up measurements. In stark contrast, the hydrogel method of Example 1 provided a clear, quantifiable signal without dilution, preserved leaf integrity, and required only 3 hours versus the 1–2 hours plus centrifugation and evaporation steps needed for methanolic extraction. This comparative example underscores the superior practicality and accuracy of the hydrogel approach for in vivo metabolite monitoring.

### Example 2

This example demonstrates the application of the hydrogel extraction method to detect pathogen-induced metabolites in cherry tomato plants infected with Ralstonia solanacearum. Three-month-old plants of cultivar Regina were grown hydroponically in a nutrient solution inoculated with R. solanacearum at a concentration exceeding 10⁶ cfu/mL and maintained at 35°C under 12-hour LED illumination (3000 lx) for 14 days. On day 14 post-inoculation, when no visible wilting or necrosis was apparent, a 2 cm × 2 cm agarose hydrogel film (4 wt%) was placed on a leaf and left for 3 hours. Post-extraction fluorescence spectroscopy showed a distinct emission peak at 428 nm, indicative of chlorogenic acid accumulation. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis of a parallel methanolic leaf extract confirmed the presence of chlorogenic acid at 0.24 mg/g fresh weight, with a retention time and fragmentation pattern matching authentic standards. The hydrogel film’s fluorescence intensity corresponded to an estimated chlorogenic acid concentration of approximately 0.025 mmol/L, consistent with the LC–MS/MS findings when adjusted for extraction efficiency. Notably, no chlorophyll fluorescence (680 nm) was detected in the hydrogel, confirming the exclusion of photosynthetic pigments. This example proves that the method can non-invasively detect specific defense metabolites upregulated during bacterial infection, even in the absence of symptoms.

### Example 3

To evaluate the effect of leaf surface pretreatment on extraction efficiency, ethanol application was tested prior to hydrogel placement on R. solanacearum-infected cherry tomato leaves (14 days post-inoculation). A 20 µL droplet of absolute ethanol was applied to a leaf area using a glass pipette and immediately blotted with filter paper to remove excess liquid, leaving a thin residue that partially dissolved the cuticular wax layer. A hydrogel film was then placed on the treated area and left for 3 hours. A control hydrogel was simultaneously applied to an adjacent, untreated area of the same leaf. Fluorescence measurements revealed that the ethanol-treated sample exhibited nearly twice the blue fluorescence intensity (peak at 428 nm) compared to the untreated control. This enhancement is attributed to improved hydrogel-leaf adhesion and increased permeability at cuticular defect sites due to transient wax disruption. The result demonstrates a simple, low-cost strategy to boost sensitivity without altering the core hydrogel composition, making the method more robust across diverse leaf morphologies and wax densities.

### Comparative Example 2

A water immersion extraction was conducted as an alternative non-hydrogel method for comparison. Half of a leaf from a salicylic acid-fed cherry tomato plant (as in Example 1) was submerged in 1.5 mL of deionized water contained in a polypropylene tube and left for 3 hours. The resulting aqueous extract was transferred to a quartz cuvette and analyzed by fluorescence spectroscopy. While salicylic acid was detectable, the procedure required careful handling to avoid leaf damage, precise volume measurement, and cuvette-based instrumentation not suited for field use. Moreover, the extraction efficiency was only ~14%, lower than the 23% achieved with the hydrogel film in Example 1. The hydrogel method’s solid-phase format eliminated liquid transfer steps, reduced contamination risk, and allowed direct UV illumination of the extraction medium, streamlining the workflow. This comparative example highlights the operational superiority of the hydrogel platform for practical agricultural diagnostics.

### Example 4

The versatility of the hydrogel method was tested on tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Tsukuba ichi-gou), a species with thicker leaves and denser cuticles than tomato. Two-month-old plants grown in potting soil were treated by pouring 300 mL of 7 mmol/L salicylic acid solution onto the soil surface 24 hours before hydrogel extraction. A 2 cm × 2 cm agarose film was applied to a mature leaf for 3 hours. Post-extraction fluorescence showed a peak at 410 nm corresponding to a salicylic acid concentration of 0.2 mmol/L in the hydrogel—lower than in tomato but still clearly above background. This difference likely stems from variations in root uptake efficiency, translocation rates, or apoplastic buffering capacity between species. Nevertheless, the successful detection confirms the method’s cross-species applicability, suggesting it can be adapted to major crops including rice, wheat, and soybean with minor protocol optimization.

### Comparative Example 3

Hydrogel films were prepared using alternative hydrophilic polymers to assess material dependency. Films of 4 wt% polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and 2 wt% polyacrylamide (PAAm) were cast using the same PTFE frame method and applied to salicylic acid-fed tomato leaves for 3 hours. Both alternative gels extracted salicylic acid, but with 30–40% lower fluorescence intensity compared to agarose. Agarose’s superior performance is attributed to its larger pore structure and higher water content (>95%), which facilitate faster diffusion kinetics. Additionally, agarose’s plant-derived origin enhances biodegradability and reduces environmental impact. This comparison validates agarose as the optimal hydrogel matrix for the intended application.

### Example 5

Spatial heterogeneity of defense metabolite distribution was investigated by applying hydrogel films to different regions of the same infected cherry tomato leaf (14 days post-R. solanacearum inoculation). Films were placed on the leaf tip, middle, and base, as well as on leaves from upper, middle, and lower canopy positions. All locations exhibited detectable blue fluorescence (428 nm peak), though intensities varied by up to 2-fold, with higher signals generally observed in younger, upper leaves. This demonstrates the method’s ability to map metabolic activity across plant architecture, providing insights into systemic defense propagation. Such spatial resolution is unattainable with bulk destructive methods and is valuable for understanding infection dynamics and optimizing sampling protocols.

### Comparative Example 4

A commercial immunochromatography kit (ImmunoStrip® for R. solanacearum) was used to test the same infected plants from Example 2 on day 14. The kit returned positive results, confirming pathogen presence. However, when applied on day 3 post-inoculation—before metabolite accumulation was detectable by hydrogel extraction—the kit yielded false negatives due to low bacterial titers below its detection threshold (~10⁴ cfu/g tissue). In contrast, the hydrogel method detected rising chlorogenic acid levels as early as day 3 in subsequent trials (data not shown), leveraging the plant’s amplified biochemical response rather than direct pathogen detection. This illustrates the invention’s unique advantage in presymptomatic diagnosis, where host response markers precede detectable pathogen loads.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The present invention possesses broad industrial applicability across multiple sectors of the agricultural and agri-tech industries. In precision farming, the hydrogel-based detection system can be integrated into routine scouting protocols to enable early, site-specific disease management, thereby reducing unnecessary pesticide applications and promoting sustainable crop protection. Seed and nursery companies can utilize the method for quality control, ensuring pathogen-free stock before distribution. Plant breeding programs may employ it as a high-throughput phenotyping tool to screen germplasm for enhanced defense metabolite production under pathogen challenge. Agrochemical developers could use the platform to evaluate the efficacy of novel elicitors or resistance-inducing compounds by monitoring real-time changes in phytoanticipin levels. Furthermore, the technology is amenable to automation and miniaturization; for instance, disposable hydrogel patches paired with smartphone-based fluorescence readers could empower smallholder farmers in developing regions to perform affordable, on-farm diagnostics without laboratory access. Given its reliance on universal plant defense pathways rather than pathogen-specific markers, the invention is applicable to a wide array of crops—including but not limited to tomatoes, potatoes, tobacco, rice, wheat, and grapes—and effective against diverse pathogens such as bacteria (e.g., Ralstonia, Xanthomonas), fungi (e.g., Fusarium, Botrytis), and viruses that trigger phenylpropanoid responses. The use of food-grade, biodegradable agarose ensures environmental safety and regulatory compliance, while the simplicity of the protocol minimizes training requirements. Consequently, the invention represents a scalable, economically viable solution for transforming plant disease management from a reactive to a proactive paradigm, with significant potential to enhance global food security and reduce agricultural environmental footprints.
