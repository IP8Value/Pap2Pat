# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) represents a pre-invasive form of breast cancer characterized by the proliferation of malignant epithelial cells confined within the lumen of mammary ducts without invasion into the surrounding stromal tissue. DCIS accounts for approximately twenty percent of all screen-detected breast cancers and presents a significant clinical challenge due to its heterogeneous biological behavior and uncertain natural history. While some DCIS lesions remain indolent over prolonged periods, others progress to invasive breast cancer (IBC), with historical data indicating that between twenty and fifty-three percent of untreated DCIS cases may develop into invasive disease over a period of ten years or more. The inability to reliably distinguish between indolent and aggressive DCIS lesions has led to a standard-of-care approach centered on local control through surgical excision—either via lumpectomy or breast-conserving surgery (BCS)—often supplemented with adjuvant radiotherapy and, in select cases, endocrine therapy. Despite these interventions, recurrence remains a substantial concern, with ten to thirty-five percent of DCIS patients experiencing local recurrence (LR) following BCS, and approximately half of these recurrences manifesting as invasive carcinomas.

The central clinical dilemma in managing DCIS lies in the risk of both under-treatment and over-treatment. On one hand, aggressive therapeutic regimens expose patients with biologically indolent disease to unnecessary morbidity, including radiation-induced toxicity and surgical complications. On the other hand, insufficient treatment may fail to prevent progression to life-threatening invasive cancer in high-risk individuals. This therapeutic conundrum underscores the urgent need for robust, reproducible, and biologically grounded prognostic biomarkers capable of stratifying DCIS patients according to their individual risk of recurrence.

Current clinical tools for risk stratification, such as the Van Nuys Prognostic Index (VNPI) and the Memorial Sloan Kettering DCIS nomogram, rely primarily on conventional clinicopathological parameters—including patient age, tumor size, nuclear grade, margin status, and the presence of comedo necrosis. However, these models suffer from limited reproducibility, inconsistent predictive performance across diverse patient populations, and an inability to capture the underlying molecular and cellular heterogeneity that drives differential clinical outcomes. Moreover, these indices do not incorporate molecular or subcellular features that may provide deeper insight into the biological aggressiveness of a given lesion.

Gene expression-based assays, such as the Oncotype DX Breast DCIS Score, have emerged as potential adjuncts to traditional risk assessment. While this commercially available test offers some prognostic value, its validation is restricted to only two clinical cohorts (ECOG E5194 and Ontario DCIS), and it demonstrates poor discrimination among patients classified as intermediate or high risk. Furthermore, the assay’s applicability is limited to specific subsets of DCIS—namely, low- or intermediate-grade tumors ≤2.5 cm or high-grade tumors ≤1 cm with clear margins ≥3 mm—thereby excluding a significant proportion of real-world DCIS cases. Importantly, such genomic approaches focus predominantly on proliferation-related gene signatures and may overlook critical phenotypic manifestations of genomic instability that occur at the organelle level.

A growing body of evidence implicates centrosome amplification (CA) as a key driver of chromosomal instability (CIN), intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH), and disease progression in various malignancies, including breast cancer. Centrosomes, which serve as the primary microtubule-organizing centers in mammalian cells, normally duplicate precisely once per cell cycle, resulting in two centrosomes during mitosis. In contrast, cancer cells frequently exhibit CA—an abnormal increase in either the number (numerical amplification) or volume (structural amplification) of centrosomes. CA is detectable even in pre-invasive lesions such as DCIS and correlates strongly with higher histological grade, larger tumor size, and increased risk of recurrence and metastasis. Dysregulation of centrosome duplication is governed by a network of proteins, including Aurora-A kinase, Nek2 kinase, cyclin D, cyclin E, and the p53/p21 axis, all of which have been implicated in DCIS pathogenesis.

Despite these associations, the prognostic utility of CA in DCIS has remained unexplored in clinical practice, largely due to the absence of standardized, quantitative methodologies for assessing centrosomal aberrations in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) clinical specimens. Traditional histopathological evaluation lacks the resolution to discern subtle numerical or volumetric changes in centrosomes, and prior studies relying on two-dimensional imaging have been unable to accurately capture the three-dimensional architecture of these organelles. Consequently, it has been unclear whether numerical CA, structural CA, or a combination thereof holds greater prognostic significance, and whether the frequency (i.e., proportion of affected cells) or severity (i.e., degree of deviation from normal) of CA is the more informative metric.

This gap in knowledge has hindered the translation of centrosomal biology into actionable clinical tools. There exists, therefore, a critical unmet need for a robust, reproducible, and clinically applicable method to quantify centrosomal abnormalities in DCIS tissue samples and to leverage this information for improved risk stratification. Such a method would not only enhance our understanding of DCIS biology but also empower clinicians to tailor therapeutic decisions—particularly regarding the use of adjuvant radiotherapy—based on objective, biologically relevant metrics rather than imprecise clinicopathological surrogates.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, quantitative methodology for centrosomal phenotyping in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) to generate a Centrosome Amplification Score (CAS) that robustly predicts the risk of local recurrence (LR) within ten years of diagnosis. The invention addresses a longstanding clinical challenge in DCIS management by offering a biologically grounded, objective, and reproducible prognostic biomarker that surpasses the predictive accuracy of existing clinicopathological indices such as the Van Nuys Prognostic Index (VNPI) and commercially available genomic assays like the Oncotype DX DCIS Score.

The core of the invention resides in a semi-automated analytical pipeline that integrates high-resolution three-dimensional (3D) immunofluorescence imaging with algorithm-driven quantitation of both numerical and structural centrosomal aberrations. Specifically, centrosomes are labeled in FFPE tissue sections using an antibody against γ-tubulin, a conserved component of the pericentriolar material, while nuclei are counterstained with Hoechst dye. Confocal microscopy is employed to acquire z-stack images of regions of interest (ROIs) within DCIS lesions, ensuring consistent imaging parameters across all samples to maintain quantitative fidelity. These 3D image stacks are then processed using specialized volumetric rendering software to reconstruct and measure the volume of each centrosome in three dimensions, thereby overcoming the limitations of two-dimensional cross-sectional analyses.

Centrosomes are categorized into two distinct classes based on empirically determined volume thresholds derived from normal breast tissue controls: individually distinguishable centrosomes (iCTRs), which fall within the normal volume range (0.2–0.74 µm³), and megacentrosomes (mCTRs), which exceed the upper limit of this range (>0.74 µm³) and represent structurally amplified organelles. This binary classification enables the independent assessment of numerical CA (excess iCTRs per cell) and structural CA (presence of mCTRs).

An algorithmic scoring system is then applied to compute two component scores: CASi, which quantifies numerical centrosome amplification, and CASm, which quantifies structural centrosome amplification. The CASi score incorporates both the frequency (percentage of cells with more than two iCTRs) and severity (average excess number of iCTRs beyond the normal diploid complement of two) of numerical CA, scaled by a tissue-specific factor to ensure comparability. Similarly, the CASm score integrates the frequency (percentage of cells containing mCTRs) and severity (z-score-transformed deviation of mCTR volumes from the normal maximum, weighted by the number of mCTRs per cell) of structural CA, again normalized by a scaling factor calibrated to balance contribution with CASi.

These component scores are summed to yield a composite Centrosome Amplification Score (CAStotal = CASi + CASm), which serves as a continuous, quantitative metric of centrosomal dysregulation in each DCIS sample. Through rigorous statistical validation in two independent, well-characterized retrospective cohorts—comprising a discovery cohort (n=133) and a validation cohort (n=119)—the invention demonstrates that CAStotal significantly stratifies DCIS patients into high- and low-risk groups for LR, with hazard ratios (HR) exceeding 6.0 in multivariate analyses that adjust for age, nuclear grade, comedo necrosis, tumor size, receptor status, and radiotherapy use. Notably, CAStotal outperforms all conventional clinicopathological variables and the VNPI in predictive accuracy, concordance index, and risk reclassification capacity.

Furthermore, the invention reveals that CAStotal is particularly effective in identifying patients who derive significant benefit from adjuvant radiotherapy. In patients treated with surgery alone (mastectomy or BCS), high CAStotal is strongly associated with LR (HR >11, p<0.001), whereas this association is markedly attenuated in patients receiving post-operative radiotherapy, suggesting that radiotherapy mitigates the elevated risk conferred by centrosome amplification. This finding positions CAS as a potential predictive biomarker for radiotherapy response, enabling personalized treatment escalation or de-escalation.

The invention thus provides a complete diagnostic platform—from tissue staining and imaging to computational scoring and clinical interpretation—that transforms centrosomal morphology into a clinically actionable prognostic indicator. By capturing organellar-level manifestations of genomic instability and intratumoral heterogeneity, the CAS offers a more comprehensive and biologically integrated assessment of DCIS aggressiveness than current standards. The methodology is broadly applicable to archival FFPE tissues, compatible with routine pathology workflows, and amenable to automation, thereby facilitating its adoption in clinical laboratories. Ultimately, the invention empowers oncologists to make more informed, individualized treatment decisions, reducing overtreatment in low-risk patients while ensuring appropriate intensification of therapy for those at highest risk of recurrence.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### DISCUSSION

The present invention establishes a paradigm-shifting approach to prognostication in ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) by introducing the Centrosome Amplification Score (CAS) as a novel, quantitative, and biologically anchored biomarker derived from the rigorous phenotyping of centrosomal abnormalities in routine clinical tissue specimens. Unlike existing risk stratification tools that rely on macroscopic or transcriptomic features, the CAS operates at the subcellular organelle level, capturing direct phenotypic readouts of chromosomal instability (CIN) and intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH)—two fundamental hallmarks of cancer progression. This innovation bridges a critical gap between basic centrosome biology and clinical oncology, offering a practical solution to the persistent challenge of overtreatment and undertreatment in DCIS management.

At the heart of the invention is a meticulously validated methodology for three-dimensional (3D) centrosomal phenotyping in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues, a substrate that constitutes the backbone of archival pathology collections worldwide. The process begins with immunofluorescent labeling of γ-tubulin, a highly conserved protein integral to the pericentriolar material that nucleates microtubules and serves as a reliable marker for centrosome localization. Co-staining with Hoechst allows precise nuclear segmentation, enabling accurate assignment of centrosomes to individual cells—a prerequisite for cell-by-cell quantitation. Confocal microscopy, operated under strictly standardized acquisition parameters, generates high-fidelity z-stack images that preserve the spatial integrity of centrosomal structures. This 3D imaging modality is essential, as two-dimensional projections inherently distort volumetric measurements and obscure the true extent of structural amplification.

Image analysis is performed using advanced volumetric rendering software capable of reconstructing centrosomes in three dimensions and computing their absolute volumes in cubic micrometers (µm³). A critical innovation lies in the empirical definition of normal centrosome volume ranges, derived from extensive analysis of both reduction mammoplasty specimens (n=40) and adjacent uninvolved breast tissue from cancer patients (n=40). By establishing a conservative upper threshold of 0.74 µm³—based on the maximum observed volume in disease-free epithelium—the invention ensures that only bona fide structural aberrations are classified as megacentrosomes (mCTRs). This thresholding strategy eliminates false positives arising from staining artifacts or optical noise, enhancing the specificity of structural CA detection.

The categorization of centrosomes into individually distinguishable centrosomes (iCTRs) and mCTRs forms the foundation for dual-axis quantitation of centrosome amplification. Numerical CA is assessed by counting iCTRs per nucleus, with any cell harboring more than two iCTRs deemed numerically amplified. Structural CA is identified by the presence of one or more mCTRs per cell, regardless of total centrosome count. This dichotomy is biologically justified: numerical amplification typically arises from unscheduled centriole duplication or cytokinesis failure, whereas structural amplification reflects coalescence of pericentriolar material or defective centrosome maturation—distinct mechanisms that may confer different degrees of mitotic dysfunction and migratory advantage.

The algorithmic computation of CASi and CASm represents a further refinement over prior qualitative or semi-quantitative approaches. Rather than treating CA as a binary trait (present/absent), the invention quantifies both the prevalence (frequency) and magnitude (severity) of each aberration type. For CASi, frequency is expressed as the percentage of cells with >2 iCTRs, while severity is calculated as the average excess number of iCTRs beyond the diploid baseline (i.e., mean(Ni – 2) for all amplified cells). Similarly, for CASm, frequency is the percentage of cells containing mCTRs, and severity is derived from a z-score transformation that normalizes the volume excess of each mCTR relative to the population standard deviation of mCTR volumes. This dual-component structure ensures that a sample with rare but extreme amplifications is not equated with one exhibiting widespread but mild abnormalities—a distinction with likely biological and clinical relevance.

The scaling factors (βi = 0.1, βm = 0.148) applied to each component are calibrated to equalize their contribution to the composite CAStotal, preventing dominance by either numerical or structural features and ensuring balanced representation of both axes of centrosomal dysregulation. The resulting CAStotal is a unitless, continuous variable that reflects the integrated burden of centrosome amplification in a given DCIS lesion.

Validation across two independent cohorts—discovery (n=133) and validation (n=119)—confirms the robustness and generalizability of the CAS. In both cohorts, high CAStotal is strongly associated with reduced recurrence-free survival (RFS), with hazard ratios consistently exceeding 6.0 in multivariate models that account for established confounders. Notably, CAS retains significance even when restricted to pure high-grade DCIS or to patients treated with BCS alone, demonstrating its utility across clinically relevant subgroups. The optimal cutoff of 1.436, determined by log-rank minimization in the discovery cohort, successfully stratifies risk in the validation cohort without recalibration, underscoring the assay’s reproducibility.

Perhaps most clinically impactful is the invention’s ability to refine risk prediction within traditionally defined high-risk groups. For instance, among DCIS patients with comedo necrosis—a feature historically associated with elevated recurrence risk—the CAS further segregates this population into low-risk (10% recurrence) and high-risk (83% recurrence) subgroups. Similar reclassification is observed across strata defined by tumor size, age, and treatment modality. This granular risk stratification directly addresses the heterogeneity that plagues current prognostic models and enables truly personalized therapeutic decisions.

Moreover, the invention provides compelling evidence that CAS functions not only as a prognostic but also as a predictive biomarker for radiotherapy benefit. The marked attenuation of the CAS–recurrence association in irradiated patients suggests that radiotherapy effectively ablates the clonogenic potential of centrosome-amplified DCIS cells, thereby neutralizing their inherent aggressiveness. This insight could guide selective omission of radiotherapy in low-CAS patients undergoing BCS—potentially sparing thousands of women annually from unnecessary radiation exposure—while reinforcing its necessity in high-CAS cases.

The biological plausibility of CAS as a surrogate for ITH and CIN is reinforced by co-immunolabeling experiments demonstrating that γ-tubulin-positive mCTRs invariably colocalize with centrin-2, a centriolar marker, confirming their identity as genuine centrosomes rather than protein aggregates. The absence of supernumerary centrin-2 foci within mCTRs further supports the interpretation that structural amplification reflects pericentriolar material expansion rather than clustered centrioles, a distinction with implications for mitotic error profiles.

In comparison to the Oncotype DX DCIS Score, the CAS offers several advantages: broader applicability (no restrictions based on tumor size, grade, or margin status), independence from RNA quality (which can be compromised in FFPE tissues), and integration of phenotypic consequences of diverse oncogenic pathways beyond proliferation. As a readout of hundreds of upstream signaling perturbations converging on centrosome homeostasis, CAS captures a more holistic view of tumor biology.

While the current study focuses on DCIS, the methodology is extensible to other epithelial malignancies where centrosome amplification is prevalent, such as prostate, lung, and ovarian cancers. Future iterations may incorporate machine learning for fully automated centrosome detection or multiplexed imaging to correlate CA with immune microenvironment features.

In summary, the invention transforms centrosome amplification from a cytological curiosity into a clinically actionable biomarker. By providing an objective, quantitative, and biologically meaningful metric of DCIS aggressiveness, the Centrosome Amplification Score empowers precision oncology, optimizes resource utilization, and ultimately improves patient outcomes through individualized risk-adapted therapy.
