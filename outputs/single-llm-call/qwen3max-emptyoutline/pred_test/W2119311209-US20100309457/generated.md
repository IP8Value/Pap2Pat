# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of optical imaging has long been dominated by systems that rely exclusively on the detection of light intensity variations to form images. Conventional image sensors, whether based on charge-coupled device (CCD) or complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) technologies, are fundamentally designed to respond only to the amplitude or intensity component of an incident light wave. This limitation mirrors the functional constraints of the human retina, which similarly lacks the capacity to directly perceive phase information carried by light. However, a substantial body of scientific evidence demonstrates that the phase front of a light wave—representing spatial variations in the optical path length through a sample—contains critical information that is often absent from intensity-based measurements alone. In particular, many biological specimens, including live cells, embryos, and certain pathogens, are effectively transparent under standard bright-field illumination because they exhibit minimal absorption contrast. These samples, while nearly invisible in conventional intensity imaging, induce measurable phase shifts in transmitted light due to refractive index variations within their internal structures. Consequently, the ability to detect and quantify these phase shifts is essential for non-invasive, label-free visualization of such specimens—a capability that has driven the development of specialized phase microscopy techniques over the past century.

Historically, differential interference contrast (DIC) microscopy, phase contrast microscopy, and Hoffman modulation contrast microscopy have served as the primary modalities for rendering phase information into visible contrast. Among these, DIC microscopy has been particularly favored in biomedical research and clinical settings due to its high resolution, optical sectioning capability, and pseudo-three-dimensional appearance of rendered images. Despite these advantages, DIC microscopy suffers from several fundamental limitations that restrict its broader applicability. First, DIC images inherently mix amplitude and phase information, resulting in ambiguous interpretations where dark features may arise either from light absorption or from negative phase gradients. This ambiguity is especially problematic when imaging stained biological samples, where absorption and phase effects coexist and cannot be disentangled without additional processing or control experiments. Second, DIC microscopy relies on polarized light and birefringent optical components, such as Wollaston or Nomarski prisms, which introduce significant artifacts when imaging birefringent specimens—such as muscle tissues, collagen matrices, or starch granules—due to interference between the sample's intrinsic birefringence and the polarization state manipulated by the microscope optics. Third, the requirement for specialized and precisely aligned optical components increases the cost, complexity, and maintenance burden of DIC systems, limiting their accessibility in resource-constrained environments such as field diagnostics or point-of-care settings.

In response to these challenges, numerous advanced phase imaging techniques have emerged in recent decades, including digital holographic microscopy, transport-of-intensity equation (TIE) methods, and various forms of interferometric phase microscopy. While these approaches offer quantitative phase retrieval and improved separation of amplitude and phase information, they typically demand coherent light sources (e.g., lasers), precise mechanical stability, and complex optical configurations involving multiple beam paths or interferometric setups. These requirements render them unsuitable for routine integration into standard laboratory microscopes or for deployment in clinical or field applications where simplicity, robustness, and cost-effectiveness are paramount. Alternative strategies, such as the TIE method, which estimates phase from intensity measurements at multiple focal planes, circumvent the need for interferometry but introduce other limitations: they require axial scanning of the sample or detector, which reduces temporal resolution and precludes real-time imaging of dynamic processes, and they are sensitive to noise and defocus aberrations that can compromise phase accuracy.

Conventional wavefront sensing technologies, such as the Shack-Hartmann sensor, provide another route to phase measurement by sampling the local tilt of a wavefront using a lenslet array. However, these devices are generally bulky, expensive, and limited in spatial resolution due to the physical size of the lenslets (typically tens to hundreds of micrometers), which constrains the number of sampling points across the wavefront. Moreover, the assembly of discrete lenslet arrays onto image sensors introduces alignment challenges and limits scalability for mass production. The predecessor to the Shack-Hartmann sensor, the Hartmann sieve—which uses an array of apertures instead of lenses—suffers from even greater diffraction-induced spot broadening, necessitating large inter-aperture spacing and further reducing spatial resolution. These limitations have historically confined wavefront sensing to niche applications in astronomy, ophthalmology, and optical metrology, rather than enabling widespread adoption in biomedical imaging.

A critical unmet need therefore exists for a compact, low-cost, and easily integrable imaging sensor that can directly and simultaneously measure both the intensity and phase gradient of an incident light field without requiring specialized illumination, complex optics, or mechanical scanning. Such a sensor would ideally be compatible with standard microscope architectures, function under broadband incoherent illumination (e.g., halogen or LED sources), and provide quantitative phase data that is free from the ambiguities and artifacts inherent in traditional phase contrast techniques. Furthermore, to achieve broad utility, the sensor should be manufacturable using standard semiconductor fabrication processes, enabling high-volume, low-cost production analogous to conventional image sensors. Prior art has not provided a solution that satisfies all these criteria simultaneously. While some research efforts have explored modified sensor designs or computational approaches to infer phase, none have achieved a fully integrated, high-density, foundry-compatible image sensor capable of direct, real-time, quantitative phase gradient measurement alongside conventional intensity imaging. The present invention addresses this gap by introducing a novel wavefront image sensor chip (WIS) that leverages principles of near-field diffraction and high Fresnel number optics to enable unprecedented performance in a compact, scalable, and practical form factor.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a wavefront image sensor chip (WIS) capable of simultaneously measuring both the intensity and the phase front variations of an incident light field, thereby enabling quantitative phase imaging without the need for complex optical components, coherent illumination, or mechanical scanning. The WIS is constructed by integrating a two-dimensional array of microscopic apertures directly onto the surface of a conventional image sensor chip—such as a CCD or CMOS sensor—with a transparent spacer layer separating the apertures from the underlying photosensitive pixels. This configuration operates in a high Fresnel number regime, wherein the proximity of the aperture plane to the sensor plane results in a self-focusing effect that confines the light transmitted through each aperture into a tightly localized projection spot on the sensor. Critically, the lateral position of each projection spot shifts in direct proportion to the local phase gradient of the incident wavefront over the corresponding aperture, while the total integrated signal within the spot provides a measure of the local light intensity. By analyzing both the position and intensity of each projection spot across the entire aperture array, the WIS independently reconstructs quantitative maps of the normalized phase gradient and intensity of the incident light field from a single image acquisition.

The key innovation of the WIS lies in its exploitation of the high Fresnel number optical regime, which enables sub-diffraction-limited spot confinement without the use of refractive or diffractive focusing elements. Unlike conventional Hartmann or Shack-Hartmann sensors that operate in the far-field (low Fresnel number) regime and suffer from broadened spots due to diffraction, the WIS places the sensor pixels sufficiently close to the apertures such that the transmitted light undergoes a natural self-focusing before diverging. This allows for high-density aperture patterning—with spacings on the order of micrometers—while maintaining well-separated, high-contrast projection spots. The result is a compact, monolithic sensor with high spatial resolution (e.g., 280 × 350 image pixels over a 3.08 mm × 3.85 mm area), excellent phase sensitivity (better than 0.1 milliradian in normalized phase gradient), and linear response over a practical dynamic range (±15 milliradians). The WIS is fabricated using standard semiconductor processing techniques, including photolithography and thin-film deposition, making it compatible with commercial CMOS foundry workflows and amenable to mass production at low incremental cost.

When deployed as a drop-in replacement for a conventional camera in a standard bright-field microscope, the WIS transforms the system into a wavefront microscope (WM) capable of simultaneous bright-field and quantitative phase gradient imaging. This configuration requires no modification to the microscope’s optical path, functions under standard broadband illumination (e.g., halogen lamp), and eliminates the need for polarizers, prisms, or other specialized phase optics. The resulting phase gradient images are inherently quantitative, free from the amplitude-phase mixing artifacts of DIC microscopy, and immune to birefringence-induced distortions, as the WIS does not rely on polarized light. Moreover, because the WIS measures orthogonal phase gradients in both the x and y directions, it captures a complete two-dimensional representation of the wavefront slope, which can be further processed to reconstruct absolute phase or derived metrics such as phase gradient magnitude or direction. The invention thus provides a simple, robust, and cost-effective solution for label-free, quantitative phase imaging across a wide range of biomedical applications—including live-cell imaging, embryo assessment, pathogen detection, and intraoperative tissue analysis—where minimal sample preparation, rapid acquisition, and unambiguous interpretation are critical.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The wavefront image sensor chip (WIS) of the present invention is a monolithic integrated device that combines a microfabricated aperture array with a conventional image sensor substrate to enable direct, simultaneous measurement of light intensity and phase gradient. The core architecture consists of a planar image sensor chip—preferably a CMOS or CCD sensor with a pixel pitch on the order of 1–10 micrometers—onto which a patterned opaque layer containing a regular two-dimensional array of circular apertures is deposited. A transparent dielectric spacer layer, with a precisely controlled thickness and refractive index, separates the aperture layer from the photosensitive surface of the image sensor. This spacer serves the dual purpose of defining the optical distance between the apertures and the sensor pixels and providing a planarization surface for aperture patterning. In a preferred embodiment, the opaque layer is composed of a thin metal film, such as aluminum or chromium, with a thickness sufficient to block incident light (e.g., 100–200 nanometers), while the spacer is formed from a photoresist material such as SU-8 or a deposited oxide with a refractive index between 1.4 and 1.7. The apertures are typically circular with diameters ranging from 2 to 10 micrometers, arranged in a square or hexagonal lattice with center-to-center spacing of 5 to 20 micrometers, depending on the desired trade-off between spatial resolution and light throughput.

The operational principle of the WIS is grounded in the physics of near-field diffraction and the high Fresnel number regime. The Fresnel number \( F \) for a given aperture is defined as \( F = a^2 / (\lambda H) \), where \( a \) is the aperture radius, \( \lambda \) is the wavelength of light, and \( H \) is the distance from the aperture to the observation plane (i.e., the sensor surface). When \( F \gg 1 \), the system operates in the high Fresnel number regime, characterized by minimal diffraction spreading and the emergence of a self-focusing effect wherein the transmitted light converges to a narrow waist near the aperture before diverging. In the WIS, the spacer thickness is engineered such that \( H \) is small enough to ensure \( F > 0.5 \) across the operational bandwidth of the illumination source (e.g., 400–700 nm for visible light), thereby confining each projection spot to a diameter significantly smaller than the aperture itself—experimental results demonstrate spot full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) as small as 3.8 micrometers for a 6-micrometer aperture at \( H = 18 \) micrometers. This tight confinement is crucial for achieving high aperture density without cross-talk between adjacent projection spots, enabling the WIS to generate image frames with hundreds of thousands of effective image pixels.

Each aperture in the array acts as a local wavefront sampler. When a plane wave impinges normally on the aperture array, the projection spot for each aperture is centered directly beneath the aperture on the sensor. However, when the incident wavefront exhibits a local phase gradient \( \partial \phi / \partial x \) over an aperture located at position \( (x, y) \), the centroid of the corresponding projection spot shifts laterally in the x-direction by an amount \( \Delta x \) given by:
\[
\Delta x = \frac{\lambda H}{2\pi n} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x},
\]
where \( n \) is the refractive index of the spacer material. To render this relationship wavelength-independent and more physically intuitive, the invention defines a normalized phase gradient \( \theta_x = (\lambda / 2\pi n) (\partial \phi / \partial x) \), which represents the local angle of incidence of the light ray relative to the optical axis. Thus, \( \Delta x = H \theta_x \), and an analogous expression holds for the y-direction. This linear relationship between spot displacement and normalized phase gradient forms the basis for quantitative phase measurement. Simultaneously, the total photocharge accumulated within the sensor pixels underlying each projection spot is proportional to the local light intensity incident on the aperture, enabling concurrent intensity imaging.

To extract both intensity and phase information from a single sensor readout, the WIS assigns a dedicated sub-array of sensor pixels—typically 3×3 to 7×7 pixels—to each aperture. In the exemplary embodiment, a 5×5 grid of 2.2-micrometer sensor pixels is allocated per aperture, yielding an effective image pixel size of 11 micrometers (matching the aperture pitch). The intensity value for each image pixel is computed by summing the signals from all pixels in the sub-array, while the phase gradient is determined by estimating the centroid of the projection spot with sub-pixel precision. A critical aspect of the invention is the use of a robust centroid estimation algorithm, herein termed the cyclic algorithm, which overcomes the noise sensitivity of traditional centroid methods. The cyclic algorithm computes a complex-valued moment for each sub-array using uni-norm complex weights that cycle with pixel position, effectively demodulating the spot position in the Fourier domain. For a 1D spot profile \( I(s) \) sampled over pixels indexed by \( m = -M, \dots, M \), the algorithm calculates:
\[
\tilde{s} = \sum_{m=-M}^{M} I(m) e^{i 2\pi m / T},
\]
where \( T = 2M + 1 \) is the sub-array width. The phase of \( \tilde{s} \) yields the spot displacement with high precision, even in low signal-to-noise conditions. This approach is extended to 2D by applying separable transforms in x and y, and has been experimentally validated to achieve centroid localization precision of better than 0.001 sensor pixel widths (e.g., 1.8 nanometers for 2.2-micrometer pixels), corresponding to a normalized phase gradient sensitivity of 0.1 milliradians under typical illumination conditions (9.2 µW/cm², 1-second integration).

The WIS is fabricated using a post-processing sequence applied to a commercial image sensor die. First, the protective glass window of the sensor is removed to expose the active surface. Next, the surface is planarized with a thick layer of SU-8 photoresist (e.g., 10 micrometers), which also serves as the spacer. An optional step involves preserving or removing the native microlens array atop the sensor pixels; in initial prototypes, the microlenses were buried under SU-8 to simplify optical modeling, but future iterations may retain them to enhance light collection efficiency. A thin metal layer (e.g., 150 nm Al) is then deposited and patterned via photolithography to define the aperture array. The alignment of the aperture grid to the underlying sensor pixel grid is critical and is achieved using high-precision stepper lithography with alignment marks on the sensor die. The resulting device is packaged in a standard camera module and connected to conventional image acquisition electronics.

Calibration of the WIS involves two key procedures: (1) determining the effective spacer thickness \( H \) by measuring the spot displacement response to known phase gradients, and (2) correcting for systematic biases introduced by intensity gradients. The first is accomplished by tilting the incident plane wave and measuring the resulting spot shift across the array; the slope of the shift versus tilt angle yields \( H \), which was found to be 28 ± 1 micrometers in the prototype. The second correction addresses a subtle but important artifact: intensity gradients across an aperture can also induce apparent spot shifts due to asymmetric illumination. The invention provides a compensation method wherein the intensity image is first used to compute the local intensity gradient \( \partial I / \partial x \), which is then converted to an apparent phase shift using a derived relationship \( \Delta x_{\text{int}} \propto (a^2 / H) (\partial I / \partial x) / I \), where \( a \) is the aperture radius. This artifact is subtracted from the raw phase gradient map to yield a corrected quantitative phase image.

In operation, the WIS is mounted in the camera port of a standard bright-field microscope equipped with a broadband incoherent source (e.g., halogen lamp). No modifications to the microscope’s condenser, objectives, or illumination path are required. The system simultaneously outputs three co-registered image channels: an intensity image identical to a conventional bright-field image, a normalized phase gradient image in the x-direction, and a normalized phase gradient image in the y-direction. These phase gradient images are quantitative, artifact-free, and orthogonal, providing a complete description of the wavefront slope. Applications demonstrated include imaging of polystyrene microspheres, unstained and stained starfish embryos, and birefringent potato starch granules, where the WIS outperformed DIC microscopy by eliminating staining artifacts and birefringence distortions. The technology is extensible to color imaging by integrating with Foveon-type or Bayer-pattern sensors, and to adaptive optics by providing real-time wavefront feedback. By virtue of its foundry-compatible fabrication, the WIS offers a scalable, low-cost pathway to democratize quantitative phase imaging across biomedical research, clinical diagnostics, and industrial inspection.
