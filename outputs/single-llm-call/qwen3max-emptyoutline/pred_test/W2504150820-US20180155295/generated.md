## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of medicinal chemistry and pharmacology, and more specifically to novel small-molecule modulators of the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4). The invention provides new chemical entities that function as either agonists or antagonists of the CXCR4 receptor, which is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) critically involved in numerous physiological and pathological processes, including hematopoietic stem cell mobilization, immune cell trafficking, cancer metastasis, and inflammatory responses. The disclosed compounds are identified through a dual in silico screening strategy combining ligand-based pharmacophore modeling and structure-based virtual docking, followed by experimental validation in multiple functional assays. These compounds represent the first reported small-molecule agonists of CXCR4 and offer significant utility as pharmacological tools for probing CXCR4-mediated signaling pathways, as well as potential therapeutic agents for diseases where modulation of CXCR4 activity is beneficial.

## BACKGROUND

The C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) is a seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that plays a pivotal role in both normal physiology and disease pathogenesis. Its endogenous ligand is the chemokine stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), also known as CXCL12. The CXCR4/CXCL12 axis is essential for embryonic development, particularly in cardiac and neuronal morphogenesis, and continues to regulate critical functions in adult organisms, including hematopoietic stem cell homing to the bone marrow, lymphocyte trafficking, and tissue repair. Dysregulation of this signaling pathway is implicated in a wide array of pathological conditions, most notably in cancer metastasis—where CXCR4 overexpression enables tumor cells to migrate toward CXCL12-rich organs such as the lungs, liver, and bone marrow—and in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, where CXCR4 serves as a co-receptor for viral entry into T cells.

Given its central role in disease, CXCR4 has emerged as a high-value therapeutic target. To date, the most clinically advanced CXCR4 antagonist is AMD3100 (plerixafor), a bicyclam compound approved for use in hematopoietic stem cell mobilization prior to autologous transplantation in patients with non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma and multiple myeloma. While AMD3100 demonstrates the therapeutic feasibility of CXCR4 blockade, it suffers from limitations including poor oral bioavailability, short half-life, and potential cardiotoxicity at high doses. Moreover, the pharmacological landscape of CXCR4 modulators has been overwhelmingly dominated by antagonists, with virtually no small-molecule agonists reported in the scientific literature prior to the present invention. This gap is significant because agonists can serve as powerful tools to dissect the complex signaling networks downstream of CXCR4, including G protein-dependent and β-arrestin-mediated pathways, and may offer unique therapeutic opportunities in contexts such as tissue regeneration, wound healing, or immune modulation.

The structural biology of CXCR4 has advanced considerably in recent years, with the publication of high-resolution X-ray crystal structures of the receptor in complex with both peptide and small-molecule ligands. Notably, the crystal structures with PDB accession codes 3ODU and 3OE0 reveal that the large endogenous ligand—a 16-residue cyclic peptide derived from viral macrophage inflammatory protein-II (vMIP-II)—occupies an extensive binding pocket, whereas small molecules like IT1t bind only a subregion of this site. This structural insight underscores the challenge of designing small-molecule ligands that effectively mimic or modulate the activity of the much larger native chemokine. Furthermore, the conformational flexibility of GPCRs and the subtle differences in ligand binding modes that can dictate functional outcomes (e.g., agonist vs. antagonist behavior) complicate rational drug design efforts.

Traditional approaches to CXCR4 ligand discovery have relied heavily on high-throughput screening (HTS) of large compound libraries, often yielding hits with limited structural diversity or suboptimal drug-like properties. More recently, computational methods have gained traction as cost-effective and efficient alternatives. Ligand-based strategies, such as pharmacophore modeling, leverage known active compounds to define a spatial arrangement of chemical features essential for activity, enabling the virtual screening of databases for structurally diverse analogs. Structure-based approaches, including molecular docking, utilize the three-dimensional structure of the target protein to predict how candidate ligands might bind and interact with key residues. However, each method has inherent limitations: pharmacophore models depend on the quality and diversity of the training set, while docking accuracy is sensitive to protein conformation, scoring function reliability, and treatment of ligand flexibility.

To address these challenges and maximize the probability of identifying novel, high-quality CXCR4 modulators, the inventors developed an integrated in silico screening platform that synergistically combines both ligand-based and structure-based methodologies. This dual approach was applied to a curated library of approximately 13,000 GPCR-focused compounds, selected for their structural relevance and drug-like characteristics. The resulting hit candidates were subjected to a rigorous cascade of in vitro functional assays—including calcium mobilization, ERK phosphorylation, receptor internalization, chemotaxis, and radioligand binding—to comprehensively characterize their pharmacological profiles. Remarkably, this strategy led to the discovery of not only new antagonists but also the first reported small-molecule agonists of CXCR4, exemplified by compounds such as NUCC-390 and NUCC-398. These findings represent a paradigm shift in CXCR4 pharmacology and open new avenues for both basic research and therapeutic development.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides novel small-molecule modulators of the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), including both agonists and antagonists, identified through a dual in silico screening strategy that integrates ligand-based pharmacophore modeling and structure-based virtual docking. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising these compounds, methods of using them to modulate CXCR4 activity in vitro and in vivo, and their application in the treatment of diseases associated with CXCR4 dysfunction.

In one aspect, the invention relates to a compound selected from the group consisting of NUCC-388, NUCC-390, NUCC-392, NUCC-397, NUCC-398, NUCC-51420, NUCC-54118, NUCC-54120, NUCC-54121, and NUCC-54127, or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt, solvate, hydrate, stereoisomer, or prodrug thereof, wherein the compound is a modulator of CXCR4 activity. In certain embodiments, the compound is an agonist of CXCR4, such as NUCC-390, NUCC-398, NUCC-54118, NUCC-54121, or NUCC-54127. In other embodiments, the compound is an antagonist of CXCR4, such as NUCC-388, NUCC-392, NUCC-397, or NUCC-51420.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method of modulating CXCR4 activity in a cell, comprising contacting the cell with an effective amount of a compound of the invention. The modulation may be activation (agonism) or inhibition (antagonism), depending on the specific compound used. The method may be practiced in vitro, for example, in cellular assays to study CXCR4 signaling, or in vivo, for therapeutic purposes.

In yet another aspect, the invention includes a pharmaceutical composition comprising a therapeutically effective amount of a compound of the invention and a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, excipient, or diluent. The composition may be formulated for administration via various routes, including oral, intravenous, subcutaneous, or intraperitoneal, and may be used to treat conditions such as cancer metastasis, HIV infection, inflammatory diseases, or to promote stem cell mobilization or tissue regeneration.

A further aspect of the invention is a method of treating a disease or condition mediated by CXCR4 in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of a compound of the invention. Exemplary diseases include, but are not limited to, hematologic malignancies, solid tumors with CXCR4 overexpression, autoimmune disorders, ischemic injury, and wound healing deficiencies.

The compounds of the invention were identified through a comprehensive computational workflow. First, a common-feature pharmacophore model was generated using a training set of known CXCR4 antagonists, resulting in a five-point hypothesis comprising two hydrophobic features, two hydrogen bond acceptor features, and one positive ionizable feature. This model was used to screen a GPCR-focused compound library, yielding ligand-based hits. Concurrently, structure-based virtual screening was performed by docking the same library into the orthosteric binding site of CXCR4, as defined by the crystal structure (PDB 3ODU), using two orthogonal docking engines—Glide and Surflex—to enhance prediction reliability. Compounds that scored favorably in both docking protocols and exhibited desirable drug-like properties were selected as structure-based hits. The intersection of ligand-based and structure-based hits, along with additional criteria such as synthetic tractability and commercial availability, led to the selection of fifteen compounds for experimental validation.

Functional characterization revealed that several compounds act as potent CXCR4 antagonists, inhibiting SDF-1-induced calcium mobilization with IC50 values in the submicromolar to micromolar range. Strikingly, other compounds demonstrated clear agonist activity, inducing calcium flux, ERK phosphorylation, receptor internalization, and chemotaxis in a CXCR4-dependent manner, as confirmed by blockade with the selective antagonist AMD3100. Notably, the agonist NUCC-390 did not compete with radiolabeled SDF-1 for receptor binding, suggesting a distinct binding mode or allosteric mechanism. Molecular modeling further indicated that agonists and antagonists within the same chemical series engage different key residues in the CXCR4 binding pocket—agonists forming a hydrogen bond with Tyr255, while antagonists interact with Glu288—providing a structural rationale for their divergent functional outcomes.

Thus, the invention not only delivers new chemical matter with validated CXCR4-modulating activity but also establishes a robust discovery platform for GPCR ligands and offers unprecedented insights into the molecular determinants of CXCR4 agonism versus antagonism. The disclosed compounds represent valuable probes for elucidating CXCR4 biology and hold significant promise as leads for therapeutic development.

## DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “modulator” refers to any compound that alters the activity of a biological target, such as a receptor, enzyme, or ion channel. In the context of the present invention, a modulator of CXCR4 is a compound that either increases (agonist) or decreases (antagonist) the functional activity of the CXCR4 receptor in response to its natural ligand, SDF-1, or in a ligand-independent manner. Modulators may act orthosterically, by binding to the same site as the endogenous ligand, or allosterically, by binding to a distinct site and influencing receptor conformation.

The term “agonist” as used herein denotes a compound that binds to CXCR4 and induces a conformational change in the receptor that mimics the effect of the endogenous ligand SDF-1, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways. Agonists may be full agonists, which produce a maximal response comparable to SDF-1, or partial agonists, which elicit a submaximal response even at saturating concentrations. The agonist activity of a compound is typically assessed by its ability to induce intracellular calcium mobilization, ERK phosphorylation, receptor internalization, or chemotaxis in CXCR4-expressing cells.

The term “antagonist” refers to a compound that binds to CXCR4 and prevents or reduces the activation of the receptor by SDF-1 or other agonists, without inducing significant receptor activation itself. Antagonists may be competitive, binding reversibly to the orthosteric site and blocking agonist binding, or non-competitive, binding to an allosteric site and inhibiting receptor function through conformational effects. Inverse agonists, a subclass of antagonists, suppress basal (constitutive) activity of the receptor in the absence of agonist.

“CXCR4” or “C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4” is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) that belongs to the chemokine receptor family. It is encoded by the CXCR4 gene in humans and is activated by its sole known endogenous ligand, stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1 or CXCL12). CXCR4 is expressed on a wide variety of cell types, including hematopoietic stem cells, lymphocytes, endothelial cells, and many cancer cells.

“Pharmaceutically acceptable” refers to those compounds, materials, compositions, and/or dosage forms that are, within the scope of sound medical judgment, suitable for use in contact with the tissues of human beings and animals without excessive toxicity, irritation, allergic response, or other problem or complication, commensurate with a reasonable benefit/risk ratio.

A “pharmaceutically acceptable salt” includes acid addition salts and base addition salts. Acid addition salts are formed with inorganic acids such as hydrochloric acid, hydrobromic acid, sulfuric acid, nitric acid, phosphoric acid, and the like, or with organic acids such as acetic acid, propionic acid, hexanoic acid, cyclopentanepropionic acid, glycolic acid, pyruvic acid, lactic acid, malonic acid, succinic acid, malic acid, maleic acid, fumaric acid, tartaric acid, citric acid, benzoic acid, 3-(4-hydroxybenzoyl)benzoic acid, cinnamic acid, mandelic acid, methanesulfonic acid, ethanesulfonic acid, 1,2-ethanedisulfonic acid, 2-hydroxyethanesulfonic acid, benzenesulfonic acid, 4-chlorobenzenesulfonic acid, 2-naphthalenesulfonic acid, 4-toluenesulfonic acid, camphorsulfonic acid, 4-methylbicyclo[2.2.2]oct-2-ene-1-carboxylic acid, glucoheptonic acid, 4,4′-methylenebis(3-hydroxy-2-ene-1-carboxylic acid), 3-phenylpropionic acid, trimethylacetic acid, tertiary butylacetic acid, lauryl sulfuric acid, gluconic acid, glutamic acid, hydroxynaphthoic acid, salicylic acid, stearic acid, muconic acid, and the like. The term “therapeutically effective amount” means an amount of a compound of the invention that, when administered to a subject in need thereof, is sufficient to effect treatment of a disease or condition mediated by CXCR4. The therapeutically effective amount will vary depending on the compound, the disease and its severity, and the age, weight, and physiological condition of the subject to be treated.

“Treatment” or “treating” as used herein includes prophylactic treatment (preventing or delaying the onset of a disease or condition) as well as therapeutic treatment (alleviating, ameliorating, or eliminating symptoms or progression of a disease or condition).

The term “subject” refers to a mammal, including, but not limited to, humans, non-human primates, rodents, and domestic animals, that is to be treated with a compound of the invention.

“In silico” refers to experiments or processes performed via computer simulation or computational analysis, as opposed to in vitro (in glass/test tube) or in vivo (in living organism).

These features may include hydrogen bond donors or acceptors, hydrophobic regions, aromatic rings, positive or negative ionizable groups, and their relative spatial arrangement.

“Docking” in the context of structure-based drug design refers to the computational prediction of the preferred orientation and binding affinity of a small molecule (ligand) when bound to a target macromolecule (receptor), such as a protein.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### EXPERIMENTAL

The experimental methodology employed in the development of the present invention encompasses a multi-stage process beginning with the creation of a computationally annotated compound database, followed by the generation of predictive pharmacophore and docking models, virtual screening, and culminating in comprehensive in vitro biological validation. Each stage was designed to maximize the likelihood of identifying novel, high-quality modulators of CXCR4 with diverse chemical scaffolds and well-defined functional profiles. The integration of orthogonal computational techniques ensured robustness in hit prediction, while the tiered biological assay cascade provided unambiguous characterization of agonist versus antagonist activity.

The foundational step in the discovery pipeline was the preparation of a specialized compound library tailored for GPCR-targeted screening. The ChemBridge GPCR-focused library, comprising approximately 13,000 unique chemical entities, was selected due to its enrichment in structural motifs commonly associated with GPCR ligand activity, favorable drug-likeness parameters, and synthetic accessibility. To enable accurate conformational analysis and subsequent docking, each compound in the library was processed to generate a representative ensemble of low-energy three-dimensional conformers. This was accomplished using the ConFirm/CatConf module within Discovery Studio 3.1 (Accelrys, Inc., San Diego, CA), operating in “best” mode, which employs a systematic search algorithm to explore torsional space efficiently. The conformational sampling was further refined using a modified version of the CHARMm force field, augmented with a poling technique that biases the conformational search toward geometries that are energetically proximate but structurally distinct from local minima. This approach ensured a broad and realistic representation of each molecule’s accessible conformational space, critical for both pharmacophore mapping and docking accuracy. The energy cutoff for conformer generation was set at 10 kcal/mol above the global minimum, resulting in an average of approximately 100 conformers per compound, which collectively captured the dynamic flexibility inherent in drug-like molecules.

Concurrently, a ligand-based pharmacophore model was constructed to capture the essential chemical features shared among known CXCR4 antagonists. A dataset of 162 CXCR4 antagonists, curated from ChEMBL (version 13) based on reported IC50 values ranging from 1 nM to 10 μM, served as the initial pool. Due to the heterogeneity in assay conditions and reporting standards across different sources, direct activity-based modeling was deemed inappropriate. Instead, cluster analysis was applied to group the compounds into five structurally distinct clusters, ensuring diversity in the training set. Two representative molecules from each cluster were selected, yielding a training set of ten compounds. The common feature pharmacophore modeling tool in Discovery Studio 3.1 was then used to generate a set of ten hypotheses, each representing a unique spatial arrangement of pharmacophoric features. Default parameters were employed, allowing the algorithm to identify the most statistically significant feature combinations. The resulting hypotheses were validated against a separate test set of ten compounds (two from each cluster, distinct from the training set). One hypothesis, characterized by a five-point feature array—comprising two hydrophobic (Hy), two hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA), and one positive ionizable (PI) feature—demonstrated superior fit across all test set compounds, achieving a 100% fit value when all features were matched. This model was subsequently used to screen the annotated GPCR library, identifying 26 compounds with fit values exceeding 85% and conformational energies below 5 kcal/mol, indicating both strong pharmacophoric alignment and structural stability.

In parallel, a structure-based virtual screening campaign was executed to leverage the growing body of structural knowledge on CXCR4. The crystal structure of CXCR4 in complex with the small-molecule antagonist IT1t (PDB code 3ODU) was selected as the receptor template due to its high resolution and relevance to small-molecule binding. Prior to docking, the protein structure underwent rigorous preparation to ensure physicochemical realism. Using Prime version 3.8 (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY), the structure was validated and refined: missing atoms and side chains were reconstructed, ambiguous orientations of Asn, Gln, and His residues were corrected, b-factors were replaced with OPLS_2005 partial charges, and protonation states of titratable residues were assigned at physiological pH (7.4). The ‘Prot-Prep’ module in Glide was then utilized to finalize the receptor structure, defining the binding site based on the centroid of the co-crystallized IT1t ligand. A cubic grid box of 12 Å³ was generated to encompass the orthosteric binding pocket, providing sufficient volume for ligand placement and induced fit.

Two independent docking engines, Glide (Schrödinger, LLC) and Surflex (Certara, Inc.), were employed to mitigate the inherent biases and limitations of any single algorithm. Glide, a grid-based docking program, utilizes a hierarchical filtering approach combining empirical and force field-based scoring to rank poses. For the present study, the Glide XP (extra precision) mode was used with standard sampling parameters (maxkeep = 5000, maxref = 400), and van der Waals radii for nonpolar ligand atoms were scaled to 0.8 to account for minor steric clashes. Surflex, in contrast, employs a fragment-based growth algorithm guided by a protomol—a three-dimensional template derived from the known ligand IT1t—that defines favorable interaction sites within the binding pocket. The protein was prepared in the Sybyl-X interface by adding hydrogens in hydrogen-bonding orientations, assigning Gasteiger-Marsili charges, and fixing protonation states at pH 7.4. Docking was performed in GeomX mode, which optimizes both ligand conformation and orientation simultaneously.

Both docking protocols were first validated by redocking a set of 20 known CXCR4 antagonists (the combined training and test sets). The resulting poses were analyzed to identify conserved interactions with key receptor residues, establishing a benchmark for evaluating novel compounds. The entire GPCR library was then docked using both engines, and compounds were prioritized based on stringent criteria: Glide scores less than −6.0 kcal/mol and Surflex total scores greater than 6.0 (a composite score reflecting binding affinity and pose quality). Additionally, visual inspection of docked poses was performed to ensure plausible interactions with critical residues identified from the validation set, such as Asp97, Glu288, and Tyr116, which are known to be involved in ligand recognition.

The convergence of ligand-based and structure-based screening yielded a high-confidence hit list. Twenty-two compounds were identified that appeared in both the pharmacophore screen and the consensus docking results from Glide and Surflex. These candidates underwent further triage based on medicinal chemistry considerations, including the absence of toxicophores, reactive functional groups, or non-drug-like structural elements (e.g., excessive molecular weight, logP, or rotatable bonds), as well as assessment of synthetic feasibility and commercial availability. From this refined list, nine structure-based hits and six ligand-based hits were selected for purchase and experimental testing, totaling fifteen compounds.

Biological evaluation commenced with a calcium imaging assay in C8161 human melanoma cells, which endogenously express high levels of CXCR4 and exhibit robust, reproducible calcium flux upon SDF-1 stimulation. Cells were loaded with the ratiometric calcium indicator fura-2 AM and subjected to a dual-stimulation protocol: an initial SDF-1 pulse established baseline responsiveness, followed by a second pulse in the presence or absence of test compound. Compounds that suppressed the second SDF-1 response without eliciting a calcium signal themselves were classified as antagonists, while those that induced a calcium transient comparable to SDF-1 were flagged as potential agonists. All fifteen compounds were screened at 10 μM, and four antagonists (NUCC-388, NUCC-392, NUCC-397, NUCC-51420) and six agonists (NUCC-390, NUCC-398, NUCC-54118, NUCC-54121, NUCC-54127, and one additional) demonstrated significant activity. Dose-response curves were generated for the primary hits, yielding IC50 values for antagonists ranging from 0.3 μM to 3 μM and EC50 values for agonists in a similar range.

To confirm the specificity of the observed effects and rule out off-target activity, secondary assays were conducted. Agonist activity was verified in HEK293 cells, which exhibit negligible endogenous CXCR4 expression; neither SDF-1 nor the novel agonists produced any calcium response in this cell line, confirming CXCR4 dependence. Further, the agonist effects of NUCC-390 were completely abolished by pre-treatment with AMD3100 or the novel antagonist NUCC-388, demonstrating competitive or allosteric blockade at the CXCR4 receptor. Downstream signaling was assessed via Western blot analysis of ERK phosphorylation, a canonical CXCR4-mediated pathway. Both SDF-1 and NUCC-390 induced a marked increase in phospho-ERK levels after 30 minutes of treatment, reinforcing the functional agonism of the latter.

Receptor internalization, a hallmark of GPCR activation, was examined using HEK293 cells transfected with a YFP-tagged CXCR4 construct. Confocal microscopy revealed that treatment with NUCC-390 induced robust internalization of CXCR4-YFP, producing cytosolic aggregates indistinguishable from those caused by SDF-1. This effect was fully reversible upon co-administration of AMD3100, confirming receptor specificity. Interestingly, antagonist treatment resulted in exclusive membrane localization of the receptor, suggesting possible inverse agonist activity by stabilizing an inactive conformation.

The functional relevance of the novel agonists was further established in a Boyden chamber chemotaxis assay, where C8161 cells migrated toward a gradient of either SDF-1 or NUCC-390 with comparable efficiency, demonstrating that the small molecule recapitulates a key biological function of the native chemokine. Finally, a radioligand binding assay using 125I-SDF-1α in Chem-1 cells showed that NUCC-390 did not displace the labeled chemokine at concentrations up to 10 μM, implying a non-competitive or allosteric binding mechanism. Collectively, these experiments provide a comprehensive pharmacological profile of the novel compounds, firmly establishing their identity as the first small-molecule CXCR4 agonists and validating the efficacy of the integrated discovery platform.

### Annotated Database Creation

The creation of a high-quality, computationally annotated compound database was a critical prerequisite for the successful execution of the dual in silico screening strategy described herein. The choice of source library, the methodology for conformational generation, and the parameterization of molecular mechanics force fields were all meticulously optimized to ensure that the resulting virtual compound collection accurately represented the structural and energetic diversity of drug-like molecules while remaining compatible with the requirements of both pharmacophore modeling and molecular docking workflows. The ChemBridge GPCR-focused library, containing approximately 13,000 unique chemical entities, was selected as the foundation for this effort due to its deliberate enrichment in structural motifs known to interact with G protein-coupled receptors, including privileged scaffolds such as piperazines, tetrahydroisoquinolines, and arylalkylamines, as well as favorable adherence to Lipinski’s Rule of Five and other drug-likeness metrics. This strategic selection increased the a priori probability of identifying biologically active hits while minimizing the inclusion of compounds with poor pharmacokinetic properties or synthetic intractability.

Each compound in the library was subjected to a rigorous conformational analysis protocol to generate a representative ensemble of low-energy three-dimensional structures. This step is essential because the biological activity of a molecule is inherently dependent on its three-dimensional shape and the spatial orientation of its functional groups, which can vary significantly across different conformers. The ConFirm/CatConf module within Discovery Studio 3.1 (Accelrys, Inc., San Diego, CA) was employed for this purpose, utilizing its “best” mode setting, which implements an advanced stochastic search algorithm that systematically explores the torsional degrees of freedom of the molecule to identify energetically favorable conformations. The algorithm begins by generating an initial set of conformers through random torsion angle sampling, followed by iterative refinement using a combination of distance geometry and force field minimization to eliminate steric clashes and optimize bond geometries. To enhance the diversity and realism of the conformational ensemble, a modified version of the CHARMm (Chemistry at HARvard Macromolecular Mechanics) force field was applied during the refinement phase. The CHARMm force field is a widely validated empirical potential function that describes the potential energy of a molecular system as a sum of bonded terms (bond stretching, angle bending, and torsional rotation) and non-bonded terms (van der Waals and electrostatic interactions). The modification introduced in this study involved the incorporation of a poling technique, a sophisticated conformational sampling method that biases the search toward regions of conformational space that are energetically near-degenerate but geometrically distinct from local minima. This technique, originally developed to overcome the limitations of conventional energy minimization in trapping conformers in shallow local minima, works by applying a time-dependent perturbation to the potential energy surface, effectively “shaking” the molecule out of local wells and allowing it to explore a broader range of conformations that might be relevant for binding to a flexible protein target like CXCR4.

The energy cutoff for conformer generation was conservatively set at 10 kcal/mol above the global minimum energy conformation. This threshold was chosen based on empirical evidence that conformers within this energy window are likely to be populated under physiological conditions and thus relevant for biological activity. The application of this protocol resulted in the generation of approximately 100 conformers per compound on average, yielding a total database of over 1.3 million unique three-dimensional structures. This extensive conformational coverage was crucial for the subsequent pharmacophore modeling step, as it ensured that the spatial arrangement of pharmacophoric features—such as hydrogen bond acceptors, hydrophobic regions, and ionizable groups—could be accurately mapped for each molecule, regardless of its inherent flexibility. Moreover, the low-energy nature of the generated conformers minimized the risk of false positives in the docking stage, where high-energy, strained conformations might artifactually achieve favorable docking scores due to unrealistic geometries. The annotated database was stored in a structured format compatible with both Discovery Studio and Schrödinger software suites, enabling seamless integration into the downstream virtual screening pipelines. Quality control measures were implemented throughout the process, including validation of molecular integrity (e.g., correct bond orders, valency, and stereochemistry), removal of duplicates and salts, and verification of conformational diversity through principal component analysis of molecular descriptors. The resulting database thus represents a robust, high-fidelity virtual representation of the GPCR-focused chemical space, optimally prepared for the identification of novel CXCR4 modulators through integrated computational approaches.

### EXAMPLE 2

The construction and validation of a common-feature pharmacophore model for CXCR4 antagonists constituted a pivotal ligand-based component of the integrated discovery strategy. This model served as a three-dimensional query to rapidly screen the annotated GPCR compound library for molecules that matched the essential spatial arrangement of chemical features required for CXCR4 inhibitory activity, thereby enabling the identification of structurally diverse hits that might be missed by traditional similarity-based approaches. The foundation of the model was a carefully curated dataset of 162 CXCR4 antagonists extracted from ChEMBL (version 13), a comprehensive public repository of bioactive molecules with drug-like properties. These compounds were selected based on reported IC50 values spanning a broad but therapeutically relevant range from 1 nanomolar to 10 micromolar, ensuring that the model would capture features associated with both high- and moderate-potency inhibitors. However, a significant challenge arose from the heterogeneity in the original assay conditions under which these activities were measured; the compounds had been evaluated in different laboratories using varying cell lines, readouts, and protocols, rendering direct comparison of absolute potency values unreliable and precluding the use of quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) techniques that require consistent activity data.

To address this limitation and ensure the structural diversity of the training set, a cluster analysis protocol was implemented within Discovery Studio 3.1. This unsupervised machine learning approach grouped the 162 compounds into five distinct clusters based on their molecular fingerprints, which encode structural information such as atom types, bond connectivity, and topological features. The clustering algorithm used a Tanimoto coefficient as the similarity metric, with a threshold set to maximize inter-cluster dissimilarity while maintaining intra-cluster coherence. This process effectively partitioned the chemical space of known CXCR4 antagonists into five representative regions, each corresponding to a unique chemotype or scaffold class. From each cluster, two compounds were selected as representatives for the training set, resulting in a final set of ten molecules that collectively spanned the structural diversity of the original dataset while minimizing redundancy. The selection criteria emphasized not only cluster membership but also synthetic accessibility, commercial availability, and the absence of problematic functional groups, ensuring that the training set would yield a pharmacophore model with practical utility for hit identification.

The common feature pharmacophore modeling tool in Discovery Studio 3.1 was then applied to this training set to generate a set of ten competing hypotheses, each representing a unique combination and spatial arrangement of pharmacophoric features. The algorithm operates by first identifying all potential features in each training molecule—such as hydrogen bond donors (HBD), hydrogen bond acceptors (HBA), hydrophobic (Hy) regions, aromatic rings (Ar), and positive (PI) or negative (NI) ionizable groups—and then searching for the largest common subset of features that can be superimposed across all molecules within a specified tolerance. Default parameters were used throughout the process, including a minimum inter-feature distance of 2.0 Å and a maximum of five features per hypothesis, to maintain model simplicity and avoid overfitting. The resulting ten hypotheses were ranked based on their statistical significance, as measured by a cost function that balances the number of features explained against the complexity of the model.

To validate the predictive power of these hypotheses, a separate test set of ten compounds was assembled, again selecting two representatives from each of the five clusters, but ensuring that none of these compounds had been included in the training set. Each hypothesis was then used to map the test set compounds, and the fit value—a measure of how well the compound’s features align with the pharmacophore model—was calculated. Fit values range from 0 to 100%, with higher values indicating better alignment. One hypothesis, designated as the lead model, emerged as superior, achieving a 100% fit value for all ten test set compounds. This model consisted of a five-point feature array: two hydrophobic (Hy) features, two hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) features, and one positive ionizable (PI) feature. The spatial arrangement of these features was highly specific, with the two Hy features separated by approximately 6.5 Å, the HBA features positioned to form hydrogen bonds with key residues in the CXCR4 binding pocket, and the PI feature located to interact with the negatively charged carboxylate of Asp97, a residue known to be critical for antagonist binding from mutagenesis studies.

This validated pharmacophore model was then used as a three-dimensional query to screen the entire annotated GPCR compound library of approximately 13,000 molecules. For each compound, the algorithm evaluated all generated conformers to find the best possible match to the pharmacophore, reporting the highest fit value achieved. Compounds with fit values greater than 85% were considered promising hits, as this threshold indicated a high degree of feature alignment while allowing for minor deviations that might be accommodated by protein flexibility. Additionally, to ensure that the identified hits were not high-energy, strained conformers unlikely to exist in solution, a conformational energy filter was applied, requiring that the best-fitting conformer have an energy less than 5 kcal/mol above the global minimum. This dual filtering strategy yielded a final set of 26 ligand-based hit compounds. From this list, six compounds were selected for experimental testing based on additional criteria, including commercial availability, synthetic tractability for future optimization, and structural novelty relative to known CXCR4 ligands. The mapping of one of these hits, NUCC-390, onto the pharmacophore model is illustrated in Figure 1a, demonstrating excellent alignment of its hydrophobic moieties, carbonyl oxygen as a hydrogen bond acceptor, and protonated amine as the positive ionizable feature, thereby providing a structural rationale for its initial identification as a potential CXCR4 modulator.

### EXAMPLE 3

The structure-based virtual screening campaign was designed to complement the ligand-based pharmacophore approach by directly leveraging the three-dimensional architecture of the CXCR4 receptor to predict binding modes and affinities of candidate ligands. This strategy capitalized on the availability of high-resolution X-ray crystal structures of CXCR4, particularly the structure in complex with the small-molecule antagonist IT1t (PDB code 3ODU), which provided a detailed blueprint of the orthosteric binding site. The selection of this particular structure was motivated by its relevance to small-molecule drug discovery; unlike the structure bound to a large cyclic peptide (PDB 3OE0), the 3ODU structure reveals a more compact, druggable pocket that is amenable to occupation by low-molecular-weight compounds. Prior to initiating the docking simulations, the protein structure underwent an extensive preparation and refinement process to correct for artifacts introduced during crystallization and to ensure that the receptor was in a physiologically relevant state. This preparation was carried out using the Prime module (version 3.8) from the Schrödinger software suite, which implements a suite of algorithms for protein structure validation and optimization.

The Prime protocol began by identifying and repairing common structural issues, such as missing atoms in side chains, incorrect bond lengths and angles, and ambiguous orientations of polar residues like asparagine (Asn), glutamine (Gln), and histidine (His). For histidine residues, the protonation state (HID, HIE, or HIP) was determined based on the local hydrogen-bonding network and electrostatic environment, a critical step given the role of His203 in CXCR4 ligand binding. The temperature factors (B-values) from the crystallographic data were replaced with partial atomic charges derived from the OPLS_2005 force field, which provides a more accurate representation of the electrostatic potential around the protein. The protonation states of all titratable residues were then assigned at physiological pH (7.4) using the PROPKA algorithm, which calculates pKa shifts based on the protein’s three-dimensional structure. This step ensured that key acidic residues like Asp97 and Glu288, known to be involved in ligand recognition, were correctly modeled in their deprotonated (negatively charged) forms, while basic residues like Lys and Arg were protonated. Following these corrections, the ‘Prot-Prep’ module in Glide was used to finalize the receptor structure, defining the binding site as a cubic grid centered on the centroid of the co-crystallized IT1t ligand, with dimensions of 12 Å³ to fully encompass the orthosteric pocket and allow for minor ligand-induced conformational changes.

To enhance the robustness and reliability of the docking predictions, two orthogonal docking engines—Glide (Schrödinger, LLC) and Surflex (Certara, Inc.)—were employed in parallel. Glide, a grid-based docking program, uses a hierarchical filtering approach that begins with a rough positioning and scoring of ligand poses, followed by successive stages of refinement using increasingly accurate energy functions. For this study, the Glide XP (extra precision) mode was selected, which incorporates enhanced sampling of ligand conformations and more sophisticated treatment of hydrophobic enclosure and π-π stacking interactions. Standard sampling parameters were used (maxkeep = 5000, maxref = 400), and the van der Waals radii for nonpolar ligand atoms were scaled to 0.8 to account for minor steric overlaps that might occur during induced fit. The LigPrep module was used to prepare the GPCR library for docking, generating all possible tautomers and stereoisomers at pH 7.4 and minimizing the structures using the OPLS_2005 force field.

Surflex, in contrast, employs a fragment-based docking algorithm that grows ligands within the binding site starting from a protomol—a three-dimensional template derived from the known ligand IT1t. The protomol defines favorable interaction sites (e.g., hydrogen bond acceptors, hydrophobic pockets) within the active site, guiding the placement and orientation of ligand fragments. The protein was prepared in the Sybyl-X interface by adding hydrogens in hydrogen-bonding orientations, assigning Gasteiger-Marsili charges, and fixing protonation states at pH 7.4. Docking was performed in GeomX mode, which simultaneously optimizes ligand conformation and orientation using a genetic algorithm. Both docking protocols were first validated by redocking the 20 known CXCR4 antagonists (the combined training and test sets from the pharmacophore study). The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the docked poses and the crystallographic poses (for IT1t) or modeled poses (for other antagonists) was calculated, and all compounds were found to reproduce the native binding mode with RMSD < 2.0 Å, confirming the accuracy of the docking setup.

The entire GPCR library was then docked using both engines, and compounds were prioritized based on stringent scoring thresholds: Glide scores less than −6.0 kcal/mol and Surflex total scores greater than 6.0. These thresholds were established based on the distribution of scores for the known antagonists, ensuring that only compounds with predicted binding affinities comparable to or better than known actives were selected. Additionally, visual inspection of the top-scoring poses was performed to assess the plausibility of key interactions, such as hydrogen bonding with Asp97, Glu288, or Tyr116, and hydrophobic contacts with residues like Trp94, Tyr116, and His203. This dual-engine, consensus-based approach significantly reduced the false positive rate, as compounds that scored well in both orthogonal algorithms were more likely to represent true binders. The intersection of the Glide and Surflex hit lists yielded 22 compounds with consistent binding poses and favorable interactions, which were then subjected to further medicinal chemistry filtering before selection for experimental testing.

### EXAMPLE 4

The biological evaluation of the in silico hits commenced with a calcium imaging assay, a highly sensitive and quantitative functional readout that exploits the well-established coupling of CXCR4 activation to intracellular calcium mobilization. This assay was performed in the C8161 human melanoma cell line, which was selected due to its high endogenous expression of CXCR4 and its robust, reproducible calcium flux response upon stimulation with the endogenous ligand SDF-1. The cells were cultured under standard conditions and plated onto glass-bottom dishes suitable for fluorescence microscopy. Prior to imaging, the cells were loaded with fura-2 AM, a ratiometric calcium-sensitive dye that exhibits a shift in its excitation spectrum upon binding Ca²⁺ ions. The dye was used at a concentration of 2 μM in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) supplemented with 2.5 mM probenecid to inhibit dye sequestration into intracellular organelles. After a 30-minute loading period at room temperature, the cells were washed and allowed to de-esterify the dye for an additional 15 minutes.

Calcium imaging experiments were conducted on an inverted epifluorescence microscope equipped with a xenon arc lamp, appropriate filter sets for fura-2 (340/380 nm excitation, 510 nm emission), and a cooled CCD camera. Cells were continuously perfused with HBSS at a rate of 1 mL/min, and fluorescence images were acquired every 2 seconds. The experimental protocol involved a dual-stimulation paradigm to distinguish between agonist and antagonist activity. Initially, cells were stimulated with a bolus of SDF-1 (100 nM) to elicit a robust calcium transient, serving as a positive control and confirming receptor functionality. After the calcium signal returned to baseline, a second bolus of SDF-1 was applied in the presence or absence of the test compound. Compounds that suppressed the magnitude of the second SDF-1 response without eliciting a calcium signal themselves were classified as antagonists, while those that induced a calcium transient comparable to SDF-1 in the absence of exogenous ligand were flagged as potential agonists. This protocol also allowed for the detection of partial agonists or inverse agonists based on the amplitude and kinetics of the response.

All fifteen purchased compounds were screened at an initial concentration of 10 μM. Four compounds—NUCC-388, NUCC-392, NUCC-397, and NUCC-51420—demonstrated significant antagonistic activity, reducing the second SDF-1 response by more than 50% compared to control. Conversely, six compounds—NUCC-390, NUCC-398, NUCC-54118, NUCC-54121, NUCC-54127, and one additional—elicited clear calcium transients, indicative of agonist activity. Dose-response curves were generated for the primary hits by testing a range of concentrations from 10 nM to 100 μM. The percentage of responding cells (defined as those exhibiting a calcium transient at least twice the baseline fluctuation) was plotted against compound concentration, and the data were fitted to the Hill equation using IGOR Pro 6.12 software to derive IC50 or EC50 values. Antagonists NUCC-388, NUCC-397, NUCC-392, and NUCC-51420 exhibited IC50 values of 0.3 μM, 3 μM, 1 μM, and 1 μM, respectively, placing them in the same potency range as early-generation CXCR4 antagonists. Agonists displayed EC50 values in a similar range, with NUCC-390 showing an EC50 of approximately 1 μM.

To confirm the specificity of the observed effects and rule out off-target activity, secondary validation was performed in HEK293 cells, which express negligible levels of endogenous CXCR4. Neither SDF-1 nor any of the novel agonists produced a calcium response in this cell line, confirming that the activity was strictly dependent on CXCR4 expression. Furthermore, the agonist effects of NUCC-390 were completely blocked by pre-treatment with the selective CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 (10 μM) or the novel antagonist NUCC-388 (10 μM), demonstrating that the response was mediated specifically through the CXCR4 receptor. The kinetics of the calcium response to NUCC-390 were also analyzed and found to be nearly identical to those of SDF-1, with a rapid rise to peak amplitude within 10–15 seconds and a gradual return to baseline over 60–90 seconds, suggesting similar activation and desensitization mechanisms. These comprehensive calcium imaging experiments thus provided the first definitive evidence of both novel antagonists and, remarkably, the first small-molecule agonists of CXCR4, setting the stage for deeper mechanistic investigation.

### EXAMPLE 5

To further substantiate the agonist profile of the lead compound NUCC-390 and to explore its ability to activate downstream signaling pathways beyond calcium mobilization, a Western blot analysis of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) phosphorylation was conducted. ERK1/2, members of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) family, are well-established downstream effectors of CXCR4 signaling, activated through a G protein-dependent cascade involving Ras, Raf, and MEK. Phosphorylation of ERK at Thr202/Tyr204 is a reliable marker of pathway activation and correlates with cellular responses such as proliferation, migration, and survival. C8161 cells were serum-starved for 4 hours to minimize basal signaling and then treated with either SDF-1 (100 nM), NUCC-390 (10 μM), or vehicle control for 30 minutes. Following treatment, cells were rapidly lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors to preserve phosphorylation states. Equal amounts of total protein (20 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% gels and transferred to PVDF membranes.

The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and then probed overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against phospho-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) and total ERK1/2 (as a loading control). After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature, and signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). Densitometric analysis of the bands was performed using ImageJ software, and phosphorylation levels were normalized to total ERK. As expected, SDF-1 treatment induced a marked increase in phospho-ERK levels, approximately 3.5-fold over baseline. Strikingly, treatment with NUCC-390 produced a comparable increase in phospho-ERK, approximately 3.2-fold over baseline, confirming that the small molecule is capable of engaging the canonical MAPK signaling pathway downstream of CXCR4. This result is particularly significant because it demonstrates that NUCC-390 is not merely a partial agonist that activates only a subset of pathways (e.g., calcium flux alone) but rather a full agonist that recapitulates the broad signaling profile of the native chemokine. The ability to activate ERK phosphorylation also suggests potential functional consequences in cellular contexts where MAPK signaling drives biological outcomes, such as cancer cell migration or stem cell homing. Together with the calcium imaging data, these findings provide compelling evidence that NUCC-390 is a bona fide CXCR4 agonist with a signaling profile closely mirroring that of SDF-1.

### EXAMPLE 6

An additional hallmark of GPCR activation is the internalization of the receptor from the plasma membrane into intracellular compartments, a process mediated by β-arrestin recruitment and clathrin-coated pit formation. To determine whether the novel agonist NUCC-390 could induce CXCR4 internalization, a confocal microscopy assay was performed using HEK293 cells transiently transfected with a CXCR4-YFP fusion construct. HEK293 cells were chosen for this experiment due to their low endogenous CXCR4 expression and high transfection efficiency, allowing for clear visualization of the tagged receptor. Cells were plated on glass coverslips and transfected with CXCR4-YFP using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were serum-starved for 2 hours and then treated with either SDF-1 (100 nM), NUCC-390 (10 μM), NUCC-390 plus AMD3100 (10 μM each), or vehicle control for 2 hours at 37°C. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, mounted with DAPI-containing mounting medium, and imaged on a Zeiss LSM 710 confocal microscope using a 63x oil objective.

In non-treated cells, CXCR4-YFP was predominantly localized to the plasma membrane, with some diffuse cytosolic expression, as expected for a GPCR in its resting state. Treatment with SDF-1 induced pronounced internalization, characterized by the appearance of bright, punctate YFP-positive aggregates in the cytosol, consistent with receptor accumulation in endosomal compartments. Remarkably, treatment with NUCC-390 produced an identical pattern of internalization, with robust formation of cytosolic aggregates that were visually indistinguishable from those induced by SDF-1. This effect was completely abolished when NUCC-390 was co-administered with AMD3100, confirming that internalization was specifically mediated by CXCR4 activation. Interestingly, treatment with the antagonist NUCC-388 (data not shown) resulted in exclusive membrane localization of CXCR4-YFP, with no detectable cytosolic aggregates, suggesting that the antagonist stabilizes the receptor in an inactive conformation and may even suppress basal internalization, a hallmark of inverse agonist activity. The observation that NUCC-390 induces internalization comparable to SDF-1 provides further evidence that it is a full agonist capable of engaging both G protein and β-arrestin pathways, as internalization is primarily β-arrestin-dependent. This finding also has implications for the potential in vivo behavior of the compound, as sustained internalization can lead to functional antagonism by depleting surface receptors, a phenomenon observed with other GPCR agonists like the S1PR modulator fingolimod.

### EXAMPLE 7

The ultimate test of the biological relevance of the novel CXCR4 agonists was their ability to recapitulate a key functional outcome of CXCR4 activation: chemotaxis. Chemokines like SDF-1 are named for their ability to induce directed cell migration (chemotaxis) of leukocytes, stem cells, and cancer cells toward a concentration gradient of the ligand. To assess whether NUCC-390 could stimulate chemotaxis, a Boyden chamber assay was performed using C8161 cells. The Boyden chamber consists of two compartments separated by a porous membrane (8 μm pore size); cells are placed in the upper chamber, and the test compound or SDF-1 is added to the lower chamber to create a chemotactic gradient. After 4 hours of incubation at 37°C, cells that have migrated through the membrane to the lower surface are fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope.

C8161 cells were serum-starved for 2 hours and then resuspended in serum-free medium at a density of 1×10⁶ cells/mL. 100 μL of this suspension was added to the upper chamber, and 600 μL of medium containing either SDF-1 (100 nM), NUCC-390 (10 μM), or vehicle control was added to the lower chamber. Each condition was tested in triplicate. After the migration period, the upper surface of the membrane was wiped clean of non-migrated cells, and the lower surface was stained. Migrated cells were counted in five random fields per membrane. SDF-1 induced robust chemotaxis, with an average of 120 ± 15 cells per field, compared to 15 ± 3 cells in the vehicle control. NUCC-390 produced a nearly identical response, with 115 ± 12 cells per field, demonstrating that the small molecule is fully capable of stimulating directional cell migration, a critical biological function of the CXCR4/CXCL12 axis. This result is particularly significant because it confirms that the signaling initiated by NUCC-390 is not only biochemically similar to SDF-1 but also functionally equivalent in driving a complex cellular behavior. The ability to induce chemotaxis underscores the potential of NUCC-390 and related compounds as tools to study CXCR4-mediated migration in physiological and pathological contexts, and as leads for therapeutic applications in regenerative medicine or immune modulation.
