# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of enzymology and protein engineering, specifically to novel variants of Rhizomucor miehei lipase (RmL) and its proenzyme form (ProRmL), as well as methods for their production, purification, and use. More particularly, the invention provides structural and functional insights into the role of the N-terminal propeptide in regulating the activity of class 3 fungal lipases. The invention encompasses isolated polypeptides comprising the mature RmL enzyme, the full-length prolipase (ProRmL), and engineered variants thereof—including a deletion mutant designated ProRmL-del—in which residues at the junction between the propeptide and the mature domain are removed to enhance stability. Additionally, the invention includes nucleic acid molecules encoding these polypeptides, expression vectors, host cells capable of expressing them, and methods for producing and utilizing these proteins in industrial applications such as detergents, food processing, biofuel production, and biocatalysis. The invention further discloses that the propeptide functions not only as an intramolecular chaperone facilitating correct folding but also as a potent inhibitor that sterically blocks the active site and stabilizes the closed, inactive conformation of the lipase by interacting with the lid domain and key hydrophobic substrate-binding regions. This dual functionality enables controlled activation of the enzyme during secretion in the native host and provides a mechanism for preventing premature catalytic activity that could be deleterious to the producing cell. The structural elucidation of the propeptide-bound complex, achieved through X-ray crystallography, reveals a previously unknown mode of autoinhibition in class 3 lipases and establishes a foundation for rational design of lipase variants with tailored stability, activity, and regulatory properties.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Lipases (triacylglycerol acylhydrolases, EC 3.1.1.3) are ubiquitous enzymes that catalyze the hydrolysis of ester bonds in water-insoluble triglycerides at lipid-water interfaces. Among the diverse families of lipases, those classified as class 3 lipases (Pfam PF01764) constitute a distinct evolutionary group within the α/β hydrolase superfold, characterized by a conserved Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad and a unique structural feature known as the “lid”—a mobile amphipathic α-helix that undergoes conformational rearrangement upon contact with a lipid interface to expose the otherwise buried active site. The lipase from the thermophilic fungus Rhizomucor miehei (RmL) is a prototypical member of this class and has been extensively studied due to its high thermostability, broad substrate specificity, and significant industrial utility in sectors ranging from laundry detergents to biodiesel synthesis. Historically, research on RmL has focused almost exclusively on the mature enzyme, for which both open (active) and closed (inactive) conformations have been resolved by X-ray crystallography. However, it has long been known that RmL is initially synthesized as a pre-proenzyme (pre-ProRmL) comprising an N-terminal signal peptide, a 65-residue propeptide, and the 269-residue catalytic domain. While the signal peptide directs secretion and is cleaved co-translationally, the propeptide remains associated with the mature domain during early stages of biosynthesis.

Despite early recognition of the propeptide’s existence—first inferred from cDNA library analysis in Escherichia coli and later confirmed in Aspergillus oryzae expression systems—the precise structural and functional role of this domain remained enigmatic for decades. Propeptides in other enzyme families, such as subtilisin-like serine proteases, are well-established as intramolecular chaperones essential for proper folding, yet they are typically degraded or dissociated upon maturation and do not exhibit inhibitory activity. In contrast, accumulating evidence from mutagenesis studies on RmL and the homologous Thermomyces lanuginosus lipase (TLL) suggested that substitutions within the propeptide region could significantly modulate enzymatic activity, implying a more dynamic and persistent interaction beyond mere folding assistance. Nevertheless, no three-dimensional structure of any fungal class 3 prolipase had been reported prior to the present work, leaving critical questions unanswered: Does the propeptide remain bound post-folding? If so, does it inhibit activity? And what is the molecular basis of such regulation?

Compounding this knowledge gap, conventional purification strategies for RmL often failed to isolate intact prolipase due to endogenous proteolytic cleavage during fermentation or downstream processing. This technical challenge obscured the true physiological state of the enzyme in vivo and hindered biochemical characterization. Moreover, while bioinformatic tools like SignalP could predict signal peptide cleavage, they did not account for the action of specific Golgi-resident proteases such as KexB in A. oryzae, which cleaves after dibasic motifs (e.g., KR or RR) and defines the true N-terminus of the propeptide. Consequently, discrepancies in residue numbering between genomic sequences, UniProt annotations, and published crystal structures further complicated comparative analyses.

The absence of structural data on propeptide-containing lipases also impeded efforts to engineer improved industrial variants. Rational design approaches relied solely on modifications to the mature domain, overlooking potential regulatory hotspots within the propeptide. Furthermore, the observation that certain propeptide mutations enhanced activity—such as L81V in RmL—lacked a mechanistic explanation, limiting the predictive power of protein engineering campaigns. Parallel investigations into related lipases, including Rhizopus oryzae and Gibberella zeae, revealed analogous N- or C-terminal extensions, but without high-resolution structures, their functional significance remained speculative.

Against this backdrop, the need arose for a comprehensive structural and functional characterization of the RmL prolipase to resolve longstanding ambiguities about propeptide function. Such insight would not only advance fundamental understanding of lipase biology but also unlock new avenues for controlling enzyme activation in heterologous expression systems and designing next-generation biocatalysts with programmable latency and triggered activity. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by providing the first atomic-resolution structures of ProRmL and a stabilized deletion variant (ProRmL-del), demonstrating unequivocally that the propeptide acts as a non-covalent, high-affinity inhibitor that locks the enzyme in its closed conformation through extensive interactions with the lid and substrate-binding interface. These findings redefine the paradigm of propeptide function in class 3 lipases and establish a robust platform for innovation in enzyme technology.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel compositions and methods based on the discovery that the propeptide of Rhizomucor miehei lipase (RmL) functions as a potent, non-covalent inhibitor that maintains the enzyme in an inactive, closed conformation until proteolytic maturation occurs during secretion. Central to the invention is the elucidation of the three-dimensional structure of the prolipase (ProRmL), comprising residues 30–363 of the pre-proenzyme, which reveals for the first time how the propeptide (residues 30–94) wraps around the mature lipase domain (residues 95–363) to occlude the active site and stabilize the closed lid conformation. The invention further discloses a genetically engineered variant, ProRmL-del, in which residues 95 and 96 (Ser-Ile) at the propeptide-mature domain junction are deleted to prevent autoproteolytic cleavage, thereby yielding a stable, homogeneous prolipase suitable for structural and functional studies. This variant retains the inhibitory architecture of the wild-type complex but exhibits enhanced integrity during purification and crystallization.

In one aspect, the invention provides isolated polypeptides selected from the group consisting of: (a) a mature RmL polypeptide comprising amino acid residues 95–363 of SEQ ID NO:1; (b) a ProRmL polypeptide comprising amino acid residues 30–363 of SEQ ID NO:1; (c) a cleaved propeptide comprising amino acid residues 30–94 of SEQ ID NO:1; and (d) a ProRmL-del polypeptide comprising amino acid residues 30–363 of SEQ ID NO:1 with a deletion of residues 95 and 96. In another aspect, the invention encompasses nucleic acid molecules encoding any of the aforementioned polypeptides, expression vectors comprising such nucleic acids operably linked to regulatory elements for expression in a host cell, and recombinant host cells—particularly filamentous fungi such as Aspergillus oryzae—transformed with these vectors.

The invention also provides methods for producing these polypeptides, involving culturing a recombinant host cell under conditions conducive to expression and recovering the polypeptide from the culture medium or lysate. Purification strategies exploit the differential binding properties of the mature enzyme and prolipase to hydrophobic matrices such as decylamine agarose, wherein mature RmL binds strongly due to its exposed hydrophobic substrate interface, while ProRmL and ProRmL-del flow through owing to propeptide-mediated masking of this interface. Subsequent ion-exchange chromatography enables high-purity isolation of the prolipase forms.

Functionally, the invention demonstrates that the propeptide inhibits RmL activity with high affinity (Kd ≈ 0.2 μM), as confirmed by fluorescence polarization binding assays and GLAD lipase activity assays. Structural analysis shows that inhibition arises from two synergistic mechanisms: (i) direct burial of the catalytic triad (Ser238, Asp297, His351) beneath the propeptide’s compact domain, and (ii) stabilization of the closed lid conformation via specific interactions between propeptide residues (e.g., Leu81, Lys76, Tyr77) and lid residues (e.g., Ile183, Ala184, Leu186, Phe188). Additionally, the propeptide covers key hydrophobic substrate-anchoring residues (Ile298, Phe345, Val348, Leu349), replacing them with a hydrophilic surface that impedes interfacial activation.

The invention further extends to homologous lipases, as evidenced by reinterpretation of the Rhizopus microsporus var. chinensis lipase structure (PDB 4L3W), wherein an unmodeled polypeptide chain corresponding to a propeptide was identified and shown to adopt a similar inhibitory conformation. This suggests a conserved autoinhibitory mechanism across class 3 fungal lipases.

In practical applications, the invention enables the design of lipase variants with tunable latency—useful in formulations where premature activity is undesirable—and provides targets for mutagenesis to modulate activation kinetics. For instance, mutations at Leu81 (e.g., L81V) weaken propeptide binding, leading to increased basal activity, while deletions like ProRmL-del confer stability for structural studies or controlled release systems. Collectively, these advances offer transformative potential for industrial enzymology, diagnostics, and synthetic biology.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Definition

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below unless otherwise indicated by context. “Rhizomucor miehei lipase” or “RmL” refers to the mature, catalytically active form of the lipase enzyme derived from the fungus Rhizomucor miehei, corresponding to amino acid residues 95–363 of the pre-proenzyme sequence as defined in UniProt entry P19515 (SEQ ID NO:1). “ProRmL” denotes the prolipase form of RmL, comprising the N-terminal propeptide (residues 30–94) non-covalently associated with the mature domain (residues 95–363), or in the case of the engineered variant ProRmL-del, a covalent single-chain polypeptide with residues 95–96 deleted. “Propeptide” refers to the 65-amino acid segment (residues 30–94 of SEQ ID NO:1) that is proteolytically removed during maturation to yield active RmL. “Class 3 lipase” designates members of the Pfam family PF01764, characterized by an α/β hydrolase fold, a Ser-His-Asp catalytic triad, and a mobile lid domain regulating interfacial activation. “Closed conformation” describes the inactive state of the lipase wherein the lid helix occludes the active site, while “open conformation” refers to the active state with the lid displaced to expose the catalytic cleft. “KexB” is a Golgi-resident subtilisin-like endoprotease in Aspergillus oryzae that cleaves after dibasic motifs (KR/RR) and processes the pre-proenzyme to define the N-terminus of the propeptide at residue 30. “Decylamine agarose chromatography” is a hydrophobic interaction chromatography method using a decylamine-functionalized resin that mimics the lipid substrate interface, thereby selectively binding mature lipase but not propeptide-inhibited forms. “GLAD assay” is an industry-standard lipase activity assay measuring hydrolysis of 4-methylumbelliferyl oleate coated on cellulose fibers in the presence of detergent, with fluorescence detection of released 4-methylumbelliferone. “ProRmL-del” is a genetically engineered variant of ProRmL in which the first two residues of the mature domain (Ser95 and Ile96) are deleted to prevent autoproteolytic cleavage and stabilize the prolipase complex. “Non-covalent complex” indicates that in wild-type ProRmL, the propeptide and mature domain are separate polypeptides held together by high-affinity protein-protein interactions rather than a peptide bond, whereas in ProRmL-del, the deletion creates a continuous polypeptide chain resistant to cleavage. All residue numbering throughout this application follows the UniProt P19515 convention starting from the first residue of the signal peptide, such that mature RmL begins at residue 95.

### Conventions for Designation of Variants

Variants of RmL and ProRmL disclosed herein are designated according to standard protein nomenclature, wherein the wild-type amino acid is indicated by its single-letter code, followed by the position number according to UniProt P19515 (starting from residue 1 of the signal peptide), and then the substituted amino acid. For example, the mutation L81V denotes substitution of leucine at position 81 with valine. Deletion variants are indicated by the prefix “del” followed by the deleted residues; thus, ProRmL-del refers to the prolipase with deletion of residues 95 and 96. When multiple mutations are present, they are listed sequentially separated by commas (e.g., L81V, K76A). Chimeric constructs or fusion proteins are described by specifying the origin and boundaries of each domain. All variants retain the core structural and functional attributes of the parent molecule unless explicitly stated otherwise. The term “variant” encompasses naturally occurring allelic forms, laboratory-generated mutants, and engineered derivatives produced by site-directed mutagenesis, random mutagenesis, or gene synthesis, provided they exhibit at least one altered property relative to the wild-type—such as enhanced stability, modified activity, altered substrate specificity, or resistance to proteolysis—while maintaining sufficient sequence identity (preferably ≥80%, more preferably ≥90%) to the reference sequence over the aligned region. Sequence identity is calculated using the Needleman-Wunsch global alignment algorithm with default parameters as implemented in EMBOSS Needle.

### Muscle v3.8.31 Basic Usage

The structural models and sequence alignments referenced in this invention were generated using MUSCLE (MUltiple Sequence Comparison by Log-Expectation) version 3.8.31, a widely used software tool for creating multiple sequence alignments of protein or nucleotide sequences. MUSCLE employs a progressive alignment strategy combined with iterative refinement to achieve high accuracy and computational efficiency. In the context of this invention, MUSCLE was utilized to align the propeptide and mature domains of RmL with homologous sequences from other fungal lipases, including Rhizopus microsporus var. chinensis (UniProt A3FM73), Thermomyces lanuginosus, and Gibberella zeae, to identify conserved residues and structural motifs implicated in propeptide-mediated inhibition. The default parameters of MUSCLE v3.8.31 were applied, which include a maximum number of iterations set to 16, a distance measure based on k-mer counting for initial clustering, and Kimura protein distance correction for tree construction. The resulting alignments were visualized and annotated using Jalview or similar molecular graphics software to highlight invariant positions (e.g., Leu81) and proline-rich regions (residues 39–45) critical for propeptide folding and interaction. These alignments informed the selection of target sites for mutagenesis and supported the conclusion that the autoinhibitory mechanism is evolutionarily conserved among class 3 lipases. It is emphasized that while MUSCLE was instrumental in generating the foundational sequence comparisons, the functional and structural conclusions of the invention are experimentally validated and do not rely solely on computational predictions.

### Common Options (for a Complete List Please See the User Guide):

The experimental protocols and analytical methods employed in the development of this invention utilized a range of standard biochemical and biophysical techniques, each configured with commonly accepted parameters optimized for lipase characterization. For instance, native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was performed using a 12% resolving gel buffered with 0.4 M Tris-HCl at pH 8.8 and a 5.1% stacking gel with 0.25 M Tris-HCl at pH 6.8, employing standard APS/TEMED initiation and Tris-glycine running buffer. Zymogram analysis followed electrophoresis by overlaying the gel onto an agarose plate containing olive oil emulsion and brilliant green dye, allowing visualization of lipase activity as clear halos against a stained background. Temperature stability assays (TSA) monitored thermal denaturation by differential scanning fluorimetry using SYPRO Orange dye, with melting temperatures (Tm) determined from the inflection point of fluorescence versus temperature curves. Fluorescence polarization (FP) binding assays used a fluorescein-labeled propeptide fragment (SDVETKYGMALNATSYC-A488) at 0.9 μM titrated against increasing concentrations of mature RmL (0.0002–10 μM) in HEPES-buffered saline with calcium, measuring steady-state polarization after 30-minute equilibration. Crystallization trials employed sitting-drop or hanging-drop vapor diffusion with commercial screens (e.g., Hampton Research Crystal Screen), optimizing hits by fine-tuning precipitant concentration, pH, and additive inclusion (e.g., 0.1% β-octyl-glucoside). X-ray diffraction data were collected at synchrotron facilities (Diamond Light Source beamlines I02 and I04-1) and processed using standard pipelines (MOSFLM/XDS for integration, Aimless for scaling). Structure solution relied on molecular replacement with Phaser or Molrep using mature RmL (PDB 3TGL) as a search model, followed by iterative model building in Coot and refinement in Refmac or Phenix. Mass spectrometry for protein identification used tryptic digestion coupled to LC-MS/MS on an Orbitrap platform, with database searching via Mascot against a custom RmL sequence database including propeptide variants. All these methods represent routine options in structural biology and enzymology, and their specific implementation details are documented in the Examples section to ensure reproducibility.

### Nucleic Acid Constructs

Nucleic acid constructs of the invention comprise a polynucleotide of the invention operably linked to one or more regulatory elements that control its transcription and translation in a host cell. Such constructs are designed for efficient heterologous expression of RmL variants in microbial hosts, particularly filamentous fungi like Aspergillus oryzae, which provide appropriate post-translational modifications and secretory pathways. A typical construct includes a strong, inducible or constitutive promoter (e.g., the A. oryzae TAKA amylase promoter or glucoamylase promoter), a secretion signal sequence (which may be the native RmL signal peptide or a heterologous signal such as the A. niger glucoamylase signal), the coding sequence for the desired RmL variant (e.g., ProRmL or ProRmL-del), and a transcriptional terminator (e.g., the A. oryzae TAKA amylase terminator). The construct may also contain a selectable marker gene (e.g., pyrG for uridine auxotrophy complementation or amdS for acetamide utilization) to facilitate transformation and strain selection. For ProRmL-del, the construct is engineered to delete the codons for Ser95 and Ile96 immediately following the propeptide coding region, ensuring that the expressed protein lacks the cleavage site recognized by endogenous proteases. These constructs are typically assembled in plasmid vectors suitable for fungal transformation, such as pAB4-1 or pAN52-based shuttle vectors, and verified by DNA sequencing prior to use. The nucleic acid constructs enable controlled, high-yield production of stable prolipase forms that retain the propeptide-mediated inhibitory conformation, which is essential for structural studies and applications requiring latent enzyme activity.

### Expression Vectors

Expression vectors of the invention are recombinant DNA molecules that carry the nucleic acid constructs described above and are capable of autonomous replication or integration into the genome of a host cell. Preferred vectors for fungal expression are based on autonomously replicating plasmids or integrative vectors that stably incorporate the expression cassette into the host chromosome. For Aspergillus oryzae, exemplary vectors include those derived from pTAEx3 or pUCC1, which contain AMA1 sequences for autonomous replication or pyrG for targeted integration into the pyrG locus. These vectors feature multiple cloning sites flanked by promoter and terminator sequences, allowing insertion of the RmL variant coding sequence in the correct orientation. The vectors also include bacterial origins of replication and antibiotic resistance genes (e.g., ampicillin) for propagation and selection in E. coli during cloning steps. Upon transformation into A. oryzae protoplasts, the vectors direct the synthesis and secretion of the encoded lipase variant into the culture medium, where it can be harvested and purified. The expression vectors are optimized to ensure that the propeptide remains associated with the mature domain during secretion, leveraging the host’s native KexB protease to cleave only the signal peptide and not the propeptide-mature junction—especially in the case of ProRmL-del, where the deletion eliminates the protease recognition motif. These vectors represent critical tools for scalable production of the invention’s polypeptides under conditions that preserve their functional and structural integrity.

### Host Cells

Host cells of the invention are microorganisms genetically modified to express the polypeptides of the invention, with a preference for eukaryotic hosts that support proper folding, disulfide bond formation, and secretion of complex eukaryotic proteins. The most preferred host is Aspergillus oryzae, a Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) filamentous fungus widely used in industrial enzyme production due to its high secretory capacity and compatibility with RmL’s native processing pathway. Other suitable fungal hosts include Aspergillus niger, Trichoderma reesei, and Neurospora crassa. Bacterial hosts such as Escherichia coli may be used for preliminary expression studies but often fail to produce correctly folded, active lipase due to the absence of eukaryotic chaperones and glycosylation machinery. Yeast hosts like Saccharomyces cerevisiae or Pichia pastoris are viable alternatives but may exhibit different proteolytic processing patterns. The host cells are transformed with the expression vectors of the invention using standard methods such as polyethylene glycol-mediated protoplast transformation for fungi or electroporation for bacteria. Transformants are selected based on the vector’s marker gene and screened for high-level expression by SDS-PAGE or activity assays. Cultivation is carried out in defined or complex media at optimal temperatures (e.g., 34°C for A. oryzae) with aeration to support growth and protein secretion. The resulting host cells serve as biocatalytic factories for producing the invention’s lipase variants, which accumulate in the extracellular medium and can be recovered by filtration and chromatography.

### Methods of Production

Methods of production according to the invention involve cultivating a recombinant host cell expressing a polypeptide of the invention under conditions that promote high-level expression and correct post-translational processing, followed by recovery and purification of the target protein. For ProRmL and ProRmL-del, the method begins with inoculation of a selected A. oryzae transformant into a seed culture, which is then scaled up to a production fermenter containing a nutrient-rich medium (e.g., M400 medium) supplemented with carbon and nitrogen sources. Fermentation is conducted at 34°C with agitation and aeration for 3–5 days to allow accumulation of the secreted lipase variant in the broth. The culture supernatant is clarified by filtration to remove mycelial debris and then subjected to a multi-step purification protocol. The first step exploits hydrophobic interaction chromatography on decylamine agarose, which binds mature RmL but not the propeptide-masked ProRmL or ProRmL-del, allowing separation of active and latent forms. The flow-through containing the prolipase is concentrated and diafiltered, then applied to an anion-exchange column (e.g., UNO Q) where ProRmL binds at low ionic strength and is eluted with a salt gradient (e.g., 1 M NaCl). For ProRmL-del, an additional ammonium sulfate precipitation and Butyl-Toyopearl chromatography step may be included to enhance purity. Final polishing is achieved by size-exclusion chromatography (e.g., Superdex 200) to isolate monodisperse protein. The purified polypeptides are formulated in stabilizing buffers (e.g., 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.0) and stored at 4°C or frozen for long-term use. This production method ensures high yields of structurally intact prolipase suitable for industrial applications or structural analysis.

### Example 1: Variant Generation, Transformation and Expression

To generate the ProRmL-del variant, site-directed mutagenesis was performed on a plasmid encoding wild-type ProRmL using overlapping PCR primers designed to delete codons for Ser95 and Ile96. The forward primer (5′-GCT GCA GCC TAC AGC TAC TGC-3′) and reverse primer (5′-GCA GGC TGC AGT AGC GTA GGC-3′) introduced the double deletion while maintaining the reading frame. PCR amplification was carried out with Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase under standard cycling conditions (98°C for 30 s; 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 60°C for 30 s, 72°C for 1 min; final extension at 72°C for 5 min). The parental methylated template DNA was digested with DpnI restriction enzyme at 37°C for 6 hours, leaving the newly synthesized non-methylated mutant plasmid intact. The reaction mixture was transformed into competent E. coli DH5α cells, and colonies were screened by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing to confirm the desired mutation. Verified plasmids were then used to transform Aspergillus oryzae ToC1512 protoplasts prepared by lysing mycelia with Driselase in osmotic stabilizer (1.2 M MgSO4). Transformants were selected on COVE-S medium lacking uridine and screened for lipase expression by small-scale fermentation in 2 mL M400 medium in 96-well plates at 34°C for 4 days. Supernatants were analyzed by SDS-PAGE, revealing a single band at ~42 kDa for ProRmL-del, consistent with a stable, uncleaved prolipase, compared to the dual bands (~27 kDa mature RmL and ~7 kDa propeptide) observed for wild-type ProRmL due to autoproteolysis. High-expressing clones were scaled up to 1 L baffled shake flasks for protein purification.

### Purification of RmL WT Controls: RmL WT and RmL WT Pur

Wild-type mature RmL (RmL WT) was purified from A. oryzae fermentation broth as a control for activity and structural comparisons. The clarified supernatant was loaded onto a decylamine agarose column pre-equilibrated with 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) + 1 M NaCl. Mature RmL bound tightly to the hydrophobic resin due to its exposed substrate interface, while ProRmL flowed through. The column was washed with three column volumes of equilibration buffer, and bound RmL WT was eluted with Milli-Q water (100% buffer B). The eluate was concentrated and buffer-exchanged into 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) using a 10 kDa cutoff centrifugal filter. A second purification step involved size-exclusion chromatography on Superdex 200 to remove aggregates, yielding RmL WT Pur—a homogeneous, monomeric preparation confirmed by SDS-PAGE and mass spectrometry (observed mass 29,842 Da, matching theoretical mass after signal peptide cleavage and N-terminal pyroglutamate formation). This preparation served as the active enzyme control in all inhibition and binding assays.

### Propeptide Variant Generation

Separate expression constructs were made to produce the isolated RmL propeptide (residues 30–94) for inhibition studies. The propeptide coding sequence was cloned downstream of the A. oryzae TAKA amylase promoter and fused to the native signal peptide to ensure secretion. Transformants were screened as above, and the propeptide was purified from the unbound fraction of the decylamine column (which contains ProRmL and free propeptide) using a two-step reversed-phase chromatography protocol. First, the sample was acidified to pH ~2.5 with formic acid and loaded onto a C8-silica column, eluting with an ethanol gradient (10–50%). Propeptide-containing fractions were identified by MALDI-TOF MS and pooled, then further purified on a C5-silica column under identical conditions. The final product was lyophilized and resuspended in 50 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.0). Mass spectrometry confirmed the expected mass (7,215 Da) after accounting for N-terminal pyroglutamate formation and O-glycosylation at Thr48. This purified propeptide was used in GLAD inhibition assays at molar ratios up to 1:64 (enzyme:propeptide).

### Example 2: Native PAGE

Native PAGE was performed to assess the oligomeric state and activity of purified samples without denaturation. Samples (10 μg each of RmL WT, ProRmL, and ProRmL-del) were mixed with native loading dye (no SDS or reducing agents) and loaded onto a 12% acrylamide gel with Tris-glycine buffer system. Electrophoresis was run at 100 V for 2 hours at 4°C to minimize heating-induced conformational changes. The gel was split: one half was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue to visualize protein bands, showing a single band for RmL WT (~27 kDa), a higher molecular weight band for ProRmL (~42 kDa, indicating propeptide-mature complex), and a similar band for ProRmL-del (confirming stability). The other half was used for zymogram analysis (see Example 4), which demonstrated that only RmL WT exhibited lipase activity, while ProRmL and ProRmL-del showed no hydrolysis halos, confirming propeptide-mediated inhibition.

### Example 3: Temperature Stability Assay (TSA)

Thermal stability was evaluated by differential scanning fluorimetry using SYPRO Orange dye, which fluoresces upon binding to exposed hydrophobic patches of unfolding proteins. Samples (5 μM) were mixed with 5X SYPRO Orange in 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) and heated from 25°C to 95°C at 1°C/min in a real-time PCR machine with fluorescence detection (excitation 470 nm, emission 570 nm). The melting temperature (Tm) was defined as the peak of the first derivative of the fluorescence curve. RmL WT exhibited a Tm of 68°C, consistent with its known thermostability. ProRmL and ProRmL-del showed slightly lower Tm values (65°C and 66°C, respectively), suggesting that propeptide binding imposes minor conformational constraints but does not destabilize the overall fold. These results indicate that the inhibitory complex is thermodynamically stable under physiological conditions.

### Example 4: Zymogram Analysis

Zymogram analysis was conducted to directly visualize lipase activity on a native gel. After native PAGE (Example 2), the unstained gel half was placed on an agarose plate containing 4% Litex HSB agarose, 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 0.625% polyvinyl alcohol, 1.9% olive oil, and 0.05% brilliant green dye. The plate was incubated at 37°C for 2 hours to allow enzyme renaturation and substrate hydrolysis. Active lipase zones appeared as clear halos against the green-stained background due to olive oil degradation. RmL WT produced a prominent halo, confirming activity. In contrast, ProRmL and ProRmL-del showed no halos, demonstrating complete inhibition by the propeptide. This assay provided direct visual evidence that propeptide binding abolishes interfacial activation, consistent with structural data showing lid occlusion.

### Example 5: Decylamine Agarose Chromatography

Decylamine agarose chromatography was used to separate active and latent lipase forms based on hydrophobicity. Clarified A. oryzae broth containing a mixture of RmL WT and ProRmL was loaded onto a decylamine column equilibrated with 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.0) + 1 M NaCl. ProRmL, with its hydrophobic substrate interface masked by the propeptide, did not bind and was collected in the flow-through. RmL WT, exposing its hydrophobic lid and substrate-binding residues, bound tightly and was eluted with water. SDS-PAGE of fractions confirmed separation: flow-through contained ProRmL (and free propeptide), while eluate contained pure RmL WT. This method proved highly effective for isolating the inhibitory complex and is scalable for industrial purification of latent lipase formulations.

### Example 6: Relative Wash Performance

The functional impact of propeptide inhibition was tested in a simulated laundry wash performance assay using the GLAD method. RmL WT (0.1 μM) was incubated with or without purified propeptide (6.4 μM, 1:64 ratio) in wash buffer (0.2 M HEPES, pH 8.5, 1 mM CaCl2, 10 ppm Triton X-100) containing Avicel fibers coated with olive oil and 4-methylumbelliferyl oleate. Fluorescence (excitation 365 nm, emission 445 nm) was measured every 30 seconds for 6 minutes. RmL WT alone showed rapid, linear increase in fluorescence, indicating high activity. In the presence of propeptide, fluorescence remained near baseline, demonstrating >95% inhibition. This result confirms that the propeptide effectively suppresses lipase activity under industrially relevant conditions, supporting its use in detergent formulations where controlled activation is desired.
