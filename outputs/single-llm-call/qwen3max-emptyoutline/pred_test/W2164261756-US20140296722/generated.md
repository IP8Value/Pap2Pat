## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Electrocardiography remains a cornerstone of cardiovascular diagnostics, providing clinicians with critical information regarding cardiac rhythm, conduction abnormalities, ischemia, infarction, and other electrophysiological disturbances. Modern electrocardiogram (ECG) machines universally employ analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) to transform the continuous analog voltage signals generated by the heart into discrete digital data streams suitable for computational analysis, archival storage, transmission, and display. While this digitization paradigm has significantly enhanced the efficiency and accessibility of ECG interpretation, it has simultaneously introduced a substantial barrier to interoperability among devices manufactured by competing entities. Specifically, each manufacturer typically employs proprietary digital formats that encode not only the raw voltage-time data but also metadata, calibration parameters, filtering characteristics, and interpretive algorithm outputs in ways that are neither standardized nor openly documented. Consequently, once an ECG is digitized on a particular manufacturer’s machine, the resulting digital file is effectively “locked” into that ecosystem, precluding straightforward reanalysis by alternative interpretive algorithms hosted on hardware from other vendors.

This limitation poses a clinically significant challenge, particularly in cases involving complex or ambiguous 12-lead ECG tracings where the automated diagnostic output from a single machine may be unreliable or contested. Although numerous studies have demonstrated that different manufacturers’ interpretive algorithms exhibit varying sensitivities and specificities for common electrocardiographic pathologies—such as myocardial infarction, bundle branch blocks, and repolarization abnormalities—clinicians currently lack a practical mechanism to obtain concurrent second opinions from multiple algorithms without physically repeating the ECG acquisition using different machines, an approach that is both time-consuming and physiologically inconsistent due to inevitable beat-to-beat variability. Historically, efforts to establish universal digital interchange standards for ECG data, such as those initiated by Bailey et al. in the 1970s and Willems et al. in the 1990s, have failed to achieve widespread clinical adoption, largely due to commercial disincentives among manufacturers who view their proprietary algorithms and data formats as competitive differentiators. As a result, the vision of a truly interoperable, multi-algorithm ECG interpretation platform remains unrealized.

An alternative strategy, pursued decades ago by Miyahara et al., involved the laborious regeneration of analog ECG signals from stored digital data using magnetic tape playback systems and custom-built signal generators, which were then fed into multiple interpretive ECG machines to solicit comparative diagnoses. While conceptually sound, this analog reconstruction method was technologically obsolete even at the time of its publication, requiring extensive manual intervention, suffering from signal degradation, and relying on outdated hardware incompatible with contemporary clinical workflows. More recently, a commercially available system—LifeSync®—has implemented a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) approach, but its utility is severely constrained: it functions only as a closed-loop system wherein the DAC accepts digital input exclusively from its paired LifeSync ADC, thereby failing to address the core problem of cross-manufacturer interoperability. Moreover, the LifeSync system is designed primarily to enhance patient mobility during continuous monitoring rather than to facilitate diagnostic reinterpretation, and it does not support remote data transmission or integration with third-party ECG hardware.

The fundamental technical obstacle underlying these limitations lies in the mathematical structure of standard 12-lead ECG acquisition. A conventional 12-lead ECG is derived from ten physical electrodes placed on the body: four limb electrodes (right arm, left arm, left leg, and right leg serving as a reference/ground) and six precordial (chest) electrodes. However, due to the principle of Wilson’s Central Terminal (WCT)—a virtual reference point defined as the average potential of the three limb electrodes (right arm, left arm, left leg)—the actual independent analog signals acquired are typically reduced to eight channels: leads I and II (derived from limb electrode differentials) and leads V1 through V6 (each referenced to WCT). The remaining four leads (III, aVR, aVL, aVF) are mathematically computed from these eight. When digitizing, most ECG machines store only these eight independent channels to minimize data volume. The challenge for any universal analog reconstruction system is therefore to reverse this process: starting from these eight digitally encoded channels, regenerate analog voltage waveforms that, when presented to any receiving ECG machine, will be interpreted by that machine’s internal circuitry as if they originated from ten physical electrodes connected to a real patient. Critically, the reconstructed analog signals must be referenced in such a way that the receiving machine’s own WCT computation reproduces the original lead derivations accurately, without requiring knowledge of or access to the receiving machine’s internal digital architecture.

Prior art in ECG simulation and signal generation has not addressed this precise problem. ECG simulators used for device testing typically generate synthetic waveforms based on mathematical models or stored templates, but they do not reconstruct patient-specific analog signals from arbitrary digital ECG files of known format. Similarly, research-grade DAC systems used in electrophysiology laboratories are generally custom-built for specific experimental protocols and lack the robustness, universality, and clinical validation required for routine diagnostic use. There exists, therefore, a long-felt but unmet need for a digital-to-analog conversion system that is format-agnostic (subject only to knowledge of the source digital schema), hardware-independent, capable of high-fidelity analog signal reproduction, and deployable in both local and remote clinical or research settings—including resource-constrained environments such as space missions, military field hospitals, offshore platforms, and remote expeditions—where access to multiple ECG interpretation platforms is otherwise impractical.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel digital-to-analog conversion (DAC) system and associated methodology for reconstructing high-fidelity analog electrocardiographic signals from digital 12-lead ECG data files or streams of known format, thereby enabling the regenerated analog signals to be re-digitized by any standard 12-lead ECG machine regardless of manufacturer, model, or proprietary data architecture. This system overcomes the longstanding interoperability barrier in clinical electrocardiography by circumventing the need for direct digital access to proprietary interpretive algorithms, instead leveraging the universal analog input interface that all ECG machines inherently possess. The invention achieves this through a combination of a specifically engineered open digital data format, a hardware-configured DAC unit with precisely defined electrode referencing constraints, and software-based preprocessing routines that transform arbitrary source digital ECG data into the optimized format compatible with the DAC.

In accordance with the invention, the core methodological insight is that accurate analog reconstruction of a 12-lead ECG requires the DAC to output analog voltages corresponding to at least nine independent electrode channels (excluding the right leg ground) in a manner that mimics the electrical potentials that would have been measured at the ten physical electrode sites on a patient during the original recording. To accomplish this without knowledge of the receiving ECG machine’s internal signal processing, the invention exploits a specific referencing scheme wherein all chest lead data in the optimized digital format are referenced to the right arm electrode potential rather than to Wilson’s Central Terminal (WCT). Concurrently, the DAC hardware is configured such that the right arm electrode input channel is held at a fixed zero-voltage reference (i.e., ER = 0). Under these conditions, the analog voltages outputted by the DAC for the left arm, left leg, and each of the six chest electrodes correspond directly to the original limb lead differentials (I and II) and precordial leads (V1–V6) when subsequently processed by the receiving ECG machine’s standard WCT-based lead derivation logic. This configuration ensures that the reconstructed analog signals, upon re-digitization, yield ECG waveforms and automated diagnostic interpretations that are functionally equivalent to those derived from the original patient recording.

The invention further comprises an optimized open digital file format, detailed in Appendix S1 of the accompanying disclosure, which specifies the precise data structure, sampling rate, bit depth, channel ordering, and referencing convention required for input into the DAC. Any digital ECG data collected on any manufacturer’s machine—provided the format is known—can be converted into this optimized format via software preprocessing, which performs the necessary mathematical transformations, resampling, and metadata alignment. This conversion step is essential to ensure compatibility with the DAC’s fixed referencing and channel mapping logic. The DAC hardware itself, as described in Appendix S2, consists of a multi-channel digital-to-analog converter circuit, precision voltage references, signal conditioning amplifiers, and electrode output terminals configured to interface directly with the input leads of any standard 12-lead ECG machine. The system supports both local operation—where the DAC is physically connected to the receiving ECG machine—and remote operation—where the digital data is transmitted over a network to a remotely located DAC unit, which then feeds the analog signals into a distant ECG device.

Validation studies conducted as part of the invention’s development demonstrate that the system reproduces original ECG waveforms with high fidelity, as evidenced by root mean square (RMS) voltage differences of less than 30 microvolts between original and reconstructed signals across a range of normal and pathological cardiac conditions, including bundle branch blocks. Moreover, automated diagnostic interpretations generated by both native and third-party algorithms (including the well-validated Leuven program) showed near-perfect concordance between original and reconstructed ECGs, with only one minor discrepancy observed across ten test cases—a finding deemed clinically insignificant. These results confirm that the invention meets the stringent accuracy requirements necessary for clinical diagnostic use.

The invention thus enables a multitude of novel clinical and research applications. First, it facilitates rapid, automated second (or third, or nth) opinions on difficult-to-interpret ECGs by allowing a single digital recording to be simultaneously or sequentially analyzed by multiple manufacturers’ interpretive algorithms via analog re-digitization. Second, it permits the use of low-cost, lightweight, or simplified ECG front-end hardware in resource-limited or remote settings (e.g., spaceflight, polar expeditions, rural clinics), with the assurance that the data can later be reprocessed on high-end, feature-rich ECG machines for definitive interpretation. Third, it provides ECG manufacturers with a standardized method to test and improve their algorithms against a common set of challenging cases, thereby accelerating the development of more accurate and robust diagnostic software. Fourth, it enhances consistency in multi-institutional research studies and epidemiological databases by enabling uniform reanalysis of legacy ECG data across different hardware platforms. Finally, by demonstrating the feasibility and clinical value of cross-manufacturer ECG reinterpretation, the invention may serve as a catalyst for greater industry collaboration toward future purely digital interoperability standards.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

The present invention is directed to a digital-to-analog conversion system for reconstructing analog 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) signals from digital ECG data of known format, enabling the regenerated analog signals to be re-digitized and interpreted by any standard 12-lead ECG machine irrespective of manufacturer or proprietary data schema. The system comprises three integrated components: (1) an optimized open digital file format; (2) a software-based digital data preprocessing and conversion module; and (3) a hardware-configured multi-channel digital-to-analog converter (DAC) unit with specific electrode referencing constraints. Each of these components is described in detail below, along with the underlying methodological principles, operational procedures, and validation results that establish the system’s clinical utility and technical robustness.

The foundational insight of the invention resides in the recognition that the standard 12-lead ECG, though derived from ten physical electrodes, is mathematically reducible to eight independent analog channels due to the use of Wilson’s Central Terminal (WCT) as a common reference for the precordial leads. WCT is defined as the average of the potentials at the right arm (ER), left arm (EL), and left leg (EF) electrodes: WCT = (ER + EL + EF)/3. The standard limb leads are then derived as follows: Lead I = EL − ER; Lead II = EF − ER; Lead III = EF − EL (though Lead III is redundant given Leads I and II). The augmented limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF) are computed as weighted combinations of the limb electrode potentials relative to WCT. The six precordial leads (V1–V6) are measured as the potential difference between each chest electrode (EC1–EC6) and WCT. Consequently, when digitizing, most ECG machines store only eight channels: Leads I, II, and V1–V6, from which all other leads can be algebraically reconstructed.

The central problem addressed by the invention is how to reverse this process: given these eight digitally encoded channels, how can one generate analog voltages that, when connected to the input terminals of any receiving ECG machine, will cause that machine to compute the same twelve leads as in the original recording? The key lies in decoupling the reconstructed analog signals from WCT during the DAC output phase, because the receiving machine will itself perform the WCT computation. If the DAC were to output signals already referenced to WCT, the receiving machine’s subsequent re-referencing would introduce errors. Therefore, the DAC must output absolute electrode potentials (relative to a common ground) that correspond to what would have been measured at each of the ten electrode sites during the original recording.

To achieve this, the invention adopts a specific referencing convention in its optimized digital format: all chest lead data (CR1–CR6) are stored as potentials referenced to the right arm electrode (ER), rather than to WCT. That is, CRi = ECi − ER for i = 1 to 6. Additionally, the limb lead data are stored as Leads I and II, which are themselves differentials: I = EL − ER and II = EF − ER. From these, the absolute potentials of the left arm and left leg electrodes relative to the right arm can be expressed as EL = I + ER and EF = II + ER. The invention then imposes a critical constraint on the DAC hardware: the right arm electrode output channel is fixed at zero volts (ER = 0). Under this condition, the absolute electrode potentials become EL = I, EF = II, and ECi = CRi for i = 1 to 6. The right leg electrode, serving as the common ground/reference in standard ECG practice, is also held at zero volts in the DAC output.

When these analog voltages—EL = I, EF = II, EC1 = CR1, ..., EC6 = CR6, ER = 0, and right leg = 0—are connected to the corresponding input terminals of any standard 12-lead ECG machine, that machine will compute its own WCT as (ER + EL + EF)/3 = (0 + I + II)/3. It will then derive Lead I as EL − ER = I − 0 = I, Lead II as EF − ER = II − 0 = II, and each precordial lead Vi as ECi − WCT = CRi − (I + II)/3. However, in the original recording, Vi was defined as ECi − WCT_original, where WCT_original = (ER_original + EL_original + EF_original)/3. For the reconstructed Vi to match the original Vi, it must hold that CRi − (I + II)/3 = ECi_original − WCT_original. Substituting CRi = ECi_original − ER_original and I = EL_original − ER_original, II = EF_original − ER_original, one finds that the equality holds identically. Thus, the reconstructed leads are mathematically identical to the original leads, provided the DAC enforces ER = 0 and the digital data are formatted with chest leads referenced to ER.

This referencing scheme is not the only possible solution; algebraically equivalent configurations could be constructed by referencing all electrodes to the left arm or left leg and setting that electrode to zero in the DAC. However, the right-arm-zeroed configuration was selected as the preferred embodiment due to historical precedence (it aligns with Einthoven’s original lead system), electrical stability (the right arm is commonly used as a reference in many ECG systems), and practical implementation considerations in modern ECG hardware design.

The optimized digital file format, specified in Appendix S1, defines the exact structure required for input into the DAC. It mandates a minimum sampling rate of 500 samples per second per channel (though higher rates such as 1000 Hz are supported), a bit depth of at least 16 bits per sample to ensure sufficient voltage resolution, and a channel order comprising nine channels: Channel 1 = Lead I, Channel 2 = Lead II, Channels 3–8 = CR1–CR6 (chest leads referenced to right arm), and Channel 9 = a placeholder or auxiliary channel (optional). Metadata fields include patient identifiers, recording timestamp, calibration markers, and gain settings, all encoded in a human-readable header. This format is deliberately open and non-proprietary, facilitating easy adoption and integration.

The software preprocessing module performs the essential task of converting digital ECG data from any known source format into this optimized format. This involves several steps: (1) parsing the source file to extract raw voltage data and metadata; (2) identifying the original referencing scheme (e.g., whether chest leads were stored as Vi referenced to WCT or as CRi referenced to ER); (3) if necessary, performing mathematical transformations to re-reference the chest leads to the right arm using the relationships Vi = CRi − (I + II)/3 and CRi = Vi + (I + II)/3; (4) resampling the data to the required sampling rate using high-quality interpolation filters to avoid aliasing; (5) scaling the voltage values to match the DAC’s expected input range; and (6) packaging the transformed data into the optimized file structure. This module can be implemented as a standalone application, a cloud-based web service, or an integrated component within ECG management software.

The DAC hardware unit, detailed in Appendix S2, consists of a high-precision, multi-channel digital-to-analog converter chip (e.g., Texas Instruments DAC8565 or equivalent), capable of simultaneous or near-simultaneous output on at least nine channels. Each output channel is buffered by a low-noise operational amplifier to drive the capacitive load of ECG lead wires without signal degradation. The right arm channel is hardwired to a precision zero-volt reference, while the other channels receive the processed digital data stream via a USB, Ethernet, or wireless interface. The unit includes safety isolation circuitry to meet medical electrical equipment standards (e.g., IEC 60601-1) and connector interfaces compatible with standard ECG patient cables (e.g., DIN 42802 or equivalent). In operation, the DAC receives the optimized digital file, converts each sample to an analog voltage, and outputs the nine electrode signals in real time or at an accelerated rate, depending on the application.

Validation of the system was performed using ten 12-lead ECG recordings (five from healthy subjects, five from patients with various cardiac pathologies) originally acquired on a Cardiax PC-ECG device. The digital files were converted to the optimized format, processed through the DAC, and re-digitized using either the same Cardiax device or a CorScience BT12 device. Quantitative analysis revealed RMS voltage differences of 20.8 ± 0.12 µV when using the same ADC for re-digitization and 28.4 ± 0.21 µV when using a different ADC—well within the noise floor of clinical ECG interpretation (typically >50 µV). Qualitative analysis of automated diagnostic statements showed complete concordance in nine out of ten cases, with only one minor discrepancy in repolarization assessment deemed clinically irrelevant. Further testing with next-generation ADCs employing true simultaneous sampling (e.g., Texas Instruments ADS1298) demonstrated a two- to three-fold reduction in reconstruction error, confirming that the system’s fidelity is ultimately limited only by the quality of the receiving ECG machine’s hardware.

In summary, the preferred embodiment of the invention provides a robust, universal, and clinically validated solution for cross-manufacturer ECG reinterpretation via analog signal reconstruction. By combining an open digital format, flexible software conversion, and purpose-built DAC hardware with a mathematically sound referencing scheme, the system enables unprecedented interoperability in electrocardiography, with broad implications for clinical diagnostics, remote healthcare, algorithm development, and biomedical research.
