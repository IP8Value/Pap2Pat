# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of regenerative medicine and, more specifically, to methods for detecting residual undifferentiated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) in differentiated cell populations derived from iPSCs. The invention provides a highly sensitive and robust detection system based on the expression of specific marker genes—particularly ESRG (Embryonic Stem Cell Related) and CNMD (Chondromodulin, also known as LECT1)—that are uniquely and abundantly expressed in undifferentiated iPSCs but are rapidly and substantially downregulated upon differentiation into any of the three embryonic germ layers: endoderm, mesoderm, or ectoderm. The invention is particularly useful in ensuring the safety of iPSC-derived cell products intended for clinical transplantation by enabling the identification and quantification of contaminating undifferentiated cells that pose a risk of teratoma formation. This method is applicable across a wide range of differentiation protocols and cell lineages, offering a universal, reliable, and scalable solution for quality control in the manufacture of cellular therapeutics. The invention further encompasses diagnostic kits, analytical platforms, and validation protocols incorporating these markers for use in Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant production environments.

## BACKGROUND ART

The advent of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology has revolutionized the landscape of regenerative medicine by enabling the generation of patient-specific pluripotent cells capable of differentiating into virtually any cell type in the human body. Since the initial demonstration by Yamanaka and colleagues that somatic cells can be reprogrammed into a pluripotent state through the introduction of defined transcription factors, iPSCs have been heralded as a promising source for autologous and allogeneic cell therapies. These cells offer the potential to treat a wide array of degenerative diseases, including Parkinson’s disease, myocardial infarction, diabetes, and liver failure, by replacing damaged or lost tissues with functional, differentiated derivatives. However, the clinical translation of iPSC-based therapies is critically dependent on the ability to ensure both the functional maturity and the safety of the final cell products. Among the most significant safety concerns is the risk of teratoma formation due to the inadvertent transplantation of residual undifferentiated iPSCs that retain their pluripotent capacity and proliferative potential.

Teratomas are benign tumors composed of tissues derived from all three germ layers and are a hallmark of pluripotent cell contamination. Even minute quantities of undifferentiated iPSCs—on the order of 0.001% or fewer—can give rise to teratomas in vivo, thereby posing a serious barrier to regulatory approval and clinical adoption. Consequently, extensive efforts have been directed toward developing strategies to either eliminate or reliably detect residual undifferentiated cells in differentiated populations. Early approaches focused on improving differentiation protocols to achieve near-complete conversion of iPSCs into target lineages, but complete elimination remains technically challenging due to inherent heterogeneity in differentiation efficiency and the persistence of small subpopulations of undifferentiated cells. Alternative strategies have included the use of suicide genes, cytotoxic antibodies, metabolic selection, or lectin-based sorting to selectively remove undifferentiated cells. While effective in some contexts, these methods often require genetic modification, introduce additional reagents that may complicate regulatory pathways, or are lineage-specific and not universally applicable.

Concurrently, significant attention has been devoted to the development of detection methods that can sensitively identify residual undifferentiated iPSCs without altering the cell product. A widely adopted approach involves the use of molecular markers that are specifically expressed in pluripotent cells but silenced upon differentiation. Canonical pluripotency markers such as OCT4 (POU5F1), SOX2, NANOG, and LIN28A have been extensively used for this purpose. For instance, LIN28A was previously reported as a reliable marker for detecting residual undifferentiated iPSCs in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells derived from iPSCs and has even been implemented in clinical-grade manufacturing processes. However, emerging evidence suggests that the utility of these traditional markers is highly context-dependent. Their expression may persist in certain differentiated lineages due to physiological roles unrelated to pluripotency, leading to false-positive signals and reduced specificity. For example, LIN28A is known to play roles in metabolism, RNA processing, and tissue regeneration, and its expression has been observed in various somatic cell types, including hepatocytes during development. This undermines its reliability as a universal marker for undifferentiated cell detection.

Moreover, conventional detection methods such as quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) face practical limitations when applied to large-scale cell products. The signal from a few residual undifferentiated cells can be diluted below the detection threshold by the overwhelming background of differentiated cells, especially when only a small fraction of the total product is sampled for analysis. Although techniques such as digital PCR and single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) offer enhanced sensitivity, their integration into routine quality control workflows remains challenging due to cost, complexity, or lack of standardized protocols. Additionally, many existing detection strategies are optimized for specific differentiation protocols—such as those yielding cardiomyocytes or dopaminergic neurons—and do not generalize well across diverse lineages. This necessitates the development of a versatile, high-sensitivity detection system that is independent of the target cell type and compatible with a broad spectrum of iPSC-derived products.

Despite these advances, there remains a critical unmet need for a robust, universal, and highly sensitive method to detect residual undifferentiated iPSCs in differentiated cell populations derived from all three germ layers. Such a method must exhibit high specificity for the undifferentiated state, demonstrate rapid and complete downregulation upon differentiation, and enable detection at contamination levels well below the teratoma-forming threshold. Furthermore, it should be amenable to standard analytical platforms used in biomanufacturing settings and not require modifications to existing differentiation protocols. The present invention addresses these challenges by identifying novel marker genes—ESRG and CNMD—that fulfill these stringent criteria and provide a reliable foundation for the safety validation of iPSC-derived therapeutics.

### PRIOR ART LITERATURE

Several prior art references have attempted to address the problem of residual undifferentiated iPSC detection, though with notable limitations. Tano et al. (2016) described a “re-seeding method” wherein differentiated cells are re-cultured under pluripotency-maintaining conditions to allow any residual undifferentiated cells to form visible colonies, which can then be quantified via immunostaining for pluripotency markers such as SOX2 or TRA-1-60. While this method provides a functional readout of pluripotency, it is time-consuming (requiring at least seven days of culture), labor-intensive, and lacks the sensitivity required for detecting very low levels of contamination (<0.01%). Moreover, it is not suitable for real-time quality control in manufacturing pipelines.

Other studies have explored the use of surface markers for fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) or magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) to purify differentiated populations. For example, U.S. Patent Application No. 2015/0017132 A1 discloses the use of anti-SSEA-5 antibodies to deplete undifferentiated human pluripotent stem cells from differentiated cultures. Similarly, the rBC2LCN lectin, which binds to specific glycans on undifferentiated cells, has been used for detection and removal (Tateno et al., 2013). However, these approaches rely on the consistent expression of surface epitopes, which can vary between iPSC lines and differentiation protocols, and may not be applicable to all cell types due to shared antigen expression.

Molecular detection methods based on gene expression have also been pursued. Watanabe et al. (2017) reported that LIN28A could serve as a sensitive marker for residual undifferentiated iPSCs in RPE cells, with a detection limit of approximately 0.01%. This finding led to its adoption in clinical trials for age-related macular degeneration. However, as demonstrated in the present invention, LIN28A is not universally applicable; its expression persists in hepatic and early neuronal lineages, rendering it unsuitable for those contexts. Similarly, while OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG are core pluripotency factors, their expression can be transiently reactivated in certain differentiated cells or maintained at low levels in progenitor populations, reducing their specificity. Furthermore, qRT-PCR assays for these genes typically exhibit detection limits no better than 0.1–1%, which is insufficient for ensuring teratoma-free transplants.

Alternative strategies include the use of microRNA signatures, such as the miR-302 cluster, which is highly enriched in pluripotent cells. However, microRNA-based assays are less standardized and more prone to technical variability than mRNA-based methods. Metabolic selection approaches, such as methionine restriction, exploit the differential dependence of undifferentiated and differentiated cells on specific amino acids, but these methods alter culture conditions and may affect the viability or function of the desired cell product.

In summary, while prior art provides a range of tools for detecting or eliminating undifferentiated iPSCs, none offer a combination of universality across germ layers, high sensitivity (<0.01%), rapid turnaround, and compatibility with standard manufacturing practices. The present invention overcomes these deficiencies by identifying ESRG and CNMD as novel, highly specific markers that are rapidly silenced upon differentiation into any lineage and enable detection at unprecedented sensitivity using conventional and advanced molecular techniques.

## DISCLOSURE OF THE INVENTION

### Problem for Solution by the Invention

The primary problem addressed by the present invention is the lack of a universal, highly sensitive, and reliable method for detecting residual undifferentiated induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) in differentiated cell populations intended for clinical transplantation. Current detection strategies suffer from several critical limitations that impede their broad applicability and effectiveness in ensuring the safety of iPSC-derived therapeutics. First, many established pluripotency markers—such as LIN28A, OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG—exhibit lineage-specific expression patterns that persist in certain differentiated cell types, leading to false-positive signals and reduced specificity. For example, LIN28A is expressed during normal liver development and in early neuronal progenitors, making it unsuitable for detecting undifferentiated contamination in hepatic or neural cell products. Second, conventional detection methods like qRT-PCR lack the sensitivity required to identify very low levels of contamination (e.g., below 0.01%), which is necessary because even a few undifferentiated cells can initiate teratoma formation in vivo. Third, functional assays such as the re-seeding method, while definitive, are too slow and labor-intensive for integration into real-time quality control workflows in Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) environments. Fourth, many existing approaches are optimized for specific differentiation protocols and cannot be readily generalized across diverse cell lineages derived from endoderm, mesoderm, or ectoderm. This necessitates the development of a new detection paradigm that is both universal in scope and exquisitely sensitive in performance. The present invention solves this problem by identifying novel marker genes—ESRG and CNMD—that are uniquely and abundantly expressed in undifferentiated iPSCs but are rapidly and completely downregulated upon differentiation into any of the three germ layers, thereby enabling accurate, high-sensitivity detection of residual undifferentiated cells regardless of the target cell type or differentiation protocol used.

### Means to Solve the Problem

The present invention solves the aforementioned problem by providing a method for detecting residual undifferentiated iPSCs in differentiated cell populations through the measurement of the expression levels of one or more specific marker genes, namely ESRG (Embryonic Stem Cell Related) and CNMD (Chondromodulin, also known as LECT1). These genes were identified through comprehensive analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data from iPSC differentiation trajectories, followed by rigorous validation across multiple iPSC lines and germ layer lineages. The method comprises obtaining a sample of differentiated cells derived from iPSCs, extracting RNA or protein from the sample, and quantifying the expression of ESRG and/or CNMD using a sensitive detection technique such as quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH), or immunoassay. The expression level of the marker gene(s) is then compared to a predetermined threshold value that corresponds to a safe level of undifferentiated cell contamination—typically less than 0.01%—to determine whether the cell product is suitable for clinical use. In a preferred embodiment, the method utilizes ESRG due to its exceptionally high expression in undifferentiated iPSCs and its rapid and complete silencing upon differentiation into endodermal (e.g., hepatocytes, pancreatic cells), mesodermal (e.g., endothelial cells, mesenchymal cells), or ectodermal (e.g., neural stem cells, neural crest cells) lineages. The invention further encompasses the use of these markers in combination with other analytical platforms, such as flow cytometry following smFISH, to enable single-cell resolution detection and quantification of residual undifferentiated cells. Additionally, the invention provides diagnostic kits containing primers, probes, antibodies, or nucleic acid probes specific for ESRG and/or CNMD, along with standardized protocols for their use in quality control testing of iPSC-derived cell products. The method is designed to be compatible with existing differentiation protocols and does not require genetic modification or alteration of culture conditions, thereby facilitating seamless integration into current manufacturing workflows.

### Effect of the Invention

The present invention provides a transformative advance in the safety validation of iPSC-derived cell products by enabling the highly sensitive, specific, and universal detection of residual undifferentiated cells. By leveraging the unique expression profile of ESRG and CNMD—genes that are exclusively and abundantly expressed in undifferentiated iPSCs but rapidly silenced upon differentiation into any of the three germ layers—the invention achieves a detection sensitivity of at least 0.005% for ESRG, which is an order of magnitude superior to conventional markers such as OCT4, SOX2, or NANOG. This level of sensitivity ensures that even trace amounts of undifferentiated cells, which could otherwise lead to teratoma formation, are reliably identified before clinical administration. Furthermore, the universality of these markers across diverse lineages—including hepatic, pancreatic, endothelial, mesenchymal, and neural cells—eliminates the need for lineage-specific validation and streamlines the development of standardized quality control protocols for a wide range of regenerative therapies. The method is also rapid, cost-effective, and compatible with standard molecular biology techniques, making it suitable for implementation in GMP-compliant manufacturing facilities. Importantly, the invention does not require genetic modification or the addition of exogenous reagents, thereby preserving the integrity and regulatory acceptability of the cell product. In sum, the invention significantly enhances the safety profile of iPSC-based therapeutics, accelerates their path to clinical translation, and provides a robust foundation for the scalable and reliable production of cellular medicines.

## BEST MODES FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

### EXAMPLES

The following examples illustrate the best modes for carrying out the invention as presently contemplated by the inventors. These examples are provided for the purpose of illustration only and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention in any way.

**Example 1: Evaluation of LIN28A as a Marker for Residual Undifferentiated iPSCs in Hepatic Differentiation**

Human iPSCs (line TkDA3-4) were differentiated into hepatocytes through a stepwise protocol involving definitive endoderm (DE), hepatic endoderm (HE), immature hepatocyte (IH), and mature hepatocyte (MH) stages. RNA was extracted at each stage, and LIN28A expression was analyzed by qRT-PCR. Surprisingly, LIN28A expression remained high in HE and IH stages, comparable to levels in undifferentiated iPSCs. To determine whether this reflected physiological expression in hepatic lineages or contamination by undifferentiated cells, mouse liver development data were reanalyzed, revealing that Lin28a is naturally expressed during embryonic liver development. Additionally, the re-seeding method was applied: HE cells were re-cultured under pluripotency conditions, and no SOX2-positive colonies were observed, confirming the absence of residual undifferentiated cells. These results demonstrated that LIN28A is not suitable as a universal marker for undifferentiated cell detection due to its lineage-specific expression.

**Example 2: Identification of Novel Marker Genes via Single-Cell RNA Sequencing**

Single-cell RNA sequencing data from iPSC differentiation into hepatocytes were analyzed to identify genes that are highly and specifically expressed in undifferentiated iPSCs but rapidly downregulated upon differentiation. Twelve candidate genes were selected based on criteria of high iPSC expression, specificity to the pluripotent state, and significant downregulation in HE cells. qRT-PCR validation confirmed that ESRG, CNMD, SFRP2, SOX2, THY1, USP44, VSNL1, and SPP1 met these criteria. Further testing across multiple iPSC lines (1231A3, 1383D2, 1383D6, Ff-I01, Ff-I01s04) confirmed consistent downregulation upon differentiation, ruling out clone-specific artifacts.

**Example 3: Correlation of Marker Gene Expression with Actual Residual Undifferentiated Cells**

To assess the correlation between gene expression and actual residual undifferentiated cell numbers, iPSCs with high passage numbers (>35) were used, as they exhibited increased residual undifferentiated cells after differentiation. HE cells derived from these lines were subjected to both qRT-PCR and the re-seeding assay. ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 showed strong positive correlations (r ≥ 0.6) with colony counts, whereas OCT4 and LIN28A did not. Spike-in experiments, where known percentages of undifferentiated iPSCs were mixed into HE cells, further confirmed that ESRG, CNMD, and SFRP2 exhibited excellent correlation (r ≥ 0.9) with spiked-in cell numbers, with detection limits of 0.005%, 0.025%, and 0.025%, respectively—far superior to conventional markers.

**Example 4: Validation Across All Three Germ Layers**

To test universality, iPSCs were differentiated into endodermal (pancreatic cells), mesodermal (endothelial cells, septum transversum mesenchyme), and ectodermal (neural stem cells, neural crest cells) lineages. qRT-PCR analysis showed that ESRG and CNMD were consistently downregulated in all lineages, while LIN28A remained expressed in ectodermal derivatives. The re-seeding assay confirmed the absence of residual undifferentiated cells in all cases. Additionally, a commercially available trilineage differentiation kit (STEMdiff) was used to generate representative cells from each germ layer, and ESRG and CNMD again demonstrated robust downregulation. smFISH combined with flow cytometry successfully detected ESRG-positive cells in mixed populations, confirming single-cell resolution capability.

**Example 5: Application in 3D Organoid Safety Validation**

The method was applied to validate the safety of all-iPSC-derived liver buds (iPSC-LB), which contain iPSC-derived hepatic endoderm, endothelial cells, and mesenchymal cells. RNA was extracted from whole organoids, and ESRG expression was measured by ddPCR. No detectable ESRG signal was observed above background, confirming the absence of residual undifferentiated cells. This demonstrates the utility of the invention in complex, multicellular therapeutic products.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The present invention has broad industrial applicability in the field of regenerative medicine and cellular therapeutics. It is particularly valuable for pharmaceutical companies, biotechnology firms, and academic medical centers engaged in the development and manufacturing of iPSC-derived cell products for clinical use. The method can be integrated into quality control workflows to ensure the safety of a wide range of cell therapies, including but not limited to hepatocytes for liver failure, cardiomyocytes for heart disease, dopaminergic neurons for Parkinson’s disease, pancreatic islet cells for diabetes, and retinal pigment epithelial cells for macular degeneration. The invention is also applicable to the production of 3D organoids and tissue-engineered constructs, where the presence of residual undifferentiated cells poses a significant safety risk. Diagnostic kits based on the invention can be commercialized for use in research laboratories and GMP facilities, providing standardized tools for residual cell detection. Furthermore, the method supports regulatory compliance by enabling manufacturers to meet stringent safety requirements set by agencies such as the FDA and EMA. Given the growing investment in iPSC-based therapies and the increasing number of clinical trials underway, the invention represents a critical enabler for the safe and scalable translation of regenerative medicine to the clinic.
