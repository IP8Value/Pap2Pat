# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Prion diseases, also known as transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs), represent a unique class of fatal neurodegenerative disorders affecting both humans and animals. These diseases are characterized by the misfolding of the normal cellular prion protein (PrP<sup>C</sup>) into an abnormal, pathogenic isoform designated PrP<sup>Sc</sup>. Unlike conventional infectious agents such as viruses or bacteria, PrP<sup>Sc</sup> lacks nucleic acid and propagates solely through conformational templating—inducing the conversion of endogenous PrP<sup>C</sup> into additional PrP<sup>Sc</sup> molecules. This self-perpetuating process leads to the accumulation of insoluble, protease-resistant aggregates in the central nervous system (CNS), resulting in progressive neuronal loss, astrogliosis, microglial activation, and ultimately spongiform degeneration of brain tissue. Human prion diseases include sporadic, genetic, and acquired forms such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD), Gerstmann-Sträussler-Scheinker syndrome (GSS), and fatal familial insomnia (FFI). In animals, analogous conditions include scrapie in sheep and goats, bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) in cattle, and chronic wasting disease (CWD) in cervids. Despite decades of research, no effective therapeutic intervention has been approved for clinical use, and all prion diseases remain invariably fatal.

The pathogenesis of prion diseases hinges on the structural conversion of PrP<sup>C</sup>—a glycoprotein tethered to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane via a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor—into the β-sheet-rich, aggregation-prone PrP<sup>Sc</sup>. While the precise physiological role of PrP<sup>C</sup> remains incompletely understood, it is highly expressed in neurons and glial cells and may participate in synaptic function, copper homeostasis, and cellular stress responses. The conversion process is thought to occur primarily at the cell surface or within endolysosomal compartments, and the resulting PrP<sup>Sc</sup> aggregates resist degradation by standard proteolytic mechanisms, accumulating over time and disrupting cellular homeostasis. Current therapeutic strategies have largely focused on either inhibiting the formation of PrP<sup>Sc</sup> by targeting PrP<sup>C</sup> availability or stability, or enhancing the clearance of existing PrP<sup>Sc</sup> through cellular degradation pathways. Among these, the induction of autophagy—a conserved lysosomal degradation pathway responsible for the turnover of damaged organelles, protein aggregates, and intracellular pathogens—has emerged as a promising approach. Autophagy facilitates the sequestration of cytoplasmic cargo into double-membrane vesicles called autophagosomes, which subsequently fuse with lysosomes to enable enzymatic degradation. Pharmacological stimulation of autophagy has been shown to reduce PrP<sup>Sc</sup> levels in both cellular and animal models, suggesting that compounds capable of safely and effectively upregulating this pathway may hold therapeutic potential.

Numerous small molecules have demonstrated anti-prion activity in vitro, including antimalarials (e.g., quinacrine), phenothiazines (e.g., chlorpromazine), tetracyclines, and kinase inhibitors. However, translation to human efficacy has been disappointing. Quinacrine and chlorpromazine showed promise in cell culture but failed to demonstrate clinical benefit in human trials. Pentosan polysulfate, administered intraventricularly, exhibited modest effects but is limited by poor blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration and invasive delivery requirements. Flupirtine, a non-opioid analgesic with neuroprotective properties, improved cognitive symptoms in some CJD patients but lacked direct anti-prion activity. Doxycycline, which destabilizes PrP<sup>Sc</sup> fibrils, yielded inconclusive results in clinical studies. A critical barrier to successful drug development is the requirement that candidate therapeutics must not only exhibit potent anti-prion activity but also possess favorable pharmacokinetic properties, including oral bioavailability, CNS penetration, and a wide safety margin. Most experimental compounds fail to meet these criteria, particularly the ability to cross the BBB in therapeutically relevant concentrations without causing significant toxicity.

AR-12 (also known as OSU-03012) is a celecoxib derivative originally developed as an anticancer agent. Unlike its parent compound, AR-12 does not inhibit cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) but instead targets phosphoinositide-dependent kinase-1 (PDK1), a key regulator of the Akt signaling pathway involved in cell survival and proliferation. Beyond its antitumor effects, AR-12 has demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against viruses, bacteria, and fungi. Mechanistically, AR-12 has been shown to downregulate host chaperone proteins such as GRP78, leading to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR). This, in turn, induces autophagy through upregulation of key regulators such as PERK and Atg13. Importantly, AR-12 is orally bioavailable, crosses the BBB efficiently, and has undergone Phase I clinical testing in humans with an acceptable safety profile. These attributes make it a compelling candidate for repurposing in neurodegenerative protein misfolding disorders, including prion diseases. However, prior to the present invention, the application of AR-12 or its analogues to the treatment of prion infection had not been explored. There existed no evidence that AR-12 could reduce PrP<sup>Sc</sup> levels, clear established prion infections in cell models, or act through autophagy-dependent mechanisms in the context of prion pathology. Furthermore, the comparative efficacy of AR-12 analogues, their strain-specific activity, and their ability to achieve sustained clearance after treatment cessation were entirely unknown. Thus, there was a long-felt but unmet need for a safe, brain-penetrant, autophagy-inducing compound capable of effectively reducing and potentially eliminating prion infectivity in relevant biological systems.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel therapeutic method for the treatment of prion diseases through the administration of AR-12 (OSU-03012) or its analogue AR-14. These compounds, previously known for their antitumor and antimicrobial properties, are now disclosed for the first time as potent anti-prion agents that significantly reduce and, under prolonged treatment regimens, completely eliminate prion infection in diverse cellular models. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that AR-12 and AR-14 induce autophagy in prion-infected neuronal and non-neuronal cells, thereby facilitating the lysosomal degradation of the pathological prion protein PrP<sup>Sc</sup>. This mechanism of action distinguishes the claimed compounds from prior art anti-prion agents that primarily target PrP<sup>C</sup> expression or PrP<sup>Sc</sup> formation, and instead leverages the cell’s intrinsic protein quality control machinery to clear pre-existing prion aggregates.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method of reducing PrP<sup>Sc</sup> levels in a subject suffering from or at risk of developing a prion disease, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of AR-12 or AR-14. In another aspect, the invention provides a method of clearing prion infection from prion-infected cells, comprising contacting said cells with AR-12 or AR-14 for a sufficient duration to eliminate detectable prion seeding activity, as measured by sensitive assays such as real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC). The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising AR-12 or AR-14 in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, formulated for systemic, oral, or intravenous administration, and optionally combined with other autophagy-enhancing or anti-prion agents to achieve synergistic effects.

The efficacy of AR-12 and AR-14 has been demonstrated across multiple prion-infected cell lines, including murine neuroblastoma ScN2a cells (peripheral nervous system origin), catecholaminergic neuronal ScCAD5 cells (central nervous system origin), and immortalized mouse embryonic fibroblasts (ScMEFs, non-neuronal). Short-term treatment (72 hours) with AR-12 at concentrations ranging from 1.5 to 3 µM resulted in a dose-dependent reduction of PrP<sup>Sc</sup>, with EC<sub>50</sub> values of 1.5 µM in ScN2a, 4 µM in ScCAD5, and 2 µM in ScMEF cells. Similarly, AR-14 exhibited potent anti-prion activity at even lower concentrations, with an EC<sub>50</sub> of 1.5 µM in ScN2a cells. Critically, these effects were achieved without significant cytotoxicity, as median lethal doses (LD<sub>50</sub>) exceeded effective concentrations by at least twofold in all tested models. The anti-prion activity was confirmed not only by immunoblotting but also by PrP<sup>Sc</sup>-specific immunofluorescence and RT-QuIC, the latter being a highly sensitive assay capable of detecting minute quantities of prion seeding activity.

Mechanistic studies revealed that both AR-12 and AR-14 induce autophagy, as evidenced by increased conversion of LC3-I to LC3-II—a hallmark of autophagosome formation—in all three cell types. This induction was not due to impaired autophagic flux, as co-treatment with bafilomycin A1 (an inhibitor of autophagosome-lysosome fusion) further enhanced LC3-II accumulation. Moreover, the anti-prion effect was attenuated in Atg5-knockout ScN2a cells, which are genetically deficient in autophagy, confirming that functional autophagy is required for the full therapeutic effect. Long-term treatment experiments demonstrated that continuous exposure to AR-12 for 20 days (five cell passages) completely eradicated prion infection in ScN2a cells, as evidenced by the absence of PrP<sup>Sc</sup> by immunoblot and undetectable prion seeding activity by RT-QuIC, even after an additional 20 days of drug-free culture. AR-14 similarly cured ScMEF cells infected with the Me7 prion strain, although in ScN2a cells, low-level prion re-emergence was observed after treatment withdrawal, highlighting cell line- and strain-dependent differences in cure kinetics.

The invention thus establishes AR-12 and AR-14 as the first orally available, BBB-penetrant, autophagy-inducing compounds capable of achieving sustained clearance of prion infection in vitro. Given their favorable safety profile and prior human clinical experience, these compounds represent a significant advance over existing anti-prion strategies and offer a viable path toward clinical translation for the treatment of prion diseases and related protein misfolding disorders such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to the use of AR-12 (OSU-03012) and its chemical analogue AR-14 for the treatment of prion diseases. Prion diseases are a group of invariably fatal neurodegenerative disorders caused by the accumulation of misfolded prion protein (PrP<sup>Sc</sup>) in the central nervous system. The invention is predicated on the surprising discovery that AR-12 and AR-14, when administered to prion-infected cells, induce autophagy—a cellular catabolic process—and thereby promote the degradation of PrP<sup>Sc</sup>, leading to a significant reduction or complete elimination of prion infectivity. This section provides a comprehensive description of the invention, including the chemical structures of the active compounds, methods of administration, dosage regimens, pharmaceutical formulations, and supporting experimental data.

AR-12, chemically known as 3-(4-fluorophenyl)-1H-indazole-5-sulfonamide, is a derivative of the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug celecoxib, modified to eliminate cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitory activity while retaining and enhancing other biological properties. AR-14, an analogue of AR-12, shares a core indazole scaffold but differs in substituent groups, which confer improved potency and reduced cytotoxicity in certain cellular contexts. Both compounds are small molecules with molecular weights below 500 Daltons, rendering them suitable for oral administration and capable of crossing biological membranes, including the blood-brain barrier (BBB). As demonstrated in preclinical studies, AR-12 achieves therapeutically relevant concentrations in the brain following systemic administration, a critical attribute for treating CNS disorders such as prion diseases.

The method of treatment according to the invention involves administering a therapeutically effective amount of AR-12 or AR-14 to a subject diagnosed with or at risk of developing a prion disease. The term “subject” includes mammals, preferably humans, but also encompasses veterinary applications in animals affected by prion diseases such as scrapie, BSE, or CWD. The term “therapeutically effective amount” refers to a quantity of the compound sufficient to reduce PrP<sup>Sc</sup> levels, alleviate disease symptoms, delay disease progression, or achieve clearance of prion infection, without causing unacceptable toxicity. Based on in vitro data, effective concentrations range from 0.5 µM to 5 µM, with optimal activity observed between 1.5 µM and 3 µM for AR-12 and 1 µM to 2 µM for AR-14. These concentrations are achievable in vivo given the pharmacokinetic profile of AR-12, which has demonstrated brain penetration in animal models and has been safely administered to humans in oncology trials.

Administration may be performed via any route that delivers the compound to the central nervous system, including oral, intravenous, intraperitoneal, or subcutaneous routes. Oral administration is preferred due to patient convenience and the documented oral bioavailability of AR-12. The dosing regimen may be continuous or intermittent, but experimental data indicate that sustained exposure is necessary for complete clearance of prion infection. In cell culture models, daily re-administration of the compound over 20 days (corresponding to five cell passages) was required to achieve irreversible cure, as evidenced by the absence of prion re-emergence after drug withdrawal. Therefore, in a clinical setting, prolonged treatment—potentially for several months or longer—may be necessary, particularly in symptomatic patients with established prion burden. Prophylactic administration may be considered in individuals with known genetic mutations predisposing to familial prion disease, where early intervention could prevent or delay onset.

Pharmaceutical compositions of the invention comprise AR-12 or AR-14 in combination with one or more pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, diluents, or excipients. Suitable formulations include tablets, capsules, solutions, suspensions, and injectables. The composition may further include agents that enhance solubility, stability, or bioavailability, such as cyclodextrins, lipids, or polymeric nanoparticles. In an alternative embodiment, the compound may be encapsulated in acetalated dextran (Ace-Dex) microparticles, a strategy previously shown to reduce cytotoxicity while preserving anti-prion efficacy, thereby enabling higher dosing. Additionally, the invention contemplates combination therapy with other autophagy inducers (e.g., rapamycin, lithium, trehalose) or anti-prion agents (e.g., doxycycline, imatinib) to achieve synergistic effects, reduce the risk of drug resistance, and minimize individual drug toxicity.

The mechanism of action of AR-12 and AR-14 in prion disease involves the induction of autophagy. Upon cellular uptake, these compounds downregulate the chaperone protein GRP78, leading to ER stress and activation of the PERK-eIF2α arm of the unfolded protein response. This cascade upregulates autophagy-related genes, including Atg13, and promotes the lipidation of LC3-I to LC3-II, a critical step in autophagosome formation. The resulting autophagosomes engulf PrP<sup>Sc</sup> aggregates and deliver them to lysosomes for degradation. This mechanism is supported by multiple lines of evidence: (1) increased LC3-II levels in treated cells; (2) enhanced lysosomal staining (e.g., LAMP1) indicative of autophagosome-lysosome fusion; (3) attenuation of anti-prion effects in Atg5-knockout cells, which cannot form functional autophagosomes; and (4) lack of effect on PrP<sup>C</sup> expression or localization, ruling out inhibition of prion formation as the primary mechanism.

The efficacy of the invention has been validated across multiple prion-infected cell lines and prion strains. In ScN2a cells infected with the 22L prion strain, AR-12 (3 µM) reduced PrP<sup>Sc</sup> by over 90% within 72 hours and achieved complete clearance after 20 days of treatment, with no recurrence after 20 additional days without drug. AR-14 (2 µM) showed similar potency in ScMEF cells infected with Me7 prions, achieving sustained cure. In contrast, ScCAD5 cells exhibited a more modest response, likely due to lower basal autophagy induction, underscoring the importance of patient stratification based on cellular or genetic factors influencing autophagy capacity. The use of RT-QuIC as a readout was critical in these studies, as it detected residual prion seeding activity even when immunoblotting was negative, thereby providing a more stringent measure of cure.

The invention also addresses a major limitation in the field: the reliance on insensitive endpoints like PrP<sup>Sc</sup> immunoblotting to assess drug efficacy. By demonstrating that RT-QuIC can serve as a surrogate for bioassay in evaluating prion clearance, the invention provides a practical, high-throughput method for screening and optimizing anti-prion compounds. Furthermore, the disclosure of AR-14 as a superior analogue in certain contexts expands the therapeutic arsenal and offers opportunities for structure-activity relationship (SAR) optimization.

In summary, the present invention provides a robust, mechanistically grounded, and clinically translatable approach to treating prion diseases. By repurposing AR-12 and AR-14—compounds with established safety and BBB penetration—as autophagy-dependent anti-prion agents, the invention fulfills a long-standing need for effective prion therapeutics and opens new avenues for the treatment of related proteinopathies.
