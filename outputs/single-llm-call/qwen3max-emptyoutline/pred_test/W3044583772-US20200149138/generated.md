# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### Technical Field

The present invention relates generally to the field of metallurgy and, more specifically, to low-carbon steel materials exhibiting enhanced surface mechanical properties without requiring post-solidification processing such as heat treatment, alloying, rolling, or surface modification. The invention provides a novel method for producing a low-carbon steel article having a hardened surface layer characterized by a unique microstructural composition comprising lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite, which collectively impart exceptional surface hardness and yield strength while preserving the ductility and toughness typical of low-carbon steels in the core region. This innovation enables the fabrication of high-performance structural components with improved wear resistance and load-bearing capacity directly from the casting process, thereby eliminating costly and time-consuming secondary operations traditionally required to achieve comparable surface properties.

### Description of the Related Art

Low-carbon steels, typically defined as steels containing less than 0.25 weight percent carbon, are widely employed in industrial applications due to their excellent formability, weldability, ductility, and impact toughness. However, these advantageous properties are often accompanied by relatively low strength and hardness, particularly at the surface, which limits their use in applications requiring resistance to wear, abrasion, or surface deformation. To overcome this limitation, various surface hardening techniques have been developed and implemented in industrial practice. These include carburizing, nitriding, induction hardening, laser surface hardening, and flame hardening, all of which aim to increase the surface carbon or nitrogen content or induce phase transformations that result in harder microstructures such as martensite.

Carburizing involves diffusing carbon into the surface of a low-carbon steel component at elevated temperatures (typically 900–950°C) followed by quenching to form a martensitic case. While effective, this process is energy-intensive, requires precise atmosphere control, and introduces dimensional changes that may necessitate post-treatment machining. Nitriding, which diffuses nitrogen into the surface to form hard nitrides, operates at lower temperatures (500–570°C) but still requires extended processing times and specialized equipment. Induction and laser hardening rely on rapid localized heating and self-quenching to transform surface austenite into martensite; however, these methods are limited to geometrically accessible surfaces and require significant capital investment in specialized machinery.

An alternative approach involves alloying with elements such as chromium, nickel, or molybdenum to enhance hardenability, allowing martensite formation during air or oil cooling. However, alloying increases raw material costs and may compromise weldability or corrosion resistance. Moreover, even with alloying, achieving a fully martensitic surface in ultra-low-carbon steels (e.g., <0.05% C) remains challenging due to the thermodynamic instability of martensite at such low carbon levels.

Recent research has explored the possibility of generating hardened surface layers through controlled solidification and cooling during casting itself, thereby avoiding post-processing altogether. For instance, rapid solidification techniques such as melt spinning or splat cooling can produce fine-grained or amorphous structures with enhanced hardness, but these methods are not scalable to bulk components. Directional solidification and chill casting have been used to refine grain structure near mold walls, but the resulting microstructures are typically ferritic or pearlitic, lacking the high hardness associated with martensite.

A critical challenge in achieving martensite in low-carbon steels lies in the carbon content requirement: conventional wisdom holds that martensite formation and its attendant hardness are strongly dependent on interstitial carbon atoms that distort the body-centered cubic (bcc) iron lattice into a body-centered tetragonal (bct) structure, thereby impeding dislocation motion. In steels with carbon content below 0.1 wt%, the resulting martensite is often described as “lath martensite” with relatively low hardness (typically <5 GPa), insufficient for demanding surface applications.

Furthermore, existing literature lacks a comprehensive understanding of how mixed microstructures—comprising martensite, bainite, and acicular ferrite—can coexist in a thin surface layer of low-carbon steel produced via sub-rapid solidification, and how the nanoscale mechanical properties of each constituent contribute to the overall surface performance. Prior art does not disclose a method for producing a low-carbon steel article wherein a hardened surface layer with nanohardness exceeding 9 GPa is achieved solely through controlled solidification and cooling, without any post-casting thermal or mechanical treatment.

Consequently, there exists a long-felt but unmet need in the art for a low-carbon steel material that combines the bulk toughness and ductility of conventional low-carbon steels with a surface layer exhibiting hardness and yield strength comparable to medium- or high-carbon martensitic steels, all achieved through a single-step casting process. The present invention addresses this need by leveraging sub-rapid solidification kinetics to induce carbon segregation and phase partitioning during cooling, resulting in a surface microstructure rich in ultrafine lath martensite with locally elevated carbon content, thereby achieving unprecedented hardness in an ultra-low-carbon matrix.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a low-carbon steel article and a method for its production, wherein the steel exhibits a hardened surface layer without requiring any post-solidification processing such as heat treatment, alloying beyond standard compositional limits, mechanical working, or surface coating. The steel article comprises a core region and a surface layer extending from the outermost surface to a depth of approximately 200 micrometers. The surface layer possesses a Vickers hardness of at least 400 HV and a nanohardness of at least 9.5 GPa in its hardest constituent phase, while the core retains the ductility and toughness characteristic of conventional low-carbon steels.

The surface layer consists of a mixed microstructure comprising three distinct phases: lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite. The lath martensite is present predominantly in the outermost 0 to 50 micrometers of the surface layer and exhibits a body-centered tetragonal crystal structure with a carbon content significantly higher than the nominal bulk carbon content of the steel, due to carbon enrichment during solid-state phase transformations. The laths of martensite have an average width of 128 nanometers, contributing to exceptional hardness through grain boundary strengthening. The upper bainite appears in two morphological variants: B1-type in the 0–50 µm depth range, characterized by laths with length-to-width ratios greater than 30, and B2-type in the 50–200 µm depth range, with laths having length-to-width ratios less than 10. Both variants contain nanoscale cementite precipitates between ferrite laths, providing additional precipitation strengthening. The acicular ferrite is confined to the 50–200 µm depth region and consists of randomly oriented, high-angle misoriented needles with widths averaging 800 nanometers.

The method of producing the steel article involves melting a low-carbon steel composition containing 0.02 to 0.03 weight percent carbon, 0.01 to 0.02 weight percent titanium, 0.3 to 0.6 weight percent manganese, and 0.1 to 0.3 weight percent silicon, with oxygen content controlled to 35–50 ppm. The molten steel is then contacted with chilled copper plates separated by a narrow gap (2.5 mm) for a brief duration (approximately 0.24 seconds), resulting in rapid extraction of heat and formation of a thin cast sheet. This step induces sub-rapid solidification with cooling rates of 900–1000 K/s in the 0–50 µm surface region and 400–900 K/s in the 50–200 µm region. The cast sheet is subsequently air-cooled for 30 seconds followed by water quenching, which arrests diffusion-controlled transformations and promotes the formation of martensite from carbon-enriched austenite.

The invention further provides that the lath martensite in the surface layer exhibits a yield strength of at least 2300 MPa, as calculated from nanohardness measurements using established empirical relationships, thereby conferring exceptional resistance to surface deformation and wear. This performance is achieved despite the ultra-low nominal carbon content of the steel, demonstrating that microstructural refinement and local carbon segregation can substitute for bulk alloying in achieving high surface hardness.

In summary, the invention offers a paradigm shift in low-carbon steel design by demonstrating that surface hardening can be intrinsically engineered during solidification, eliminating the need for costly and environmentally burdensome post-processing steps while delivering mechanical properties previously thought unattainable in such compositions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a novel low-carbon steel article and a method for its manufacture, wherein the steel possesses a hardened surface layer formed entirely during the casting and initial cooling stages, without any subsequent thermal, mechanical, or chemical treatment. This hardened surface layer arises from a unique combination of microstructural features—specifically, the coexistence of lath martensite, upper bainite, and acicular ferrite—each contributing distinct mechanical characteristics that collectively enhance surface performance while maintaining a tough, ductile core.

The steel composition according to the invention is carefully controlled to enable the desired microstructural evolution during solidification and cooling. The base composition includes carbon in the range of 0.02 to 0.03 weight percent, which is sufficiently low to ensure good weldability and toughness in the core but, under the specific processing conditions disclosed herein, allows for the formation of martensite in the surface layer through localized carbon enrichment. Manganese is present in the range of 0.3 to 0.6 weight percent to provide mild solid-solution strengthening and to stabilize austenite during cooling, thereby influencing transformation kinetics. Silicon, at 0.1 to 0.3 weight percent, acts as a deoxidizer and also suppresses carbide precipitation during cooling, promoting the retention of carbon in austenite for subsequent martensitic transformation. Titanium is added in the narrow range of 0.01 to 0.02 weight percent to form fine TiN or Ti(C,N) particles that serve as nucleation sites for acicular ferrite, thereby refining the microstructure in the intermediate depth regions. Oxygen content is tightly controlled to 35–50 ppm through the addition of FeO, which helps regulate inclusion chemistry and promotes uniform solidification.

The manufacturing process begins with melting the aforementioned composition in an induction furnace at a temperature of approximately 1600°C to ensure complete homogenization. The molten steel is then brought into contact with two parallel copper plates, each 9 mm thick, separated by a 2.5 mm gap. The copper plates act as highly efficient heat sinks due to their high thermal conductivity. Upon contact, heat is rapidly extracted from the melt, causing near-instantaneous solidification at the steel-copper interface. The duration of contact is precisely controlled to 0.24 seconds, which is sufficient to form a solidified shell but short enough to prevent complete solidification through the thickness. This results in a flat steel sheet measuring 2.5 mm in thickness, 75 mm in width, and 100 mm in length. The solidification front progresses inward from both surfaces simultaneously, creating steep thermal gradients that drive sub-rapid solidification conditions.

Thermal simulation using ProCAST software confirms that the cooling rate in the outermost 0–50 µm of the surface reaches 900–1000 K/s, while the region from 50–200 µm experiences cooling rates of 400–900 K/s. These rates fall within the sub-rapid solidification regime, which is known to refine prior austenite grain size. Indeed, metallographic analysis reveals an average prior austenite grain diameter of 152 µm near the surface, significantly finer than typical as-cast grains in conventional casting (>500 µm). This refined austenite structure provides a favorable template for subsequent phase transformations during cooling.

Following removal from the copper plates, the partially solidified sheet undergoes 30 seconds of air cooling, during which time the interior continues to solidify and the temperature drops to approximately 600–700°C. This stage allows for the initiation of diffusion-controlled transformations such as bainite and acicular ferrite formation in the subsurface regions. The sheet is then immediately quenched in water, which rapidly cools the entire cross-section below the martensite start (Ms) temperature, thereby suppressing further diffusion and promoting martensitic transformation in carbon-enriched austenite domains.

The resulting microstructure varies with depth from the surface. In the 0–50 µm zone, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) reveal a mixture of lath martensite and B1-type upper bainite. The lath martensite consists of parallel arrays of ultrafine laths with an average width of 128 nm, bounded by high-angle grain boundaries. SAED patterns confirm a body-centered tetragonal (bct) lattice, with a measurable difference in interplanar spacing between the (110) and (011) planes of approximately 0.4%, consistent with a local carbon content of about 0.14 wt%—six times the nominal bulk carbon content. This enrichment occurs because, during the air-cooling stage, upper bainite forms first at austenite grain boundaries, expelling carbon into the adjacent austenite. As bainite grows inward, carbon accumulates between laths (precipitating as cementite) and, more importantly, in the austenite regions between bainite blocks. When the water quench is applied, this carbon-rich austenite transforms diffusionlessly into martensite, inheriting the elevated carbon concentration.

In the 50–200 µm depth region, the microstructure comprises acicular ferrite and B2-type upper bainite. Acicular ferrite nucleates heterogeneously on Ti-containing inclusions within austenite grains during the air-cooling stage, forming randomly oriented, high-aspect-ratio needles that effectively subdivide the austenite grains. These needles then serve as nucleation sites for upper bainite laths, which grow with limited length due to physical constraints imposed by the acicular ferrite network. Consequently, the B2 bainite laths exhibit length-to-width ratios below 10, compared to over 30 for B1 laths in the outer zone.

Nanoindentation testing, performed using the continuous stiffness method with a Berkovich indenter, quantifies the mechanical properties of each microstructural constituent. Lath martensite exhibits an average nanohardness of 9.87 ± 0.51 GPa, which is remarkably high for a steel with only 0.023 wt% bulk carbon—comparable to values reported for 0.4 wt% carbon steels. This extraordinary hardness is attributed to the combined effects of carbon-induced lattice distortion and extreme lath refinement (Hall-Petch strengthening). Upper bainite shows a nanohardness of 4.18 ± 0.39 GPa, consistent with literature values, arising from fine lath spacing and cementite precipitation. Acicular ferrite registers 2.93 ± 0.30 GPa, slightly higher than typical values due to indentation across grain boundaries, which impedes dislocation motion.

Using the empirical relationship σ_Y = H / 3 (where σ_Y is yield strength and H is nanohardness), the yield strengths are calculated as 2378 MPa for martensite, 1007 MPa for upper bainite, and 706 MPa for acicular ferrite. The presence of the high-strength martensite in the outermost layer thus provides exceptional resistance to surface yielding and wear, while the underlying bainite and acicular ferrite offer a gradual transition in properties to the softer core, minimizing stress concentrations.

This microstructural gradient is self-generated during processing and requires no external intervention, representing a significant advance over prior art. The invention thus enables the production of low-carbon steel components with integrated surface hardening, suitable for applications such as gears, rails, automotive chassis parts, and wear plates, where surface durability and core toughness are simultaneously required.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Production of Hardened Low-Carbon Steel Sheet**

A low-carbon steel with the following composition was prepared: 0.023 wt% C, 0.015 wt% Ti, 0.45 wt% Mn, 0.20 wt% Si, balance Fe, with oxygen content adjusted to 42 ppm. The steel was melted in an induction furnace at 1600°C. Two copper plates (9 mm thick, 2.5 mm gap) were pressed into the melt for 0.24 s, producing a 2.5 × 75 × 100 mm sheet. The sheet was air-cooled for 30 s and then water-quenched.

Metallographic analysis revealed a surface layer extending to 200 µm depth. SEM and TEM identified lath martensite and B1 upper bainite (lath aspect ratio >30) in the 0–50 µm zone, and acicular ferrite with B2 upper bainite (aspect ratio <10) in the 50–200 µm zone. Nanoindentation yielded nanohardness values of 9.87 GPa (martensite), 4.18 GPa (upper bainite), and 2.93 GPa (acicular ferrite). The surface Vickers hardness averaged 420 HV, while the core remained at 140 HV, confirming a hardened surface without core embrittlement.

**Example 2: Variation of Cooling Rate**

Using the same composition as Example 1, the contact time with copper plates was reduced to 0.15 s, resulting in thinner solidified shells and lower surface cooling rates (~700 K/s at 0–50 µm). The resulting microstructure showed reduced martensite volume fraction and lower surface hardness (350 HV), demonstrating the critical role of sub-rapid solidification in achieving the full effect.

**Example 3: Effect of Titanium Content**

A steel with 0.005 wt% Ti (below the claimed range) was processed identically to Example 1. Acicular ferrite formation was suppressed, leading to coarse upper bainite in the 50–200 µm zone and increased susceptibility to crack propagation. This confirms the necessity of Ti within the specified range to promote acicular ferrite and refine the subsurface microstructure.

These examples illustrate that the invention’s benefits are reproducible and dependent on precise control of composition and solidification kinetics, as defined in the claims.
