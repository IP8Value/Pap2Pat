# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of predictive maintenance and fault forecasting in electrical power systems, and more particularly to a machine learning-based method and system for predicting imminent faults in transmission and distribution power lines—including both overhead lines and underground or underwater cables—using only existing voltage and current measurements without requiring the deployment of additional sensors. The invention leverages edge computing, feature engineering, and a specialized deep neural network architecture based on Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units to analyze time-series data derived from disturbance recordings triggered by high-sensitivity protection algorithms. The system is designed to provide early warnings of potential grid failures up to one week in advance, thereby enabling proactive maintenance, reducing unplanned outages, minimizing financial losses, and enhancing the overall reliability and resilience of electrical infrastructure. The technical domain encompasses power system monitoring, real-time data processing, cloud-edge computing architectures, time-series forecasting, and artificial intelligence applications in industrial automation and smart grid technologies.

## BACKGROUND ART

Electrical power systems are critical infrastructures that underpin modern society, supporting residential, commercial, industrial, and governmental operations. As these systems grow in scale and complexity—spanning generation plants, high- and medium-voltage transmission networks, distribution feeders, and end-user loads—they become increasingly susceptible to faults. Common fault types include partial or complete short circuits between phases or between a phase and ground, which can arise from a multitude of causes. These include aging and mechanical degradation of conductors and insulators; environmental stressors such as extreme temperatures, chemical exposure, moisture ingress, and abrasion; operational overloads; wildlife interference (e.g., birds or squirrels bridging conductors); vegetation encroachment; and physical damage during installation or maintenance. Such faults not only disrupt power delivery but also incur substantial economic costs through equipment damage, service interruptions, regulatory penalties, and lost productivity.

To mitigate these risks, utilities and industrial operators have long sought methods for early fault detection and prediction. Traditional approaches have focused on monitoring insulation integrity through partial discharge (PD) analysis, which detects localized dielectric breakdowns that often precede catastrophic failure. While effective in controlled environments, PD-based methods suffer from significant limitations in real-world grid applications. They require expensive, high-precision sensors capable of capturing nanosecond-scale electromagnetic transients, and their performance degrades severely in electrically noisy substations or along long cable runs. Moreover, PD analysis is inherently unsuitable for underground and underwater cables, where signal attenuation and shielding prevent reliable measurement. Critically, many grid faults do not originate from insulation degradation at all—rendering PD-based systems blind to a large class of failure modes.

Alternative strategies have emerged in recent years, including thermal monitoring using distributed temperature sensors or fiber-optic cables embedded in power lines, and visual inspection via unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) equipped with infrared or high-resolution cameras. While these techniques offer valuable insights into conductor overheating or physical damage, they are similarly constrained: thermal sensors cannot detect non-thermal precursors such as transient overvoltages or harmonic distortions, and UAV inspections are limited to overhead lines, weather-dependent, and incapable of assessing subsurface cable conditions. Furthermore, both approaches necessitate the installation of new hardware across vast networks—a capital-intensive undertaking that involves not only sensor procurement but also the establishment of robust, low-latency communication backbones, data storage infrastructure, and real-time analytics platforms. For large utilities managing thousands of kilometers of lines, such deployments are often economically and logistically prohibitive.

Concurrently, advances in digital substation technology have led to the widespread adoption of IEC 61850 standards, which enable interoperable communication between protection relays, merging units, and control systems using sampled values (e.g., IEC 61850-9-2LE) and generic object-oriented substation events (GOOSE, per IEC 61850-8-1). These protocols facilitate high-fidelity, time-synchronized acquisition of voltage and current waveforms at sampling rates typically ranging from 1 kHz to 10 kHz. Modern digital relays and edge devices already capture this data for real-time protection functions, such as overcurrent, distance, and differential schemes. However, this rich dataset has remained largely underutilized for predictive purposes, primarily due to the computational and algorithmic challenges associated with extracting meaningful precursors from high-dimensional, irregularly sampled time-series signals.

Machine learning has recently been explored as a tool for power system diagnostics, but existing efforts face critical gaps. Some studies attempt to forecast faults using exogenous variables like weather data (e.g., wind speed, lightning strikes, humidity), but these models ignore the internal electrical state of the grid and thus cannot detect faults caused by internal degradation or operational anomalies unrelated to meteorological conditions. Others apply generic time-series forecasting models to raw waveform data, but without appropriate feature engineering or temporal context, these approaches lack the sensitivity to distinguish subtle precursor signatures from normal operational variations or benign disturbances (e.g., motor startups, capacitor bank switching). Moreover, most proposed architectures fail to account for the irregular timing of disturbance recordings—events are not uniformly spaced but occur stochastically in response to threshold crossings—posing a challenge for conventional recurrent neural networks that assume regular sampling intervals.

A further limitation of prior art is the absence of mechanisms to filter irrelevant data. High-sensitivity protection settings, while beneficial for capturing incipient anomalies, also trigger recordings during routine transients that carry no predictive value. Feeding all such data into a forecasting model introduces noise that dilutes the signal-to-noise ratio and degrades performance. Additionally, the scarcity of labeled fault data—since actual failures are rare by design—exacerbates the difficulty of training robust classifiers, leading many approaches to either overfit to limited examples or produce excessive false positives, which erode operator trust and increase unnecessary maintenance costs.

In summary, the background art lacks a comprehensive, sensor-free, and computationally efficient solution that can: (1) operate exclusively on existing voltage and current measurements without new hardware; (2) handle both overhead and underground cable systems; (3) detect faults arising from diverse root causes beyond insulation failure; (4) process irregularly sampled disturbance recordings within a temporally coherent forecasting window; (5) intelligently filter out non-predictive events; and (6) deliver high specificity to minimize false alarms while maintaining acceptable recall. The present invention addresses these unmet needs through an integrated framework combining edge-based pre-filtering, physics-informed feature engineering, and a novel LSTM-based neural architecture tailored to the unique characteristics of power system disturbance data.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method and system for predicting electrical faults in power transmission and distribution networks up to one week in advance, utilizing only existing voltage and current measurements obtained from standard substation instrumentation, without requiring the installation of any additional sensors. The invention comprises a multi-stage pipeline that begins with the collection of high-fidelity disturbance recordings at the edge of the network, followed by intelligent feature extraction, temporal sequence construction, and deep learning-based forecasting using a specialized neural network architecture.

In accordance with the invention, an edge computing device deployed within a substation receives synchronized voltage and current waveform data via IEC 61850-9-2LE and IEC 61850-8-1 communication protocols from instrument transformers and primary equipment. This edge device executes high-sensitivity protection algorithms that continuously monitor the electrical signals and automatically trigger the recording of transient disturbances whenever predefined thresholds are exceeded. These recordings—captured at a sampling rate of approximately 4 kHz—constitute the raw input data for fault prediction. By performing this initial filtering at the edge, the system drastically reduces the volume of data transmitted to central processing facilities, optimizing bandwidth and computational resources.

The recorded waveforms are then subjected to a feature engineering process that transforms each high-dimensional time-series segment into a compact, informative feature vector. This transformation involves computing a suite of electrical quantities—including root mean square (RMS) values, impedance magnitude and angle, active and reactive power, harmonic content (e.g., total harmonic distortion, individual harmonic magnitudes up to the 50th order), and phase imbalances—and deriving statistical descriptors such as maximum, minimum, mean, standard deviation, skewness, and kurtosis for each quantity over the duration of the disturbance. The resulting feature vector, typically comprising around 300 scalar values, serves as a lower-dimensional yet highly representative encoding of the original waveform, preserving critical diagnostic information while enabling efficient downstream processing.

These feature vectors are organized into multidimensional time series, where each time step corresponds to a recorded disturbance event, and the sequence spans a rolling window of one week prior to the prediction time. The invention defines the prediction task as a binary classification problem: for any given hour, the model outputs a probability score indicating whether a fault will occur on the monitored feeder within the subsequent seven-day period. To address this task, the invention employs a deep neural network composed of three key components: a relevance filtering module, an LSTM-based temporal encoder, and a classification head.

The relevance filtering module is a fully connected neural network trained on a manually labeled subset of disturbance recordings to distinguish between "relevant" anomalies—those exhibiting electrical signatures indicative of incipient failure—and "irrelevant" transients, such as load switching or capacitor energization, which do not contribute to fault prediction. Only recordings classified as relevant are passed to the next stage, thereby focusing the model’s attention on diagnostically significant events and improving signal fidelity.

The filtered feature vectors are then processed by an LSTM layer, which is uniquely suited to handle the irregular temporal spacing of disturbance events. Unlike fixed-interval models, the LSTM dynamically integrates information across variable time gaps by leveraging its internal memory cells and gating mechanisms, effectively learning which historical anomalies are most predictive of future failure. In embodiments involving multiple interconnected feeders or substations, separate LSTM encoders process each location’s time series independently, and their final hidden states are concatenated before being fed into a shared classification head. This architecture enables the model to capture both local degradation patterns and system-wide interactions that may influence fault likelihood.

The classification head consists of two fully connected layers followed by a sigmoid activation function, producing a continuous output between 0 and 1 that represents the estimated probability of a fault occurring within the next week. The model is trained using a binary cross-entropy loss function, with hyperparameters—including learning rate and decay schedule—optimized to balance recall (the ability to detect true impending faults) and specificity (the avoidance of false alarms), with a strong emphasis on the latter to ensure operational practicality.

To overcome data scarcity, the invention incorporates a data augmentation strategy that permutes the phase labels of the three-phase electrical signals (e.g., swapping Phase A with Phase B, etc.), effectively multiplying the training set size by a factor of six without introducing synthetic artifacts. This technique enhances model generalization while respecting the physical symmetries inherent in balanced three-phase systems.

The entire system operates within a cloud-edge computing paradigm: edge devices perform initial data capture and filtering, cloud infrastructure handles scalable storage and batch processing, and a dedicated machine learning service executes the forecasting model. Prediction results are returned to a central monitoring platform for visualization, alerting, and integration with asset management workflows.

In summary, the invention delivers a sensor-free, data-efficient, and highly specific fault prediction capability that leverages existing substation infrastructure to provide actionable early warnings, thereby enabling utilities to transition from reactive to predictive maintenance paradigms and significantly enhance grid reliability.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, illustrating how the various components and processes described in the Summary section are implemented in practice. It should be understood that these embodiments are provided for illustrative purposes only and do not limit the scope of the invention, which is defined solely by the appended claims.

In a first embodiment, the invention is deployed in a medium-voltage (20 kV) distribution substation serving a mix of urban and rural feeders, including both overhead lines and underground cables. The substation is equipped with digital protective relays compliant with the IEC 61850 standard, which communicate sampled analog values (voltage and current waveforms) via the IEC 61850-9-2LE protocol and discrete status signals (e.g., circuit breaker position, isolator status) via IEC 61850-8-1 GOOSE messages. An edge computing device—implemented as a ruggedized industrial PC or embedded gateway—is installed within the substation control room and connected to the station bus. This edge device runs a suite of high-sensitivity protection algorithms, such as adaptive overcurrent, negative-sequence current detection, and transient overvoltage monitoring, configured to trigger disturbance recording upon detection of anomalies that fall below conventional trip thresholds but may indicate early-stage degradation.

Each disturbance recording captures 200 milliseconds of three-phase voltage and current waveforms at a sampling rate of 4 kHz, yielding 800 samples per phase per recording. Upon triggering, the edge device timestamps the event using IEEE 1588 Precision Time Protocol (PTP) to ensure microsecond-level synchronization across all measurements. The raw waveform data, along with metadata (e.g., feeder identifier, event type, timestamp), is temporarily stored in local flash memory. To manage storage constraints, the edge device implements a circular buffer that retains only the most recent 72 hours of recordings, unless flagged for upload due to high anomaly scores.

Periodically—e.g., every 15 minutes—the edge device evaluates the accumulated recordings against a relevance criterion. In this embodiment, relevance is initially determined by a rule-based filter that flags recordings exhibiting sustained harmonic distortion above 8%, negative-sequence current exceeding 5% of rated current, or transient overvoltages greater than 1.3 per unit. These criteria are derived from empirical observations of precursor symptoms in historical fault data. Flagged recordings are compressed using lossless ZIP algorithms and securely transmitted via encrypted TLS 1.3 connections to a cloud-based data lake hosted on a commercial cloud platform (e.g., Microsoft Azure or Amazon Web Services). Non-flagged recordings are purged after 24 hours to conserve local storage.

Upon arrival in the cloud, the recordings undergo the feature engineering pipeline. Each waveform segment is first normalized to per-unit values based on the feeder’s nominal voltage and current ratings. Then, for each 20 ms window (corresponding to one cycle at 50 Hz), the system computes instantaneous power quantities using the p-q theory: active power *p(t)* = *v_a(t)i_a(t) + v_b(t)i_b(t) + v_c(t)i_c(t)*, and reactive power *q(t)* derived from cross-products of phase-shifted voltages and currents. RMS values are calculated over the full 200 ms duration using the standard formula *V_rms = sqrt(1/N Σ v(t)^2)*. Impedance is estimated as *Z = V_ph / I_ph* for each phase, with magnitude and angle extracted via complex division. Harmonic analysis is performed using a 10-cycle Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), yielding magnitudes for harmonics 2 through 50, from which total harmonic distortion (THD) is computed as *THD = sqrt(Σ_{h=2}^{50} V_h^2) / V_1*. Additional features include zero-sequence and negative-sequence components, calculated using symmetrical component transformation, and phase angle differences between voltage and current to assess power factor deviations.

For each of these 25 base electrical quantities (e.g., V_rms_A, I_rms_B, P_total, Q_total, Z_mag_C, THD_V, etc.), the system derives 12 statistical descriptors: min, max, mean, median, standard deviation, variance, skewness, kurtosis, 10th percentile, 90th percentile, range (max–min), and crest factor (max / RMS). This yields a feature vector of 300 elements (25 × 12), which is stored in a columnar database (e.g., Apache Parquet format) indexed by timestamp and feeder ID.

The machine learning service, implemented as a containerized application orchestrated by Kubernetes, periodically queries this database to construct input sequences for prediction. For each hourly prediction time *t*, the system retrieves all feature vectors corresponding to disturbance recordings with timestamps in the interval [*t* − 7 days, *t*]. These vectors are sorted chronologically to form a sequence *X = [x₁, x₂, ..., xₙ]*, where *n* varies depending on the number of disturbances in the past week (typically 5 to 50 events in practice). A binary target label *y* is assigned as 1 if any verified fault (e.g., a sustained short circuit requiring manual intervention) occurred on the feeder in the interval (*t*, *t* + 7 days], and 0 otherwise. Fault verification is performed by cross-referencing SCADA alarm logs, maintenance work orders, and customer outage reports.

The neural network architecture is instantiated using TensorFlow 2.x. The relevance filtering module is a three-layer fully connected network with 256, 128, and 1 neuron, respectively, using ReLU activations in the hidden layers and a sigmoid output. It is trained on a dataset of 2,000 manually labeled recordings (1,200 relevant, 800 irrelevant), annotated by senior protection engineers based on post-event analysis. During inference, recordings with filter output > 0.7 are retained; others are discarded.

The LSTM encoder consists of a single layer with 128 hidden units, initialized with orthogonal weights to mitigate vanishing gradients. To handle variable sequence lengths, the input is padded with zeros, and a masking layer ignores padded timesteps during computation. In multi-feeder embodiments, each feeder’s sequence is processed by a dedicated LSTM instance (weight sharing is optional), and the final hidden states are concatenated into a joint representation of dimension 128 × *F*, where *F* is the number of feeders.

The classification head comprises two dense layers with 64 and 32 neurons (ReLU-activated), followed by a single sigmoid neuron. Dropout (rate=0.3) is applied after each dense layer to reduce overfitting. The model is trained using the Adam optimizer with a cyclic learning rate schedule: starting at 3×10⁻⁴, decaying by 5% every 10 epochs, and resetting after 50 epochs. Batch size is set to 32, and training proceeds for 200 epochs with early stopping if validation loss plateaus for 15 epochs.

Data augmentation is applied during training: for each original recording, five additional variants are generated by permuting the phase assignments (e.g., [A,B,C] → [B,C,A], [C,A,B], etc.). Since the electrical system is symmetric under phase rotation, these permutations represent physically valid scenarios, effectively expanding the training set without compromising realism.

During operation, the trained model generates hourly predictions for each feeder. A prediction score ≥ 0.5 triggers a “high-risk” alert in the monitoring dashboard, displayed alongside contextual information such as recent disturbance trends, weather forecasts, and asset age. Operators can drill down into the contributing recordings, visualizing the original waveforms and extracted features to assess credibility. False positives are logged and used to retrain the relevance filter in subsequent cycles, enabling continuous improvement.

In a second embodiment, the system is extended to high-voltage (110 kV) transmission networks. Here, disturbance recordings are triggered by traveling-wave fault locators and differential protection schemes, capturing higher-energy transients. The feature set is augmented with traveling-wave arrival times and polarity indicators, and the LSTM is replaced with a bidirectional variant to capture both pre- and post-disturbance context. Cloud processing is accelerated using GPU instances, reducing prediction latency to under 2 minutes.

In a third embodiment, the invention is integrated with a digital twin of the grid. The prediction probabilities are fed into a risk-based maintenance scheduler that optimizes crew dispatch, spare part inventory, and switching plans to preemptively isolate vulnerable segments. Economic impact is quantified by estimating avoided outage costs using historical SAIDI/SAIFI metrics.

Throughout all embodiments, the core innovation remains the synergistic combination of edge-based data triage, physics-guided feature reduction, and an LSTM architecture adapted to irregular event streams—all operating without new sensors. This ensures scalability, cost-effectiveness, and immediate deployability in existing IEC 61850-compliant substations worldwide.
