## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of computational biology and bioinformatics, and more specifically to methods and systems for automated clustering and alignment of cell populations in high-dimensional flow cytometry and mass cytometry data. The invention provides a novel algorithmic framework for robust, efficient, and biologically meaningful comparison of cellular subsets across multiple biological samples, enabling accurate identification, matching, and quantification of phenotypically distinct cell populations even in the presence of significant inter-sample variability in marker expression levels, population frequencies, or complete absence of specific cell types in certain samples. The invention is particularly applicable to immunological studies, cancer research, drug development, and clinical diagnostics where comparative analysis of complex cellular compositions is essential.

## BACKGROUND

Flow cytometry and mass cytometry have become indispensable tools in modern biomedical research and clinical diagnostics, enabling high-throughput, multiparametric analysis of single-cell characteristics such as surface marker expression, intracellular protein levels, and functional states. These technologies generate vast amounts of high-dimensional data that require sophisticated computational methods for meaningful interpretation. A fundamental challenge in cytometry data analysis is the comparative assessment of cell populations across multiple samples, which is essential for identifying disease-associated changes, treatment responses, or developmental differences in cellular composition.

Traditional approaches to this comparative analysis have relied heavily on manual gating, where expert analysts visually inspect two-dimensional projections of the data and draw boundaries around apparent cell populations. While this approach has proven effective for low-dimensional analyses and well-characterized cell types, it suffers from several critical limitations when applied to modern high-dimensional datasets. Manual gating is inherently subjective, time-consuming, and difficult to reproduce consistently across different analysts or even across different sessions by the same analyst. Moreover, the exponential increase in possible two-dimensional projections as the number of measured parameters grows makes comprehensive manual analysis practically impossible for datasets with more than a handful of markers.

In response to these limitations, numerous automated clustering algorithms have been developed to identify cell populations objectively and efficiently. These methods can be broadly categorized into two main approaches: separate clustering followed by post-hoc matching, and joint clustering across multiple samples simultaneously. The first approach involves clustering each sample independently using methods such as mixture models, density-based clustering, or graph-based community detection, followed by alignment of corresponding clusters across samples based on similarity metrics. Examples include FLAME (FLOw analysis with Automated Multivariate Estimation) and the flowMatch package. While computationally efficient for individual samples, this approach struggles when cell populations exhibit significant positional shifts between samples, appear or disappear entirely in certain conditions, or when the curse of dimensionality compromises clustering accuracy in high-dimensional spaces.

The second approach, exemplified by methods such as Joint Clustering and Matching (JCM), ASPIRE (Automated Selection of Populations for Identification and REfinement), and FlowSOM, attempts to overcome these limitations by pooling data from multiple samples and performing simultaneous clustering to create a unified template of meta-clusters representing biologically relevant cell types. This strategy treats individual samples as different realizations of an underlying biological model, potentially improving robustness to inter-sample variability. However, these joint clustering methods face their own set of challenges. They are typically computationally expensive, especially as the number of samples and dimensions increases. More critically, they often rely on fitting complex mathematical models to the data, which becomes increasingly problematic in high-dimensional spaces due to the curse of dimensionality—the phenomenon where the volume of the parameter space grows exponentially with dimension, making statistical estimation unreliable and computationally intractable.

A central component of both clustering approaches is the choice of dissimilarity measure used to compare cell populations or individual cells. Common distance metrics include Euclidean distance, Mahalanobis distance, Kullback-Leibler divergence, and chi-square distance. However, many of these measures fail to satisfy key requirements for biologically meaningful comparisons in cytometry data. Specifically, an ideal dissimilarity measure should possess metric properties (non-negativity, symmetry, triangle inequality, and identity of indiscernibles), exhibit continuity with respect to small perturbations (so that minor instrument drift or technical variation doesn't produce large distance changes), be non-parametric to accommodate the complex, often non-Gaussian distributions typical of cytometry data, and be computationally efficient for high-throughput applications.

The Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) has been proposed as a suitable measure that satisfies these requirements, as it accounts for both positional and frequency differences between distributions while maintaining continuity properties. However, EMD is computationally intensive, with complexity that scales poorly with dataset size and dimensionality, limiting its practical utility for large-scale cytometry studies. Alternative measures such as the chi-square distance, while computationally efficient, fail to properly account for spatial separation between non-overlapping populations, leading to counterintuitive results where widely separated populations may appear similar if they don't overlap in the measured space.

The quadratic form (QF) distance, originally proposed for univariate histogram comparison in flow cytometry, offers a promising middle ground between computational efficiency and biological relevance. However, previous implementations were limited to one-dimensional applications and did not address the cluster matching problem directly. Extending this approach to high-dimensional settings while maintaining its favorable properties presents significant technical challenges, particularly in defining appropriate binning strategies and similarity matrices that preserve the continuity and metric properties essential for meaningful biological interpretation.

Thus, there remains a critical need for a robust, computationally efficient method for automated clustering and alignment of cell populations in high-dimensional cytometry data that can handle real-world complexities such as inter-sample variability in marker expression, population frequency differences spanning orders of magnitude, and complete absence of specific cell types in certain samples, while maintaining biological interpretability and computational tractability.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method and system for automated clustering and alignment of cell populations in high-dimensional flow cytometry and mass cytometry data, designated as QFMatch. The invention overcomes the limitations of existing approaches by implementing a multivariate extension of the quadratic form distance measure that maintains computational efficiency while providing biologically meaningful comparisons of cell populations across multiple samples.

The core innovation of the present invention lies in the development of a computationally efficient dissimilarity measure based on a multivariate quadratic form distance that satisfies key requirements for biological data analysis: it possesses metric properties, exhibits continuity with respect to small perturbations, accommodates high-dimensional data without suffering from the curse of dimensionality, and can be computed rapidly even for large datasets. This is achieved through a combination of adaptive binning strategies and carefully designed similarity matrices that reflect the spatial relationships between data bins.

The QFMatch algorithm operates through a systematic six-step process that enables robust alignment of cell populations between samples. First, the algorithm performs adaptive binning on the combined data from two samples to be compared, creating a shared reference frame that adapts to the data structure. Second, this common binning pattern is applied to each individual sample to generate histograms representing the distribution of each pre-identified cell cluster within the shared reference frame. Third, pairwise dissimilarity scores are calculated between all possible combinations of clusters from the two samples using the multivariate quadratic form distance. Fourth, clusters with the smallest dissimilarity scores are identified as matched pairs, while remaining unmatched clusters are treated as potential merging candidates. Fifth, the algorithm evaluates whether combining merging candidates with their nearest neighbors improves the match quality, thereby detecting cases where clusters have been artificially split during the initial clustering step or where populations are genuinely missing from one sample. Finally, the algorithm provides additional information about matched clusters, including their relative frequencies and the distances between their geometric means in each dimension, facilitating comprehensive biological interpretation.

The invention demonstrates superior performance compared to existing methods in several critical scenarios. It successfully matches cell populations even when marker expression levels vary dramatically between samples, as demonstrated with basophil populations in human peripheral blood and CD34+CD45mid cells in acute myeloid leukemia patients. It accurately detects the complete absence of specific cell populations, as shown in the comparison between wild-type and RAG knockout mice lacking T and B lymphocytes. It effectively aligns shared cell populations between different tissue compartments while correctly identifying tissue-specific populations, as demonstrated in the comparison between peritoneal cavity and spleen samples in mice. Furthermore, the invention enables detailed characterization of subtle phenotypic differences between matched populations across different biological contexts.

The computational efficiency of the QFMatch algorithm makes it suitable for high-throughput applications, with processing times that scale linearly with the number of bins rather than exponentially with dimensionality. The algorithm is robust to variations in binning parameters and sample sizes, ensuring consistent performance across diverse experimental conditions. The invention can be integrated into existing cytometry data analysis pipelines and works independently of the specific clustering method used to initially identify cell populations, making it compatible with both manual gating and various automated clustering approaches.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings specified below:

"Adaptive binning" refers to a data partitioning method that recursively divides a k-dimensional measurement space into hyper-rectangular bins such that each bin contains approximately the same number of data points. The algorithm begins by calculating the median and variance of the data for each dimension, then selects the dimension with maximum variance and splits the data along its median value. This process continues recursively until a predefined stopping criterion is met, such as a minimum number of data points per bin.

"Quadratic form distance" refers to a dissimilarity measure between two histograms h and f defined as D²(h,f) = (h - f)ᵀA(h - f), where A is a similarity matrix whose elements aᵢⱼ reflect the spatial dissimilarity between bins i and j. In the preferred embodiment, aᵢⱼ = 1 - dₘᵢⱼ/dₘₐₓ, where dₘᵢⱼ is the Euclidean distance between the centers of mass of bins i and j, and dₘₐₓ is the maximum value among all dₘᵢⱼ values.

"Cluster matching" refers to the process of identifying corresponding cell populations between two or more biological samples based on their phenotypic characteristics as measured by flow cytometry or mass cytometry.

"High-dimensional data" refers to cytometry data comprising measurements of three or more parameters per cell, though the methods described herein are particularly advantageous for datasets with ten or more parameters.

"Merging candidate" refers to an unmatched cluster that is evaluated for potential combination with neighboring clusters to determine whether the initial clustering step resulted in artificial splitting of a single biological population.

"Geometric mean" refers to the multidimensional centroid of a cell population, calculated as the point whose coordinates are the geometric means of the corresponding marker expression levels across all cells in the population.

"Relative frequency" refers to the proportion of cells belonging to a specific cluster relative to the total number of cells in the sample, expressed as a percentage or fraction.

"Curse of dimensionality" refers to the phenomenon in which the volume of the parameter space increases exponentially with the number of dimensions, leading to data sparsity and computational challenges in statistical estimation and machine learning algorithms.

"Logicle transformation" refers to a bi-exponential transformation commonly used in flow cytometry data preprocessing to handle the wide dynamic range of fluorescence intensities while preserving the resolution of both low-intensity and high-intensity signals.

"Density-based merging (DBM)" refers to a two-dimensional clustering algorithm that identifies cell populations based on local density maxima and merges adjacent regions according to density thresholds.

"Meta-cluster" refers to a higher-order cluster that represents a biologically relevant cell type identified through the aggregation of corresponding clusters across multiple samples.

"Pathfinder tool" refers to a visualization utility that displays marker expression patterns across all measured parameters using pseudocolor horizontal bars, enabling rapid identification of the most informative dimensions for distinguishing cell populations.

### Example 1—Workflow for Automated Clustering and Alignment of Cell Populations in Flow Cytometry Data

The present invention provides a comprehensive workflow for automated clustering and alignment of cell populations in flow cytometry data that addresses the fundamental challenges of high-dimensional data analysis while maintaining biological interpretability and computational efficiency. The workflow consists of two primary phases: data preprocessing and cluster matching, each implemented through specific algorithmic components that work synergistically to produce robust results.

In the data preprocessing phase, raw flow cytometry data undergoes a series of transformations to prepare it for subsequent analysis. First, fluorescence compensation is applied to correct for spectral overlap between different fluorochromes, ensuring that the measured signal for each parameter accurately reflects the true expression level of the corresponding marker. This step is critical for maintaining the integrity of multidimensional relationships in the data and is performed using standard compensation algorithms implemented in the AutoGate software platform.

Following compensation, the data is transformed using the Logicle transformation, which maps the wide dynamic range of fluorescence intensities onto a scale that preserves resolution at both low and high signal levels. The Logicle transformation is particularly well-suited for flow cytometry data because it maintains the natural logarithmic behavior of fluorescence signals while providing linear behavior near zero, thus accommodating both dim and bright populations within the same analytical framework.

The transformed data is then subjected to clustering using the density-based merging (DBM) algorithm, which operates in two-dimensional subspaces to identify phenotypically distinct cell populations. The DBM algorithm begins by identifying local density maxima in the two-dimensional projection, then iteratively merges adjacent regions based on density thresholds to form coherent clusters. This approach is advantageous because it does not require specification of the number of clusters a priori and can adapt to varying population densities within the data. Importantly, the two-dimensional nature of DBM clustering helps mitigate the curse of dimensionality that plagues many high-dimensional clustering algorithms, while still capturing essential biological relationships through careful selection of informative parameter pairs.

Once cell populations have been identified through the preprocessing phase, the cluster matching phase commences using the QFMatch algorithm. The algorithm begins by selecting two samples to be compared, which may represent different experimental conditions, time points, or biological replicates. The first step of QFMatch involves merging the pre-clustered samples and performing adaptive binning on the combined dataset. This adaptive binning creates a shared reference frame that captures the overall structure of both samples while ensuring that each bin contains approximately equal numbers of events. The recursive nature of the adaptive binning algorithm ensures that the resulting bins adapt to the data distribution, providing higher resolution in regions of high data density and coarser resolution in sparse regions.

The binning pattern derived from the combined samples is then applied back to each individual sample, generating histograms that represent the distribution of each pre-identified cluster within the shared reference frame. Each histogram is normalized such that the sum of relative frequencies equals one, ensuring comparability across clusters of different sizes. This step is crucial because it transforms the original high-dimensional cluster representations into a common format that can be directly compared using the quadratic form distance measure.

Pairwise dissimilarity scores are then calculated between all possible combinations of clusters from the two samples using the multivariate quadratic form distance. The similarity matrix A is constructed such that its off-diagonal elements reflect the spatial relationships between bins, with closer bins having higher similarity values. This construction ensures that the resulting distance measure not only accounts for differences in cluster frequency but also incorporates information about the spatial separation between populations, providing a more biologically meaningful comparison than frequency-only measures.

Clusters with the smallest dissimilarity scores are identified as matched pairs, representing corresponding cell populations between the two samples. Unmatched clusters are automatically designated as merging candidates and evaluated for potential combination with their nearest neighbors. This evaluation involves recalculating the dissimilarity score after merging the candidate cluster with its neighbor and comparing it to the original score. If the merged configuration yields a lower dissimilarity score, this indicates that the initial clustering step artificially split a single biological population, and the merge is accepted. Conversely, if the dissimilarity score increases upon merging, this suggests that the unmatched cluster represents a genuinely distinct population that is either absent from the other sample or sufficiently different to warrant separate classification.

The final output of the QFMatch algorithm includes not only the matched cluster pairs but also supplementary information that facilitates biological interpretation. For each matched pair, the algorithm reports the relative frequencies of the clusters in their respective samples, enabling quantitative comparison of population abundances. Additionally, the algorithm calculates the distance between the geometric means of the matched clusters in each dimension, expressed in standard deviation units of the reference cluster. This information reveals subtle phenotypic differences between corresponding populations across samples, such as shifts in marker expression levels that might indicate activation states or differentiation stages.

The entire workflow has been implemented in the AutoGate software platform and is available to users at non-profit organizations through the CytoGenie.org website. The implementation supports both fluorescence flow cytometry and mass cytometry data formats and can be integrated into existing analytical pipelines. The computational efficiency of the QFMatch algorithm ensures that even large datasets can be processed in reasonable time frames, with typical processing times ranging from a few seconds for simple comparisons to several minutes for complex high-dimensional analyses.

### Example 2—Matching of Basophil Populations Between Patient Samples, Even when Marker Expression Levels Vary Between Patients

The present invention demonstrates exceptional capability in matching cell populations that exhibit substantial inter-individual variability in marker expression levels, as exemplified by the successful alignment of basophil populations across different patient samples. Basophils, a rare granulocyte population involved in allergic responses and immune regulation, are typically identified in human peripheral blood by their high expression of the interleukin-3 receptor alpha chain (CD123). However, CD123 expression levels on basophils can vary dramatically between individuals due to genetic factors, disease states, or environmental influences, presenting a significant challenge for consistent population identification and comparison across samples.

In a representative application of the QFMatch algorithm, three patient samples were analyzed using a standardized gating strategy that included forward scatter area (FSC-A)/side scatter area (SSC-A) for white blood cell identification, FSC-A/FSC-height (FSC-H) for singlet discrimination, CD41a/live/dead staining for platelet exclusion and viability assessment, and finally Dump channel (comprising CD3, CD66b, and HLA-DR)/CD123 for basophil identification as Dump-negative, CD123-high events. Despite employing identical staining protocols and instrument settings, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD123 varied substantially across the three patients, ranging from 1,033 to 6,672 arbitrary units. This represents more than a six-fold difference in marker expression levels, which would likely confound traditional gating approaches that rely on fixed threshold values.

When the QFMatch algorithm was applied to these samples, it successfully identified and aligned the basophil populations across all three patients despite the dramatic differences in CD123 expression. The algorithm achieved this by considering the complete multidimensional phenotype of the basophil populations rather than relying on single-parameter thresholds. Specifically, the adaptive binning step created a shared reference frame that captured the overall distribution of events in the combined samples, while the quadratic form distance measure accounted for both the frequency differences and the spatial relationships between populations in the multidimensional space.

The matched basophil populations exhibited relative frequencies of the same order of magnitude across patients, confirming their biological correspondence despite the marker expression differences. However, the distances between their geometric means revealed significant positional shifts in the multidimensional space, with distances of [0.6; 0.7]σ when comparing Patient 1 with Patient 2 and [5.1; 1.6]σ between Patient 1 and Patient 3. These results demonstrate that the QFMatch algorithm can distinguish between genuine biological variation in marker expression and technical artifacts, providing confidence that the aligned populations represent the same biological entity despite their phenotypic differences.

This capability is particularly valuable in clinical applications where patient-to-patient variability is expected and must be accounted for in diagnostic or monitoring protocols. Traditional approaches might either miss basophil populations in patients with low CD123 expression or incorrectly include non-basophil populations in patients with high background staining. The QFMatch algorithm overcomes these limitations by using a holistic, multidimensional approach to population matching that is robust to single-parameter variations while maintaining sensitivity to genuine biological differences.

The success of QFMatch in this application extends beyond basophil identification to other cell populations that exhibit variable marker expression across individuals. For instance, in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients, the CD34+CD45mid blast population shows tremendous variability in both marker expression levels and relative frequencies. CD34 MFI ranged from 36 to 144, CD45 MFI varied from 74 to 500, and the relative frequency of CD34+CD45mid cells spanned from 4.7% to 58% across different patients. Despite this extreme heterogeneity, the QFMatch algorithm successfully aligned these blast populations across healthy controls and AML patients, demonstrating its utility in cancer diagnostics and monitoring where population characteristics can vary widely between individuals and disease states.

### Example 3—Detection of Missing Lymphocyte Populations in the Peritoneal Cavity of RAG Knockout (RAG−/−) Mice

The present invention excels at detecting the complete absence of specific cell populations in biological samples, a capability that is crucial for validating genetic models and understanding immune system development. This functionality was demonstrated through the comparison of peritoneal cavity cells from wild-type BALB/c mice and RAG knockout (RAG−/−) mice, which lack functional T and B lymphocytes due to the absence of recombination-activating genes essential for antigen receptor rearrangement.

In this application, cells from both mouse strains were stained for surface markers CD5 and CD19, which identify T lymphocytes (CD5hiCD19−) and B lymphocytes (CD19hiCD5lo/−), respectively. The QFMatch algorithm was applied to align the cell populations between the wild-type and knockout samples. As expected, the wild-type sample contained distinct populations corresponding to both T and B lymphocytes, while the RAG−/− sample completely lacked these populations due to the genetic defect.

The QFMatch algorithm correctly identified the absence of lymphocyte populations in the RAG−/− sample through its systematic evaluation of unmatched clusters. Initially, the algorithm attempted to match all clusters between the two samples based on their dissimilarity scores. The lymphocyte populations in the wild-type sample remained unmatched in the RAG−/− sample, automatically designating them as merging candidates. The algorithm then evaluated whether combining these unmatched clusters with neighboring populations in the RAG−/− sample would improve the match quality. However, all such merging attempts resulted in increased dissimilarity scores, confirming that the lymphocyte populations were genuinely absent rather than artificially split during the initial clustering step.

This result stands in stark contrast to the performance of existing cluster matching methods such as PhenoGraph and FlowSOM, which failed to correctly identify the absence of lymphocyte populations in the RAG−/− mice. Both PhenoGraph and FlowSOM, despite extensive parameter optimization, incorrectly assigned the lymphocyte-deficient regions of the RAG−/− sample to meta-clusters that included lymphocyte populations from the wild-type sample. This failure stems from their reliance on joint clustering approaches that force all samples into a common meta-cluster framework, potentially obscuring genuine biological absences.

The ability of QFMatch to detect missing populations has important implications for both basic research and clinical applications. In basic immunology, it enables accurate validation of genetic knockout models and provides confidence in conclusions about immune cell development and function. In clinical settings, it could be used to detect minimal residual disease in leukemia patients following treatment, where the absence of malignant populations indicates successful therapy. Similarly, it could identify immunodeficiency states characterized by the absence of specific lymphocyte subsets, providing diagnostic information that might be missed by methods that force all samples into predetermined population categories.

The computational efficiency of QFMatch further enhances its utility in these applications, as it can rapidly process large numbers of samples to identify rare instances of population absence. In the mouse peritoneal cavity example, the algorithm processed approximately 268,000 total cells in just 14 seconds using 256 bins, demonstrating its suitability for high-throughput screening applications where rapid identification of abnormal samples is essential.

### Example 4—Cluster Matching of the Murine Lymphoid, Myeloid and Granuloid Lineages Between PerC and Spleen

The present invention demonstrates remarkable capability in aligning cell populations across different tissue compartments while correctly identifying tissue-specific populations, as illustrated by the successful matching of murine immune cell lineages between peritoneal cavity (PerC) and spleen samples. These two tissues harbor distinct immune cell compositions reflecting their different physiological roles: the peritoneal cavity contains specialized resident macrophage populations (small and large peritoneal macrophages, SPM and LPM) and mast cells that are largely absent from the spleen, while the spleen contains higher proportions of monocytes and neutrophils that are relatively scarce in the naïve peritoneal cavity. However, both tissues share certain immune cell types, including dendritic cells (DC), natural killer (NK) cells, and eosinophils, making this an ideal test case for evaluating cluster matching algorithms.

In this application, wild-type C57BL/6 mice were used to generate single-cell suspensions from both PerC and spleen, which were then stained with a 12-parameter flow cytometry panel including side scatter area (SSC-A, correlating with cell granularity) and CD11b surface marker, among others. The QFMatch algorithm was applied to align the cell populations between these two tissue samples. The algorithm successfully identified and matched the shared immune cell subsets—NK cells (lymphoid lineage), dendritic cells (myeloid lineage), and eosinophils (granuloid lineage)—while correctly identifying the tissue-specific populations as unmatched.

The matched populations were highlighted in the same colors in the visualization output, clearly indicating their correspondence between tissues, while unmatched populations appeared in distinct colors (blue for PerC-specific populations and violet for spleen-specific populations). This clear visual distinction enables researchers to immediately identify both conserved and tissue-specific immune cell populations, facilitating comparative analysis of immune system organization across different anatomical sites.

Beyond simple population matching, the QFMatch algorithm enabled detailed characterization of phenotypic differences between matched populations across tissues. For instance, when eosinophil populations from PerC and spleen were further analyzed using the Pathfinder tool integrated into the AutoGate platform, subtle but biologically significant differences emerged. The Pathfinder visualization revealed that splenic eosinophils expressed higher levels of Gr-1 (a granulocyte differentiation antigen) compared to their peritoneal counterparts. This finding is consistent with established knowledge that Gr-1 expression varies by tissue microenvironment and inflammatory status, demonstrating that the QFMatch algorithm can detect and quantify biologically relevant phenotypic differences between corresponding cell populations in different contexts.

The sequential matching of eosinophil populations across all possible combinations of informative parameter pairs provided additional confidence in the alignment results. By systematically comparing the populations in multiple two-dimensional projections, the algorithm ensured that the matching was robust across different aspects of the multidimensional phenotype, reducing the risk of false alignments based on coincidental similarities in single parameter pairs.

This capability to perform cross-tissue population matching while preserving information about tissue-specific adaptations has broad applications in immunology and inflammation research. It enables systematic comparison of immune cell phenotypes across different anatomical sites, facilitating understanding of how tissue microenvironments shape immune cell function and differentiation. In disease models, it could reveal how pathological processes alter the normal tissue-specific programming of immune cells, providing insights into disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets.

### Example 5—Guided Gating of SSc Eosinophil Data

The present invention provides powerful capabilities for guided gating of complex cytometry data, enabling researchers to navigate high-dimensional datasets efficiently while maintaining biological relevance. This functionality was demonstrated through the analysis of eosinophil populations in systemic sclerosis (SSc) patient samples, where the QFMatch algorithm facilitated systematic exploration of phenotypic characteristics across multiple parameters.

In this application, the Pathfinder tool integrated into the AutoGate platform played a crucial role in identifying the most informative parameters for distinguishing eosinophil populations. The Pathfinder visualization displayed each measured parameter as a horizontal bar using pseudocolor convention to indicate staining intensity, with a user-definable threshold for positive staining indicated by a vertical dashed line. This intuitive representation enabled rapid scanning of all marker dimensions to identify those showing the most distinctive patterns between populations of interest.

Once the most informative parameters were identified (marked in red in the Pathfinder output), the QFMatch algorithm was used to sequentially match eosinophil populations across all possible combinations of these informative parameter pairs. This systematic approach ensured comprehensive coverage of the multidimensional phenotype while focusing computational resources on the most discriminative features. The matched subsets appeared in the same colors across different parameter combinations, providing visual confirmation of consistent population alignment throughout the multidimensional space.

The guided gating approach enabled by QFMatch offers several advantages over traditional manual gating strategies. First, it reduces the subjectivity inherent in manual gate placement by providing objective criteria for population identification based on multidimensional similarity. Second, it increases efficiency by automatically identifying the most informative parameter combinations, eliminating the need for exhaustive trial-and-error exploration of all possible two-dimensional projections. Third, it maintains consistency across samples by applying the same matching criteria uniformly, reducing inter-sample variability due to analyst bias.

In the context of SSc research, this guided gating approach revealed subtle but potentially significant differences in eosinophil phenotypes between patient groups or disease stages. By systematically comparing matched eosinophil populations across multiple informative parameters, researchers could identify specific marker combinations that best distinguished different clinical subtypes or correlated with disease activity measures. This information could inform the development of more precise diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers based on eosinophil phenotypic characteristics.

The integration of QFMatch with visualization tools like Pathfinder creates a powerful analytical environment that combines the objectivity and efficiency of automated algorithms with the interpretability and flexibility of interactive visualization. This hybrid approach leverages the strengths of both computational and human intelligence, enabling researchers to explore complex high-dimensional datasets systematically while retaining the ability to make informed decisions based on biological knowledge and experimental context.

The guided gating functionality extends beyond eosinophil analysis to any cell population of interest in complex cytometry datasets. Whether studying rare immune cell subsets in cancer immunotherapy, characterizing stem cell differentiation trajectories, or monitoring immune reconstitution following transplantation, the QFMatch-enabled guided gating approach provides a robust framework for systematic, reproducible, and biologically meaningful population identification and comparison.
