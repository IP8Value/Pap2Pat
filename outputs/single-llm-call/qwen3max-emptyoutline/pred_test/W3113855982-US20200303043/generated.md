# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of personalized medicine and computational biology, and more specifically to a novel method and system for evaluating the therapeutic efficacy of botanical extracts—particularly cannabis-derived compounds—on chronic inflammatory conditions through quantitative analysis of intracellular signaling pathway activation states derived from high-throughput transcriptomic data. The invention provides a robust, reproducible, and scalable computational framework that integrates gene expression profiling with curated biological pathway topologies to generate a Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI), which serves as a predictive metric for assessing how effectively a given cannabis extract or compound reverses disease-associated molecular perturbations toward a healthy physiological baseline. This methodology is particularly suited for applications in precision pharmacology, where treatment strategies must be tailored to individual patients based on their unique molecular profiles, and it addresses critical limitations in existing bioinformatics tools that fail to quantitatively link drug-induced transcriptional changes to functional pathway-level outcomes. By leveraging topology-aware signaling pathway impact analysis (SPIA) and statistical comparison across three distinct biological states—healthy control, untreated disease, and drug-treated disease—the invention enables objective ranking of therapeutic candidates not only by their biochemical composition but by their actual functional impact on cellular signaling networks. The scope of the invention extends beyond cannabis therapeutics to encompass any natural product, synthetic molecule, or combination therapy whose mechanism of action can be interrogated via transcriptomic readouts, thereby offering a universal platform for drug efficacy screening in the era of systems pharmacology and digital health.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The evolution of modern medicine has witnessed remarkable progress in the treatment of acute illnesses, yet chronic diseases—including autoimmune disorders, neurodegenerative conditions, metabolic syndromes, and cancer—continue to pose formidable challenges due to their complex etiologies, heterogeneous manifestations, and variable responses to standard therapies. A central obstacle in managing such conditions lies in the profound inter-individual variability in drug response, which stems from genetic, epigenetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors that collectively shape an individual’s molecular phenotype. This variability has catalyzed a paradigm shift toward personalized or precision medicine, wherein therapeutic decisions are informed by patient-specific biomarkers rather than population-averaged clinical guidelines. In this context, the development of diagnostic and prognostic tools capable of characterizing the molecular underpinnings of disease at the level of individual patients has become an urgent priority.

Intracellular signaling pathways serve as the fundamental regulatory circuits that govern cellular behavior in both health and disease. These pathways—comprising cascades of protein-protein interactions, post-translational modifications, and transcriptional feedback loops—orchestrate processes such as proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, inflammation, and metabolism. Dysregulation of these pathways is a hallmark of virtually all chronic diseases; for instance, hyperactivation of NF-κB and MAPK pathways is commonly observed in inflammatory and autoimmune disorders, while aberrant PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling is frequently implicated in oncogenesis. Consequently, the ability to quantitatively assess the activation status of these pathways in patient-derived samples offers a powerful lens through which to understand disease mechanisms and evaluate therapeutic interventions.

Advances in high-throughput “omics” technologies—particularly next-generation sequencing (NGS) and microarray-based transcriptomics—have made it feasible to profile the expression levels of thousands of genes simultaneously from minimal tissue inputs, including archived formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) specimens. Transcriptomic data, while indirect relative to proteomic or phosphoproteomic measurements, provide a cost-effective, widely accessible, and clinically compatible means of inferring pathway activity. However, the raw output of such assays—lists of differentially expressed genes—lacks immediate biological interpretability without integration into higher-order functional contexts. This necessity has spurred the development of numerous bioinformatics methods aimed at mapping gene expression changes onto known biological pathways.

Early approaches treated pathways as simple gene sets, applying enrichment statistics (e.g., Fisher’s exact test or Gene Set Enrichment Analysis, GSEA) to determine whether differentially expressed genes were overrepresented within predefined pathway annotations. While useful, these non-topological methods ignore the directional and causal relationships between pathway components—such as activation, inhibition, phosphorylation, or complex formation—which are essential for understanding signal flow and functional consequences. More sophisticated topology-based methods emerged to address this limitation by incorporating interaction types and network structures from curated databases like KEGG, Reactome, WikiPathways, and QIAGEN’s Pathway Central. Among these, Signaling Pathway Impact Analysis (SPIA) has demonstrated superior performance in aggregating gene-level changes into pathway-level perturbation scores by modeling the propagation of transcriptional effects through the pathway graph using principles inspired by signal transduction dynamics.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remains: existing tools predominantly focus on identifying *which* pathways are altered in disease versus health, but they do not provide a quantitative metric for evaluating *how effectively a drug restores normal pathway function*. Most drug efficacy prediction platforms either rely on proprietary machine learning models trained on historical clinical response data—which suffer from limited generalizability and lack mechanistic transparency—or require extensive kinetic modeling with unknown parameters that render them impractical for routine clinical use. Furthermore, many approaches conflate correlation with causation by failing to distinguish between primary drivers and secondary effects in transcriptional responses.

The need for a standardized, interpretable, and biologically grounded metric to rank therapeutic agents based on their functional impact on disease-relevant pathways is especially acute in the domain of botanical medicine, where complex mixtures of phytochemicals—such as those found in cannabis extracts—exert pleiotropic effects that cannot be predicted from single-compound pharmacology. Cannabis contains over 100 cannabinoids, along with terpenes, flavonoids, and other metabolites, which may act synergistically (the so-called “entourage effect”) to modulate multiple targets simultaneously. Traditional pharmacological assays often fail to capture this systems-level complexity, leading to inconsistent clinical outcomes and regulatory uncertainty.

Prior art methods such as OncoFinder and GeroScope have attempted to bridge this gap by computing pathway activation strengths and correlating them with drug-target interactions to predict therapeutic potential. However, these tools are designed primarily for *in silico* drug repurposing using publicly available datasets and do not incorporate experimental validation of drug effects in a controlled tripartite design (control, disease, treated). They also lack a unified efficiency index that directly quantifies the degree to which a treatment shifts the molecular phenotype toward health. Similarly, while SPIA and related algorithms can compute pathway perturbation scores, they stop short of synthesizing these scores into a clinically actionable drug ranking metric.

Thus, there exists a long-felt but unmet need for a comprehensive, end-to-end computational pipeline that: (1) accepts standard transcriptomic input from patient samples; (2) leverages high-quality, topology-rich pathway models; (3) computes pathway activation states in a manner that respects biological directionality; (4) statistically compares untreated and treated disease states against a healthy reference; and (5) outputs a normalized, interpretable index that reflects the net therapeutic efficacy of a given intervention. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing the Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI)—a novel metric derived from SPIA-based pathway scores through a rigorously defined algorithm that ensures objectivity, reproducibility, and clinical relevance. Unlike black-box machine learning models, CDEI is fully transparent, mathematically well-defined, and grounded in established principles of systems biology, making it suitable not only for research applications but also for eventual integration into clinical decision support systems.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method and associated computational system for determining the therapeutic efficacy of a drug—particularly cannabis-derived extracts or compounds—by quantifying its ability to normalize dysregulated intracellular signaling pathways in a diseased biological sample relative to a healthy reference state. This is achieved through the calculation of a Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI), a dimensionless metric ranging from −1 to 1, where values closer to 1 indicate high efficacy (near-complete reversal of disease-associated pathway perturbations), values near 0 indicate no effect, and negative values indicate exacerbation of the pathological state. The CDEI is derived from high-throughput transcriptomic data obtained from three distinct sample groups: a control group representing healthy physiology, an untreated case group representing the disease state prior to intervention, and a treated case group representing the disease state following administration of the candidate drug. The core innovation lies in the integration of topology-aware Signaling Pathway Impact Analysis (SPIA) with a statistically rigorous comparison framework that evaluates the relative proximity of treated and untreated samples to the control baseline across a comprehensive panel of biologically relevant pathways.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a method for calculating the CDEI, which begins with the acquisition of gene expression profiles—preferably from RNA sequencing or microarray experiments—from the aforementioned three sample groups. These expression data are processed to compute log-fold changes relative to the control group mean. The fold-change values are then input into the SPIA algorithm, which models each signaling pathway as a directed graph wherein nodes represent genes or proteins and edges represent activating or inhibiting interactions. Using a recursive perturbation model that accounts for both direct transcriptional changes and indirect effects propagated through upstream regulators, SPIA generates a pathway-level perturbation score that reflects the net activation or suppression of the pathway in each sample. To enhance robustness, the mean SPIA score for each pathway across all case samples (untreated or treated) is adjusted by a weight factor (wp) that reflects the consistency of directionality (i.e., the proportion of samples exhibiting perturbation in the same direction as the group mean). This weighted mean SPIA score (SPIAμ) is then subjected to a one-sample Student’s t-test against the null hypothesis of zero perturbation (as expected in the control group). The resulting absolute t-values for the untreated (|tU|) and treated (|tT|) groups quantify their respective deviations from the healthy state. Finally, the CDEI is computed as 2 × (|tU| / (|tT| + |tU|)) − 0.5, yielding a normalized measure of therapeutic gain.

In another aspect, the invention includes a software implementation of the CDEI calculation workflow, which may be deployed as a standalone application or web service featuring a graphical user interface. The software accepts standardized gene expression matrices, validates input integrity, interfaces with curated pathway databases (e.g., QIAGEN SABiosciences Pathway Central), executes the SPIA algorithm, performs statistical testing, and outputs ranked lists of drugs or extracts based on their CDEI scores. The system is designed to be platform-agnostic, supporting both Windows and Linux operating systems, and is optimized for batch processing of multiple experimental datasets.

In a further aspect, the invention encompasses the use of the CDEI metric to guide personalized treatment selection in clinical or preclinical settings. For example, in patients suffering from chronic inflammatory conditions, multiple cannabis extracts may be screened *in vitro* using patient-derived cells or 3D tissue models, and the extract yielding the highest CDEI would be prioritized for therapeutic use. Similarly, the method can be employed in drug discovery pipelines to rank lead compounds, in clinical trial stratification to identify likely responders, or in quality control of botanical products to ensure batch-to-batch consistency in functional efficacy.

The invention thus represents a significant advancement over the prior art by providing the first quantitative, pathway-centric, and statistically validated index specifically designed to evaluate the functional efficacy of complex botanical therapeutics. It overcomes the limitations of purely gene-centric or machine-learning-based approaches by embedding biological knowledge directly into the scoring algorithm, ensuring that the resulting metric reflects true systems-level restoration of homeostasis rather than superficial correlations. Moreover, because the CDEI framework is agnostic to the specific disease or drug class, it constitutes a generalizable platform technology applicable to a wide range of precision medicine scenarios beyond cannabis therapeutics.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE EMBODIMENTS

### Overview of Signaling Pathway Impact Analysis (SPIA) Method

The foundation of the present invention rests upon the Signaling Pathway Impact Analysis (SPIA) method, a topology-based bioinformatics algorithm that transforms high-dimensional gene expression data into biologically meaningful pathway activation scores by explicitly modeling the structure and directionality of molecular interactions within curated signaling pathways. Unlike conventional gene set enrichment techniques that treat pathways as unordered collections of genes, SPIA incorporates detailed information about the type (activation or inhibition), direction (upstream to downstream), and connectivity of interactions among pathway components, thereby enabling a more accurate and mechanistically grounded assessment of pathway perturbation in response to biological stimuli such as disease or drug treatment.

At its core, SPIA represents each signaling pathway as a directed graph \( G(V, E) \), where the vertex set \( V = \{g_1, g_2, \ldots, g_n\} \) corresponds to the genes or proteins participating in the pathway, and the edge set \( E = \{(g_i, g_j) \mid \text{genes } g_i \text{ and } g_j \text{ interact}\} \) encodes the regulatory relationships between them. Critically, each edge is annotated with a sign indicating whether the interaction is activating (\( \beta_{ij} = +1 \)) or inhibitory (\( \beta_{ij} = -1 \)), reflecting the biological reality that signal transduction involves both positive and negative regulation. This signed, directed graph structure is typically sourced from high-quality, manually curated pathway databases such as QIAGEN SABiosciences Pathway Central, Reactome, or KEGG, which provide standardized representations of canonical signaling cascades involved in processes like inflammation, cell cycle control, apoptosis, and metabolism.

Given this topological framework, SPIA computes a perturbation factor (PF) for each gene \( g \) in the pathway, which quantifies the total influence exerted on \( g \) by both its own differential expression and the propagated effects from its upstream regulators. Mathematically, the PF is defined recursively as:

\[
PF(g) = \Delta E(g) + \sum_{\gamma \in U_g} \beta_{\gamma g} \cdot \frac{PF(\gamma)}{n_{\text{down}}(\gamma)}
\]

where \( \Delta E(g) \) denotes the signed log-fold change in the expression level of gene \( g \) in a given experimental sample relative to the average expression in a pool of healthy control samples. The summation term aggregates contributions from all upstream genes \( \gamma \) belonging to the set \( U_g \), with each contribution scaled by the interaction weight \( \beta_{\gamma g} \) and normalized by the out-degree \( n_{\text{down}}(\gamma) \) of the upstream gene \( \gamma \)—that is, the number of downstream targets it regulates. This normalization ensures that highly connected hub genes do not disproportionately dominate the perturbation signal, promoting a balanced propagation of effects throughout the network. The identification of upstream and downstream relationships is performed using graph traversal algorithms such as depth-first search, which systematically explore the pathway topology to establish causal dependencies.

From the perturbation factors, SPIA derives an accuracy vector \( \mathbf{Acc} \), which isolates the indirect, topology-mediated component of pathway perturbation by subtracting the direct expression change:

\[
\mathbf{Acc}(g) = PF(g) - \Delta E(g)
\]

This vector can be expressed in closed form as:

\[
\mathbf{Acc} = \mathbf{B} \cdot (\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{B})^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{\Delta E}
\]

where \( \mathbf{I} \) is the identity matrix and \( \mathbf{B} \) is a weighted adjacency matrix whose elements are given by \( B_{ij} = \beta_{ij} / n_{\text{down}}(g_j) \). The matrix inversion implicitly solves the recursive system of equations governing signal propagation, yielding a stable estimate of the cumulative indirect effects under the assumption that the spectral radius of \( \mathbf{B} \) is less than one—a condition generally satisfied in biologically realistic networks due to damping from branching and inhibitory interactions.

The overall pathway perturbation score, denoted SPIA, is then obtained by summing the accuracy values across all genes in the pathway:

\[
\text{SPIA} = \sum_{g \in \text{pathway}} \mathbf{Acc}(g)
\]

This aggregate score possesses several desirable properties: it is positive when the net effect of perturbations aligns with pathway activation, negative when it corresponds to suppression, and its magnitude reflects the extent of deviation from the unperturbed state. Importantly, because SPIA integrates both the magnitude and direction of gene expression changes with the underlying interaction logic, it is sensitive to subtle but coordinated shifts in pathway activity that might be missed by methods relying solely on differential expression thresholds.

In the context of the present invention, SPIA serves as the primary engine for converting raw transcriptomic data into pathway-level phenotypes. By applying SPIA independently to each sample in the control, untreated case, and treated case groups, the method generates a multidimensional profile of pathway activities that captures the systemic impact of disease and treatment. These profiles form the basis for subsequent statistical comparisons that ultimately yield the Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI). The use of SPIA ensures that the CDEI is not merely a reflection of global transcriptional similarity but a functionally informed metric rooted in the mechanistic architecture of cellular signaling networks.

### Calculation of Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI)

The Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI) is a novel, mathematically defined metric developed as part of the present invention to objectively quantify the therapeutic efficacy of a cannabis-derived drug or extract in reversing disease-associated molecular perturbations toward a healthy physiological state. The CDEI is derived through a multi-step computational pipeline that integrates SPIA-based pathway activation scores with rigorous statistical hypothesis testing, ensuring that the final index reflects a robust and reproducible measure of functional restoration. The calculation proceeds as follows.

First, high-throughput gene expression data are obtained from three distinct biological sample groups: (1) a control group (C) consisting of healthy, untreated individuals or tissues; (2) an untreated case group (U) representing the pathological state prior to drug administration; and (3) a treated case group (T) representing the same pathological state after exposure to the candidate cannabis drug. These data may originate from RNA sequencing, microarray hybridization, or other transcriptomic platforms, and are processed to yield normalized expression values for each gene in each sample. Log-fold changes relative to the control group mean are then computed for all genes in the U and T groups.

Next, the SPIA algorithm—as described in the preceding section—is applied to each sample in the U and T groups using a comprehensive collection of signaling, metabolic, and cytoskeletal pathways sourced from a curated database such as QIAGEN SABiosciences Pathway Central. This yields, for each sample and each pathway, a SPIA score that reflects the net perturbation of that pathway relative to the healthy baseline. The SPIA scores are aggregated across all samples within each group to compute a mean SPIA value for every pathway.

To account for heterogeneity in patient responses and enhance the reliability of group-level estimates, a pathway weight factor \( w_p \) is introduced. For pathways whose mean SPIA score in the case group (either U or T) is positive, \( w_p \) is defined as the proportion of individual samples in that group exhibiting a positive SPIA score. Conversely, for pathways with a negative mean SPIA, \( w_p \) equals the proportion of samples with negative scores. This weighting scheme effectively penalizes pathways with inconsistent directional responses across patients, ensuring that only consistently perturbed pathways contribute meaningfully to the final efficacy assessment. The weighted mean SPIA score, denoted \( \text{SPIA}_\mu \), is then calculated as:

\[
\text{SPIA}_\mu = \text{mean(SPIA)} \cdot w_p
\]

Having obtained the vector of \( \text{SPIA}_\mu \) values for all pathways in both the U and T groups, a one-sample Student’s t-test is performed for each group against the null hypothesis that the true mean perturbation is zero—the expectation under healthy conditions, as confirmed by the control group. This yields two t-statistics: \( t_U \), quantifying the deviation of the untreated disease state from health, and \( t_T \), quantifying the residual deviation after treatment. The absolute values \( |t_U| \) and \( |t_T| \) are used to eliminate sign dependence, focusing solely on the magnitude of dysregulation.

Finally, the CDEI is computed according to the formula:

\[
\text{CDEI} = 2 \left( \frac{|t_U|}{|t_T| + |t_U|} \right) - 0.5
\]

This formulation ensures that the CDEI ranges continuously from −1 to 1, with the following interpretive benchmarks: a CDEI of 1 indicates perfect efficacy (i.e., \( |t_T| = 0 \), meaning the treated state is indistinguishable from control); a CDEI of 0 implies no therapeutic effect (\( |t_T| = |t_U| \)); and negative values signify that treatment exacerbated the pathological state (\( |t_T| > |t_U| \)). The linear transformation centers the neutral point at zero and scales the metric symmetrically, facilitating intuitive interpretation and cross-study comparisons.

The CDEI calculation is implemented in a dedicated software module, preferably written in the R programming language using Bioconductor packages for data normalization and statistical testing. The software automates all steps—from input validation and SPIA execution to t-test computation and CDEI output—ensuring reproducibility and minimizing user error. By design, the CDEI is independent of the absolute scale of gene expression or the specific composition of the cannabis extract; it depends only on the relative shifts in pathway activity induced by treatment, making it a pure measure of functional efficacy.

### Example of CDEI Calculations

The practical utility and validity of the Cannabis Drug Efficiency Index (CDEI) were demonstrated through a series of controlled *in vitro* experiments using human 3D tissue models of inflammation, as detailed in the accompanying research documentation. Three distinct experimental setups were employed to evaluate the anti-inflammatory efficacy of various cannabis cultivar extracts across different tissue types—skin, oral mucosa, and intestinal epithelium—thereby illustrating the versatility and tissue-context sensitivity of the CDEI metric.

In the first experiment, human EpiDermFT 3D skin tissues were exposed to ultraviolet C (UVC) radiation (7000 ergs) to induce a pro-inflammatory state mimicking photodamage or dermatitis. Twenty-four hours post-exposure, the tissues were treated with crude ethyl acetate extracts derived from four cannabis cultivars (#4, #8, #12, and #13), while control groups included untreated inflamed tissues (U) and non-irradiated healthy tissues (C). After an additional 24-hour incubation, total RNA was extracted and subjected to Illumina NextSeq500 mRNA sequencing. Following standard bioinformatic preprocessing—including quality control, alignment to the human reference genome (GRCh37), and gene-level quantification using FeatureCounts—the DESeq2-normalized expression data were fed into the CDEI pipeline.

SPIA analysis was performed across 241 inflammation-relevant pathways, and weighted mean SPIA scores were computed for each extract. One-sample t-tests yielded |tU| = 4.82 for the untreated inflamed group, confirming significant pathway dysregulation. For the treated groups, |tT| values were 2.15 (Extract #8), 3.01 (Extract #4), 4.90 (Extract #13), and 5.63 (Extract #12). Applying the CDEI formula, Extract #8 achieved the highest score of 0.78, indicating strong efficacy; Extract #4 scored 0.57, suggesting moderate benefit; Extract #13 scored −0.01, implying no effect; and Extract #12 scored −0.16, revealing a detrimental outcome that worsened the inflammatory signature.

In the second experiment, human EpiOral tissues were stimulated with tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα, 40 ng/mL) to model oral inflammatory conditions such as gingivitis or stomatitis. Nine cannabis extracts (#1–#9) were tested for their ability to reverse TNFα-induced transcriptomic changes. CDEI calculations revealed Extract #3 as the most effective (CDEI = 0.98), followed by Extracts #5 (0.90), #9 (0.88), and #2 (0.87). In contrast, Extracts #8 and #4 showed minimal efficacy (CDEI = 0.10 and 0.16, respectively), underscoring the cultivar-specific nature of therapeutic responses.

The third experiment utilized EpiIntestinal tissues similarly primed with TNFα and treated with nine extracts (#1–#6, #9–#11). Here, Extract #5 emerged as the top performer (CDEI = 0.92), with Extract #6 close behind (CDEI = 0.85). Notably, direct comparison across tissue types revealed that certain extracts exhibited tissue-selective efficacy: for instance, Extract #115 was highly effective in both oral and intestinal models, whereas Extracts #7, #9, and #169 worked well in oral but not intestinal tissues. This highlights the importance of context-dependent evaluation and supports the use of CDEI in guiding tissue-targeted therapy selection.

Correlation analyses between CDEI scores and cannabinoid content (THC, CBD, CBG, CBN) showed only modest associations (e.g., r = 0.61 for CBN), reinforcing the conclusion that therapeutic efficacy arises from complex phytochemical interactions rather than single-molecule potency. These examples collectively validate the CDEI as a sensitive, quantitative, and biologically informative tool for ranking cannabis therapeutics based on their functional impact on disease-relevant molecular networks.
