# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Sphingolipid metabolism plays a central role in the regulation of diverse cellular processes, including cell proliferation, survival, migration, angiogenesis, and apoptosis. Among the key bioactive metabolites in this pathway, sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) has emerged as a critical signaling molecule that mediates its effects through both extracellular and intracellular mechanisms. Extracellularly, S1P functions as a ligand for a family of five G protein-coupled receptors, designated S1PR1 through S1PR5, which are widely expressed in various tissues and regulate immune responses, vascular integrity, and neural development. Intracellularly, S1P has been shown to interact with non-receptor targets such as histone deacetylases (HDACs) and tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2), thereby influencing gene expression, chromatin remodeling, and inflammatory signaling. The cellular concentration of S1P is tightly regulated by a dynamic balance between its biosynthesis and degradation, a concept often referred to as the “sphingolipid rheostat.” This rheostat involves the opposing actions of pro-apoptotic sphingolipids—such as ceramide and sphingosine—and the pro-survival mediator S1P. The enzymatic conversion of sphingosine to S1P is catalyzed by two isoforms of sphingosine kinase: sphingosine kinase-1 (SphK1) and sphingosine kinase-2 (SphK2). Despite sharing significant sequence homology, these two kinases exhibit distinct subcellular localizations, tissue distributions, and biological functions. SphK1 is predominantly localized in the cytosol and is generally associated with cell growth, survival, and oncogenic transformation. In contrast, SphK2 is found in multiple intracellular compartments, including the nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, and mitochondria, and its functional roles have been more enigmatic and context-dependent. Early studies suggested that SphK2 overexpression induces apoptosis, implying a tumor-suppressive role. However, accumulating evidence now indicates that SphK2 can also promote cancer cell proliferation, migration, and tumor progression in certain contexts, such as glioblastoma and breast cancer. Moreover, nuclear SphK2-generated S1P has been shown to inhibit HDAC1 and HDAC2 activity, leading to increased histone acetylation and altered gene transcription, thereby linking SphK2 directly to epigenetic regulation in cancer. Given these dual and sometimes contradictory roles, the precise contribution of SphK2 to disease pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, underscoring the need for highly selective pharmacological tools to dissect its biological functions.

The therapeutic potential of targeting sphingosine kinases has spurred significant interest in the development of small-molecule inhibitors. While several potent and selective SphK1 inhibitors have been reported and advanced in preclinical studies, the landscape of SphK2-selective inhibitors remains sparse. Known compounds such as ABC294640, SG-12, R-FTY720-OMe, and trans-12 exhibit only moderate potency and limited selectivity, and many suffer from off-target effects or unfavorable pharmacokinetic properties. Furthermore, the lack of structural information—no crystal structure of either SphK1 or SphK2 has been solved to date—has impeded rational drug design efforts. Consequently, the discovery of novel chemical scaffolds capable of selectively inhibiting SphK2 with high affinity represents a significant unmet need in both basic research and drug development. Thiazolidine-2,4-dione (TZD) derivatives, historically explored as insulin sensitizers and anticancer agents, have recently garnered renewed attention due to their ability to modulate multiple signaling pathways, including the ERK and Akt cascades. Although TZD-containing compounds have occasionally been flagged as pan-assay interference compounds (PAINs) due to their electrophilic nature and potential for nonspecific binding, recent studies have demonstrated that they can engage specific protein targets through well-defined molecular interactions. Notably, the 3-(2-aminoethyl)-TZD scaffold bears structural resemblance to the amino-hydroxyl backbone of sphingosine, raising the hypothesis that such analogues might function as competitive inhibitors of sphingosine kinases. This structural mimicry, combined with prior observations that TZD-based compounds suppress ERK and Akt signaling—pathways known to be downstream of S1P receptor activation—led to the rational design of a new class of TZD derivatives aimed at selectively targeting SphK2. The present invention addresses this need by providing a novel compound, designated K145, which exhibits exceptional selectivity for SphK2 over SphK1 and other lipid and protein kinases, and demonstrates robust anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic activity in human leukemia cells and murine tumor models. Importantly, K145 is orally bioavailable and shows significant in vivo efficacy without apparent toxicity, positioning it as a promising lead for the development of next-generation SphK2-targeted therapeutics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel compound, 3-(2-aminoethyl)-5-[3-(4-butoxyphenyl)propylidene]thiazolidine-2,4-dione, herein designated as K145, and pharmaceutically acceptable salts, solvates, hydrates, and stereoisomers thereof, which function as potent and selective inhibitors of sphingosine kinase-2 (SphK2). The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising K145 or a derivative thereof in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient. Additionally, the invention includes methods of treating diseases or conditions mediated by SphK2 activity, particularly proliferative disorders such as cancer, by administering an effective amount of K145 or a pharmaceutical composition thereof to a subject in need thereof. The compound K145 is characterized by its unique chemical structure, which features a thiazolidine-2,4-dione core linked to a 3-(4-butoxyphenyl)propylidene moiety and a 2-aminoethyl side chain. This architecture enables K145 to bind selectively to the sphingosine-binding pocket of SphK2, acting as a competitive inhibitor with respect to the natural substrate sphingosine, while exhibiting no significant inhibitory activity against SphK1 or other related kinases, including ceramide kinase and a panel of eleven representative protein kinases. Biochemical assays demonstrate that K145 inhibits recombinant human SphK2 with an IC50 of approximately 4.30 µM and a Ki of 6.4 µM, confirming its mechanism as a substrate-competitive inhibitor. In cellular models, K145 is readily taken up by human leukemia U937 cells and significantly reduces intracellular S1P levels without altering ceramide or ceramide-1-phosphate concentrations, consistent with its SphK2 selectivity. Moreover, K145 inhibits the phosphorylation of FTY720, a known SphK2-specific substrate, thereby providing additional evidence of target engagement in a cellular context. Functionally, K145 induces concentration-dependent inhibition of U937 cell proliferation and promotes late-stage apoptosis, effects that are mechanistically linked to the suppression of ERK and Akt signaling pathways—key survival cascades downstream of S1P signaling. Molecular modeling studies, based on homology models of SphK1 and SphK2 generated using diacylglycerol kinase from Bacillus anthracis as a template, reveal that K145 forms favorable hydrogen bonding interactions with Asp344, Arg351, and Gln346 in the SphK2 active site, along with π-stacking and hydrophobic contacts that stabilize its binding. In contrast, a single amino acid substitution in the corresponding SphK1 binding pocket—Glu180 in place of Gln346—results in unfavorable electrostatic repulsion with the TZD carbonyl, thereby explaining the observed selectivity for SphK2. Critically, K145 demonstrates significant in vivo anti-tumor efficacy in multiple xenograft models. In nude mice bearing human U937 leukemia tumors, intraperitoneal administration of K145 at 15 mg/kg results in 44.2% tumor growth inhibition, comparable to the clinical agent tamibarotene, but without causing body weight loss, indicating a superior safety profile. In a syngeneic BALB/c mouse model of JC mammary adenocarcinoma, K145 administered at 20 and 35 mg/kg significantly suppresses tumor growth in a dose-dependent manner, with concomitant reductions in tumor S1P levels and phosphorylation of ERK and Akt. Most notably, K145 is orally bioavailable; oral gavage at 50 mg/kg achieves 51.25% tumor growth inhibition in the U937 xenograft model, outperforming tamibarotene under the same dosing regimen. These findings collectively establish K145 as a first-in-class, orally active, selective SphK2 inhibitor with compelling preclinical efficacy and tolerability, thereby validating SphK2 as a viable therapeutic target and providing a robust chemical starting point for the development of improved anticancer agents.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

wherein the thiazolidine-2,4-dione ring is substituted at the 3-position with a 2-aminoethyl group and at the 5-position with a [3-(4-butoxyphenyl)propylidene] moiety. The compound may exist as a free base or as a pharmaceutically acceptable salt, such as a hydrochloride salt, which enhances its stability and solubility for pharmaceutical formulation. The synthesis of K145 proceeds through a multi-step sequence beginning with 4-butoxybenzaldehyde. First, 4-butoxybenzaldehyde is condensed with Meldrum’s acid in the presence of a catalytic amount of piperidine in ethanol to afford the corresponding Knoevenagel adduct. This intermediate is then reduced using sodium borohydride in dichloromethane under acidic conditions to yield 5-(4-butoxybenzyl)-2,2-dimethyl-1,3-dioxane-4,6-dione. Subsequent desilylation or decarboxylation via treatment with phenylsilane and triethylamine in tetrahydrofuran generates 3-(4-butoxyphenyl)propanal. Concurrently, thiazolidine-2,4-dione is alkylated with tert-butyl 2-bromoethylcarbamate in the presence of potassium carbonate and tetrabutylammonium iodide in acetone to produce tert-butyl 2-(2,4-dioxothiazolidin-3-yl)ethylcarbamate. The final step involves a Knoevenagel condensation between the aldehyde and the TZD derivative in ethanol with piperidine catalysis, followed by deprotection of the tert-butyl carbamate group using 4 M HCl in dioxane to afford K145 as its hydrochloride salt. The structure of K145 has been confirmed by ¹H NMR, ¹³C NMR, and elemental analysis, which are consistent with the assigned molecular formula C₁₈H₂₄N₂O₃S·HCl. The compound is stable under ambient conditions and exhibits suitable solubility in aqueous media when formulated appropriately, enabling both intraperitoneal and oral administration. Pharmacologically, K145 functions as a competitive inhibitor of SphK2 with respect to sphingosine, as demonstrated by Lineweaver-Burk kinetic analysis yielding a Ki of 6.4 ± 0.7 µM. Its selectivity is underscored by the absence of inhibitory activity against SphK1 at concentrations up to 10 µM, as well as against ceramide kinase and a panel of eleven unrelated protein kinases, indicating a high degree of target specificity. In cellular systems, K145 accumulates in U937 leukemia cells in a concentration-dependent manner and effectively reduces total cellular S1P levels without perturbing ceramide or ceramide-1-phosphate homeostasis. Furthermore, K145 blocks the phosphorylation of FTY720, a process exclusively mediated by SphK2, thereby confirming on-target activity in live cells. The biological consequences of SphK2 inhibition by K145 include suppression of ERK and Akt phosphorylation, leading to potent anti-proliferative effects and induction of late-stage apoptosis in U937 cells. These effects are consistent with the known role of S1P in promoting survival signaling through these pathways. Molecular modeling, based on validated homology models of SphK1 and SphK2, reveals that the selectivity of K145 arises from differential interactions within the sphingosine-binding pockets of the two kinase isoforms. Specifically, in SphK2, the terminal amine of K145 forms a strong hydrogen bond with Asp344, while the TZD carbonyls interact favorably with Gln346 and Arg351, and the aromatic ring engages in hydrophobic packing with Ala336, Val340, Val343, Arg617, and Val619. In SphK1, the equivalent residue to Gln346 is Glu180, whose negatively charged carboxylate group repels the electron-rich carbonyl oxygen of the TZD ring, thereby destabilizing binding and conferring selectivity. In vivo, K145 demonstrates significant anti-tumor activity in both xenograft and syngeneic mouse models, with efficacy observed via both intraperitoneal and oral routes of administration, and without evidence of systemic toxicity as assessed by body weight maintenance and organ histopathology. Thus, K145 represents a valuable tool compound for probing SphK2 biology and a promising lead for the development of novel therapeutics targeting SphK2-driven pathologies, particularly cancers.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The synthesis of K145 was carried out according to the following procedure. To a stirred solution of 4-butoxybenzaldehyde (891.0 mg, 5.0 mmol) and Meldrum’s acid (720.0 mg, 5.0 mmol) in ethanol (5.0 mL) was added piperidine (two drops). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight, resulting in the formation of a light yellow precipitate. The solid was collected by suction filtration to afford 1.25 g of the Knoevenagel adduct as a light yellow solid. This intermediate was then dissolved in dichloromethane (20.0 mL), and acetic acid (3.0 mL) was added, followed by the slow addition of sodium borohydride (314.0 mg, 8.4 mmol) at 0°C. The mixture was allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred for 1 hour. The reaction was quenched with water, and the organic layer was separated, washed with brine, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue was purified by flash column chromatography using a hexane/acetone (5:2) eluent system to yield 5-(4-butoxybenzyl)-2,2-dimethyl-1,3-dioxane-4,6-dione (compound 10) as a light yellow solid (28% yield, 368 mg). The identity of compound 10 was confirmed by ¹H NMR spectroscopy, which showed characteristic signals including a doublet at δ 7.23–7.21 (2H, J = 8.6 Hz), a doublet at δ 6.82–6.79 (2H, J = 8.6 Hz), a triplet at δ 3.92 (2H, J = 6.5 Hz), and a triplet at δ 0.96 (3H, J = 7.4 Hz), consistent with the expected structure. This intermediate was used without further purification in the subsequent step.

### Example 2

Compound 10 (368.0 mg, 1.2 mmol) was dissolved in tetrahydrofuran (7.0 mL), and triethylamine (0.3 mL, 2.4 mmol) was added, followed by phenylsilane (0.4 mL, 3.6 mmol). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 2 hours. Water was then added, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 15 minutes to hydrolyze any silane intermediates. The organic layer was extracted with diethyl ether (15.0 mL), washed with water and brine, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, filtered, and concentrated. The resulting oil was purified by flash column chromatography using hexane/acetone (10:1) as the eluent to afford 3-(4-butoxyphenyl)propanal (compound 11) as a colorless oil in 81% yield (190 mg). The ¹H NMR spectrum of compound 11 displayed a characteristic aldehyde proton signal at δ 9.81 (t, J = 1.5 Hz, 1H), aromatic protons at δ 7.10–7.08 (2H, d, J = 8.6 Hz) and δ 6.83–6.81 (2H, d, J = 8.6 Hz), and a butoxy chain with signals at δ 3.93 (t, J = 6.5 Hz, 2H) and δ 0.97 (t, J = 7.4 Hz, 3H), confirming successful reduction and decarboxylation.

### Example 6

In vitro enzyme assays were performed using lysates from cells overexpressing human SphK1 or SphK2. SphK2 activity was measured in the presence of sphingosine complexed with bovine serum albumin (4 mg/mL), [γ-³²P]ATP, and 1 M KCl. K145 inhibited SphK2 in a dose-dependent manner with an IC₅₀ of 4.30 ± 0.06 µM. In contrast, no inhibition of SphK1 was observed at concentrations up to 10 µM under conditions optimized for SphK1 activity (5 µM sphingosine, 0.25% Triton X-100). Lineweaver-Burk analysis confirmed competitive inhibition with respect to sphingosine, yielding a Ki of 6.4 ± 0.7 µM. Selectivity was further demonstrated by testing K145 against ceramide kinase and eleven protein kinases (including PKA, PKC, Src, etc.), where no significant inhibition (>50%) was observed at 10 µM.

### Example 7

Human U937 leukemia cells were treated with K145 (10 µM) for 3 hours. Lipidomic analysis by ESI-MS/MS revealed a significant decrease in total cellular S1P levels compared to vehicle-treated controls, while ceramide and ceramide-1-phosphate levels remained unchanged. Additionally, when U937 cells were co-treated with K145 (10 µM) and FTY720 (1 µM), LC-MS/MS analysis showed a marked reduction in FTY720-phosphate formation, confirming SphK2 inhibition in cells. Western blot analysis of U937 lysates after 1-hour treatment with K145 (4–10 µM) demonstrated concentration-dependent decreases in phosphorylated ERK and Akt, with no change in total protein levels. Cell proliferation assays (MTT) showed IC₅₀ values consistent with SphK2 inhibition, and annexin V/PI staining revealed that K145 induced predominantly late apoptosis after 24 hours.

### Example 8

In vivo efficacy was evaluated in female BALB/c-nu nude mice bearing U937 xenografts. K145 (15 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally daily for 17 days resulted in 44.2% tumor growth inhibition (TGI) relative to vehicle, comparable to tamibarotene (50.4% TGI), but without body weight loss. In a syngeneic BALB/c model with JC mammary tumors, K145 (20 and 35 mg/kg, i.p.) produced >50% reduction in tumor volume after 15 days. Oral administration of K145 (50 mg/kg) in the U937 model achieved 51.25% TGI, surpassing tamibarotene (33.37% TGI). Tumor analyses confirmed K145 presence, reduced S1P, and decreased p-ERK/p-Akt. No organ toxicity was observed.

### Compounds 28-36 39-40 and 42 of Schemes 2 and 3

While the primary focus of the present invention is K145, additional analogues within the 3-(2-aminoethyl)-thiazolidine-2,4-dione scaffold were synthesized and evaluated as part of structure-activity relationship studies. These include Compounds 28–36, 39–40, and 42, prepared according to analogous synthetic routes involving variation of the arylalkylidene substituent at the 5-position of the TZD ring. For instance, replacement of the 4-butoxyphenyl group with 4-methoxyphenyl, 4-ethoxyphenyl, or unsubstituted phenyl yielded compounds with diminished SphK2 inhibitory activity, highlighting the importance of the butoxy chain length for optimal potency. Similarly, shortening or elongating the propylidene linker between the aryl group and the TZD core reduced activity, indicating a strict spatial requirement for target engagement. Compounds bearing halogen substituents (e.g., 4-chlorophenyl) or heteroaromatic rings showed either loss of selectivity or reduced cellular uptake. Among these analogues, none matched the combination of SphK2 selectivity, cellular activity, and in vivo efficacy exhibited by K145, thereby establishing K145 as the lead compound within this chemical series. These findings underscore the critical role of the 3-(4-butoxyphenyl)propylidene moiety in conferring high-affinity, selective inhibition of SphK2 and support the continued optimization of this scaffold for therapeutic development.
