# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF INVENTION

The field of rotating machinery has long relied upon mechanical bearings to support the rotor within the stator, enabling smooth and stable operation across a wide range of industrial, commercial, and aerospace applications. Mechanical bearings—whether rolling-element, fluid-film, or magnetic in nature—serve the essential purpose of constraining the degrees of freedom of the rotor so that it may rotate about a fixed axis without excessive vibration, wear, or instability. In conventional systems, this approach is both practical and economical, particularly when routine maintenance is feasible and operating conditions do not impose extreme demands on bearing performance. However, as technological requirements have evolved toward higher rotational speeds, more compact designs, and operation in inaccessible or hostile environments, the limitations of traditional mechanical bearings have become increasingly apparent, prompting the development of alternative suspension strategies.

One such alternative is the use of active magnetic bearings (AMBs), which employ electromagnetic forces to levitate the rotor without physical contact. These systems typically consist of separate radial and axial magnetic actuators strategically positioned along the rotor shaft to provide full five-axis control—two translational degrees of freedom in each of two radial planes (x and y at two axial locations) and one axial degree of freedom (z). While AMBs eliminate friction, wear, and the need for lubrication, they introduce significant drawbacks in terms of system complexity, weight, volume, and cost. Specifically, the iron cores and copper windings used in magnetic bearings do not contribute to torque production; they exist solely to generate levitation forces. This functional segregation results in a longer axial machine length, which in turn lowers the natural bending frequencies of the rotor. For high-speed applications—such as flywheel energy storage systems in spacecraft or turbomachinery in jet engines—this reduction in structural stiffness can be detrimental, as it brings the operational speed dangerously close to or beyond the first critical bending mode, leading to severe dynamic instabilities that are difficult to control.

To address these challenges, researchers have pursued integrated bearingless motor architectures that combine motoring and levitation functions within a single electromagnetic structure. In such designs, the same stator iron and windings are used to produce both torque and levitation forces, thereby reducing overall size, weight, and material usage while improving dynamic performance. Early bearingless motor concepts achieved this integration by incorporating dedicated levitation windings with a different pole count than the main torque-producing windings. This approach, implemented in various motor topologies including induction, permanent magnet synchronous, switched reluctance, and synchronous reluctance machines, decouples the control of torque and levitation to some extent by exploiting the orthogonality of magnetic fields generated by windings with dissimilar spatial harmonics. While effective, this method still partitions the available copper between motoring and levitation functions, limiting the system’s ability to dynamically allocate electrical resources based on real-time operational demands. For instance, during periods of high levitation load (e.g., during rapid acceleration or under external disturbance), the fixed allocation of winding capacity may constrain the available force, or conversely, during high-torque operation, levitation capability may be compromised.

Moreover, the requirement for multiple pole counts increases winding complexity and reduces manufacturing flexibility. The presence of additional windings also introduces cross-coupling effects between torque and levitation channels, necessitating sophisticated decoupling control strategies that can be sensitive to parameter variations and modeling inaccuracies. These limitations highlight a fundamental trade-off in prior art: either accept the inefficiency and bulk of separate magnetic bearings, or adopt an integrated design that sacrifices full utilization of all conductive and magnetic materials for both functions simultaneously.

Another critical limitation of existing bearingless motors lies in their inability to provide full five-axis levitation using only a single motor stage. Most reported designs achieve only radial levitation (two or four axes), requiring a separate axial actuator or mechanical thrust bearing to constrain axial motion. This undermines the goal of complete contactless suspension and reintroduces points of potential failure or maintenance. Achieving axial force generation within the same electromagnetic structure as radial forces demands a novel geometric and electromagnetic configuration—one that can translate controlled imbalances in magnetic flux into forces with both radial and axial vector components.

The present invention arises from the recognition that full five-axis levitation can be achieved without dedicated levitation windings or separate actuators by fundamentally rethinking the electrical connectivity and spatial arrangement of the motor windings. Specifically, by disconnecting the individual pole pairs of a multi-pole motor and independently controlling the d-axis current in each pole pair’s rotor reference frame, it becomes possible to create deliberate, controllable imbalances in the magnetic flux distribution around the rotor periphery. These imbalances generate net lateral forces that can be steered in any radial direction. Furthermore, by shaping the air gap between rotor and stator into a conical geometry, these radial force vectors acquire an axial component, enabling direct control of axial position through coordinated manipulation of d-axis currents in opposing conical sections. This dual innovation—pole-pair-separated winding architecture combined with conical air-gap topology—enables a truly integrated, fully levitating motor in which every gram of copper and every cubic centimeter of iron contributes dynamically to either torque production, levitation, or both, as dictated by the control system.

Importantly, this approach does not require windings with different pole numbers, simplifying manufacturing and eliminating cross-harmonic interference. It leverages standard field-oriented control (FOC) principles but extends them to a distributed, multi-channel architecture where each pole pair is treated as an independent electromagnetic actuator. The resulting system offers unprecedented flexibility: during steady-state rotation, minimal d-axis current is used for levitation, maximizing efficiency; during transient disturbances or high-force maneuvers, additional current can be diverted from torque production to levitation without hardware modification. Additionally, the independent drive of each pole pair confers inherent fault tolerance—if one power electronics channel fails, the remaining channels can compensate by redistributing force commands, maintaining levitation and operation albeit at reduced performance.

Prior art has not contemplated this combination of disconnected pole-pair windings and conical air gaps as a means to achieve full five-axis levitation with complete material utilization. Conventional wisdom holds that motor windings must be symmetrically connected to avoid parasitic forces and vibrations; the present invention deliberately violates this principle in a controlled, feedback-regulated manner to harness those very forces for levitation. Similarly, while conical rotors have been used in some sensor or actuator applications, their integration into a bearingless motor for simultaneous radial and axial force generation represents a novel application of geometric asymmetry to solve a longstanding control challenge.

Thus, there exists a clear and unmet need in the art for a bearingless motor architecture that minimizes axial length, maximizes material efficiency, provides full five-axis levitation without auxiliary actuators, and offers dynamic resource allocation between motoring and levitation functions. The present invention fulfills this need through a synergistic combination of electromagnetic, geometric, and control innovations that together enable a new class of high-performance, maintenance-free rotating machinery suitable for the most demanding applications—from space-based flywheels to downhole drilling tools and hypersonic propulsion systems.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

The present invention provides a fully magnetically levitated bearingless motor capable of five-axis suspension and torque production using a single integrated electromagnetic structure, wherein all stator iron and copper windings contribute dynamically to either levitation or rotation as required by operational demands. This is achieved through two principal innovations: (1) a pole-pair-separated winding architecture in which the individual pole pairs of the motor stator are electrically isolated and independently driven, enabling precise control of magnetic flux imbalance around the rotor periphery to generate controllable radial levitation forces; and (2) a conical air-gap geometry that imparts an axial component to these radial forces, allowing coordinated control of axial position through differential manipulation of d-axis currents in opposing conical motor sections.

In accordance with the invention, the stator windings are configured such that each pole pair forms an independent three-phase subsystem, with no internal electrical connections between pole pairs. This contrasts sharply with conventional motor designs, where pole pairs are connected in series or parallel to ensure uniform current distribution and force balance. By decoupling the pole pairs, the invention enables independent field-oriented control of the d-axis and q-axis currents in the rotor reference frame for each pole pair. Torque is produced through conventional q-axis current control, while levitation forces are generated by modulating the d-axis current in each pole pair to create a controlled asymmetry in the permanent magnet flux linkage. This flux imbalance results in a net electromagnetic force on the rotor, the magnitude and direction of which can be precisely regulated by adjusting the relative d-axis currents across the pole pairs.

The conical shape of the air gap—defined by a taper in either the stator bore or rotor outer diameter—ensures that any radial force vector generated by the flux imbalance possesses a non-zero axial projection. By arranging two such conical motor sections on a common rotor shaft with opposing cone angles (i.e., one section tapered upward and the other downward), the invention enables independent control of net axial force. Specifically, adding a uniform d-axis current offset to all pole pairs in the upper conical section while subtracting the same offset from all pole pairs in the lower section produces a pure axial force without altering the net radial force vector. This decoupling of axial and radial force control is a critical feature that enables full five-axis levitation using only the motor windings, eliminating the need for separate axial magnetic bearings.

The control system implements a hierarchical architecture comprising inner current loops, intermediate force generation blocks, and outer position/speed loops. At the highest level, rotor position sensors provide feedback on x, y, and z displacements, which are compared against reference trajectories to generate desired force commands. These force commands are decomposed into contributions from individual pole pairs using a force basis derived from precomputed or real-time magnetic circuit models. The decomposition accounts for the spatial orientation of each pole pair relative to the rotor angle, ensuring that the resultant force vector matches the commanded direction and magnitude. Axial force commands are processed separately but are compensated for unintended coupling effects arising from radial force generation, using an axial stiffness model that relates the difference in total d-axis current between upper and lower conical sections to net axial force.

A key advantage of the invention is its complete utilization of all conductive and magnetic materials. Unlike prior bearingless motors that reserve specific windings for levitation, the present design allows every turn of copper to contribute to torque, levitation, or a combination thereof, depending on instantaneous control needs. This dynamic allocation improves power density, reduces weight, and enhances fault tolerance. In the event of a power electronics failure affecting one pole pair, the control system can redistribute force commands among the remaining functional channels, maintaining levitation and continued operation.

The invention is applicable to a broad range of motor topologies, including permanent magnet synchronous, synchronous reluctance, and induction machines, provided that the rotor carries a source of magnetic flux (either from permanent magnets or induced currents) that can be modulated by stator d-axis currents. The prototype described herein employs surface-mounted permanent magnets on a conical rotor, but the underlying principles extend to interior permanent magnet, wound-rotor, or even salient-pole reluctance configurations.

Experimental validation confirms that the invention achieves stable five-axis levitation and rotation, with measured force responses closely matching predictions from magnetic circuit and finite element models. The system demonstrates robustness to mechanical resonances and external disturbances, and the conical geometry successfully enables independent axial control without degrading radial performance. These results establish the feasibility of a new class of ultra-compact, high-speed, maintenance-free rotating machinery suitable for aerospace, energy, and industrial applications where reliability, efficiency, and dynamic performance are paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention will now be described in detail with reference to its preferred embodiment, which comprises a dual-conical, pole-pair-separated permanent magnet bearingless motor. It should be understood, however, that the principles disclosed herein are not limited to this specific implementation and may be adapted to other electromagnetic machine topologies, including but not limited to synchronous reluctance motors, induction motors, and switched reluctance motors, as long as the fundamental mechanism of flux imbalance via independent d-axis current control is preserved.

The motor assembly includes a rotor mounted for rotation about a central axis, and a stator surrounding the rotor with a defined air gap therebetween. Critically, the air gap is not cylindrical but conical, meaning that the radial distance between rotor and stator varies linearly with axial position. In the preferred embodiment, two identical conical motor sections are arranged back-to-back on a common rotor shaft, with the upper section having a positive cone angle (i.e., the air gap widens in the +z direction) and the lower section having a negative cone angle (air gap widens in the –z direction). This symmetric arrangement ensures that radial forces generated in each section can be vectorially summed to produce a net radial force in any direction in the x-y plane, while differential control of the two sections enables pure axial force generation.

The rotor is constructed with a ferromagnetic core and carries permanent magnets mounted on its conical surface. The magnets are arranged in a multi-pole pattern, with the number of pole pairs denoted as *p*. In the prototype, *p* = 3, yielding six magnetic poles per conical section. The stator comprises a laminated iron core with teeth and slots configured to accommodate windings. Each conical section contains *p* pole pairs, and for each pole pair, a complete three-phase winding set is provided. Thus, for *p* = 3, there are three independent three-phase winding groups per conical section, totaling six three-phase groups for the entire motor (three in the upper cone, three in the lower cone).

Crucially, these winding groups are electrically isolated from one another. That is, the windings corresponding to pole pair 1 are not connected to those of pole pair 2 or 3; each forms a standalone three-phase circuit. This pole-pair-separated architecture is a foundational aspect of the invention and departs radically from conventional motor winding practices, where all pole pairs are interconnected to ensure balanced magnetic fields and zero net lateral force. Here, the intentional disconnection permits independent control of the magnetic state of each pole pair.

Each three-phase winding group is connected to its own power electronic inverter stage. Given that each three-phase group requires six semiconductor switches (e.g., IGBTs or MOSFETs) in a standard H-bridge configuration, the total switch count for the prototype is 6 groups × 6 switches = 36 switches. However, as noted in the research paper, the voltage and current ratings per switch can be reduced proportionally, potentially offsetting the increase in device count through the use of smaller, lower-voltage components. Moreover, the distributed nature of the drive enhances fault tolerance: if one inverter leg fails, the associated pole pair can be disabled, and the control system can recalculate force commands using the remaining functional groups.

Operation of the motor relies on field-oriented control (FOC) extended to the multi-pole-pair domain. A position encoder or resolver provides real-time measurement of the rotor’s angular position, θ. This signal is used to transform the measured phase currents of each winding group into the rotor reference frame (d-q frame) for that specific pole pair. Because each pole pair occupies a fixed angular sector around the stator circumference, the transformation angle for pole pair *k* is given by θ_k = θ + (2π(k−1))/p, where *k* = 1, 2, ..., *p*. This accounts for the spatial offset between pole pairs.

In the d-q frame, the q-axis current (i_q,k) governs torque production, while the d-axis current (i_d,k) modulates the magnetic flux linkage from the permanent magnets. In a conventional surface-mounted permanent magnet motor, i_d is typically held at zero to maximize torque per ampere (maximum torque per ampere, MTPA). In the present invention, however, i_d,k is actively controlled to adjust the effective air-gap flux density under each pole pair. A positive i_d,k (aligned with the magnet flux) increases the total flux, while a negative i_d,k (opposing the magnet flux) decreases it. Because the rotor is symmetric but the stator excitation is now asymmetric due to differing i_d,k values, a net lateral force arises.

This force originates from the Maxwell stress tensor acting on the rotor surface. When the flux density is higher on one side of the rotor than the opposite side, the resulting pressure imbalance integrates to a net force vector. The direction of this force aligns with the gradient of flux density, which can be steered by selecting appropriate combinations of i_d,k across the *p* pole pairs. For *p* = 3, there are six basic force vectors available (positive and negative i_d for each of three pole pairs), which span the x-y plane and define six sectors. Any desired radial force within a sector can be synthesized by linear combination of the two bounding vectors.

The relationship between i_d,k and force is characterized by a "current stiffness" parameter, *k_i*, defined as the force produced per unit d-axis current. This parameter is determined through magnetic circuit modeling or finite element analysis (FEA) and may vary slightly with rotor angle due to slotting effects, but is treated as constant for control purposes. The force vector **F**_radial is thus computed as:

**F**_radial = *k_i* Σ_{k=1}^p i_d,k **u**_k(θ)

where **u**_k(θ) is a unit vector in the direction of the force produced by pole pair *k* at rotor angle θ. The functions **u**_k(θ) are periodic with period 2π/p and are precomputed or fitted to analytical expressions.

For axial force generation, the conical geometry plays a decisive role. In a conical air gap, the normal vector to the rotor surface has both radial and axial components. Consequently, the electromagnetic force derived from flux imbalance, which acts normal to the surface, also has axial projection. The axial force component from a single conical section is proportional to the sum of all i_d,k in that section, scaled by an axial stiffness *k_z*. Therefore, if the upper conical section has total d-axis current I_d,upper = Σ i_d,k,upper and the lower section has I_d,lower = Σ i_d,k,lower, then the net axial force is:

F_z = *k_z* (I_d,upper − I_d,lower)

This equation reveals that a pure axial force (with no change in radial force) can be generated by adding a constant Δi to all i_d,k in the upper section and subtracting Δi from all i_d,k in the lower section. Since this operation preserves the relative differences among i_d,k within each section, the radial force vector remains unchanged, while F_z varies linearly with Δi.

The control system exploits this property through a dedicated axial force block. During operation, the radial position controller computes the i_d,k values needed to track x and y references. These values inherently produce some unintended F_z due to imbalance between upper and lower sections. The axial controller measures the desired F_z command (from z-position error), subtracts the unintended component, and computes the required Δi to apply as a common-mode offset to the upper and lower d-axis currents. This compensation ensures decoupled control of radial and axial dynamics.

The complete control loop operates as follows: outer-loop PID controllers for x, y, and z positions generate force commands F_x, F_y, F_z. The radial force vector (F_x, F_y) is decomposed into i_d,k contributions using the inverse of the force basis matrix formed by **u**_k(θ). Simultaneously, the axial force command is adjusted for coupling and converted to Δi. The resulting i_d,k and i_q,k references are transformed back to the stationary frame for each winding group and sent to the respective inverters. Inner current controllers regulate the actual phase currents to track these references, closing the loop at high bandwidth.

Experimental results from the prototype validate this approach. The motor was levitated in five axes and rotated up to 5,000 rpm without mechanical contact. Step responses in x, y, and z showed stable tracking with minimal overshoot. Frequency response tests revealed adequate gain and phase margins, and the system rejected external disturbances effectively. Minor discrepancies between simulation and experiment were traced to mechanical resonances in the test fixture, not the motor itself, confirming the fidelity of the electromagnetic model.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, efficient, and fully integrated solution for contactless rotating machinery. By combining pole-pair-separated windings with conical air gaps and advanced multi-channel field-oriented control, it achieves what prior art could not: full five-axis levitation using only the motor’s intrinsic electromagnetic structure, with complete and dynamic utilization of all materials. This breakthrough enables lighter, shorter, faster, and more reliable machines for the most challenging applications.
