# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention resides in the field of microbiology, biochemistry, and agricultural biotechnology, specifically relating to novel microbial secondary metabolites with iron-chelating properties and their application in promoting plant growth and health. More particularly, the invention concerns a newly identified siderophore compound produced by Gram-negative bacteria of the genus *Paraburkholderia*, herein designated as “gramibactin,” along with methods for its production, isolation, structural characterization, and use in agricultural and environmental contexts. The invention further encompasses compositions comprising gramibactin, formulations for its delivery to plants or soil, and methods of enhancing iron uptake in plants under iron-limiting conditions through the application of gramibactin or gramibactin-producing bacterial strains. Additionally, the invention includes diagnostic and analytical methods for detecting and quantifying gramibactin, as well as genetic constructs and engineered microorganisms capable of producing gramibactin for commercial or research purposes. The field also extends to the role of gramibactin in microbial ecology, particularly in rhizosphere interactions between plant growth-promoting bacteria and host plants, and its potential utility in sustainable agriculture as an alternative or supplement to synthetic iron chelates.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Iron is an essential micronutrient for virtually all living organisms, serving as a cofactor in critical enzymatic reactions involved in respiration, DNA synthesis, and photosynthesis. Despite its abundance in the Earth’s crust, iron is largely unavailable to biological systems under aerobic and neutral pH conditions due to its tendency to form insoluble ferric (Fe³⁺) hydroxides. In terrestrial and aquatic environments, the concentration of bioavailable iron can be as low as 10⁻¹⁸ M, far below the 10⁻⁶ M typically required for microbial and plant growth. To overcome this limitation, microorganisms have evolved sophisticated iron acquisition strategies, the most prominent of which involves the biosynthesis and secretion of low-molecular-weight compounds known as siderophores. Siderophores are high-affinity iron(III)-chelating agents that solubilize environmental iron and facilitate its transport into cells via specific receptor-mediated uptake systems. Over 500 structurally diverse siderophores have been characterized to date, falling into major chemical classes such as catecholates, hydroxamates, and carboxylates, each exhibiting distinct coordination chemistries and affinities for Fe³⁺.

In the context of plant-microbe interactions, siderophore-producing rhizobacteria play a pivotal role in enhancing plant iron nutrition—a process often referred to as “siderophore-mediated iron uptake” or “the green pathway.” Certain plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR), when colonizing plant roots, secrete siderophores that chelate Fe³⁺ from the soil. The resulting Fe³⁺-siderophore complexes can either be directly taken up by plant roots via yet-to-be-fully-elucidated mechanisms or indirectly benefit the plant by depriving phytopathogens of essential iron, thereby conferring biocontrol activity. Notably, some plants, particularly grasses (Poaceae), produce their own phytosiderophores (e.g., mugineic acids) to acquire iron under deficiency conditions. However, non-graminaceous plants, including many economically important crops such as tomatoes, soybeans, and Arabidopsis, lack this capability and rely heavily on microbial siderophores or soil acidification for iron acquisition.

Historically, the majority of well-characterized bacterial siderophores have been isolated from pathogenic or soil-dwelling bacteria such as *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* (producing pyoverdine and pyochelin), *Bacillus subtilis* (producing bacillibactin), and *Escherichia coli* (producing enterobactin). Enterobactin, a cyclic trilactone of 2,3-dihydroxybenzoylserine, remains one of the strongest known Fe³⁺ chelators, with a formation constant (Kf) exceeding 10⁴⁹. However, its utility in agricultural settings is limited due to rapid hydrolysis at neutral to alkaline pH and susceptibility to degradation by plant-derived esterases. Similarly, hydroxamate-type siderophores like ferrichrome exhibit moderate stability but often lack the specificity or efficiency required for effective iron delivery in complex soil matrices.

Recent advances in metagenomics and genome mining have revealed a vast, untapped reservoir of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in environmental bacteria, suggesting that many novel siderophores remain undiscovered. Among these, members of the genus *Paraburkholderia*—formerly classified within *Burkholderia* but reclassified based on phylogenomic analyses—have emerged as prolific producers of secondary metabolites with ecological and biotechnological relevance. *Paraburkholderia* species are commonly found in the rhizosphere of diverse plants, where they contribute to nutrient cycling, stress tolerance, and disease suppression. Strain *Paraburkholderia graminis* DSM 11363, originally isolated from the rhizosphere of *Phalaris arundinacea* (reed canary grass), has been shown to promote plant growth under iron-deficient conditions, yet the molecular basis for this phenotype remained unclear prior to the present invention.

Despite extensive research into microbial iron acquisition, there exists a significant gap in the art regarding siderophores that are both highly stable under agronomic conditions (e.g., neutral to alkaline pH, presence of competing cations) and efficiently recognized by plant uptake systems. Many known siderophores suffer from poor environmental persistence, low bioavailability to plants, or unintended ecological consequences such as promoting the growth of opportunistic pathogens. Furthermore, synthetic iron chelates like ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) or ethylenediaminedi(o-hydroxyphenylacetic acid) (EDDHA), while widely used in agriculture, are non-biodegradable, potentially toxic, and increasingly regulated due to environmental concerns. There is thus a pressing need in the art for novel, naturally derived, biodegradable iron-chelating agents that can enhance plant iron nutrition sustainably and safely.

Prior attempts to harness microbial siderophores for agricultural use have been hampered by difficulties in large-scale production, structural instability, and incomplete understanding of plant-siderophore interactions. For instance, although pyoverdine from *Pseudomonas* spp. has demonstrated plant growth-promoting effects, its complex structure and strain-specific variability complicate standardization and regulatory approval. Moreover, no siderophore has been conclusively shown to be directly assimilated by non-graminaceous plants via dedicated transporters, raising questions about the mechanism of action in many reported cases. Consequently, the identification of a new siderophore with favorable physicochemical properties, compatibility with plant physiology, and producible by non-pathogenic, environmentally benign bacteria represents a significant advancement over the prior art.

The present invention addresses these unmet needs by disclosing gramibactin—a previously unknown macrocyclic siderophore produced by *Paraburkholderia graminis*—which exhibits exceptional iron-binding affinity, remarkable stability across a broad pH range, and demonstrable efficacy in promoting the growth of iron-deficient plants. Unlike classical catecholate or hydroxamate siderophores, gramibactin features a unique structural motif involving nitroso-functionalized aromatic residues arranged in a conformation that enables hexadentate coordination of Fe³⁺. This unprecedented architecture not only confers superior metal selectivity but also resists enzymatic and chemical degradation, making gramibactin uniquely suited for agricultural applications. The discovery of gramibactin fills a critical void in the art and provides a foundation for developing next-generation biofertilizers and soil amendments that leverage natural microbial chemistry to improve crop productivity under iron-limiting conditions.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel siderophore compound, herein named gramibactin, which is produced by certain strains of *Paraburkholderia*, particularly *Paraburkholderia graminis* DSM 11363. Gramibactin is characterized by a unique macrocyclic structure comprising two modified aromatic amino acid residues bearing nitroso functional groups, enabling high-affinity, hexadentate chelation of ferric iron (Fe³⁺). The invention further encompasses isolated and purified gramibactin, pharmaceutical and agricultural compositions containing gramibactin, and methods for using gramibactin to enhance iron uptake and promote growth in plants, especially under iron-deficient conditions. Additionally, the invention includes gramibactin-producing bacterial strains, recombinant microorganisms engineered to express the gramibactin biosynthetic gene cluster, and methods for the large-scale fermentation and purification of gramibactin. Diagnostic assays for detecting and quantifying gramibactin in environmental or biological samples are also disclosed. In one embodiment, the invention provides a method of treating iron chlorosis in non-graminaceous plants by applying gramibactin or a gramibactin-producing bacterium to the plant or its growing medium. In another embodiment, gramibactin is formulated as a component of a biofertilizer or soil amendment to improve iron bioavailability in calcareous or alkaline soils. The invention also covers the use of gramibactin in microbial ecology studies to investigate rhizosphere iron dynamics and plant-microbe signaling. Structurally, gramibactin is defined by its molecular formula, spectroscopic signatures (including NMR and mass spectrometry data), and its ability to form a stable 1:1 complex with Fe³⁺ with a dissociation constant in the femtomolar range. The biosynthetic pathway of gramibactin has been elucidated, and the core genes responsible for its production—designated *gbtA* through *gbtG*—have been identified and cloned, enabling heterologous expression in suitable microbial hosts. The invention thus represents a comprehensive platform for the production, application, and study of a new class of environmentally compatible, high-performance siderophores with significant potential in sustainable agriculture and bioremediation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to gramibactin, a novel siderophore isolated from *Paraburkholderia graminis* DSM 11363, and its various applications. Gramibactin is a macrocyclic compound that functions as a high-affinity chelator of ferric iron, facilitating iron acquisition by the producing bacterium and, unexpectedly, by co-cultivated plants. The compound was discovered during investigations into the molecular basis of plant growth promotion by *P. graminis* under iron-limited conditions. Through a combination of culture-based screening, analytical chemistry, and genomic analysis, gramibactin was identified as the key metabolite responsible for the observed phenotypic effects.

Gramibactin is produced when *P. graminis* is grown in iron-depleted media, consistent with the typical regulation of siderophore biosynthesis by Fur-family transcriptional repressors that respond to intracellular iron levels. The compound is secreted into the extracellular milieu, where it binds Fe³⁺ with extraordinary affinity, forming a stable octahedral complex. Structural elucidation revealed that gramibactin consists of a 22-membered macrocyclic ring formed by two identical tetrapeptide-like subunits, each containing a modified phenylalanine residue in which the para position of the aromatic ring is functionalized with a nitroso group (–N=O). These nitrosoaryl moieties serve as bidentate ligands, each coordinating Fe³⁺ through the nitrogen and oxygen atoms, thereby providing four coordination sites. The remaining two coordination sites are occupied by carbonyl oxygen atoms from amide linkages within the macrocycle, completing the hexadentate coordination sphere. This arrangement results in a highly preorganized binding pocket that minimizes entropic penalties upon metal binding, contributing to the exceptional thermodynamic stability of the Fe³⁺–gramibactin complex.

The molecular formula of apo-gramibactin (iron-free form) is C₄₀H₄₈N₁₀O₁₂, with a calculated monoisotopic mass of 848.3472 Da. Upon complexation with Fe³⁺, the formula becomes C₄₀H₄₅FeN₁₀O₁₂, with a mass of 913.2789 Da. High-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) confirmed these masses with error margins below 2 ppm. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, including ¹H, ¹³C, COSY, HSQC, and HMBC experiments, was employed to assign all proton and carbon resonances and establish connectivity within the molecule. Key diagnostic signals include downfield-shifted aromatic protons adjacent to the nitroso group (δ ~8.5 ppm) and characteristic amide NH resonances (δ ~10–11 ppm). The macrocyclic nature of gramibactin was confirmed by the absence of free N- or C-termini in MS/MS fragmentation patterns and by the observation of through-space NOE correlations consistent with a folded conformation.

Gramibactin exhibits remarkable stability under a wide range of environmental conditions. It remains intact in aqueous solution from pH 3 to pH 10, with no detectable hydrolysis or decomposition over 30 days at 25°C. This contrasts sharply with catecholate siderophores like enterobactin, which undergo rapid lactone hydrolysis above pH 7. Furthermore, gramibactin is resistant to degradation by common plant esterases and proteases, as demonstrated by incubation with root exudates from *Arabidopsis thaliana* and soybean. The Fe³⁺–gramibactin complex is also highly selective for iron over other divalent and trivalent cations commonly found in soil, such as Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, and Mn²⁺, as confirmed by competitive binding assays using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).

Biologically, gramibactin enables *P. graminis* to grow in media containing as little as 0.1 µM Fe³⁺, whereas mutant strains lacking the gramibactin biosynthetic genes fail to grow under the same conditions unless supplemented with exogenous iron or purified gramibactin. Complementation of the mutant with the wild-type biosynthetic cluster restores both siderophore production and growth, confirming the essential role of gramibactin in iron acquisition. Surprisingly, when *Arabidopsis thaliana* seedlings were grown in iron-deficient agar plates supplemented with purified gramibactin, a significant rescue of chlorosis and restoration of chlorophyll content was observed, comparable to the effect of Fe-EDTA. This effect was dose-dependent, with optimal activity at concentrations between 1 and 10 µM. Control experiments with structurally unrelated siderophores (e.g., ferrioxamine B) showed minimal or no rescue, indicating a specific interaction between gramibactin and plant iron uptake machinery.

The biosynthetic gene cluster for gramibactin spans approximately 25 kb and contains seven open reading frames (*gbtA–gbtG*). Bioinformatic analysis predicts that *gbtA* and *gbtB* encode nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) modules responsible for assembling the peptide backbone, while *gbtC* encodes a flavin-dependent monooxygenase likely involved in the nitroso group installation. *gbtD* shows homology to transporters of the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) and is presumed to export gramibactin, and *gbtE–gbtG* encode regulatory and tailoring enzymes. Heterologous expression of the entire *gbt* cluster in *Pseudomonas putida* KT2440 resulted in detectable gramibactin production, confirming the sufficiency of these genes for biosynthesis.

The invention thus provides gramibactin as a new chemical entity with defined structure and function, along with methods for its production, formulation, and application. The compound’s unique combination of high iron affinity, environmental stability, biodegradability, and plant-beneficial activity distinguishes it from all known siderophores and synthetic chelates, positioning it as a valuable tool for sustainable agriculture.

## Methods of Administration

The present invention provides multiple methods for administering gramibactin or gramibactin-producing microorganisms to plants or soil to achieve enhanced iron nutrition and improved plant growth. In a preferred embodiment, purified gramibactin is formulated as an aqueous solution or suspension and applied directly to the soil or growth medium surrounding plant roots. The concentration of gramibactin in the formulation typically ranges from 0.1 µM to 100 µM, with optimal efficacy observed between 1 µM and 10 µM. Application may be performed as a drench, drip irrigation, or foliar spray, although root-zone application is generally more effective due to the localized nature of iron uptake. For foliar application, gramibactin is combined with surfactants (e.g., Tween 20 at 0.01–0.1%) to enhance leaf penetration and retention.

Alternatively, gramibactin may be incorporated into solid or granular fertilizer formulations. In such cases, gramibactin is adsorbed onto carriers such as clay minerals (e.g., kaolinite or montmorillonite), biochar, or polymer matrices that provide controlled release over time. These formulations are particularly useful in calcareous soils where rapid iron precipitation occurs, as the slow release of gramibactin maintains a steady supply of bioavailable iron. The carrier material may also include additional nutrients (e.g., nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium) to create a multifunctional biofertilizer.

In another embodiment, live bacterial inoculants comprising gramibactin-producing strains of *Paraburkholderia* (e.g., *P. graminis* DSM 11363 or genetically engineered derivatives) are applied to seeds, seedlings, or soil. The inoculant is prepared by culturing the bacterium to late-logarithmic phase in iron-limited medium, harvesting cells by centrifugation, and resuspending in a protective carrier such as peat, alginate beads, or liquid broth containing cryoprotectants (e.g., trehalose or glycerol). The final cell density is adjusted to 10⁷–10⁹ colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of carrier or per milliliter of liquid. Seed coating is achieved by mixing seeds with the inoculant slurry followed by drying, ensuring close proximity between the bacterium and germinating roots. Soil application involves mixing the inoculant into the planting hole or broadcasting it over the field prior to sowing.

For greenhouse or hydroponic systems, gramibactin is added directly to the nutrient solution at concentrations of 1–10 µM. In recirculating systems, periodic replenishment may be necessary to compensate for microbial degradation or plant uptake. The invention also contemplates the use of gramibactin in combination with other plant growth-promoting substances, such as phytohormones (e.g., indole-3-acetic acid), phosphate-solubilizing microbes, or mycorrhizal fungi, to achieve synergistic effects on plant health.

Administration timing is critical for maximizing efficacy. For annual crops, application is preferably performed at sowing or transplanting, coinciding with early root development when iron demand is high. In perennial systems, spring application aligns with active growth phases. Repeated applications may be beneficial in long-season crops or in soils with high iron-fixing capacity. Environmental factors such as soil pH, organic matter content, and moisture should be considered when determining dosage and frequency, as these influence gramibactin stability and mobility.

Safety considerations include ensuring that the gramibactin-producing strains are non-pathogenic and do not carry antibiotic resistance markers that could be transferred to environmental microbes. All strains used in the invention comply with biosafety level 1 (BSL-1) guidelines and have been tested for absence of virulence factors. Purified gramibactin is non-toxic to mammals, as demonstrated by acute oral toxicity studies in rodents (LD₅₀ > 2,000 mg/kg), supporting its safe use in agricultural settings.

## EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Isolation and Identification of Gramibactin-Producing Bacteria**

Soil samples were collected from the rhizosphere of *Phalaris arundinacea* growing in iron-deficient, calcareous soil in southern Germany. Serial dilutions were plated on CAS agar (Chrome Azurol S agar), a chromogenic medium that turns from blue to orange in the presence of siderophores. A bacterial isolate exhibiting a strong orange halo was selected and identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing as *Paraburkholderia graminis* DSM 11363. This strain was deposited in the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures (DSMZ) and used for all subsequent experiments.

**Example 2: Production and Purification of Gramibactin**

*P. graminis* was cultured in 10 L of iron-depleted MM9 medium supplemented with 0.1% casamino acids at 30°C with shaking (200 rpm) for 72 hours. The culture supernatant was separated by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 20 min) and filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane. Siderophores were extracted using XAD-16 resin, eluted with methanol, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude extract was subjected to preparative reversed-phase HPLC (C18 column, gradient of water/acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid) to yield pure gramibactin as a white powder (yield: 8.5 mg/L). Purity was confirmed by analytical HPLC (>98%) and HR-ESI-MS.

**Example 3: Structural Elucidation of Gramibactin**

The structure of gramibactin was determined using a combination of spectroscopic techniques. HR-ESI-MS gave a [M+H]⁺ ion at m/z 849.3541, consistent with C₄₀H₄₉N₁₀O₁₂⁺. ¹H and ¹³C NMR spectra revealed 40 carbon signals, including 12 aromatic/olefinic carbons, indicative of symmetry. COSY and TOCSY correlations established spin systems corresponding to modified phenylalanine residues. HMBC correlations from aromatic protons to quaternary carbons bearing nitroso groups confirmed the substitution pattern. The macrocyclic topology was deduced from ROESY cross-peaks indicating spatial proximity between distant protons. The Fe³⁺ complex showed a single set of sharp NMR signals, confirming a symmetric 1:1 complex.

**Example 4: Plant Growth Promotion Assay**

*Arabidopsis thaliana* (Col-0) seeds were surface-sterilized and sown on agar plates containing iron-deficient Hoagland’s medium (1 µM Fe³⁺ as FeCl₃). Plates were supplemented with 10 µM gramibactin, 10 µM ferrioxamine B, or 10 µM Fe-EDTA as controls. After 14 days of growth under long-day conditions (16 h light/8 h dark), seedlings treated with gramibactin exhibited green cotyledons and true leaves, whereas untreated controls were severely chlorotic. Chlorophyll content, measured spectrophotometrically, was restored to 85% of the Fe-EDTA control level. Root length and biomass were also significantly increased (p < 0.01, ANOVA).

**Example 5: Biosynthetic Gene Cluster Characterization**

Genomic DNA from *P. graminis* was sequenced using Illumina MiSeq, and the *gbt* cluster was identified by antiSMASH analysis. Targeted gene knockouts were constructed via homologous recombination using a suicide vector. The Δ*gbtA* mutant failed to produce gramibactin and could not grow in iron-deficient medium unless complemented with purified gramibactin or the wild-type *gbtA* gene. Heterologous expression of the *gbt* cluster in *P. putida* conferred gramibactin production, as confirmed by LC-MS.

**Example 6: Stability and Selectivity Studies**

Gramibactin (10 µM) was incubated in buffers ranging from pH 3 to 10 at 25°C. Samples taken at 0, 7, 14, and 30 days were analyzed by HPLC; no degradation was observed. In competitive binding assays, Fe³⁺–gramibactin retained >95% iron in the presence of 1 mM Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, or Al³⁺, whereas Fe³⁺–enterobactin lost >60% iron under the same conditions. Incubation with *Arabidopsis* root exudates for 48 hours resulted in <5% degradation of gramibactin, compared to >80% degradation of enterobactin.

## Materials and Methods

### Bacterial Strains and General Culture Conditions

*Paraburkholderia graminis* DSM 11363 was obtained from the DSMZ and routinely cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth at 30°C with shaking at 200 rpm. For siderophore production, iron-depleted minimal media were used. *Escherichia coli* DH5α was used for cloning and plasmid propagation, grown in LB at 37°C. *Pseudomonas putida* KT2440 served as a heterologous host and was cultured in LB or M9 minimal medium at 30°C. Antibiotics were used at the following concentrations when required: ampicillin (100 µg/mL), kanamycin (50 µg/mL), gentamicin (30 µg/mL). All strains were stored in 25% glycerol at –80°C.

### MM9 Media Preparation(2, 3)

MM9 minimal medium was prepared as follows: 64 mM Na₂HPO₄, 22 mM KH₂PO₄, 8.5 mM NaCl, 18.7 mM NH₄Cl, 2 mM MgSO₄, 0.1 mM CaCl₂, and 0.4% glucose (w/v). To ensure iron limitation, all components were treated with Chelex-100 resin (Bio-Rad) to remove trace metals, and glassware was acid-washed. Casamino acids (0.1%, w/v) were added as a nitrogen source for *P. graminis* cultures. The pH was adjusted to 7.0 with NaOH before autoclaving.

### CAS Agar Preparation

CAS agar was prepared according to the method of Schwyn and Neilands. Briefly, 100 mL of CAS solution (10 mg Chrome Azurol S, 7.29 mg FeCl₃·6H₂O, 30.3 mg HDTMA in 40 mL distilled water) was mixed with 900 mL of molten LB agar cooled to 50°C. The mixture was poured into Petri dishes and allowed to solidify. Siderophore-producing colonies appear surrounded by an orange halo due to displacement of Fe³⁺ from the CAS dye.

### Siderophore Production in Liquid Media

For large-scale production, *P. graminis* was inoculated into 10 L of iron-depleted MM9 medium in a 15-L fermenter. Cultivation was carried out at 30°C, pH 7.0, with agitation at 300 rpm and aeration at 1 vvm for 72 hours. Growth was monitored by optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀). At the end of fermentation, cells were removed by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 20 min, 4°C), and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-µm membrane for downstream processing.

### General Analytical Procedures

HPLC analyses were performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity system equipped with a diode array detector (DAD) and a Zorbax SB-C18 column (4.6 × 150 mm, 5 µm). Mobile phases consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both containing 0.1% formic acid. A linear gradient from 5% to 95% B over 20 minutes was used at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. UV-Vis spectra were recorded from 200 to 600 nm. LC-MS was conducted on an Agilent 6545 Q-TOF instrument with ESI in positive mode. NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz spectrometer using standard pulse sequences.

### Isolation of Gramibactin (6)

The cell-free supernatant from *P. graminis* culture was passed through a column of Amberlite XAD-16 resin (200 mL bed volume) at a flow rate of 5 mL/min. The resin was washed with 500 mL of distilled water and then eluted with 500 mL of methanol. The methanol fraction was concentrated under reduced pressure to yield a crude extract. This extract was dissolved in 10 mL of 20% acetonitrile and purified by preparative HPLC using a Phenomenex Luna C18 column (21.2 × 250 mm, 10 µm) with a gradient of 10–40% acetonitrile in water (0.1% formic acid) over 30 minutes at 15 mL/min. Fractions containing gramibactin (retention time ~18 min) were pooled and lyophilized to obtain pure compound.

### Structure Elucidation of Gramibactin

High-resolution mass spectrometry was performed in positive ion mode with a capillary voltage of 3.5 kV, fragmentor voltage of 175 V, and gas temperature of 325°C. NMR samples were prepared in deuterated methanol (CD₃OD) at a concentration of 5 mM. ¹H NMR spectra were referenced to residual CHD₂OD at δ 3.31 ppm; ¹³C NMR to CD₃OD at δ 49.0 ppm. Two-dimensional experiments (COSY, TOCSY, HSQC, HMBC, ROESY) were acquired with standard parameters. Structure modeling was performed using Molecular Operating Environment (MOE) software, with geometry optimization at the MMFF94x force field level.

### Griess Test (5)

Nitrite production, a potential indicator of nitroso group metabolism, was assessed using the Griess reagent. Culture supernatants were mixed with equal volumes of 1% sulfanilamide in 5% phosphoric acid and 0.1% N-(1-naphthyl)ethylenediamine dihydrochloride. After 10 minutes at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 540 nm. No significant nitrite was detected in gramibactin-containing samples, confirming the stability of the nitroso functionality.

### 15N Labeling of Gramibactin

To confirm the origin of nitrogen atoms in gramibactin, *P. graminis* was grown in MM9 medium with ¹⁵NH₄Cl (99% isotopic enrichment) as the sole nitrogen source. Purified gramibactin was analyzed by ¹⁵N NMR and HR-MS. The mass spectrum showed a +10 Da shift, consistent with incorporation of ten ¹⁵N atoms, matching the molecular formula. ¹⁵N NMR revealed signals corresponding to nitroso nitrogen (δ ~450 ppm) and amide nitrogens (δ ~100–120 ppm).

### Data

All experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Each experiment was repeated at least three times with independent biological replicates. Error bars in figures represent standard deviation. Raw spectral and sequence data are available from the authors upon reasonable request.
