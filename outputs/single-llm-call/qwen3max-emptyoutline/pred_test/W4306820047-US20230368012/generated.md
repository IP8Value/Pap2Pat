# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of computer vision has undergone transformative advancements in recent decades, driven by the proliferation of digital imaging technologies and the rapid development of machine learning algorithms. Object recognition—encompassing tasks such as image annotation, pedestrian detection, vehicle tracking, and facial identification—has become a cornerstone of modern artificial intelligence systems. Conventional approaches to object recognition rely on a multi-stage technological pipeline: first, an optical system (e.g., a lens-based camera) forms a real or virtual image of the target object; second, an optoelectronic sensor (such as a CMOS or CCD array) converts the analog optical signal into a digital representation; and third, a digital processor executes computationally intensive artificial neural networks (ANNs) to classify or interpret the digitized image. While this architecture has achieved remarkable success in numerous applications, it suffers from several fundamental limitations that hinder its scalability, efficiency, and security in emerging contexts.

Chief among these limitations is the inherent latency introduced by the sequential nature of the processing pipeline. The time required to capture, digitize, transmit, and compute upon visual data imposes a bottleneck that is particularly problematic in real-time applications such as autonomous navigation, industrial robotics, and augmented reality. Furthermore, the power consumption of digital processors—especially when executing deep convolutional neural networks with millions of parameters—renders conventional systems unsuitable for deployment on battery-powered or energy-constrained platforms, such as mobile devices, wearable sensors, or Internet-of-Things (IoT) edge nodes. Additionally, the digitization of visual data creates vulnerabilities to cyber-attacks, data breaches, and unauthorized surveillance, as sensitive information is exposed in the digital domain during transmission and storage. These challenges are exacerbated by the exponential growth in data volume generated by high-resolution imaging systems and the increasing demand for privacy-preserving, low-latency, and energy-efficient perception capabilities.

In response to these limitations, researchers have explored alternative computing paradigms that operate directly in the physical domain, bypassing the need for intermediate digitization and electronic computation. Among these, optical neural networks (ONNs) have emerged as a promising platform for ultrafast, low-power, and secure analog computing. ONNs leverage the wave nature of light to perform mathematical operations—such as matrix multiplications and nonlinear transformations—through passive optical components, thereby enabling computation at the speed of light with minimal energy dissipation. Unlike electronic systems, which require active power to maintain state and perform logic operations, ideal ONNs can be entirely passive, requiring no external power supply beyond the initial optical input. Moreover, because the computational function is physically encoded in the structure of the optical medium, the underlying algorithm is not represented in software or firmware, offering inherent resistance to reverse engineering and tampering—a feature of critical importance for secure authentication and confidential data processing.

Early implementations of ONNs were based on integrated photonic circuits, where optical signals are guided through waveguides and manipulated using tunable elements such as Mach-Zehnder interferometers (MZIs). In these architectures, linear transformations are realized by adjusting the phase and amplitude of light in each waveguide path, effectively implementing a programmable unitary matrix. While such systems have demonstrated proof-of-concept for optical inference, they are constrained by the physical size of the MZIs, which are typically much larger than the wavelength of light. This limits the number of neurons that can be packed into a given area, thereby restricting the expressive power of the network and its ability to solve complex, high-dimensional recognition tasks. Furthermore, the reliance on active tuning mechanisms (e.g., thermal or electro-optic phase shifters) reintroduces power consumption and latency, undermining the core advantages of optical computing.

An alternative approach to ONN design exploits the principles of free-space diffraction and wavefront engineering. In diffractive ONNs, the input optical field—scattered or transmitted by an object—is processed through a series of spatially patterned layers that modulate the phase, amplitude, or polarization of the light. Each layer acts as a trainable "diffractive surface," shaping the propagating wavefront such that, after multiple diffraction steps, the output intensity distribution encodes the classification result. This architecture aligns naturally with image-based tasks, as both the input and output are inherently spatial and continuous. Early demonstrations of diffractive ONNs employed 3D-printed masks operating in the terahertz (THz) regime or combinations of digital micromirror devices (DMDs) and spatial light modulators (SLMs) in the visible spectrum. However, these implementations face significant practical barriers: THz sources and detectors are not widely available or compact, while SLMs and DMDs are bulky, expensive, and incapable of subwavelength pixelation, limiting the spatial resolution and thus the computational capacity of the network.

More recently, hybrid optoelectronic architectures have been proposed, wherein an optical front-end performs linear transformations via diffraction or interference, and a subsequent electronic backend implements nonlinear activation functions and final classification. While these systems benefit from the speed of optical linear algebra, they still inherit the power consumption, latency, and security vulnerabilities of digital electronics, failing to fully realize the potential of all-optical computing.

A breakthrough in overcoming these limitations has come from the field of nanophotonics, specifically through the development of metasurfaces—ultra-thin, planar arrays of subwavelength nanostructures that can precisely control the phase, amplitude, and polarization of light across a wavefront. Metasurfaces, as two-dimensional counterparts to metamaterials, enable unprecedented manipulation of electromagnetic waves with minimal footprint and loss. By engineering the geometry, orientation, and material composition of individual "meta-units" (e.g., nanopillars, nanofins, or split-ring resonators), it is possible to achieve full 2π phase coverage, arbitrary amplitude modulation, and polarization-dependent responses—all within a single layer thinner than the wavelength of light. Critically, metasurfaces can be fabricated using standard complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) compatible processes, enabling mass production, integration with existing photonic platforms, and operation in the near-infrared and visible spectral ranges where high-performance light sources and detectors are readily available.

These attributes make metasurfaces an ideal platform for realizing compact, scalable, and high-performance diffractive ONNs. Unlike previous diffractive systems limited by macroscopic pixel sizes, metasurface-based ONNs can exploit millions of subwavelength degrees of freedom to encode complex computational functions directly into the nanostructure layout. This dramatically increases the expressive power of even a single-layer network, enabling tasks that previously required deep, multi-layered architectures to be accomplished with minimal hardware. Moreover, because the computation is performed entirely through passive optical propagation and scattering, metasurface ONNs operate at the speed of light, consume no power during inference, and provide physics-guaranteed security: the input image is never digitized, and the classification result is obtained solely through the physical interaction of light with the nanostructured surface.

Despite these advantages, prior work on metasurface neural networks has been limited to simple binary classification tasks, such as distinguishing between a small number of handwritten digits or basic geometric shapes. The scalability of these systems to more complex, real-world recognition problems—such as multi-class classification of diverse objects, typographic style analysis, or biometric verification—remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, experimental demonstrations have often suffered from performance degradation due to fabrication imperfections, alignment errors, and environmental perturbations, highlighting the need for robust design methodologies that account for real-world operating conditions. There is also a pressing need to develop strategies for enhancing the computational capacity of metasurface ONNs beyond the capabilities of single-layer phase-only devices, including the use of polarization multiplexing, multi-layer cascading, amplitude-phase control, and wavelength diversity.

The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel class of metasurface-based optical neural networks—referred to herein as "metasurface smart glasses"—that enable high-accuracy, multi-task, and robust object recognition directly in the optical domain. These smart glasses leverage the full vectorial properties of light, advanced nanofabrication techniques, and physics-informed training algorithms to achieve performance levels that rival or exceed those of conventional digital ANNs, while offering unparalleled advantages in speed, power efficiency, and security. The following sections describe the invention in detail, including its architectural principles, experimental implementation, and demonstrated capabilities across a range of recognition tasks.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a metasurface-based optical neural network, herein referred to as a "metasurface smart glass," configured to perform object recognition directly in the optical domain without the need for digital processing, analog-to-digital conversion, or external power supply during inference. The smart glass comprises one or more metasurfaces, each composed of a two-dimensional array of subwavelength meta-units engineered to modulate the phase, amplitude, and/or polarization of an incident optical wavefront scattered by an input object. The metasurface is trained such that, upon illumination by coherent light, the diffracted optical field propagates to a detection plane and produces an intensity distribution wherein the spatial location of maximum intensity corresponds to the classification label of the input object.

In one embodiment, the smart glass includes a single metasurface layer fabricated from a high-refractive-index, low-loss dielectric material such as amorphous silicon, arranged on a transparent substrate such as silicon dioxide. The meta-units are designed with cross-sectional geometries that provide isotropic or birefringent optical responses, enabling either polarization-independent or polarization-multiplexed operation. The phase profile of the metasurface is optimized through a training process that simulates the propagation of light from a set of labeled input objects through the diffractive network using rigorous electromagnetic theory, and iteratively adjusts the meta-unit parameters to minimize a loss function based on the cross-entropy between the predicted and target intensity distributions at the detection plane.

The invention further encompasses a polarization-multiplexing architecture, wherein a single birefringent metasurface is trained to perform two distinct recognition tasks simultaneously using orthogonally polarized incident light. For example, horizontally polarized light may be used to classify alphanumeric characters, while vertically polarized light may be used to distinguish typographic styles such as normal versus italic. This dual-functionality is achieved by designing meta-units with two-fold rotational symmetry, which exhibit different phase responses for light polarized along orthogonal axes, thereby encoding two independent computational functions within the same physical device.

In another embodiment, the smart glass comprises a doublet of metasurfaces separated by a free-space propagation distance, thereby increasing the depth and expressive power of the optical neural network. This multi-layer architecture enables the recognition of more complex, grayscale images, such as human facial photographs, by performing hierarchical feature extraction analogous to convolutional neural networks. The doublet is trained to map each input image to a low-dimensional intensity code on the detection plane, and facial verification is accomplished by comparing the Euclidean distance between the codes of two input images against a predefined threshold.

The invention also includes methods for enhancing the robustness of the metasurface smart glass against experimental imperfections, such as misalignment, illumination non-uniformity, and fabrication tolerances. These methods involve incorporating stochastic perturbations of system parameters—such as object position, propagation distances, and detector placement—into the training process, as well as modifying the loss function to maximize the contrast between the correct classification zone and competing zones on the detection plane.

Furthermore, the invention provides a scalable framework for increasing the computational capacity of metasurface ONNs through multiple degrees of freedom, including: (1) increasing the width of the network by using meta-units capable of independent phase and amplitude control; (2) leveraging wavelength multiplexing by engineering the dispersion of meta-units to perform different computations at different wavelengths; (3) employing arrays of distinct metasurfaces within a single layer to process incoherent or multi-spectral inputs; and (4) cascading multiple metasurface layers to form deep diffractive networks.

The metasurface smart glass operates entirely in the optical domain, requiring no power during inference, providing intrinsic data security through the absence of digital representation, and achieving computational speeds limited only by the propagation time of light. It is fabricated using CMOS-compatible nanofabrication techniques, enabling miniaturization, mass production, and integration with existing optical systems. The invention is applicable to a wide range of object recognition tasks, including but not limited to handwritten digit and letter classification, typographic analysis, facial verification, and industrial inspection, and is particularly suited for deployment in resource-constrained, privacy-sensitive, or high-speed environments.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The metasurface smart glass of the present invention represents a paradigm shift in object recognition technology by integrating sensing, analog processing, and classification into a single passive optical device. At its core, the smart glass exploits the wave nature of light and the principles of diffraction to perform analog neural computation without the need for digital electronics. The architecture is inspired by the layered structure of artificial neural networks but is implemented entirely through physical optics, wherein each layer corresponds to a plane of optical field propagation and transformation.

The input to the system is an optical object—typically a binary or grayscale image—illuminated by a coherent light source such as a laser. The object may be a physical mask, a printed pattern, or a spatially modulated beam representing a real-world scene. Upon illumination, the object scatters or transmits light, generating a complex optical wavefront characterized by spatially varying amplitude and phase. This wavefront propagates through free space over a predetermined distance, known as the object distance, before encountering the metasurface layer.

The metasurface is a nanostructured thin film composed of a periodic or aperiodic array of subwavelength meta-units, each designed to impart a specific local phase shift (and optionally amplitude attenuation or polarization rotation) to the incident light. The meta-units are typically fabricated from high-refractive-index, low-absorption dielectric materials such as amorphous silicon, titanium dioxide, or gallium nitride, which support strong Mie resonances or geometric phase effects. The cross-sectional shape, orientation, and dimensions of each meta-unit are tailored to achieve the desired optical response at the operating wavelength, which in the preferred embodiment is in the near-infrared range (e.g., 1550 nm) for compatibility with telecommunications infrastructure and low-loss propagation in silica substrates.

During the training phase, the phase profile of the metasurface is optimized using a computational model that simulates the entire optical pathway from input object to detection plane. The propagation of light is calculated using the Rayleigh-Sommerfeld diffraction integral or other rigorous electromagnetic solvers, ensuring accurate modeling of near-field and far-field effects. A loss function is defined to quantify the discrepancy between the actual intensity distribution at the detection plane and the target distribution, which assigns unit intensity to the zone corresponding to the correct class label and zero intensity elsewhere. The optimization is performed using gradient-based algorithms such as Adam, with gradients computed via backpropagation through the optical model. To enhance robustness, the training process incorporates random variations in system parameters, including lateral shifts of the input object, axial displacements of the metasurface and detector, and non-uniform illumination profiles.

Once trained, the phase profile is discretized and mapped to a library of pre-characterized meta-units. Each meta-unit in the library corresponds to a specific phase shift (and possibly amplitude or polarization response) and is selected to best approximate the desired local modulation at each pixel location. The final metasurface design is then fabricated using electron-beam lithography or deep-ultraviolet photolithography, followed by reactive ion etching to define the nanostructures on the substrate.

In operation, the smart glass requires only a coherent light source and a simple intensity detector array, such as an InGaAs camera for near-infrared wavelengths. The input object is placed at the object plane, illuminated, and the resulting diffraction pattern after the metasurface is captured at the detection plane. The classification decision is made by identifying the detection zone with the highest integrated intensity, which directly corresponds to the recognized class. No digital processing is required beyond this simple readout, and the entire inference occurs at the speed of light propagation through the system—typically on the order of picoseconds for centimeter-scale devices.

The invention supports multiple operational modes. In the simplest configuration, a single-layer isotropic metasurface is used for polarization-independent classification of binary objects, such as handwritten digits from the MNIST dataset. Experimental results demonstrate recognition accuracies exceeding 99% for four-class digit classification and approximately 80% for ten-class classification, with performance limited primarily by the reduced inter-zone intensity contrast in more complex tasks.

To overcome this limitation, the invention introduces a polarization-multiplexing scheme using birefringent metasurfaces. These metasurfaces are composed of meta-units with two-fold rotational symmetry (e.g., rectangular or elliptical nanopillars), which exhibit different phase responses for light polarized along orthogonal axes. By training the metasurface separately for each polarization channel, two independent recognition tasks can be performed using the same physical device. Demonstrations include simultaneous classification of alphanumeric characters and their typographic styles, achieving accuracies of over 90% for both tasks.

For even more complex recognition problems, such as facial verification, the invention employs a doublet architecture comprising two metasurfaces separated by a free-space gap. This multi-layer design increases the network depth and enables hierarchical feature extraction, analogous to deep convolutional neural networks. The doublet is trained to map grayscale facial images to compact 3×3 intensity codes, and verification is performed by comparing the Euclidean distance between codes. Theoretical simulations show verification accuracy of approximately 80%, comparable to a three-layer digital CNN.

The invention further provides a roadmap for scaling the expressive power of metasurface ONNs. Strategies include: (1) using meta-units with independent phase and amplitude control, achieved through structural birefringence or bilayer designs; (2) implementing wavelength multiplexing by engineering dispersive meta-units that perform different computations at different wavelengths; (3) deploying arrays of distinct metasurfaces within a single layer to handle incoherent or multi-spectral inputs; and (4) incorporating nonlinear optical materials to enable true nonlinear activation functions, thereby expanding the class of computable functions.

Critically, the metasurface smart glass offers unique advantages in security and privacy. Because the input image is never digitized and the computation is physically encoded in the nanostructure, there is no digital representation of the subject to be intercepted or reconstructed. This makes the system ideal for applications such as biometric authentication, medical imaging, and surveillance, where data confidentiality is paramount.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Four-Class Handwritten Digit Recognition**

A single-layer metasurface smart glass was designed and fabricated to classify four classes of handwritten digits—{0, 1, 3, 4}—from the MNIST database. The metasurface was modeled as a 500λ × 500λ phase mask (λ = 1550 nm), discretized into 1000 × 1000 pixels, and composed of isotropic meta-units made of amorphous silicon on a silicon dioxide substrate. The object and imaging distances were both set to 2000λ. The detection plane featured four square zones, each corresponding to one digit class. Training was performed using 10,000 labeled digit images, with the loss function minimized via the Adam optimizer. The trained phase profile was mapped to a library of circular nanopillars with varying diameters to achieve 2π phase coverage. The fabricated metasurface was tested with 116 unseen digits (25–30 per class), achieving an experimental recognition accuracy of 99.14%, matching the theoretical prediction. The intensity in the correct detection zone was on average 22% higher than in competing zones, ensuring robustness against experimental noise.

**Example 2: Ten-Class Handwritten Digit Recognition**

The same single-layer architecture was extended to classify all ten handwritten digits. The detection plane was reconfigured with ten circular zones arranged in a ring. Training with 60,000 MNIST images yielded a theoretical accuracy of 86.50%. Experimentally, 208 test digits were classified with an accuracy of 78.37%. The reduced performance was attributed to lower inter-zone intensity contrast (~10%), making the system more sensitive to alignment errors and illumination non-uniformity. This example illustrates the trade-off between task complexity and robustness in single-layer designs.

**Example 3: Polarization-Multiplexed Digit Classification**

To improve ten-digit recognition, a polarization-multiplexing strategy was implemented. The digits were divided into two groups: {1, 3, 4, 7, 8} for horizontal polarization and {0, 2, 5, 6, 9} for vertical polarization. A birefringent metasurface composed of rectangular nanopillars was trained to provide distinct phase profiles for each polarization. Theoretical accuracies were 94.80% and 94.00% for the two groups, respectively. Experimental testing with 111 and 97 digits achieved 90.99% and 81.44% accuracy, significantly outperforming the isotropic single-polarization design. This demonstrates the utility of polarization as an additional degree of freedom for increasing computational capacity.

**Example 4: Multi-Task Typographic Recognition**

A birefringent metasurface smart glass was trained to perform two simultaneous tasks: classifying four letters {A, B, C, D} under horizontal polarization, and distinguishing normal versus italic font styles under vertical polarization. The detection plane featured four letter zones and two style zones. Testing with 168 inputs (4 letters × 21 fonts × 2 styles) yielded 92.81% letter classification accuracy and 100% style recognition accuracy. This example highlights the potential for multi-functional optical computing in document analysis and anti-counterfeiting applications.

**Example 5: Facial Verification with Metasurface Doublet**

A theoretical model of a doublet metasurface smart glass was developed for human facial verification. The system mapped grayscale facial images (100 individuals, 14 images each) to 3×3 intensity codes. The doublet was trained using 90 individuals and tested on the remaining 10. With a Euclidean distance threshold of 0.8, the system achieved equal error rates of ~10% for false acceptance and false rejection, corresponding to 80% verification accuracy—comparable to a three-layer digital CNN. This demonstrates the feasibility of metasurface ONNs for complex biometric tasks.

These examples collectively validate the versatility, scalability, and performance of the metasurface smart glass platform across a spectrum of object recognition challenges, from simple digit classification to multi-task and biometric verification, while maintaining the core advantages of passive, ultrafast, and secure optical computing.
