# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of antimicrobial therapeutics, specifically to novel combinations of ribosome-targeting antibiotics that act synergistically to inhibit bacterial protein synthesis. More particularly, the invention concerns the identification and characterization of the binding sites and mechanisms of action of lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM), two naturally occurring antibiotics co-produced by *Streptomyces rochei* strain 7434AN4. The invention further encompasses methods for exploiting the synergistic interaction between LC and LM for the development of enhanced antibacterial agents, including chemically modified derivatives and conjugates designed to optimize simultaneous binding to adjacent sites within the large ribosomal subunit. The structural elucidation of the precise molecular interactions of LC with the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) and of LM with the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET) provides a rational basis for engineering improved antibiotic formulations that leverage cooperative inhibition of successive steps in protein biosynthesis. This invention thus bridges structural biology, microbiology, and medicinal chemistry to provide new strategies for combating bacterial infections, particularly those caused by Gram-positive pathogens, through the targeted design of synergistic antibiotic pairs based on the natural LC/LM paradigm.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The ribosome, as the central machinery for protein synthesis in all living cells, has long served as a prime target for antimicrobial agents. A substantial proportion of clinically used antibiotics function by inhibiting bacterial translation through binding to specific functional regions of the ribosome, thereby disrupting essential steps in the elongation cycle. Over the past several decades, extensive biochemical, genetic, and, more recently, high-resolution crystallographic studies have delineated two primary antibiotic-binding loci within the large ribosomal subunit: the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) and the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET). The PTC, located at the heart of the 50S subunit, is the catalytic site responsible for forming peptide bonds between amino acids. It serves as the binding pocket for a diverse array of structurally distinct antibiotic classes, including phenicols (e.g., chloramphenicol), lincosamides (e.g., clindamycin), pleuromutilins (e.g., tiamulin, retapamulin), oxazolidinones (e.g., linezolid), and the streptogramin A (S<sub>A</sub>) compounds (e.g., dalfopristin). These inhibitors typically block the accommodation or proper positioning of the aminoacyl moiety of the A-site tRNA, thereby preventing the nucleophilic attack required for peptide bond formation. In contrast, the NPET is a conduit through which the newly synthesized polypeptide chain exits the ribosome. This tunnel is targeted by macrolide antibiotics (e.g., erythromycin) and streptogramin B (S<sub>B</sub>) compounds (e.g., quinupristin), which bind in its upper segment, adjacent to the PTC. Their binding sterically hinders the progression of the nascent peptide chain, leading to premature termination of translation.

A key principle in pharmacology is that the simultaneous inhibition of consecutive steps in a single biochemical pathway can produce a synergistic effect, where the combined activity of two agents is significantly greater than the sum of their individual effects. Nature appears to have exploited this principle in the evolution of certain antibiotic-producing microorganisms. The most prominent example is the streptogramin family, which is secreted by various *Streptomyces* species as a fixed combination of an S<sub>A</sub> and an S<sub>B</sub> component. Individually, each component exhibits only weak antibacterial activity; however, when administered together, they act in a highly synergistic manner. This synergy arises from their ability to bind simultaneously to adjacent but non-overlapping sites in the PTC and NPET, respectively, resulting in a conformational change in the ribosome that dramatically increases the affinity of the S<sub>B</sub> component and leads to a potent, irreversible blockade of protein synthesis. This natural strategy has been successfully translated into clinical practice with the semisynthetic formulation Synercid, a combination of dalfopristin and quinupristin, which is used to treat severe Gram-positive infections.

Lankacidin and lankamycin represent another pair of antibiotics co-produced by *Streptomyces rochei*. Lankacidin C (LC) is a unique 17-membered macrocyclic compound with a bridged lactone structure, known for its antibiotic and antitumor properties and its use in veterinary medicine. Its mechanism of action was suspected to involve the ribosome, based on competition studies with chloramphenicol, a classic PTC inhibitor. Lankamycin (LM), on the other hand, is a 14-membered ring macrolide, structurally reminiscent of erythromycin (ERY), suggesting it might function as a typical macrolide by binding to the NPET. The co-regulation of their biosynthetic pathways strongly implies an evolutionary advantage to their co-production, hinting at a potential functional interaction. However, prior to the present invention, the precise binding sites of LC and LM on the ribosome were unknown, and there was no evidence to confirm whether they could bind simultaneously or act synergistically. The structural similarity of LM to ERY raised a critical question: if LM binds like ERY, would it compete with LC for ribosome binding, rendering their co-production counterproductive? This fundamental gap in knowledge prevented any rational exploitation of the LC/LM pair for therapeutic development. The present invention addresses this critical void by providing the first definitive structural and functional characterization of the LC and LM binding sites, demonstrating their capacity for simultaneous binding, and establishing their synergistic antibacterial activity, thereby opening a new avenue for the design of next-generation synergistic antibiotics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution to the long-standing problem of defining the molecular mechanisms of action for the co-produced antibiotics lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) and harnessing their potential for therapeutic application. The core of the invention lies in the unexpected discovery, supported by rigorous structural and biochemical evidence, that LC and LM bind to adjacent, non-competing sites on the bacterial large ribosomal subunit and act in a synergistic manner to inhibit protein synthesis. Specifically, the invention identifies the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) as the binding site for LC and the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET) as the binding site for LM. This finding resolves the paradox of their co-production by demonstrating that, unlike the structurally similar erythromycin (ERY), LM does not compete with LC for ribosome binding.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for inhibiting bacterial protein synthesis comprising administering to a bacterial cell an effective amount of a composition containing both lankacidin C and lankamycin. This method exploits the synergistic interaction between the two compounds, which results in a bactericidal or bacteriostatic effect that is significantly greater than the effect of either compound alone.

In another aspect, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising a therapeutically effective amount of lankacidin C and lankamycin in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier. This composition is designed to deliver the synergistic pair to the site of a bacterial infection for the treatment of diseases caused by susceptible Gram-positive bacteria.

In a further aspect, the invention provides a method for designing a synergistic antibiotic pair. This method comprises the steps of (a) determining the three-dimensional structure of a first antibiotic bound to the PTC of a bacterial ribosome; (b) determining the three-dimensional structure of a second antibiotic bound to the NPET of a bacterial ribosome; and (c) evaluating the spatial relationship between the two binding sites to ensure they are adjacent and non-overlapping, thereby enabling simultaneous binding and synergistic inhibition of protein synthesis. The structural data obtained from the LC/LM complex serves as a blueprint for this rational design process.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides novel chemical derivatives and conjugates of lankacidin and/or lankamycin. These derivatives are engineered based on the atomic-level structural information of the LC/LM-ribosome complex to enhance binding affinity, improve pharmacokinetic properties, or directly link the two pharmacophores into a single molecule to guarantee their co-localization on the ribosome, thereby maximizing the synergistic effect. For instance, a derivative may be created by modifying the C5-linked sugar of lankamycin to introduce functional groups that can form additional favorable interactions with the ribosome or with the lankacidin molecule itself.

The invention also provides a diagnostic method for identifying a bacterial strain that is susceptible to synergistic inhibition by LC and LM. This method involves exposing the bacterial strain to sub-inhibitory concentrations of LC and LM individually and in combination and measuring the degree of growth inhibition, with a concave fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index curve indicating susceptibility to the synergistic pair.

Overall, the present invention transforms the previously enigmatic co-production of LC and LM from a biological curiosity into a validated and exploitable platform for the development of powerful new antibacterial therapies, offering a promising strategy to combat the growing threat of antibiotic resistance.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Lankacidin Binding Site

The present invention definitively establishes that lankacidin C (LC) exerts its antibacterial effect by binding directly to the peptidyl transferase center (PTC) of the bacterial large ribosomal subunit. This conclusion is based on high-resolution X-ray crystallographic analysis of the *Deinococcus radiodurans* 50S ribosomal subunit (D50S) in complex with LC. The electron density map, calculated at a resolution of 3.5 Å, unambiguously reveals the location and conformation of the LC molecule nestled within a shallow depression on the wall of the PTC A-site. The binding of LC is stabilized by a sophisticated network of interactions that involve both direct contacts with key nucleotides and an induced-fit mechanism that reorganizes the local rRNA architecture.

The macrocyclic ring of LC fits precisely into its binding pocket, forming critical van der Waals interactions with the bases of nucleotides U2504, G2505, and U2506. The importance of these interactions is underscored by structure-activity relationship studies, which demonstrate that hydrogenation of the macrocyclic ring, a modification that alters its conformation, leads to a significant reduction in the compound's inhibitory activity. A key anchoring point for LC is its 2-methyl group, situated at the lactone edge of the macrocycle. This hydrophobic moiety inserts itself into a cleft formed by the splayed-out bases of A2451 and C2452. This same cleft is a crucial functional element of the PTC, as it normally accommodates the aminoacyl moiety of the A-site-bound aminoacyl-tRNA during the peptidyl transfer reaction. The occupation of this cleft by LC’s 2-methyl group provides a direct structural explanation for its mechanism of action: by physically blocking this site, LC prevents the correct placement of the incoming amino acid, thereby inhibiting peptide bond formation. This binding locus also explains the previously observed competition between LC and chloramphenicol, as their binding sites substantially overlap within this critical region of the PTC.

Furthermore, LC binding induces a unique and specific rearrangement of the surrounding rRNA nucleotides, creating a stable interaction network that locks the drug in place. This network involves five key nucleotides: U2506, G2505, G2581, C2610, and G2576. Upon LC binding, G2576 stacks upon G2505, while C2610 stacks upon G2581. A particularly notable interaction is the formation of a hydrogen bond between the exocyclic amine of G2505 and the O2 atom of C2610, facilitated by a CG rS* base pairing motif. Additionally, the amino group of G2502 is positioned within hydrogen-bonding distance of the O4 atom of U2506, which itself is shifted towards the 1,3-dicarbonyl system of LC. This concerted reorientation of nucleotides stabilizes a conformation of the PTC that is highly favorable for LC binding. Beyond this primary shell of interactions, LC also engages in a series of secondary, or “second-shell,” contacts with a broader set of nucleotides, including G2576, A2062, C2530, U2531, C2507, U2584, G2581, C2610, and A2059. This extensive interaction network, which exploits the inherent flexibility of the PTC, is characteristic of an induced-fit binding mechanism, allowing the ribosome to mold itself around the antibiotic for a tight and specific fit.

A comparison of the LC binding site with those of other PTC inhibitors reveals both similarities and critical distinctions. While LC shares a general location with streptogramin A compounds like dalfopristin and virginiamycin M, its smaller size and distinct chemical structure lead to a different pattern of interactions and conformational changes in the ribosome. For example, LC binding causes a significant 45-degree rotation of the flexible nucleotide A2602, a residue known to play a vital role in tRNA translocation. In contrast, in the ribosome-Synercid (dalfopristin/quinupristin) complex, A2602 adopts a different conformation, likely influenced by the presence of the bulky quinupristin molecule in the adjacent NPET site. Similarly, the nucleotide U2585, another key player in tRNA dynamics, undergoes only a minor shift in the LC-bound ribosome, whereas it is rotated by 180 degrees in the Synercid-bound complex. These differences highlight the unique mode of action of LC. Despite these variations, common features exist, such as the interaction of both LC and dalfopristin with G2061 and the re-orientation of U2506, although the specific nature of the U2506 shift is unique to LC. The functional consequence of this precise binding is a potent inhibition of the peptidyl transferase reaction, as confirmed by biochemical assays showing that LC effectively blocks the puromycin reaction in both *Staphylococcus aureus* 70S ribosomes and isolated *D. radiodurans* 50S subunits, with IC<sub>50</sub> values in the sub-micromolar to low micromolar range.

### Lankamycin Binding Site

The present invention further elucidates that lankamycin (LM), the macrolide partner of LC, binds to the nascent peptide exit tunnel (NPET) of the bacterial large ribosomal subunit. This conclusion is drawn from a combination of structural modeling, biochemical inhibition assays, and, most definitively, RNA chemical probing experiments. Given the striking structural similarity between LM and the well-characterized macrolide erythromycin (ERY), it was initially hypothesized that LM would occupy the canonical macrolide binding site in the upper portion of the NPET, adjacent to the PTC. However, a simple docking of the ERY structure onto the LC-bound ribosome suggested a potential steric clash between the desosamine sugar of ERY and the macrocyclic ring of LC, which was corroborated by competition experiments showing that LC could displace radiolabeled ERY from the ribosome. This raised the pivotal question of whether LM, despite its similarity to ERY, had evolved a distinct binding mode that would allow it to coexist with LC on the same ribosome.

This question was resolved through RNA probing using chemical modifying reagents on the *D. radiodurans* 50S subunit. The binding of classical 14-membered ring macrolides like ERY is known to confer a specific protection pattern on the 23S rRNA, notably shielding nucleotides A2058 and A2059 from dimethyl sulfate (DMS) modification and protecting U2609 from 1-cyclohexyl-3-(2-morpholinoethyl) carbodiimide metho-p-toluene sulfonate (CMCT) modification. The RNA probing experiments of the present invention demonstrated that LM binding produces an identical protection signature: it strongly protects U2609 from CMCT and A2058/A2059 from DMS. This result confirms that LM binds to the same general NPET site as ERY. However, the critical distinction revealed by the invention is that, unlike ERY, LM does not compete with LC for ribosome binding. This implies that the subtle structural differences between LM and ERY—particularly in the nature of their C5-linked sugar moieties—are sufficient to alter their precise orientation or the spatial demands within the binding pocket, thereby avoiding the steric hindrance that occurs between ERY and LC. The ability of LM to bind to the NPET without displacing LC from the PTC is the foundational discovery that explains the evolutionary logic of their co-production by *Streptomyces rochei*. The biochemical data supporting this binding is the observation that LM inhibits an *E. coli* cell-free translation system, albeit with a relatively high IC<sub>50</sub> of 275 µM, confirming its role as a protein synthesis inhibitor that targets the ribosome. Thus, the invention defines the LM binding site as the canonical macrolide pocket in the NPET, but crucially characterizes it as a site that is compatible with the simultaneous occupancy of the adjacent PTC by LC, a property not shared by all macrolides, such as ERY.

### Lankacidin and Lankamycin Can Simultaneously Bind to the Ribosome

A central and non-obvious finding of the present invention is the demonstration that lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) can bind to the bacterial ribosome simultaneously. This discovery is the key that unlocks the functional rationale for their co-evolution and co-production by *Streptomyces rochei*. The evidence for this simultaneous binding is derived from a clever application of RNA chemical probing, which exploits the unique and non-overlapping protection patterns conferred by each antibiotic on specific 23S rRNA nucleotides.

As established, LC binding to the PTC results in a strong protection of nucleotides U2506 and U2585 from modification by CMCT. Conversely, LM binding to the NPET results in a strong protection of nucleotide U2609 from CMCT modification. Critically, the probing experiments showed that the presence of LM has no effect on the LC-mediated protection of U2506 and U2585, and similarly, the presence of LC has no effect on the LM-mediated protection of U2609. When both antibiotics are incubated with the ribosome together, the resulting modification pattern shows the combined protections: U2506, U2585, and U2609 are all shielded from the chemical probe. This additive protection pattern provides direct and conclusive evidence that both drugs are bound to their respective sites on the same ribosomal particle at the same time. This stands in stark contrast to the behavior observed with erythromycin (ERY). When ERY is tested in the same assay, its binding competes with LC: the LC-mediated protection of U2506 and U2585 is partially reversed in the presence of ERY, and the ERY-mediated protection of U2609 is diminished when LC is present. This competitive binding between LC and ERY is consistent with the predicted steric clash from structural modeling. The ability of LM, but not ERY, to bind concurrently with LC highlights a remarkable evolutionary adaptation in the structure of LM. The minute differences in its chemical structure, likely centered on its sugar substituents, have been fine-tuned to avoid conflict with its natural partner, LC, while still allowing it to effectively engage its target site in the NPET. This simultaneous binding is the essential prerequisite for the synergistic antibacterial activity that is the hallmark of the LC/LM pair and the foundation for the therapeutic applications disclosed herein.

### LC and LM Act Synergistically Upon Bacterial Cells

The functional consequence of the simultaneous binding of lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) to adjacent sites on the ribosome is a powerful synergistic inhibition of bacterial cell growth. The present invention provides robust experimental proof of this synergy through both in vivo and in vitro assays. In vivo, the synergistic effect was demonstrated using a checkerboard assay against a susceptible strain of the Gram-positive pathogen *Staphylococcus aureus*. In this assay, the minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of LC and LM were determined across a matrix of varying concentrations of the two drugs. The resulting data were plotted as a fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) index curve. The plot exhibited a distinct concave shape, which is the definitive hallmark of a synergistic interaction between two antimicrobial agents. This means that the combined effect of LC and LM at sub-MIC concentrations is far greater than would be expected from a simple additive effect of their individual activities.

This in vivo observation was further corroborated by an in vitro cell-free translation system using an *E. coli* extract. In this system, the synthesis of a reporter protein (either luciferase or GFP) was measured in the presence of various concentrations of LC and LM alone and in combination. The results confirmed that the combination of the two antibiotics produced a level of inhibition of protein synthesis that was significantly greater than the sum of the inhibitions caused by each antibiotic individually. This biochemical synergy directly mirrors the cellular synergy observed in the whole-cell assay and provides a clear mechanistic link: the simultaneous blockade of two successive and critical steps in the protein synthesis pathway—peptide bond formation in the PTC by LC and nascent chain progression in the NPET by LM—creates a more profound and effective shutdown of translation than either blockade alone. This synergistic paradigm is analogous to that of the streptogramins, but the present invention establishes it for an entirely new and distinct chemical class of antibiotics. The discovery of this synergy is of immense practical significance, as it suggests that the moderate intrinsic activity of LM can be dramatically amplified by its natural partner, LC. This evolutionary strategy allows the producing organism to deploy a highly effective antibacterial weapon from two components that, on their own, might be less potent or more easily circumvented by resistance mechanisms. The invention thus provides a validated synergistic pair that can serve as a template for the development of new combination therapies to combat bacterial infections.

## Materials and Methods

### Crystallography

The high-resolution structural data underpinning the present invention was obtained through X-ray crystallography of the *Deinococcus radiodurans* 50S ribosomal subunit (D50S) in complex with lankacidin C (LC). Crystals of the D50S subunit were grown using established protocols. These pre-formed crystals were then soaked in a cryo-protectant solution containing 0.025 mM of purified LC for a period of 20 hours at 20°C to allow for diffusion and binding of the antibiotic into the ribosomal lattice. Following the soaking step, the crystals were transferred into a fresh cryo-buffer and rapidly shock-frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve their structure for data collection. X-ray diffraction data were collected at a temperature of 85-100 K from these frozen crystals using a highly collimated synchrotron X-ray beam. Data collection was performed at two facilities: station 19ID at the Advanced Photon Source (Argonne National Laboratory) and station ID23-2 at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF). The data were recorded on charge-coupled device (CCD) detectors at a wavelength of approximately 1.0 Å and 0.837 Å, respectively, with a crystal-to-detector distance of 430 mm and an oscillation range of 0.3° per image. The raw diffraction images were processed and scaled using the HKL2000 software suite to generate a complete and merged dataset. The structure of the D50S-LC complex was solved by molecular replacement, using the coordinates of the native D50S structure as a search model. Rigid-body refinement was initially performed using the CNS software package. To obtain an unbiased electron density map for model building, a composite omit map was calculated for the entire unit cell. The atomic model of LC was built into the clear electron density observed in the PTC using the COOT molecular graphics program, guided by the known chemical structure of lankacidin C. Subsequent rounds of refinement involved restrained minimization and grouped B-factor refinement, again using CNS, to optimize the fit of the model to the experimental data. A random 5% of the reflections were set aside for the calculation of the free R-factor to monitor against overfitting. The final refined model was validated, and the specific interactions between LC and the ribosomal RNA were analyzed and visualized using the LigPlot and LPC software programs. All molecular graphics figures were generated using PyMOL. The final atomic coordinates and structure factors for the D50S-LC complex have been deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) under accession code 3JQ4.

### Antibiotic Binding

The binding of lankacidin C (LC) and lankamycin (LM) to the ribosome, and their competition with other antibiotics, was assessed using a gel filtration-based binding assay. In a typical experiment, purified *Deinococcus radiodurans* 50S ribosomal subunits (at a concentration of 0.1 µM) were pre-incubated with a range of concentrations of the test antibiotic (e.g., LC, LM, or erythromycin) in a binding buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 250 mM NH<sub>4</sub>Cl, 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol) for 15 minutes at 37°C to allow equilibrium to be reached. In competition experiments, the ribosomal subunits were first incubated with a fixed concentration of one antibiotic (e.g., LC) before the addition of a radiolabeled competitor (e.g., [<sup>14</sup>C]-erythromycin). Following the incubation, the ribosome-antibiotic complexes were separated from unbound antibiotic by rapid gel filtration chromatography using BioGel P30 spin columns that had been pre-equilibrated with the same binding buffer. The eluted fractions containing the ribosomal subunits were collected, and the amount of bound radioactivity was quantified using a liquid scintillation counter. The data were analyzed using non-linear regression software (Prism 4, GraphPad Software) to determine binding constants (K<sub>d</sub>) or to quantify the degree of competition between different compounds. This method provided direct biochemical evidence for the binding of LM to the ribosome and for the competitive interaction between LC and erythromycin, while indirectly supporting the non-competitive binding of LC and LM.

### Inhibition of Cell-Free Protein Synthesis by LC and LM

The functional impact of LC and LM on protein synthesis was evaluated using a commercially available *E. coli* cell-free transcription-translation system (Promega). This system utilizes a circular plasmid DNA template (pBestLuc) that encodes the firefly luciferase reporter gene. The reaction mixture was pre-incubated with varying concentrations of the test antibiotic(s) for 5 minutes at 20°C. The transcription-translation reaction was then initiated by the addition of the plasmid DNA and allowed to proceed for 40 minutes at 20°C. The reaction was stopped by placing the samples on ice. The amount of luciferase protein synthesized during the reaction, which serves as a direct measure of the system's translational activity, was quantified using the Bright-Glo Luciferase Assay System according to the manufacturer's instructions. Luminescence was measured in a 96-well plate luminometer. The data were normalized to a no-antibiotic control and analyzed using Prism 4 software to calculate the concentration of antibiotic that inhibited protein synthesis by 50% (IC<sub>50</sub>). This assay confirmed that both LC and LM are inhibitors of protein synthesis, with LC being a far more potent inhibitor (IC<sub>50</sub> = 1.5 µM) than LM (IC<sub>50</sub> = 275 µM).

### Inhibition of the Peptidyl Transferase Reaction by LC

To pinpoint the exact step in protein synthesis inhibited by LC, a specific assay for peptidyl transferase activity was employed. This assay measures the ability of the ribosome to catalyze the transfer of a peptidyl moiety from a P-site-bound substrate to puromycin, an A-site analog that accepts the peptide chain. The reaction was performed using either intact 70S ribosomes from *Staphylococcus aureus* or isolated 50S subunits from *Deinococcus radiodurans*. The ribosomal complexes were pre-incubated with varying concentrations of LC for 30 minutes at 37°C in a suitable reaction buffer. Puromycin was then added to a final concentration of 0.5 mM, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 15 minutes at 37°C. The reaction was stopped, and the amount of peptidyl-puromycin product formed was quantified using established analytical methods. The data were analyzed using Prism 4 software to determine the IC<sub>50</sub> for LC inhibition of the peptidyl transferase reaction. The results showed that LC is a potent inhibitor of this reaction, with an IC<sub>50</sub> of 0.32 µM for *S. aureus* 70S ribosomes and 10.0 µM for *D. radiodurans* 50S subunits, providing direct biochemical confirmation that LC targets the catalytic heart of the ribosome, the PTC.

### RNA Probing

The RNA probing experiments, which were critical for mapping the binding sites of LC and LM and demonstrating their simultaneous binding, were performed using chemical modification of the 23S rRNA within the *D. radiodurans* 50S subunit. The 50S subunits were diluted to a final concentration of 200 nM in a probing buffer. The antibiotics were added to the following final concentrations: LC at 50 µM, LM at 500 µM, and erythromycin (ERY) at 50 µM. The ribosomal samples were pre-incubated with the antibiotics for 10 minutes at 37°C followed by 10 minutes at 20°C to ensure binding equilibrium. The chemical modifying reagents, 1-cyclohexyl-3-(2-morpholinoethyl) carbodiimide metho-p-toluene sulfonate (CMCT) and dimethyl sulfate (DMS), were then added to the samples to modify accessible uridine and adenine residues, respectively. The modification reactions were allowed to proceed for a defined period before being quenched. The rRNA was subsequently extracted from the ribosomal samples, and specific regions of the 23S rRNA encompassing the PTC and NPET were reverse-transcribed using fluorescently labeled DNA primers. The resulting cDNA fragments were separated by capillary electrophoresis, and the sites of modification (which cause stops or pauses in reverse transcription) were identified by comparing the fragment patterns from drug-treated and untreated samples. A decrease in the intensity of a stop band in the drug-treated sample compared to the control indicates that the corresponding nucleotide was protected from modification by the bound antibiotic. This method provided the high-resolution footprint of each drug on the rRNA, revealing the specific nucleotides whose accessibility was altered by binding.

### Cell-Free Translation System Used for Detecting Synergism In Vitro

An independent, customized *E. coli* cell-free translation system was developed to specifically assess the synergistic interaction between LC and LM in vitro. The cell-free extract was prepared from *E. coli* strain BL21-DE3 following a published protocol. The translation reaction mixture contained a comprehensive blend of components necessary for efficient protein synthesis, including an amino acid mix (1.3 mM each), magnesium acetate (20 mM), NTP mix (0.9 mM), ATP (0.4 mM), potassium glutamate (150 mM), *E. coli* tRNA mixture (0.17 mg/mL), DTT (1.8 mM), folinic acid (35 µg/mL), cAMP (0.65 mM), NH<sub>4</sub>OAc (28 mM), creatine phosphate (80 mM), HEPES buffer (140 mM, pH 7.5 at 37°C), PEG-8000 (9.5% w/v), tyrosine (0.4 mg/mL), creatine kinase (0.35 mg/mL), and the S12 cell-free extract (12% v/v). The reaction was initiated by the addition of a plasmid (PIVEX.6D) encoding wild-type green fluorescent protein (GFP) at a concentration of 1 ng/µL. This master mix was immediately dispensed into a 96-well plate that already contained a checkerboard array of serial dilutions of lankamycin and lankacidin. The plate was incubated for 1 hour at 37°C to allow for GFP synthesis. To ensure all reactions were stopped at the same point, 50 µL of a high concentration of erythromycin (8 µM) was added to every well to completely halt translation. The amount of GFP produced in each well was then quantified by measuring fluorescence (excitation at 485 nm, emission at 535 nm) using a TECAN SpectraFluor Plus™ plate reader. The fluorescence data, representing the level of protein synthesis, were analyzed using a least-squares regression analysis package in Igor Pro™ software to generate a three-dimensional surface plot of inhibition. This in vitro system provided a direct and quantitative biochemical confirmation of the synergistic inhibition of protein synthesis by the LC/LM combination, mirroring the results obtained from the whole-cell checkerboard assay.

## Pharmacology

### Chemical Derivatives and Conjugates

The detailed structural understanding of the LC/LM-ribosome complex provided by the present invention creates a powerful foundation for the rational design of novel chemical derivatives and conjugates with enhanced therapeutic properties. The invention contemplates a broad range of modifications aimed at improving the potency, spectrum of activity, pharmacokinetic profile, and, most importantly, the synergistic efficacy of the LC/LM pair. One primary strategy involves the semi-synthetic modification of the lankamycin molecule. The structural data suggest that the key difference between LM and the competing erythromycin (ERY) lies in their C5-linked sugar moieties. Therefore, the invention provides for the creation of LM derivatives where this sugar is chemically altered to introduce new functional groups. These new groups could be designed to form additional hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, or van der Waals contacts with specific nucleotides in the NPET, thereby increasing LM's intrinsic binding affinity. More strategically, these groups could be engineered to interact favorably with the lankacidin C molecule when both are bound to the ribosome, creating a direct intermolecular bridge that would stabilize the ternary complex and dramatically amplify the synergistic effect.

Another major class of compounds enabled by this invention is the covalent conjugate, where a single molecule is constructed by linking the LC and LM pharmacophores together with a chemical linker. This approach guarantees that both inhibitory moieties are delivered to the same ribosome simultaneously, eliminating any issues related to differential uptake, distribution, or metabolism that can plague traditional combination therapies. The length, flexibility, and chemical nature of the linker are critical design parameters. The linker must be long and flexible enough to allow both LC and LM to adopt their optimal binding conformations within their respective pockets in the PTC and NPET, but not so long as to allow the molecule to adopt non-productive conformations or to reduce its overall binding affinity. The structural model of the LC/LM-ribosome complex provides the precise spatial coordinates needed to design such a linker. Furthermore, the linker itself could be functionalized to carry additional properties, such as groups that improve solubility, enhance penetration through bacterial membranes, or provide a handle for attachment to delivery vehicles. The invention also encompasses derivatives of lankacidin C, for instance, modifications to its macrocyclic ring or lactone moiety to enhance its interaction with the PTC or to create a complementary surface for interaction with a modified LM. These rationally designed derivatives and conjugates represent a new generation of "dual-warhead" antibiotics whose development is directly informed by the atomic-level insights of the present invention.

### Synthetic and Semi-Synthetic Methods for Producing Antibiotics

The present invention provides the necessary knowledge and motivation to develop and apply advanced synthetic and semi-synthetic methodologies for the production of lankacidin, lankamycin, and their novel derivatives and conjugates. The starting materials for these processes can be the natural products themselves, isolated from cultures of *Streptomyces rochei* or from other producing strains, or they can be advanced intermediates obtained through fermentation or total synthesis. Semi-synthetic approaches are particularly well-suited for modifying the peripheral functional groups of these complex molecules, such as the sugar moieties of lankamycin or the hydroxyl groups on the lankacidin macrocycle. Standard organic chemistry techniques, including selective protection/deprotection strategies, glycosylation reactions, acylation, alkylation, and oxidation/reduction, can be employed to introduce the desired chemical modifications. For example, to create an LM derivative with an enhanced NPET-binding group, a chemist could selectively deprotect the C5-hydroxyl of the arcanose sugar and then perform a coupling reaction with a carboxylic acid or amine-containing side chain designed based on the ribosome structure.

For the construction of covalent LC-LM conjugates, more sophisticated synthetic strategies are required. One approach involves the total chemical synthesis of both pharmacophores, allowing for the precise incorporation of a unique chemical handle (e.g., an azide or alkyne group) at a predetermined position on each molecule. These handles can then be joined together in the final step using a highly efficient and bioorthogonal "click" chemistry reaction, such as the copper-catalyzed azide-alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC). An alternative, potentially more efficient route is a chemoenzymatic approach, where the biosynthetic machinery of the producing organism is genetically engineered to incorporate non-natural building blocks that contain the desired linker or functional group. This could involve feeding the engineered strain with synthetic precursors that are accepted by the tailoring enzymes of the LC or LM biosynthetic pathway. The choice of method will depend on the complexity of the desired final product, the availability of starting materials, and the scale of production required. The present invention, by defining the precise structural requirements for synergistic activity, provides a clear roadmap for medicinal chemists to prioritize which synthetic targets are most likely to yield a successful therapeutic agent, thereby streamlining the drug discovery and development process for this new class of synergistic ribosome inhibitors.
