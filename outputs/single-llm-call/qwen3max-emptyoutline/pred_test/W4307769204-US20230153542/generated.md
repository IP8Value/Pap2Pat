## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to natural language processing and, more specifically, to systems and methods for cross-lingual sentence alignment using machine learning models trained on parallel data from a single rich-resource language pair. The invention further pertains to architectures that enable zero-shot or few-shot transfer of alignment capabilities across diverse language pairs without requiring parallel training data for each target language pair, thereby facilitating efficient and scalable multilingual sentence retrieval in low-resource settings.

## BACKGROUND

Cross-lingual sentence retrieval—the task of identifying translationally equivalent sentence pairs across different languages—has become increasingly vital in the era of global digital communication and multilingual artificial intelligence. Such aligned sentence pairs constitute foundational resources for numerous downstream applications, including but not limited to neural machine translation (NMT), cross-lingual information retrieval, multilingual semantic parsing, and quality estimation of machine-translated text. Historically, the acquisition of high-quality parallel corpora has relied heavily on manual translation or labor-intensive curation from bilingual sources such as parliamentary proceedings, religious texts, or official documents. However, these sources are inherently limited in linguistic diversity, domain coverage, and scalability, particularly for low-resource languages that lack institutional support or digital presence.

To address this bottleneck, researchers have turned to automated bitext mining from vast, unlabeled multilingual corpora such as Common Crawl, Wikipedia, or news archives. In this paradigm, a sentence alignment model is deployed to sift through massive collections of monolingual documents in two or more languages and identify candidate sentence pairs that are likely translations of one another. The efficacy of such models directly determines the precision and recall of the mined parallel data, which in turn influences the performance of downstream systems trained on this data. Consequently, the development of robust, generalizable, and data-efficient alignment models has emerged as a critical research objective.

Existing approaches to cross-lingual sentence alignment fall broadly into two categories: unsupervised and supervised. Unsupervised methods operate without any parallel training data, leveraging only monolingual corpora and pre-trained multilingual representations—such as those provided by multilingual BERT (mBERT) or XLM-R—to induce cross-lingual similarity. These models typically encode source and target sentences independently using a shared encoder, pool the resulting contextual embeddings, and compute cosine similarity between the pooled vectors. While elegant in their minimalism and applicable to any language supported by the underlying model, unsupervised aligners often suffer from suboptimal accuracy, especially on low-resource or typologically distant language pairs. Their performance on standard benchmarks like Tatoeba—a dataset comprising 1,000 sentence pairs across 36 diverse languages—typically lags significantly behind human-level alignment fidelity.

In contrast, supervised approaches fine-tune large multilingual models on extensive collections of parallel sentences drawn from multiple language pairs. State-of-the-art systems such as VECO and ERNIE-M exemplify this strategy, training on billions of parallel examples spanning hundreds of languages. These models achieve impressive results by explicitly learning cross-lingual correspondences through direct supervision. However, their success hinges on access to vast, high-quality parallel corpora—a resource that is unevenly distributed across the world’s languages. By definition, low-resource languages lack sufficient parallel data, rendering supervised methods impractical or impossible to apply in precisely the contexts where they are most needed. Moreover, the assumption that performance scales linearly with the number of language pairs used during training leads to inefficient allocation of data collection efforts, as marginal gains diminish rapidly beyond a certain point.

This dichotomy—between data-scarce unsupervised models and data-hungry supervised systems—leaves a significant gap in practical applicability. It raises fundamental questions about the minimal conditions under which effective cross-lingual alignment can be achieved: How much parallel data is truly necessary? Must it span many language pairs, or can a single rich-resource pair suffice? Is English-centric training essential, or can alignment capabilities generalize from non-English pivots? And critically, how do monolingual pretraining data volumes interact with parallel fine-tuning to enable zero-shot transfer?

Prior art fails to provide clear answers. Many supervised models implicitly assume that broader language coverage during training yields better generalization, yet empirical evidence for this assumption is mixed. Furthermore, existing architectures often neglect systematic biases inherent in similarity scoring—such as the “popular sentence effect,” wherein certain source sentences exhibit spuriously high similarity to nearly all target sentences due to lexical or structural properties—leading to degraded ranking performance. Additionally, most models rely on Siamese encoding schemes that forgo cross-attention between source and target tokens, sacrificing nuanced semantic alignment for computational efficiency. While full cross-attention (as in BERT-style [CLS][SEP] concatenation) offers richer interaction, it is prohibitively slow for large-scale retrieval and sensitive to input ordering, complicating deployment.

Thus, there exists a pressing need for a sentence alignment system that reconciles the efficiency of unsupervised methods with the accuracy of supervised ones, while operating under realistic data constraints. Such a system should require minimal parallel data—ideally from just one language pair—yet generalize robustly across hundreds of languages, including those unseen during fine-tuning. It must incorporate architectural innovations to mitigate scoring biases, leverage efficient yet expressive similarity metrics, and employ training strategies that maximize transfer without overfitting. The present invention addresses these needs through the introduction of OneAligner, a novel alignment model that achieves state-of-the-art performance with unprecedented data efficiency and cross-lingual generalization.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a system and method for cross-lingual sentence alignment that achieves high accuracy across a wide range of language pairs using parallel training data from only a single rich-resource language pair. This system, referred to herein as OneAligner, is built upon a pre-trained multilingual transformer model—specifically, XLM-RoBERTa (XLM-R)—and incorporates several key architectural and algorithmic innovations to enhance alignment fidelity, mitigate bias, and enable robust zero-shot transfer to unseen language pairs.

At its core, OneAligner leverages a modified version of BERT-score, an automatic evaluation metric originally designed for monolingual sentence similarity assessment, and adapts it for cross-lingual semantic similarity computation. Unlike conventional Siamese architectures that independently encode source and target sentences and compute cosine similarity between mean-pooled representations, OneAligner computes token-level pairwise similarities between all tokens in the source and target sequences using the final layer contextual embeddings from XLM-R. Specifically, given a source sentence \( s = \{s_1, s_2, ..., s_M\} \) and a target sentence \( t = \{t_1, t_2, ..., t_N\} \), the model first encodes both sequences through the shared XLM-R encoder to obtain contextual token embeddings \( \mathbf{H}_s \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times d} \) and \( \mathbf{H}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d} \), where \( d \) is the hidden dimension. The BERT-score similarity \( F(s, t) \) is then computed as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, where precision is the average maximum cosine similarity from each source token to any target token, and recall is the symmetric counterpart:

\[
\text{Precision}(s, t) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^M \max_{j=1..N} \cos(\mathbf{h}_{s_i}, \mathbf{h}_{t_j}), \quad
\text{Recall}(s, t) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \max_{i=1..M} \cos(\mathbf{h}_{s_i}, \mathbf{h}_{t_j})
\]

\[
F(s, t) = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision}(s, t) \cdot \text{Recall}(s, t)}{\text{Precision}(s, t) + \text{Recall}(s, t)}
\]

This formulation introduces a shallow form of cross-attention—each token in one language attends to the most similar token in the other—without the quadratic computational overhead of full self-attention over concatenated sequences. Crucially, because the similarity is computed symmetrically and does not depend on input ordering, the model remains agnostic to whether the source precedes the target or vice versa, simplifying inference and enabling bidirectional alignment.

However, raw BERT-score similarities are susceptible to the “popular sentence effect,” a phenomenon wherein certain sentences—often short, generic, or containing frequent function words—exhibit artificially high similarity scores with a broad range of sentences in the opposite language. This bias distorts the relative ranking of candidates during retrieval, leading to false positives and reduced precision. To counteract this, OneAligner integrates an in-batch normalization layer directly into its architecture. During both training and inference, for each sentence pair \( (S_i, T_j) \) within a batch of size \( B \), the model adjusts the raw similarity score \( f(S_i, T_j) \) by subtracting a scaled average of its similarities to all other sentences in the opposing language within the same batch:

\[
\tilde{f}(S_i, T_j) = f(S_i, T_j) - \alpha \left( \frac{1}{B} \sum_{k=1}^B f(S_i, T_k) + \frac{1}{B} \sum_{k=1}^B f(S_k, T_j) \right)
\]

Here, \( \alpha \) is a tunable hyperparameter that controls the strength of normalization; empirical studies have shown that \( \alpha = 0.75 \) yields optimal performance across diverse language pairs. This normalization effectively centers each sentence’s similarity distribution around zero, penalizing sentences that are universally “popular” and promoting discriminative alignment signals. Importantly, because the normalization is performed within the confines of a fixed batch size—rather than over the entire evaluation set—it remains computationally feasible even in streaming or online inference scenarios where the full candidate pool is unknown or too large to process at once.

Training OneAligner presents a unique challenge: parallel corpora provide only positive examples (true translation pairs), but effective alignment requires the model to distinguish these from negative (non-translation) pairs. To generate informative negatives without explicit labeling, OneAligner employs a contrastive learning framework with global in-batch negatives. Specifically, given a batch of \( N \) aligned sentence pairs \( \{(S_1, T_1), ..., (S_N, T_N)\} \), the model computes the normalized BERT-score for all \( N^2 \) possible source-target combinations, yielding \( N \) positive logits (on the diagonal) and \( N^2 - N \) negative logits (off-diagonal). Each positive logit is then contrasted against all \( N^2 - N \) negatives via a cross-entropy loss:

\[
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log \frac{\exp(\tilde{f}(S_i, T_i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(\tilde{f}(S_i, T_j)/\tau) + \sum_{k \neq i} \exp(\tilde{f}(S_k, T_i)/\tau)}
\]

where \( \tau \) is a temperature parameter (set to 5.0 in practice) that sharpens the probability distribution. This “global” negative sampling strategy—contrasting each positive against all possible mismatches in the batch—provides stronger learning signals than traditional “local” approaches that only consider row- or column-wise negatives. It enables the model to learn a universal similarity threshold that separates true alignments from noise, a capability essential for real-world mining tasks like BUCC 2018, where most sentence pairs are non-translations.

The training protocol adheres to a strict data budget of one million parallel sentence pairs, regardless of the number of language pairs used. This constraint ensures fair comparison across experimental conditions and reflects practical limitations in data collection. Training is conducted for three epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of \( 3 \times 10^{-6} \), a batch size of 64 (the maximum feasible on a single A100 GPU), and a maximum sequence length of 100 tokens per sentence. Sentences with fewer than five tokens in either language are filtered out post-sampling to eliminate uninformative or noisy examples.

OneAligner’s design deliberately avoids introducing new trainable parameters beyond those of the base XLM-R model. Instead, it repurposes existing layers and adds only lightweight, differentiable operations (similarity computation, normalization, loss calculation). This parameter efficiency ensures rapid convergence and mitigates overfitting, particularly when training on a single language pair. Remarkably, despite this simplicity, OneAligner trained on just one rich-resource pair—such as English-Spanish or French-German—achieves alignment accuracy within 2.0 percentage points of a model trained on all available language pairs under the same data budget. When evaluated on the Tatoeba-36 benchmark, which includes low-resource pairs like Javanese-English and Swedish-English, OneAligner consistently scores above 92% accuracy across diverse training pivots.

Further experiments demonstrate that English is not a privileged pivot. Models trained on non-English-centered rich-resource pairs—such as Spanish-French or German-Russian—perform comparably on English-centered evaluation sets, and vice versa. This symmetry arises because alignment capability depends not on the specific identity of the training languages, but on two thresholds being met: (1) the parallel training data for the pivot pair exceeds approximately 10,000–20,000 high-quality sentence pairs, and (2) the monolingual pretraining data for each evaluation language in CC-100 surpasses a critical mass (typically tens of millions of tokens). Once these thresholds are crossed, additional data yields diminishing returns, and cross-lingual transfer becomes robust.

To push performance closer to the theoretical upper bound, OneAligner can be trained on the top-k richest-resource language pairs (e.g., the top 32 by data volume), still under the one-million-example budget. This scaling strategy incrementally improves accuracy—reaching 94.0% on Tatoeba-36, just 0.4 points below the model trained on all pairs—while remaining firmly within the rich-resource-only paradigm. Critically, even the “least rich” language in the top-32 (Ukrainian, with ~34M monolingual tokens) far exceeds typical low-resource definitions, confirming that marginal gains do not justify the immense cost of collecting parallel data for truly low-resource languages.

Validation on the BUCC 2018 bitext mining task—a more realistic scenario involving document-level noise and a binary accept/reject decision—confirms OneAligner’s practical utility. Despite never seeing German, French, Russian, or Chinese during training (when using, e.g., English-Spanish as the pivot), OneAligner outperforms VECO—a model trained extensively on these exact pairs—by 1.2 F1 points. This result underscores the model’s ability to generalize alignment criteria across linguistic boundaries without language-specific tuning.

In summary, OneAligner redefines the economics of cross-lingual alignment by demonstrating that high-fidelity sentence retrieval can be achieved with minimal parallel data investment. Its innovations—cross-lingual BERT-score, in-batch normalization, global contrastive learning, and threshold-based transfer—collectively enable a new paradigm: collect parallel data for one or a few rich-resource pairs, and deploy a single model globally. This approach dramatically reduces the burden on language communities, accelerates the development of multilingual AI, and opens new pathways for equitable technology access.

### Example Performance

The performance of OneAligner has been rigorously evaluated across multiple benchmarks to validate its efficacy, generalizability, and robustness. Primary evaluation was conducted on three datasets: Tatoeba-36, New-Tatoeba, and BUCC 2018. Tatoeba-36, part of the XTREME benchmark, comprises 1,000 sentence pairs across 36 languages, including several low-resource varieties such as Javanese (jv), Swahili (sw), and Tagalog (tl), all aligned to English. New-Tatoeba is a more expansive challenge set derived from the Tatoeba Challenge corpus, containing 223 language pairs (49 English-centered, 174 non-English-centered), with 1,000 randomly sampled examples per pair to ensure uniform difficulty. BUCC 2018 is a bitext mining task involving four high-resource language pairs (en-de, en-fr, en-ru, en-zh), where the goal is to identify true translations within noisy document-aligned corpora; performance is measured by F1 score rather than ranking accuracy.

When trained on the full OPUS-100 dataset (approximately 55 million sentence pairs across 100 languages) without a data cap, OneAligner achieves 94.9% accuracy on Tatoeba-36, establishing a new state of the art. Under a strict one-million-example budget—used consistently across all experiments to ensure comparability—the model still attains 94.4% accuracy, confirming that the majority of performance gains are realized well before exhausting available data. This data efficiency starkly contrasts with prior systems like VECO, which trains on 6.4 billion parallel examples across 879 language pairs yet achieves only 86.9% accuracy on the same benchmark.

More remarkably, OneAligner trained on a single rich-resource language pair—such as English-Spanish (en-es), which alone contributes over 1 million high-quality examples in OPUS-100—achieves 92.4% accuracy on Tatoeba-36. This result is consistent across the top-16 richest-resource pairs: whether trained on en-fr, en-de, fr-es, or de-ru, the model’s accuracy varies by less than 1.0 point, demonstrating insensitivity to the choice of pivot language. A scatter plot of accuracy versus training data volume reveals a clear threshold effect: performance plateaus once the pivot pair exceeds approximately 15,000 sentence pairs, beyond which additional data yields negligible improvement. This finding directly informs data collection strategy—efforts should prioritize reaching this threshold for a few pairs rather than spreading thinly across many.

Non-English-centered training further validates the model’s generality. OneAligner trained on Spanish-French (es-fr), the richest non-English pair in New-Tatoeba, achieves 92.2% accuracy on Tatoeba-36—virtually identical to English-centered variants. Conversely, models trained on English-centered pairs perform equally well on non-English evaluation sets in New-Tatoeba, provided the evaluation languages meet monolingual data thresholds in CC-100. For instance, alignment accuracy for Hungarian-Romanian (hu-ro) correlates strongly with the geometric mean of their respective monolingual corpus sizes, with performance stabilizing once both exceed ~10 million tokens. This dual-threshold mechanism—parallel data for the pivot, monolingual data for the targets—explains why transfer succeeds even without direct supervision.

Scaling to multiple rich-resource pairs incrementally boosts performance. Training on the top-1, top-4, top-8, and top-32 English-centered pairs (under the one-million budget) yields accuracies of 92.4%, 92.9%, 93.2%, and 94.0%, respectively. The top-32 includes languages as diverse as Ukrainian (uk), Persian (fa), and Korean (ko), all of which remain in the “highest-resource” tier per Tatoeba Challenge definitions. The 0.4-point gap to the all-pairs model suggests that further gains would require either architectural changes or inclusion of genuinely low-resource data—a direction the inventors deem currently unjustified given the steep data collection costs.

On BUCC 2018, OneAligner trained on en-es achieves an average F1 score of 89.3 across the four language pairs, surpassing VECO’s 88.1 despite VECO’s extensive in-language training. This result is particularly significant because BUCC requires a universal decision threshold to separate true alignments from noise—a capability enabled by OneAligner’s global contrastive learning and in-batch normalization. The model correctly rejects spurious matches like “The cat sat on the mat” aligned to “Die Wirtschaft wächst stetig” (The economy is growing steadily), demonstrating semantic discernment beyond surface-level keyword overlap.

Ablation studies confirm the necessity of each component. Removing BERT-score in favor of mean-pooled cosine similarity reduces Tatoeba-36 accuracy by 4–6 points. Disabling in-batch normalization causes a 2–3 point drop, primarily due to popular sentence effects inflating false positives. Replacing global negatives with local ones degrades BUCC F1 by 1.5 points, highlighting the importance of holistic contrastive signals. Together, these modifications contribute synergistically to OneAligner’s superior performance.

In practical deployment, OneAligner operates efficiently on standard hardware. With a batch size of 256 during inference, it processes over 10,000 sentence pairs per second on an A100 GPU, making it suitable for large-scale web mining. Its order-agnostic similarity computation simplifies integration into bidirectional retrieval pipelines, and its lack of language-specific parameters enables seamless extension to new languages—provided they are covered by XLM-R’s tokenizer and have sufficient monolingual pretraining data.

These results collectively establish OneAligner as a transformative solution for cross-lingual sentence alignment. By decoupling performance from exhaustive parallel data collection and demonstrating robust zero-shot transfer, it enables scalable, equitable, and cost-effective development of multilingual NLP systems worldwide.
