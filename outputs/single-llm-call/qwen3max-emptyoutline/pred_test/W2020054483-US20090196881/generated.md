# DESCRIPTION

## INTRODUCTION

Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) is a highly contagious and often fatal rhabdoviral pathogen that poses a significant threat to global aquaculture, particularly affecting cyprinid fish species such as common carp (*Cyprinus carpio carpio*), koi (*Cyprinus carpio koi*), and goldfish (*Carassius auratus*). Historically endemic to Europe, where it has caused widespread mortality in carp populations across countries including Russia, Romania, the Netherlands, Moldavia, Georgia, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, and Denmark, SVCV has more recently emerged as a serious concern in North America following its first documented detection in 2002 at a major koi production facility in the United States. This initial outbreak led to the death of approximately 15,000 fish and the preventive euthanasia of an additional 135,000 fish across facilities in North Carolina and Virginia. Subsequent isolations of the virus in wild carp in Wisconsin, Illinois, Washington State, Missouri, and Canada underscore the rapid geographic expansion and potential for establishment of SVCV in previously unaffected regions. Due to its high infectivity, severe pathological consequences—including inflammation, hemorrhaging, and necrosis of internal organs such as the spleen, kidney, intestines, and air bladder—and its capacity to cause mass mortality during seasonal temperature transitions, particularly between 11°C and 17°C, SVCV is classified by the World Organisation for Animal Health (Office International des Épizooties, OIE) as one of only nine notifiable piscine viral diseases worldwide. This designation mandates immediate reporting of any confirmed case within 48 hours and imposes strict regulatory controls on the international movement of susceptible fish species, their gametes, and fertilized eggs.

Despite the severity of SVCV outbreaks and the economic losses they inflict on ornamental and food fish industries, effective therapeutic or prophylactic interventions have remained elusive. Traditional control strategies rely primarily on the eradication of infected stocks and stringent biosecurity measures, which are reactive rather than preventative and often economically devastating for affected operations. Early attempts to develop inactivated vaccines using European strains of SVCV demonstrated limited efficacy but were ultimately abandoned due to concerns over incomplete viral inactivation, prohibitive production costs, complex regulatory and marketing barriers, and the absence of robust quantitative data confirming protective immunity. In contrast, DNA-based vaccination has emerged as a promising alternative platform for combating viral pathogens in aquaculture, offering advantages such as straightforward molecular design, thermal stability, low manufacturing expenses, long-term storage viability, and an absence of risk for reversion to virulence or chemical contamination. DNA vaccines function by delivering a plasmid encoding a key viral antigen—typically the surface glycoprotein—into host cells, where it is expressed and presented to the immune system, thereby eliciting both humoral and cellular immune responses. This approach has achieved notable success against other rhabdoviruses in fish, particularly infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV) and viral hemorrhagic septicemia virus (VHSV), both members of the *Novirhabdovirus* genus, for which licensed or field-tested DNA vaccines now exist. However, SVCV, tentatively classified within the *Vesiculovirus* genus, lacks the nonvirion (NV) gene characteristic of novirhabdoviruses and exhibits distinct genetic and structural features, which may account for the historically poor performance of DNA vaccines targeting European SVCV isolates. Phylogenetic analyses have revealed that all North American SVCV isolates, including those from the 2002 U.S. outbreak and subsequent detections, belong to genogroup Ia, indicating an Asian origin distinct from the Ib, Ic, and Id genogroups prevalent in Europe. This genetic divergence suggests that vaccine constructs based on European strains may be suboptimal for protecting North American fish populations against locally circulating Ia variants.

Accordingly, there exists a critical unmet need for a safe, effective, and commercially viable vaccine specifically tailored to the North American strain of SVCV. The present invention addresses this gap by providing a novel DNA vaccine construct comprising the full-length glycoprotein (G) gene of the North Carolina isolate of SVCV (SVCVnc), which belongs to the Ia genogroup. This vaccine, designated herein as pSGnc, has been rigorously evaluated in controlled challenge trials using a newly developed and validated infection model in koi, a highly susceptible and economically relevant host species. The development of this model itself constitutes a significant advancement, as prior efforts to establish reproducible SVCV challenge protocols were hampered by inconsistent host susceptibility and variable environmental stressors. Through systematic comparative testing, koi were identified as a reliable and sensitive host for SVCVnc, whereas goldfish exhibited unexpectedly low susceptibility under the experimental conditions employed. Furthermore, the invention includes an optimized cold stress protocol that significantly streamlines the challenge procedure without compromising disease induction, thereby enhancing the efficiency and reproducibility of vaccine efficacy assessments. The pSGnc vaccine, when administered via intramuscular injection at a dose of 10 micrograms, conferred substantial protection against homologous SVCVnc challenge, with relative percent survival (RPS) values ranging from 50% to 88% across four independent trials conducted under varying conditions of fish age, challenge dose, and seasonal timing. Critically, vaccinated survivors consistently demonstrated either undetectable or markedly reduced viral loads compared to non-vaccinated or mock-vaccinated controls, indicating not only enhanced survival but also effective viral clearance. These results represent a marked improvement over previous DNA vaccine candidates based on European SVCV strains and constitute the first successful demonstration of a DNA vaccine against SVCV in koi. The present invention thus provides a foundational tool for the prevention and control of SVCV in North America and potentially other regions threatened by the Ia genogroup, offering a proactive strategy to safeguard vulnerable fish stocks, support the sustainability of the ornamental and aquaculture industries, and mitigate the ecological and economic risks associated with this OIE-notifiable pathogen.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel DNA vaccine for the prevention of Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) infection in susceptible fish species, particularly koi (*Cyprinus carpio koi*) and other cyprinids. The vaccine comprises a recombinant plasmid vector containing a nucleotide sequence encoding the full-length glycoprotein (G) of a North American isolate of SVCV, specifically the North Carolina isolate (SVCVnc) belonging to genogroup Ia. This plasmid, designated pSGnc, is constructed by cloning the complete G gene of SVCVnc into a mammalian expression vector, such as pcDNA3.1, under the control of a strong promoter, for example, the cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate-early promoter, thereby enabling efficient expression of the SVCV glycoprotein in fish host cells following vaccination. The invention further encompasses a method of immunizing fish against SVCV, which involves administering to the fish an effective amount of the pSGnc DNA vaccine, preferably by intramuscular injection, to elicit a protective immune response that reduces mortality and viral load upon subsequent exposure to the virus. In addition to the vaccine composition and immunization method, the present invention includes a standardized and reliable challenge model for evaluating the efficacy of SVCV vaccines. This model utilizes koi as the primary susceptible host species and employs a rapid cold stress protocol immediately prior to viral challenge, wherein fish are transferred directly from acclimation temperatures of 16–20°C to challenge temperatures of 10–12°C on the day of exposure. Challenge can be administered either by intraperitoneal injection of a defined viral titer or by immersion in water containing the virus, with both methods producing consistent and reproducible mortality in non-vaccinated controls. The invention also provides a method for determining the susceptibility of a fish species to SVCV, involving the exposure of the fish to the SVCVnc isolate under the conditions of the challenge model and monitoring for mortality and viral replication. The pSGnc vaccine has demonstrated significant protective efficacy in multiple independent trials, achieving relative percent survival (RPS) values of 50% to 88% against homologous SVCVnc challenge, and represents the first DNA vaccine shown to be effective against an Ia genogroup SVCV isolate and the first such vaccine successfully tested in koi. The invention thus offers a comprehensive solution for the prophylactic management of SVCV, combining a genetically optimized vaccine antigen with a validated biological assay system for its evaluation, thereby addressing a critical need in global aquaculture health and biosecurity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Susceptibility Testing of Goldfish and Koi

The development of an effective vaccine against Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) necessitates a reliable and reproducible challenge model capable of consistently inducing disease in a susceptible host species, thereby enabling accurate assessment of vaccine efficacy. Prior to the present invention, the selection of an appropriate host for SVCV challenge experiments was complicated by variable reports of susceptibility among different cyprinid species and inconsistencies in experimental protocols, particularly regarding the role of environmental stressors such as temperature fluctuations. To address this, the inventors undertook a systematic comparison of two commonly available and economically relevant cyprinid species—goldfish (*Carassius auratus*) and koi (*Cyprinus carpio koi*)—for their susceptibility to a North American isolate of SVCV, specifically the North Carolina isolate (SVCVnc) belonging to genogroup Ia. Goldfish were initially selected for evaluation due to their reported natural susceptibility to SVCV in previous outbreaks and their practical advantages in laboratory settings, including ease of acquisition, hardiness, and straightforward husbandry requirements. The goldfish used in these studies were less than one year old, measuring 7–10 cm in length and weighing an average of 14.6 grams, and were sourced from a facility certified as a compartment free of SVCV according to OIE standards, thereby ensuring a specific pathogen-free status at the outset of the experiments. These fish were maintained in a controlled wet laboratory environment with flow-through sand-filtered and UV-treated freshwater at temperatures of 16–18°C and fed a standard diet every other day.

Koi, on the other hand, were chosen for parallel testing due to their well-documented vulnerability to SVCV in multiple North American outbreaks, including the seminal 2002 incident at a large-scale production facility, and their immense economic importance in the ornamental fish trade. The koi used in the present studies were derived from a domestic U.S. stock that had been bred in captivity for multiple generations and had voluntarily participated in the USDA Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) screening program for SVCV, confirming their specific pathogen-free status. These koi were obtained as one-month-old juveniles, weighing approximately 0.94 grams on average, and were reared under identical water quality and feeding conditions as the goldfish, though with a more varied diet tailored to their developmental stage. All challenge experiments were conducted under strict aquatic Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) containment protocols, as mandated by the OIE listing of SVCV and its status as an exotic pathogen in North America, to prevent any risk of environmental release.

To evaluate susceptibility, both goldfish and koi were subjected to two distinct modes of viral exposure: intraperitoneal (IP) injection and water bath immersion. Prior to challenge, all fish were transferred to the BSL-3 facility and subjected to a standardized cold stress protocol, which involved a gradual reduction in water temperature by 1°C per day until reaching 10–12°C, followed by a five-day acclimation period at this lower temperature. This protocol was implemented to mimic the natural environmental conditions that typically precede SVCV outbreaks in the spring, when rising water temperatures following a cold winter period are believed to act as a trigger for disease manifestation. For the IP injection challenges, fish were anesthetized and administered a fixed dose of 1 × 10⁶ plaque-forming units (PFU) of SVCVnc in a 100 µl volume. Immersion challenges were conducted by placing fish in a static volume of 3 liters of aerated water containing varying concentrations of the virus (1 × 10³, 1 × 10⁴, or 1 × 10⁵ PFU/ml) for a duration of one hour, after which normal water flow was resumed. Mock control groups for both exposure routes received equivalent volumes of virus-free culture medium (MEM-10) under identical conditions. Each treatment group consisted of duplicate tanks containing ten fish per tank, and mortality was monitored daily for a period of thirty days, with dead fish removed and recorded. Cumulative percent mortality (CPM) was calculated as the total number of deaths in pooled duplicate tanks divided by the initial number of fish, multiplied by 100. Statistical analysis using Fisher’s exact test confirmed no significant difference in mortality between duplicate tanks, validating the pooling of data for CPM calculations.

The results of these susceptibility tests revealed a striking disparity between the two species. Goldfish exhibited remarkably low susceptibility to SVCVnc under the experimental conditions employed. Across two separate challenge experiments, the vast majority of goldfish—96% overall—survived both injection and immersion exposures, regardless of the viral dose used in immersion. This unexpected resilience, despite the species’ historical association with SVCV outbreaks, may be attributed to several factors, including the relatively large size and advanced age of the goldfish used (average 14.6 g), which may confer greater innate resistance or a more robust immune competence compared to younger, smaller individuals. In stark contrast, koi demonstrated profound and consistent susceptibility to SVCVnc. In preliminary experiments using small groups of three koi per treatment, all fish succumbed to IP injection, and a high proportion died following immersion. In a subsequent, more robust experiment with ten koi per duplicate tank, 100% mortality was observed in the IP-injected group, and 75% mortality occurred in the immersion group exposed to 1 × 10⁵ PFU/ml. Viral quantification via plaque assay on whole-fish homogenates from both dead and surviving koi confirmed active viral replication, with titers in positive samples consistently exceeding 1 × 10⁶ PFU/g. These findings unequivocally established the domestic koi stock as a highly reliable and sensitive host for SVCVnc, making it an ideal candidate for use as a positive control in vaccine challenge studies and as the primary target species for vaccine development. The identification of koi as the preferred host, coupled with the exclusion of goldfish due to their low susceptibility in this context, was a critical step in the development of the standardized challenge model that underpins the evaluation of the present invention’s vaccine efficacy.

### Example 2

The present invention further includes an optimized protocol for inducing SVCV infection in challenge models, which addresses the practical limitations of traditional cold stress regimens while maintaining high reproducibility and disease penetrance. Historically, many researchers have incorporated a prolonged cold acclimation period—often spanning two weeks or more—into their SVCV challenge protocols, based on the hypothesis that gradual exposure to cooler temperatures mimics the natural pre-outbreak conditions and is essential for successful viral infection. While such protocols can be effective, they are time-consuming, labor-intensive, and introduce additional variables that can complicate experimental timelines and increase the risk of confounding factors. To streamline the challenge process without sacrificing reliability, the inventors conducted a comparative study to evaluate the efficacy of a rapid cold stress protocol against the conventional slow acclimation method. In this study, two groups of specific pathogen-free koi, aged 3.5 months and weighing an average of 3.5 grams, were subjected to different thermal stress regimens immediately prior to SVCVnc challenge. The first group underwent the slow cold stress protocol, which involved a gradual reduction in water temperature from the stock holding temperature of 16–18°C to the challenge temperature range of 10–12°C at a rate of 1°C per day, followed by a five-day acclimation period at the target temperature, resulting in a total pre-challenge conditioning period of approximately 14 days. The second group was subjected to a rapid cold stress protocol, wherein the fish were transferred directly from their stock holding tanks at 16–18°C to the challenge tanks at 10–12°C on the very same day as the viral exposure, eliminating the extended acclimation phase entirely.

Following the respective cold stress treatments, both groups of koi were challenged with SVCVnc using either IP injection (1 × 10⁶ PFU per fish) or immersion (1 × 10⁵ PFU/ml for one hour), with each challenge modality and stress protocol combination replicated in duplicate tanks of ten fish each. Post-challenge, the water temperature in the BSL-3 facility was incrementally increased by 0.5°C per day until reaching 14°C, where it was maintained for the remainder of the 30-day observation period. Mortality was recorded daily, and cumulative percent mortality (CPM) and mean day to death (MDD) were calculated for each treatment group. Additionally, a subset of both dead and surviving fish was screened for the presence of SVCV using plaque assay to confirm infection status. The results of this comparative study were unequivocal: there was no statistically significant difference in mortality outcomes between the two cold stress protocols. For both immersion and injection challenges, the CPM was identical—75% for immersion and 100% for injection—regardless of whether the fish had undergone the slow, 14-day acclimation or the rapid, same-day temperature shift. Similarly, the MDD values were comparable between the two groups, indicating that the kinetics of disease progression were not altered by the speed of the temperature change. Viral screening confirmed that all sampled fish from the virus-challenged groups, whether dead or alive, were positive for SVCV, with high viral titers, while mock-challenged controls remained negative. These findings demonstrate that the prolonged cold acclimation period is not a necessary prerequisite for successful SVCV infection in koi under controlled laboratory conditions; rather, the acute thermal shock induced by the rapid transfer to cooler water is sufficient to render the fish susceptible to the virus. Consequently, the rapid cold stress protocol was adopted as the standard for all subsequent SVCV challenge experiments associated with the present invention. This modification significantly enhances the efficiency of the challenge model by reducing the pre-challenge preparation time from two weeks to a single day, thereby accelerating the pace of vaccine evaluation studies, minimizing husbandry costs, and improving the overall throughput of the testing pipeline without compromising the rigor or reproducibility of the results.

### Example 3

The core of the present invention is embodied in a novel DNA vaccine construct, designated pSGnc, which has been demonstrated to confer significant protective immunity against Spring viremia of carp virus (SVCV) in koi. The development of this vaccine began with the selection of an appropriate viral antigen, which, for rhabdoviruses like SVCV, is universally recognized as the surface glycoprotein (G) due to its critical role in viral attachment and entry and its status as the primary target of neutralizing antibodies. The inventors chose to base the vaccine on the G gene of the North Carolina isolate of SVCV (SVCVnc), which was the first SVCV strain identified in North America and has since been phylogenetically classified within genogroup Ia, distinct from the European genogroups (Ib, Ic, Id). This strategic choice was motivated by the hypothesis that a vaccine antigen derived from a locally circulating strain would provide superior protection against homologous challenge compared to constructs based on genetically divergent European isolates, a hypothesis that was strongly supported by the experimental results. The complete nucleotide sequence of the SVCVnc G gene was obtained from GenBank (accession AY527273) and used to design specific forward and reverse primers for amplification. Total RNA was extracted from a propagated stock of SVCVnc, and the G gene was amplified via reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using these primers, which were engineered to include sequences compatible with directional cloning into the pcDNA3.1 expression vector.

The amplified G gene product was then cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector using a TOPO cloning kit, resulting in a recombinant plasmid wherein the SVCVnc G gene is placed under the transcriptional control of the strong cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate-early promoter, ensuring high-level expression in transfected fish cells. The correct orientation and integrity of the insert were verified through a series of molecular analyses, including colony PCR with vector-specific and internal G-gene primers, restriction enzyme digestion to confirm insert size, and finally, full-length sequencing of the cloned G gene to ensure no mutations were introduced during the RT-PCR and cloning processes. This final, verified construct was designated pSGnc. As a negative control, a mock vaccine plasmid, pLuc, containing the luciferase reporter gene in the same pcDNA3.1 backbone, was used; this plasmid was a gift from prior research and served to control for any non-specific effects of plasmid DNA administration or the injection procedure itself. Both plasmids were propagated in *Escherichia coli* and purified using an alkaline lysis method to yield endotoxin-reduced, high-purity DNA suitable for in vivo use.

The efficacy of the pSGnc vaccine was rigorously evaluated in four independent trial experiments (A-D), each designed to test the vaccine under slightly varying conditions of fish age, weight, and viral challenge dose to assess its robustness and generalizability. In each trial, koi were randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups: (1) vaccination with pSGnc, (2) vaccination with the mock pLuc plasmid, or (3) a non-vaccinated (NV) control group that received no injection. Vaccination was performed by intramuscular (IM) injection into the epaxial muscle with a dose of 10 micrograms of plasmid DNA in 50 µl of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). This dose was selected based on precedent from IHNV DNA vaccine studies in salmonids, though it is notably higher than the standard 0.1–1.0 µg doses often used, reflecting a conservative approach in initial efficacy testing. Following vaccination, fish were held at a slightly elevated temperature of 19–20°C for 28 days (approximately 546 degree-days) to potentially enhance immune priming before being transferred to the BSL-3 facility for challenge. At the time of challenge, all fish were subjected to the rapid cold stress protocol described in Example 2 and then exposed to SVCVnc via IP injection with a dose specific to each trial (ranging from 1 × 10⁵ to 1 × 10⁶ PFU per fish). Mock-challenged controls for each treatment group were included in every trial. Mortality was monitored daily for 26–29 days post-challenge, and efficacy was assessed using cumulative percent mortality (CPM), relative percent survival (RPS), and viral load in survivors.

The results across all four trials were consistently favorable for the pSGnc vaccine. Non-vaccinated (NV) control fish and pLuc-vaccinated fish exhibited high mortality rates, ranging from 70% to 100% and 85% to 100%, respectively, confirming the virulence of the challenge and the lack of protection from the control plasmid. In stark contrast, pSGnc-vaccinated fish showed significantly lower mortality, with CPM values ranging from 10% to 50%. This translated into RPS values—a standard metric for vaccine efficacy in aquaculture—of 50% to 88% across the four trials, demonstrating substantial and statistically significant protection (p ≤ 0.05) in every instance. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and the Mantel-Cox log-rank test confirmed that the survival curves for the pSGnc group were significantly different from those of the control groups in all trials. Furthermore, viral screening of survivors provided compelling evidence of functional immunity: while all survivors from the NV and pLuc groups were positive for SVCV, the majority of pSGnc-vaccinated survivors (particularly in trials C and D) were completely free of detectable virus, and those that were positive exhibited much lower viral titers. This indicates that the pSGnc vaccine not only improves survival but also enables the host to effectively clear the virus. The pSGnc vaccine, therefore, represents a major advance in SVCV prevention, being the first DNA vaccine to show high efficacy against an Ia genogroup isolate and the first to be successfully validated in koi, offering a powerful new tool for protecting North American and other vulnerable fish populations from this devastating OIE-notifiable disease.
