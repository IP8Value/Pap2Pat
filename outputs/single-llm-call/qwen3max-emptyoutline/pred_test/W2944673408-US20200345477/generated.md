# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to medical prosthetic devices, and more particularly to breast implants designed for reconstructive or cosmetic augmentation of the human breast. Specifically, the invention concerns a novel class of saline-filled breast implants characterized by an internal structural architecture that confers enhanced mechanical stability under compressive loading conditions, thereby reducing shape deformation, minimizing interaction with the surrounding periprosthetic capsule, and improving clinical outcomes related to capsular contracture, rupture, and fold flaw formation. The invention further encompasses methods for evaluating the mechanical behavior of breast implants through quantification of multi-dimensional strain responses and tangent moduli under standardized compressive testing protocols, which serve as predictive indicators of in vivo performance and durability.

### Description of Related Art

Breast implants have evolved significantly since their introduction in the mid-twentieth century, progressing from rudimentary silicone rubber shells filled with various substances to today’s highly engineered devices constructed from biocompatible, cross-linked silicone elastomers filled either with cohesive or non-cohesive silicone gels or sterile saline solution. Current commercially available breast implants fall broadly into two categories: single-lumen silicone gel-filled implants and single-lumen saline-filled implants. Both types are subject to rigorous mechanical testing protocols mandated by regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as part of the pre-market approval (PMA) process. These tests, often aligned with ASTM International and International Organization for Standardization (ISO) standards, include assessments of tensile strength, ultimate elongation, tear resistance, cyclic fatigue endurance, and ultimate burst strength. While these conventional mechanical evaluations provide critical data on material integrity and shell durability, they do not fully capture the dynamic geometric response of the entire implant when subjected to physiologically relevant compressive forces encountered during daily activities such as walking, running, sleeping, or mammographic compression.

A persistent clinical challenge associated with breast implantation is the formation of a fibrous capsule around the implant—a normal foreign body response—and its potential pathological progression to capsular contracture (CC), wherein the capsule tightens, causing firmness, pain, and visible distortion of the breast. The Baker grading system classifies CC severity from Grade I (soft, natural appearance) to Grade IV (hard, painful, distorted). Despite advances in implant surface texturing—intended to disrupt fibroblast alignment and reduce CC incidence—smooth-surfaced implants remain widely used due to concerns over rare but serious complications like breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) linked to textured surfaces. Notably, smooth silicone gel implants continue to exhibit relatively high CC rates in long-term clinical studies, typically ranging from 10.9% to 16.2% over 7–8 years post-implantation.

Another significant mode of implant failure is shell rupture or deflation, which may occur via distinct mechanisms depending on loading conditions. High-magnitude cyclic loads can induce tear-type failures, while low-magnitude, high-cycle fatigue may lead to pinhole perforations, both predominantly occurring along the shell’s equatorial perimeter where tensile stresses concentrate during compression. Additionally, repeated folding or creasing of the shell—termed “fold flaws”—can initiate micro-damage that propagates into full-thickness perforations over time. Traditional mechanical testing has not adequately addressed how an implant’s overall geometric stability under compression influences these failure modes or modulates biomechanical interactions with the periprosthetic capsule.

Recent clinical data have revealed a third category of breast implant—the structured saline implant, exemplified by the IDEAL IMPLANT®—which features a dual-lumen design with nested silicone shells and internal baffle structures within the outer lumen. This architecture, filled with saline, reportedly mimics the tactile properties of silicone gel while offering substantially lower rupture rates (2.1% vs. 7.4–13.6%) and reduced capsular contracture incidence (6.6% vs. 10.9–16.2%) compared to approved silicone gel implants over an eight-year follow-up period. Moreover, no fold flaw failures have been reported for this device in clinical trials, and its ultimate compressive strength exceeds 15.2 kN, surpassing known values for conventional implants. However, prior art lacks a comprehensive mechanical framework to explain these superior clinical outcomes, particularly because the individual shell thicknesses of the structured implant are comparable to those of standard devices, suggesting that material properties alone cannot account for the observed differences.

Existing literature contains abundant data on shell-level mechanical properties but is devoid of methodologies to quantify the total implant’s shape change—i.e., multi-dimensional strain—under compressive loading. While Jewell et al. assessed form stability during orientation changes and resistance to gel deformation using biomechanical tissue characterization (BTC), these approaches focused on fill material rheology rather than holistic implant geometry. Similarly, flat-plate compression testing has been employed for fatigue and ultimate strength evaluation but without extracting geometric parameters such as diameter, projection, or surface area as continuous functions of load. Consequently, there remains a critical gap in the art: the absence of validated models and standardized metrics to characterize and compare the shape stability of entire breast implants under compression, especially in relation to clinical endpoints like CC, rupture, and fold flaws.

Furthermore, the role of interfacial friction between the implant surface and surrounding tissues—or test platens in laboratory settings—has been largely overlooked. In vivo, the implant resides within a synovial-like fluid environment that may lubricate its interface with the capsule, potentially altering deformation mechanics compared to dry laboratory conditions. No prior study has systematically investigated how lubrication affects implant geometry under load, despite its potential relevance to simulating physiological conditions and interpreting fatigue test results.

Finally, while implant “softness” or palpability is a key aesthetic and functional attribute, it has been evaluated subjectively or through indirect means. There exists no objective, quantitative method correlating localized low-load deformation—such as fingertip pinching—with overall shape stability. This disconnect has led to the misconception that high shape stability necessarily implies firmness, whereas clinical observations suggest that structured saline implants can be both shape-stable and soft-feeling.

In summary, the related art fails to provide: (1) a reproducible methodology to compute total implant geometry during compression from basic load-displacement data; (2) validated metrics of multi-dimensional strain and tangent moduli as surrogates for shape stability; (3) comparative analysis of structured versus conventional implants under both dry and lubricated conditions; and (4) correlation between global compressive response and localized tactile properties. The present invention addresses these deficiencies by introducing a novel analytical framework and associated implant design principles that bridge mechanical testing with clinical performance.

It is a further object of the invention to establish a standardized, reproducible methodology for characterizing the geometric response of breast implants to compressive forces, enabling quantitative comparison of shape stability across different implant designs.

It is yet another object of the invention to define and validate mathematical descriptors of implant mechanical behavior—including multi-dimensional strains (projection, diametric, and areal) and corresponding tangent moduli—that serve as predictive indicators of in vivo durability and safety.

It is still another object of the invention to demonstrate that reduced implant-capsule interaction, achieved through enhanced shape stability, correlates with improved clinical outcomes, particularly lower rates of capsular contracture and implant failure.

It is also an object of the invention to provide experimental evidence that interfacial friction between an implant and compressive surfaces significantly influences measured deformation characteristics, thereby necessitating consideration of lubricated testing conditions to better approximate in vivo environments.

It is an additional object of the invention to develop a complementary testing protocol for assessing localized, low-load deformation—simulating fingertip palpation—to objectively quantify implant softness and decouple it from global shape stability metrics.

It is a further object of the invention to show that high shape stability and soft tactile properties are not mutually exclusive, thereby overcoming a longstanding limitation in breast implant design where firmness was often assumed necessary for structural integrity.

It is yet another object of the invention to provide a geometric model based on composite cylinder-torus assumptions that accurately predicts implant diameter and surface area during compression from measured load and plate spacing data, with errors within ±2% for loads above 5 Newtons.

It is still another object of the invention to validate a quasi-equilibrium assumption for dynamic compression testing, confirming that continuously recorded load-displacement data can be used interchangeably with static measurements for geometric and mechanical analysis without introducing significant error.

It is also an object of the invention to establish that the dual-lumen architecture with internal baffles in structured saline implants confers mechanical advantages that cannot be replicated by simply increasing shell thickness or fill viscosity, thus representing a non-obvious advancement over prior art.

It is an additional object of the invention to correlate lower multi-dimensional strains and higher tangent moduli with reduced maximum tensile stress at the implant perimeter during compression, thereby explaining the observed higher ultimate strength and absence of fold flaw failures in structured implants.

It is a further object of the invention to propose that repeated micro-trauma to the periprosthetic capsule, induced by excessive implant shape change under cyclic loading, is a contributing factor to chronic inflammatory conditions including capsular contracture, BIA-ALCL, and breast implant illness, and that minimizing such trauma through enhanced shape stability offers a prophylactic benefit.

It is yet another object of the invention to provide a foundation for revising regulatory testing standards to include shape stability metrics alongside traditional material-level mechanical evaluations, thereby enabling more comprehensive assessment of implant safety and performance.

It is still another object of the invention to enable rapid screening of implant design modifications—such as profile changes, fill volume adjustments, or baffle configurations—by predicting their impact on shape stability through the disclosed methodology, prior to conducting lengthy and costly fatigue or explant studies.

It is yet another object of the invention to introduce localized diametric strain as a metric for implant palpability, validated through pinch-force measurements that confirm structured saline implants require similar or lower forces to deform locally compared to standard gel implants.

It is still another object of the invention to support clinical claims of superior performance for structured implants through objective, physics-based mechanical analysis rather than relying solely on observational or statistical correlations.

It is also an object of the invention to facilitate the development of next-generation breast implants optimized for both mechanical resilience and patient comfort by providing design targets derived from strain and modulus thresholds associated with favorable clinical outcomes.

It is an additional object of the invention to create a benchmark for evaluating emerging implant technologies—such as form-stable “gummy bear” implants or bio-integrative surfaces—against the established performance envelope of structured saline devices using the disclosed analytical framework.

It is a further object of the invention to contribute to the scientific understanding of implant-tissue biomechanics by establishing a causal link between macro-scale implant deformation and micro-scale biological responses in the periprosthetic environment.

It is yet another object of the invention to provide a complete, standalone technical disclosure that enables replication of the testing methodology, validation of the geometric model, and application of the shape stability metrics by researchers, manufacturers, and regulatory bodies without reference to external publications.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel breast implant and associated evaluation methodology that fundamentally redefines the relationship between implant structure, mechanical behavior under compression, and clinical performance. At its core, the invention introduces a structured saline-filled breast implant comprising at least two concentric lumens formed by nested silicone elastomer shells, with the outer lumen containing one or more perforated baffle structures designed to regulate saline movement and provide internal support. This architecture imparts exceptional shape stability under compressive loads—quantified through newly defined multi-dimensional strain metrics and tangent moduli—while preserving a soft, natural tactile feel indistinguishable from silicone gel implants. The invention further discloses a validated geometric model that accurately predicts implant diameter and surface area during compression from basic load and plate spacing measurements, enabling comprehensive mechanical characterization without invasive instrumentation. Through systematic testing under both dry and lubricated conditions, the invention demonstrates that reduced implant deformation minimizes dynamic interaction with the periprosthetic capsule, thereby lowering the risk of capsular contracture, shell rupture, and fold flaw formation. Additionally, a complementary pinch-test protocol objectively quantifies implant softness at low, localized loads, confirming that high global shape stability does not compromise patient comfort. The disclosed methodology establishes a new paradigm for breast implant evaluation, linking measurable mechanical properties directly to clinical outcomes and providing a robust framework for future implant design and regulatory assessment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### First Invention Embodiment

The first embodiment of the present invention is a structured saline-filled breast implant specifically engineered to achieve superior shape stability under compressive loading while maintaining a soft, natural tactile feel. This implant comprises an outer shell and an inner shell, both fabricated from medical-grade, cross-linked polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) elastomer, forming two distinct lumens. The inner lumen is completely enclosed within the inner shell and is filled with sterile 0.9% USP saline solution through a posterior fill valve located on a patch affixed to the back of the implant. Surrounding the inner shell is the outer lumen, which is bounded externally by the outer shell and internally by the outer surface of the inner shell. The outer lumen is filled with saline through a separate anterior fill valve situated on the front surface of the implant. Critically, floating freely within the outer lumen is a baffle structure composed of one to three nested, perforated baffle shells. These baffle shells are manufactured from the same silicone elastomer as the primary shells and contain multiple slits or apertures that permit unrestricted saline flow between and around the baffles while simultaneously providing structural reinforcement to the outer shell.

This dual-lumen, baffled architecture serves multiple synergistic functions. First, the inner shell acts as a rigid core that resists collapse under vertical loading when the patient is upright, preventing the upper pole of the implant from flattening—a common issue with conventional saline implants that contributes to an unnatural appearance. Second, the baffle structure within the outer lumen creates hydraulic resistance to rapid saline displacement during dynamic movements, imparting an “apparent viscosity” that mimics the cohesive behavior of silicone gel without the associated risks of silent rupture or gel bleed. Third, and most importantly for the purposes of the present invention, the combined effect of the inner shell and baffles significantly enhances the implant’s resistance to shape deformation under compressive forces encountered during daily activities. When subjected to loads exceeding approximately 100 Newtons—corresponding to vigorous exercise, mammographic compression, or accidental impact—the structured implant exhibits markedly lower multi-dimensional strains compared to single-lumen gel or saline implants of equivalent size and initial geometry.

The geometric configuration of this embodiment is optimized to minimize diameter expansion and surface area increase during compression. Under a load of 534 N, representative of extreme but plausible physiological forces, the structured implant demonstrates a diametric strain (ϵD) that is 6–19% lower than comparable gel implants under dry platen conditions and 24–42% lower under lubricated conditions. Similarly, its areal strain (ϵA) is reduced by 5–25% (dry) and 40–55% (lubricated) relative to gel counterparts. These reductions in strain translate directly into lower maximum tensile stresses at the implant’s equatorial perimeter—the region most susceptible to fatigue-induced failure—thereby explaining the clinically observed absence of fold flaw failures and the exceptionally high ultimate strength exceeding 15.2 kN. Furthermore, the constrained deformation limits the dynamic sliding and frictional interaction between the implant surface and the periprosthetic capsule, reducing the repetitive micro-trauma hypothesized to trigger capsular contracture and other chronic inflammatory responses.

Despite this enhanced structural integrity, the first embodiment retains a soft, compliant feel during palpation. Localized compression testing using simulated fingertips reveals that the force required to achieve a given localized diametric strain (ϵLD) is statistically equivalent to that of standard silicone gel implants and significantly lower than cohesive gel variants. This decoupling of global shape stability from local softness is achieved through the strategic distribution of structural elements: the baffles and inner shell provide macro-scale rigidity against large-area compression, while the thin, flexible outer shell and fluid-filled lumens allow micro-scale compliance under point loading. Thus, the first embodiment fulfills the dual objectives of mechanical resilience and aesthetic naturalness, addressing long-standing trade-offs in breast implant design.

### Second Invention Embodiment

The second embodiment of the present invention is a standardized methodology for evaluating and comparing the shape stability of breast implants under compressive loading, based on a validated geometric model and quasi-equilibrium assumption. This methodology begins with the placement of an unimplanted, commercially available breast implant between two parallel, polished stainless-steel platens mounted on a computer-controlled universal testing machine equipped with a calibrated load cell (e.g., Instron 5500R with 1 kN capacity). The implant is oriented such that the compressive load is applied along the anterior-posterior axis, from the center of the front surface to the center of the back surface, which maximizes tensile stress at the equatorial perimeter—the clinically relevant failure zone. The testing is conducted at room temperature (22°C) in air, with data acquisition recording the applied load (F) and corresponding plate spacing (H) at high temporal resolution throughout the compression cycle.

The core innovation of this methodology lies in its ability to derive complete geometric and mechanical descriptors of the implant from only these two primary measurements—F and H—without requiring direct observation or contact-based measurement of the implant’s deforming surface during dynamic testing. This is accomplished through a geometric model that approximates the compressed implant shape as a composite of a central cylindrical “puck” and an outer half-torus. Given that the implant volume (V) remains constant during compression (assuming incompressible fill material), the model solves a quadratic equation to determine the contact diameter (d) between the implant and platens as a function of H and V. The total implant diameter (D) is then calculated as D = H + d, under the assumption that the perimeter profile between platens forms a semi-circle of diameter H. Subsequently, the external surface area (A) is computed using a formula that combines the areas of the cylindrical faces and the outer toroidal segment.

This geometric model has been rigorously validated against static manual measurements across a load range of 0.445 N to 534 N. For loads above 5 N, the model predicts implant diameter with an average error of less than ±2% for all tested implant types, including structured saline, cohesive gel, and standard gel devices. The slight overestimation observed for the structured implant (attributed to its complex internal geometry not fully captured by the simplified model) actually renders the resulting shape stability comparisons conservative, as it leads to an underestimation of the true performance advantage. Concurrently, a quasi-equilibrium assumption has been validated by demonstrating near-perfect agreement (<1% difference) between static step-wise compression data and continuously recorded dynamic compression data for the same implant. This validation confirms that the transient states during dynamic loading can be treated as equilibrium states for the purpose of geometric and mechanical analysis, enabling efficient, automated testing without sacrificing accuracy.

Using this methodology, three key multi-dimensional strain metrics are computed: projection strain (ϵH = -(H - Ho)/Ho × 100%), diametric strain (ϵD = (D - Do)/Do × 100%), and areal strain (ϵA = (A - Ao)/Ao × 100%). These strains provide a comprehensive description of how the implant’s shape evolves under load, with lower values indicating greater shape stability. Additionally, tangent moduli—defined as the local slope of the stress-strain curve at each point—are calculated to quantify the implant’s instantaneous resistance to further deformation. These moduli, expressed in pressure units (kPa), increase with strain for the structured implant beyond threshold levels (e.g., 25% projection strain), reflecting a stiffening response that contrasts with the more linear or softening behavior of gel implants. The second embodiment thus provides a complete, objective, and reproducible framework for characterizing implant mechanical behavior, with a relative standard deviation of less than 0.6% across replicate tests, enabling meaningful comparisons between different implant designs and supporting evidence-based clinical decision-making.

### Third Invention Embodiment

The third embodiment of the present invention addresses the critical influence of interfacial friction on breast implant deformation mechanics by incorporating lubricated testing conditions into the evaluation methodology. Recognizing that the in vivo environment surrounding an implanted breast prosthesis includes synovial-like fluid that lubricates the interface between the implant shell and the periprosthetic capsule, this embodiment systematically compares implant response under both “dry” (unlubricated stainless-steel platens) and “lubricated” (platens coated with a thin layer of medical-grade silicone oil, e.g., DPDM 400) conditions. The testing protocol follows the same dynamic compression procedure as the second embodiment, with continuous recording of load (F) and plate spacing (H) from 0.445 N to 534 N at a constant cross-head speed of 25.4 cm/minute, but now performed in duplicate for each implant type under both surface conditions.

The results reveal that reduced friction significantly amplifies implant deformation across all tested devices, but with disproportionate effects on conventional gel implants compared to the structured saline design. Under lubricated conditions, the projection (H) of gel implants decreases more rapidly with increasing load, and their diameter (D) expands more extensively, leading to substantially higher multi-dimensional strains. For example, at 500 N, the areal strain (ϵA) of gel implants increases by 40–55% relative to the structured implant under lubrication, compared to only 5–25% under dry conditions. This finding has profound implications for laboratory testing standards: conventional dry platen tests may overestimate the in vivo shape stability of gel implants by failing to account for the lubricious nature of the periprosthetic space, potentially leading to inaccurate fatigue lifetime predictions and ultimate strength assessments.

For the structured implant, the presence of internal baffles and the dual-lumen architecture provides inherent resistance to deformation that is less dependent on external frictional constraints. Consequently, while its strains also increase under lubrication, the magnitude of this increase is smaller, and its absolute strain values remain consistently lower than those of gel implants across the entire load spectrum. This robustness to varying interfacial conditions underscores the biomechanical advantage of the structured design in real-world physiological environments. Moreover, the surface area (A)—the direct interface with the capsule—is shown to be less sensitive to load for the structured implant, especially under lubricated conditions, implying reduced dynamic interaction with the capsule wall during cyclic loading. Since repeated micro-movements and shear stresses at this interface are hypothesized to stimulate fibroblast activation and collagen deposition leading to capsular contracture, the third embodiment provides a mechanistic explanation for the clinically observed lower CC rates associated with structured implants.

This embodiment also highlights the necessity of considering lubrication in future regulatory testing guidelines. By demonstrating that frictional effects can alter strain measurements by up to 30% for gel implants, it challenges the adequacy of current ASTM/ISO standards that specify dry testing conditions. The inclusion of lubricated protocols, as disclosed herein, would yield more physiologically relevant data and improve the predictive validity of pre-clinical mechanical evaluations. Furthermore, the methodology enables researchers to simulate a spectrum of in vivo scenarios—from highly adherent capsules with minimal fluid (dry-like) to loose capsules with abundant lubrication (wet-like)—thereby facilitating a more nuanced understanding of implant performance across diverse patient anatomies and healing responses.

### Fourth Invention Embodiment

The fourth embodiment of the present invention is a complementary testing protocol designed to objectively quantify the tactile properties or “softness” of breast implants under localized, low-magnitude compressive loads that simulate manual palpation. Unlike the global compression tests of previous embodiments, which assess overall shape stability under large-area loading, this protocol focuses on the implant’s response to diametrically opposed, point-like forces applied at the perimeter—mimicking the action of human fingertips during clinical examination or patient self-assessment. The setup consists of a digital force gauge (e.g., Torbal FC10 with 10 N capacity) fitted with a hemispherical silicone probe (18 mm diameter) to simulate a fingertip, opposing a fixed identical probe mounted on an ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) base plate. The UHMWPE surface is wetted to minimize friction, allowing the implant to slide freely during compression and ensuring that the measured force reflects only the implant’s intrinsic resistance to localized deformation.

Testing is performed by placing the implant between the two probes and incrementally reducing the distance between them to predefined final pinch distances (Xf), corresponding to localized diametric strains (ϵLD = -(Xf - Do)/Do × 100%) ranging from 35% to 75%. At each Xf, the peak compressive force (F) required to achieve the pinch is recorded over 30 replicate trials to account for variability. The resulting force-strain curves provide a direct, quantitative measure of implant compliance: lower forces indicate greater softness or palpability. When applied to the structured saline implant, standard gel implant, and cohesive gel implant, this protocol reveals that the structured device requires pinch forces statistically equivalent to the standard gel implant across most strain levels (differing by only -6% to +10%, with non-significance at ϵLD=54%), and significantly lower forces (88–98% less) than the cohesive gel variant.

This finding is critically important because it definitively disproves the assumption that high global shape stability necessarily entails firmness or unnatural feel. The structured implant achieves its superior resistance to large-area compression through internal structural elements (baffles and inner shell) that do not impede localized shell flexion under point loading. In contrast, the cohesive gel implant’s high cross-link density increases resistance to both global and local deformation, resulting in a firmer palpation profile. The fourth embodiment thus establishes localized diametric strain (ϵLD) and corresponding pinch force (F) as valid, objective metrics for evaluating implant softness, replacing subjective clinician ratings with reproducible biomechanical data. These metrics can be used to optimize implant designs for patient comfort without compromising structural integrity, and to validate marketing claims regarding natural feel. Moreover, by decoupling global and local mechanical responses, this embodiment provides a more holistic understanding of implant performance, recognizing that an ideal device must excel in both domains—resisting shape change during dynamic activities while remaining soft to the touch.

### Fifth Invention Embodiment

The fifth embodiment of the present invention integrates the mechanical insights from the preceding embodiments into a predictive framework for clinical outcomes, specifically linking quantifiable shape stability metrics to reduced risks of capsular contracture, implant rupture, and fold flaw formation. This embodiment is not a physical device or test method per se, but rather a correlative analysis that interprets clinical trial data through the lens of the disclosed mechanical characterization. Central to this embodiment is the recognition that multi-dimensional strains (ϵH, ϵD, ϵA) and tangent moduli (E_SH, E_SD, E_SA) serve as surrogate biomarkers for in vivo implant behavior. Lower strains indicate less shape change during physiological loading, which in turn reduces the amplitude and frequency of dynamic interactions between the implant surface and the periprosthetic capsule. Since repetitive micro-trauma at this interface is theorized to activate fibroblasts, promote collagen alignment, and trigger inflammatory cascades leading to capsular contracture, implants with superior shape stability—like the structured saline design—would logically exhibit lower CC rates.

Indeed, clinical data confirm this hypothesis: the structured implant demonstrates a CC rate of 6.6% over eight years, substantially below the 10.9–16.2% range observed for approved silicone gel implants, despite being a smooth-surfaced device (which typically carry higher CC risk than textured variants). Similarly, the structured implant’s rupture rate of 2.1% is markedly lower than the 7.4–13.6% seen in gel implants, and no fold flaw failures have been reported in clinical trials. The fifth embodiment explains these outcomes through mechanical causality: the structured implant’s lower strains under compression translate to reduced maximum tensile stress at the shell’s equatorial perimeter—the locus of fatigue-induced failures. Finite element analyses (not shown but implied by the stress-strain relationships) would confirm that for a given load, the structured implant experiences lower peak stresses due to its constrained deformation, thereby extending fatigue life and increasing ultimate strength. The absence of fold flaws is further attributed to the implant’s resistance to wrinkling and creasing, as the internal baffles prevent the outer shell from collapsing into sharp folds during compression.

This embodiment also addresses the paradox of how a saline-filled implant can outperform silicone gel devices in durability metrics. Conventional wisdom holds that gel’s viscosity dampens shell motion and reduces stress concentrations, but the present invention reveals that internal structural architecture—not fill material rheology—is the dominant factor. The dual-lumen, baffled design creates a mechanically integrated system that distributes loads more evenly and resists localized buckling, effects that cannot be achieved by simply increasing shell thickness (as the structured implant’s shell is comparable in thickness to competitors). Thus, the fifth embodiment establishes a new design principle: that strategic internal structuring can confer mechanical advantages superior to those of homogeneous gel fills, overturning long-standing assumptions in the field.

Furthermore, this embodiment proposes that the disclosed strain and modulus thresholds can serve as design targets for future implants. For instance, maintaining diametric strain below 35% and areal strain below 50% at 500 N load—values achieved by the structured implant—may be indicative of low CC and rupture risk. Regulatory agencies could adopt these metrics as supplementary criteria in PMA reviews, complementing traditional material tests. Manufacturers could use the methodology to iteratively refine baffle geometry, shell thickness distribution, or fill volume to optimize shape stability before committing to costly clinical trials. In essence, the fifth embodiment transforms mechanical testing from a pass/fail compliance exercise into a proactive tool for outcome-driven design, closing the loop between engineering performance and patient benefit.

### Additional Embodiments

Additional embodiments of the present invention extend the core principles to broader applications and refinements. One such embodiment involves the adaptation of the geometric model and testing methodology to non-round implant profiles, such as anatomical or teardrop-shaped devices. While the current model assumes axial symmetry appropriate for round implants, modifications to the volume and surface area equations—incorporating elliptical cross-sections or variable curvature profiles—would enable accurate strain quantification for shaped implants, which are increasingly popular in reconstructive surgery. Another embodiment envisions the integration of real-time imaging modalities, such as high-speed videography or optical coherence tomography, to directly validate the assumed cylinder-torus geometry during dynamic compression, potentially refining the model’s accuracy at very low loads (<5 N) where deviations are currently observed.

Yet another embodiment applies the disclosed methodology to explanted implants retrieved from patients, enabling direct comparison of in vivo aged devices with pristine controls. By measuring post-explantation strains and moduli, researchers could quantify the effects of long-term implantation—such as shell degradation, calcification, or biofilm formation—on mechanical performance, providing invaluable data for failure analysis and design improvement. A further embodiment explores the use of alternative lubricants in testing, such as hyaluronic acid solutions or patient-derived seroma fluid, to better simulate specific pathological conditions like double capsule formation or late seroma associated with BIA-ALCL.

Additionally, the invention contemplates the development of computational finite element models parameterized by the experimentally derived strain and modulus data, allowing virtual prototyping of novel implant architectures before physical fabrication. Such simulations could investigate the individual contributions of baffle number, slit geometry, or shell cross-link density to overall shape stability, accelerating innovation cycles. Another embodiment extends the localized pinch test to multi-point or distributed loading scenarios, using arrays of micro-probes to map regional variations in compliance across the implant surface, which could inform zonal reinforcement strategies.

Finally, the invention includes embodiments directed toward regulatory harmonization, proposing standardized test protocols based on the disclosed methodology for inclusion in FDA guidance documents and international standards (ASTM F2058, ISO 14607). These protocols would specify load ranges (0.445–534 N), strain rate (25.4 cm/min), platen conditions (dry and lubricated), and required output metrics (ϵH, ϵD, ϵA, E_SH, E_SD, E_SA), ensuring consistent evaluation across manufacturers and facilitating meta-analyses of implant performance. Through these additional embodiments, the present invention establishes a comprehensive ecosystem for breast implant innovation, spanning design, testing, regulation, and clinical correlation.
