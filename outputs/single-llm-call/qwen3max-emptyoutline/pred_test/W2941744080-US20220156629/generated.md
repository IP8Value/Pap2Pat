# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of quantum computing, and more specifically to fault-tolerant quantum error correction architectures implemented using semiconductor spin qubits. The invention provides a novel surface code architecture based on silicon spin qubits that incorporates specialized structural elements to mitigate both computational errors and leakage errors—particularly charge leakage errors that can propagate through quantum dot arrays and corrupt the computational subspace. The architecture is designed to be compatible with conventional semiconductor manufacturing processes while maintaining high qubit density and enabling efficient implementation of surface code stabilizer measurements. The invention addresses critical challenges in scaling semiconductor spin qubit systems to the level required for practical fault-tolerant quantum computation, including the management of classical control wiring, heat dissipation, cross-talk minimization, and the inherent vulnerability of dense quantum dot arrays to correlated leakage errors that cannot be corrected by standard quantum error correction protocols.

## BACKGROUND

Quantum computing holds the promise of exponential speedup for certain computational problems, including quantum simulation, cryptography, and optimization. However, practical realization of large-scale quantum computers requires robust error correction to protect fragile quantum information from decoherence and operational errors. The surface code has emerged as one of the most promising quantum error correction schemes due to its high error threshold (approximately 1% for depolarizing noise), two-dimensional nearest-neighbor connectivity requirements, and compatibility with various physical qubit platforms including superconducting circuits, trapped ions, and semiconductor spin qubits.

Semiconductor spin qubits, particularly those implemented in silicon quantum dots, offer significant advantages for scalable quantum computing. These include extremely high potential qubit densities (up to 10⁹ cm⁻²), long coherence times when implemented in isotopically enriched silicon, and compatibility with established semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure. However, scaling silicon spin qubit systems to the millions of physical qubits required for practical fault-tolerant quantum computation presents several formidable challenges.

One major challenge is the routing of classical control lines to individual qubits in dense two-dimensional arrays. Various architectures have been proposed to address this issue, including crossbar geometries that reduce the number of control lines from N to √N for N qubits, and modular approaches that connect smaller quantum dot arrays through electron shuttling. While these architectures help manage the classical control overhead, they introduce new constraints such as requirements for qubit homogeneity and limitations on parallel operation.

A more fundamental challenge that has received less attention in existing architectures is the problem of leakage errors. Unlike standard computational errors (bit flips and phase flips) that keep the quantum system within the computational subspace, leakage errors cause the system to escape from the computational subspace entirely. In semiconductor spin qubit systems, leakage errors can occur in two forms: spin leakage, where the spin configuration moves outside the intended computational subspace, and charge leakage, where electrons escape from their designated quantum dots.

Charge leakage errors are particularly problematic in dense quantum dot arrays because they can propagate through the system. When an electron escapes from one quantum dot during gate operations, it can affect neighboring dots through capacitive coupling or direct tunneling, creating spatially correlated errors that can overwhelm standard error correction protocols. Furthermore, conventional quantum error correction codes like the surface code are designed to correct errors within the computational subspace but cannot directly address leakage errors. While various leakage reduction protocols have been proposed, they typically require additional ancilla qubits, complex circuit modifications, or specialized detection mechanisms that add significant overhead and complexity to the quantum processor architecture.

Existing approaches to managing leakage errors in semiconductor spin qubit systems face several limitations. Leakage reduction units require additional qubits that are difficult to integrate into dense arrays without compromising qubit density or introducing additional sources of error. Leakage detection circuits similarly require additional resources and can significantly increase circuit depth and runtime. Physical charge detectors interspersed throughout the array could potentially detect leakage events, but integrating such detectors into densely packed quantum dot arrays presents significant fabrication challenges and may interfere with normal qubit operations.

The fundamental issue is that conventional quantum dot architectures treat leakage errors as external problems to be addressed through additional error correction layers, rather than designing the physical architecture to inherently mitigate leakage at the hardware level. This approach leads to complex, resource-intensive solutions that may not scale effectively to the large systems required for practical quantum computation.

Furthermore, the trade-off between qubit density and operational fidelity presents another challenge. Direct exchange interactions between neighboring quantum dots enable fast two-qubit gates but require very small inter-dot spacing (tens of nanometers), which makes it difficult to integrate necessary control and readout electronics. On the other hand, larger inter-dot spacing facilitates integration of control electronics but reduces the strength of direct exchange interactions, leading to slower gate operations that are more susceptible to decoherence.

The need remains for a quantum computing architecture that simultaneously addresses the challenges of scalability, classical control routing, and leakage error resilience while maintaining compatibility with existing semiconductor manufacturing processes. Such an architecture should provide inherent protection against leakage errors without requiring significant additional resources or complex error correction protocols, while also enabling efficient implementation of surface code stabilizer measurements with high fidelity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel surface code architecture for semiconductor spin qubits that addresses the critical challenges of leakage error resilience, scalability, and compatibility with conventional semiconductor manufacturing processes. The architecture comprises three key components: data qubits implemented as single-electron quantum dots, ancilla qubits implemented as double-dot systems containing electron pairs in singlet states, and elongated mediator dots that facilitate two-qubit interactions between data and ancilla qubits while providing increased physical separation between computational elements.

The data qubits store quantum information in the spin states of single electrons confined in electrostatically defined quantum dots. Each data qubit can be individually controlled using global electron spin resonance (ESR) or electrically-driven spin resonance (EDSR) techniques, with demonstrated single-qubit gate fidelities exceeding 99.6%. The use of single-dot data qubits ensures that all spin configurations remain within the computational subspace, eliminating spin leakage errors for data qubits.

The ancilla qubits are implemented as double quantum dot systems, each containing a pair of electrons initialized in a singlet state. This configuration enables efficient parity measurement through Pauli spin blockade readout, where failed stabilizer checks transform the ancilla state from singlet to triplet, which can be detected through charge sensing or dispersive readout techniques. Critically, the double-dot ancilla design treats both dots symmetrically, allowing both halves of the ancilla to interact with data qubits in parallel, thereby halving the time required for stabilizer measurements compared to conventional single-dot ancilla approaches.

The mediator dots are elongated quantum dots positioned between data and ancilla qubits that do not store quantum information but serve to mediate exchange interactions between computational qubits. These mediator dots are typically occupied by two or more electrons and enable Ruderman-Kittel-Kasuya-Yosida (RKKY) exchange interactions between spins in the data and ancilla dots. The key innovation is that the mediator dots provide sufficient physical separation (approximately 300 nm) between computational elements to accommodate integrated charge reservoirs, control electronics, and readout devices while maintaining strong enough exchange interactions for high-fidelity two-qubit gates.

The architecture provides inherent resilience against both spin and charge leakage errors through several mechanisms. For spin leakage errors, the single-dot data qubit design ensures that all spin configurations remain within the computational subspace, while any spin leakage in the ancilla qubits is automatically removed during each stabilizer cycle through reinitialization of the ancilla in the singlet state. This prevents temporal accumulation of spin leakage errors and eliminates spatial propagation of spin leakage through the array.

For charge leakage errors, the architecture provides an elegant solution through the combination of mediator dots and integrated charge reservoirs. When an electron escapes from a computational qubit dot (data or ancilla), the resulting charge deficit is rapidly restored through relaxation of electrons from adjacent mediator dots into the empty computational dot. This charge relaxation process occurs on timescales of approximately 10 nanoseconds, which is much faster than the microsecond timescales of quantum gate operations. The charge deficit is thus transferred from the computational qubits to the mediator dots, where it can be safely removed through connection to integrated charge reservoirs without affecting the quantum information stored in the computational subspace.

The surface code implementation is optimized through several architectural features. The stabilizer check circuit is partitioned into four disjoint sets that can be executed sequentially, with each set containing non-interacting plaquettes that prevent spatial correlation of errors. During the active period of one partition, the mediator dots in other partitions can be reset through their connections to charge reservoirs, preventing temporal accumulation of charge leakage errors. The double-dot ancilla symmetry and virtual Z-gate implementation further reduce circuit depth and execution time by eliminating the need for explicit single-qubit gates on ancilla qubits and enabling most Z rotations on data qubits to be implemented virtually through phase shifts in subsequent gate pulses.

The architecture demonstrates excellent tolerance to both computational and leakage errors. Numerical simulations show that the surface code threshold for computational errors is approximately 0.86% when using S-gates and 0.76% when using √SWAP gates, which are comparable to thresholds obtained with standard depolarizing noise models. More importantly, the architecture shows significant tolerance to charge leakage errors, with a pure charge leakage threshold of 0.66% in the absence of other gate errors, and practical charge leakage thresholds of 0.23-0.27% when operating at reasonable gate error rates of 0.5%. This demonstrates that charge leakage errors can be effectively reduced to the level of standard computational errors through the inherent charge relaxation mechanism.

The physical layout of the architecture is specifically designed to facilitate integration with conventional semiconductor manufacturing processes. The 300 nm separation provided by the mediator dots creates sufficient space for planar fan-out of metallic gate structures, integration of charge reservoirs adjacent to each mediator dot, and placement of readout devices without compromising qubit density. The architecture can be fabricated using standard CMOS technology nodes, with the potential for further miniaturization as semiconductor manufacturing processes advance.

Additional advantages include the ability to integrate micromagnets for EDSR implementation without interfering with ancilla operations, since the ancilla qubits do not require single-qubit gates. The magnetic field gradients created by micromagnets not only enable faster single-qubit gates on data qubits but also create the necessary conditions for implementing CZ gates through the Ω ≫ J regime, where Ω represents the difference in Zeeman splitting between data and ancilla qubits.

The invention thus provides a comprehensive solution to the major challenges facing scalable semiconductor spin qubit quantum computing, combining high qubit density, efficient classical control routing, inherent leakage error resilience, and compatibility with existing semiconductor manufacturing infrastructure in a single integrated architecture.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive quantum computing architecture specifically designed for semiconductor spin qubits that addresses the critical challenges of leakage error resilience, scalability, and manufacturability. The architecture is built upon a carefully engineered physical layout that integrates three distinct types of quantum dot structures: data qubits, ancilla qubits, and mediator dots, each serving specific functions in the quantum error correction scheme while working together to provide inherent protection against both computational and leakage errors.

The data qubits form the foundation of the quantum information storage system and are implemented as single-electron quantum dots defined through electrostatic confinement in a semiconductor heterostructure, preferably silicon-based materials including silicon metal-oxide-semiconductor (SiMOS) or silicon-silicon germanium (Si/SiGe) platforms. Each data qubit stores quantum information in the spin state of a single electron, with the computational basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩ corresponding to the spin-down and spin-up states, respectively, relative to an applied external magnetic field. The use of single-electron occupancy ensures that all possible spin configurations remain within the computational subspace, effectively eliminating spin leakage errors for data qubits. This is a critical advantage over multi-electron qubit implementations where higher spin states could lead to leakage out of the computational subspace.

The physical implementation of data qubits leverages the well-established properties of silicon-based quantum dots, including long coherence times achievable through isotopic enrichment with ²⁸Si to eliminate nuclear spin decoherence. Coherence times T₂* can reach 120 microseconds in systems without micromagnets and 20 microseconds in systems with micromagnets, while dynamical decoupling techniques such as Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) sequences can extend coherence times to 28 milliseconds and 3 milliseconds, respectively. These long coherence times, combined with the inherent protection against spin leakage, provide a robust foundation for quantum information storage.

Single-qubit operations on data qubits can be implemented through either electron spin resonance (ESR) or electrically-driven spin resonance (EDSR) techniques. ESR utilizes oscillating magnetic fields to drive spin rotations, typically achieving gate speeds on the order of 1 MHz. EDSR, which exploits either intrinsic spin-orbit coupling in silicon or magnetic field gradients created by integrated micromagnets, can achieve significantly faster gate speeds exceeding 10 MHz while maintaining high fidelities of up to 99.9%. The choice between ESR and EDSR depends on the specific implementation requirements, with EDSR offering faster operation at the potential cost of increased susceptibility to charge noise.

The ancilla qubits serve as the measurement apparatus for the surface code stabilizer checks and are implemented as double quantum dot systems, each containing a pair of electrons. Unlike conventional approaches that use single-dot ancillas or asymmetric double-dot structures, the present invention employs a symmetric double-dot design where both dots participate equally in the stabilizer measurement process. Each ancilla qubit is initialized in the singlet state |S⟩ = (|↑↓⟩ - |↓↑⟩)/√2, which serves as the logical |0⟩ state for the ancilla, while the triplet states |T₀⟩ = (|↑↓⟩ + |↓↑⟩)/√2, |T₊⟩ = |↑↑⟩, and |T₋⟩ = |↓↓⟩ collectively represent the logical |1⟩ state.

This symmetric double-dot design provides several critical advantages. First, it enables parallel interaction between both halves of the ancilla and the surrounding data qubits, effectively halving the time required to complete a stabilizer measurement cycle compared to conventional single-dot ancilla approaches. Second, the symmetric nature of the ancilla ensures that global single-qubit operations applied to all spins in the system do not affect the ancilla measurement, since such operations preserve the symmetry between the two dots. This property is particularly valuable when switching between X-type and Z-type stabilizer measurements in the surface code, as it allows the use of global ESR pulses to rotate all data qubits simultaneously without disturbing the ancilla states.

The readout of ancilla qubits is performed through Pauli spin blockade (PSB) detection, which exploits the different tunneling rates between singlet and triplet states in double quantum dot systems. When the ancilla is in the singlet state, electrons can tunnel freely between the two dots, while in the triplet state, tunneling is blocked due to spin conservation rules. This difference in tunneling behavior can be detected through either charge sensing using quantum point contacts or single-electron transistors, or through gate-based dispersive readout techniques that measure the shift in resonant frequency of a tank circuit coupled to the quantum dots. The initialization of ancilla qubits in the singlet state is achieved through rapid relaxation near the (1,1):(0,2) charge transition, where the (0,2) charge configuration has a singlet ground state that can be quickly populated through thermal relaxation.

The mediator dots represent the key innovation of the present architecture and serve as the bridge between computational functionality and leakage error resilience. These are elongated quantum dots, typically measuring approximately 30 nm × 300 nm, positioned between data and ancilla qubits to mediate exchange interactions while providing increased physical separation between computational elements. The mediator dots are occupied by an even number of electrons, preferably two electrons, though four-electron or other even-occupancy configurations may be used to optimize performance characteristics such as valley-orbit splitting mitigation.

The mediator dots facilitate Ruderman-Kittel-Kasuya-Yosida (RKKY) exchange interactions between spins in the data and ancilla dots through virtual electron hopping processes. The exchange interaction strength J is given by the expression J = t⁴/(Δ²_on Δ_M), where t represents the tunneling energy between adjacent dots (approximately 1 GHz), Δ_on represents the energy detuning when the exchange interaction is turned on (approximately 10 GHz), and Δ_M represents the energy scale associated with the mediator dot (approximately 10 GHz). This configuration yields an exchange interaction strength of approximately 1 MHz when activated, with a residual interaction strength of only 100 Hz when nominally turned off, resulting in an off/on ratio of 10⁻⁴ that is well below the surface code error threshold.

The physical separation provided by the mediator dots (approximately 300 nm center-to-center spacing) creates sufficient space to integrate essential components that would otherwise be difficult to accommodate in densely packed quantum dot arrays. Specifically, this spacing allows for the integration of charge reservoirs adjacent to each mediator dot, planar fan-out of metallic gate structures for quantum dot definition and control, and placement of readout devices without compromising the overall qubit density. The charge reservoirs serve dual purposes: they provide electrons for initial population of the quantum dot array during system startup, and they enable continuous reset of the mediator dots during quantum computation to remove any charge deficits that may accumulate due to charge leakage events.

The surface code implementation in this architecture is carefully optimized to maximize error correction performance while minimizing resource requirements. The stabilizer check circuit is partitioned into four disjoint sets based on a four-coloring of the surface code lattice, where plaquettes of the same color share no data qubits between them. This partitioning enables sequential execution of stabilizer measurements, with each partition being activated while the others remain idle. During the active period of one partition, the mediator dots in the idle partitions can be reset through their connections to charge reservoirs, preventing temporal accumulation of charge leakage errors.

The stabilizer check circuit itself is optimized through several architectural features. The symmetric double-dot ancilla design eliminates the need for single-qubit gates on ancilla qubits, since the singlet initialization and PSB readout are naturally compatible with the surface code measurement requirements. Most Z rotations required on data qubits can be implemented virtually through phase shifts in subsequent gate pulses, eliminating the need for explicit Z-gate operations and reducing circuit depth. The only explicit single-qubit gate required in the stabilizer circuit is a Z_π rotation sandwiched between two √SWAP operations when implementing CZ gates through the exchange interaction pathway.

Two-qubit gates between data and ancilla qubits can be implemented through two different regimes depending on the system parameters. In the Ω ≫ J regime, where Ω represents the difference in Zeeman splitting between data and ancilla qubits, CZ gates can be implemented directly through dipole-dipole-like interactions mediated by the exchange interaction, requiring only a single exchange pulse of duration π/J. In the Ω ≪ J regime, CZ gates are implemented through a sequence of √SWAP operations combined with Z rotations, following the standard Loss-DiVincenzo protocol. The choice between these regimes depends on the specific implementation, with the Ω ≫ J regime being accessible through the natural variation in electron g-factors in silicon quantum dots or through the intentional creation of magnetic field gradients using integrated micromagnets.

The architecture provides inherent resilience against both spin and charge leakage errors through multiple complementary mechanisms. For spin leakage errors, the single-dot data qubit design ensures that all spin configurations remain within the computational subspace, while any spin leakage that might occur in the ancilla qubits is automatically removed during each stabilizer cycle through reinitialization in the singlet state. This prevents both temporal accumulation and spatial propagation of spin leakage errors, ensuring that spin leakage does not lead to correlated errors that could overwhelm the surface code.

For charge leakage errors, the architecture provides an elegant solution through the combination of fast charge relaxation and mediator reset. When an electron escapes from a computational qubit dot during gate operations, the resulting charge deficit is rapidly restored through relaxation of electrons from adjacent mediator dots into the empty computational dot. This charge relaxation process occurs on timescales of approximately 10 nanoseconds, which is much faster than the microsecond timescales of quantum gate operations. The charge deficit is thus transferred from the computational qubits to the mediator dots, where it can be safely removed through connection to integrated charge reservoirs without affecting the quantum information stored in the computational subspace.

The effectiveness of this charge leakage protection mechanism is demonstrated through numerical simulations of the surface code performance under various error models. In the absence of charge leakage errors, the architecture achieves surface code thresholds of 0.86% for S-gate implementations and 0.76% for √SWAP implementations, which are comparable to thresholds obtained with standard depolarizing noise models. More importantly, when charge leakage errors are included in the error model, the architecture demonstrates significant tolerance, with a pure charge leakage threshold of 0.66% in the absence of other gate errors, and practical charge leakage thresholds of 0.23-0.27% when operating at reasonable gate error rates of 0.5%. This demonstrates that the charge leakage protection mechanism effectively reduces charge leakage errors to the level of standard computational errors, making them correctable by the surface code.

The physical implementation of the architecture is specifically designed to facilitate integration with conventional semiconductor manufacturing processes. The quantum dot structures can be defined using standard gate electrode patterns fabricated through photolithography or electron-beam lithography on silicon-based heterostructures. The 300 nm separation provided by the mediator dots creates sufficient space for the planar fan-out of metallic gate structures required to define and control the two-dimensional quantum dot array, while maintaining overall qubit densities that are competitive with other semiconductor spin qubit approaches.

The integration of charge reservoirs adjacent to each mediator dot is achieved through gated connections that allow tunable tunnel coupling between the reservoirs and mediators. During system initialization, these connections can be opened to allow rapid population of the quantum dot array with electrons. During quantum computation, the tunnel coupling can be adjusted to provide appropriate reset times for the mediator dots during their idle periods, without introducing significant charge noise into the computational qubits during active periods.

The architecture also supports the integration of micromagnets for EDSR implementation, with the magnetic field gradients created by these micromagnets serving dual purposes. First, they enable faster single-qubit gates on data qubits through EDSR, with demonstrated gate speeds exceeding 10 MHz. Second, they create the necessary conditions for implementing CZ gates through the Ω ≫ J regime, where the difference in Zeeman splitting between data and ancilla qubits enables direct implementation of CZ gates through dipole-dipole-like interactions. The fact that ancilla qubits do not require single-qubit gates means that micromagnets can be placed adjacent to data qubits without interfering with ancilla operations, simplifying the overall device layout.

The operational timeline of the architecture is carefully coordinated to maximize efficiency and minimize error accumulation. A complete stabilizer cycle consists of four sequential partitions, each requiring approximately 8 microseconds when operating in the fast regime with EDSR and S-gates, or longer times when using ESR and √SWAP gates. During each partition's active period, the stabilizer measurements for that partition are performed while the mediator dots in other partitions are reset through their connections to charge reservoirs. This parallel operation ensures that mediator reset does not add overhead to the stabilizer cycle time, while preventing temporal accumulation of charge leakage errors.

The architecture is also compatible with advanced error correction techniques and can be extended to support more complex quantum error correction codes beyond the surface code. The modular nature of the design, with clearly separated computational and mediating elements, facilitates the implementation of concatenated codes or other hierarchical error correction schemes. Additionally, the architecture can be adapted to support different qubit encodings or measurement schemes as required by specific applications or error correction requirements.

In summary, the present invention provides a comprehensive quantum computing architecture that addresses the critical challenges of leakage error resilience, scalability, and manufacturability in semiconductor spin qubit systems. Through the innovative combination of single-dot data qubits, symmetric double-dot ancillas, and elongated mediator dots with integrated charge reservoirs, the architecture provides inherent protection against both spin and charge leakage errors while maintaining high qubit density and compatibility with conventional semiconductor manufacturing processes. The optimized surface code implementation, with its partitioned stabilizer cycles and virtual gate techniques, achieves excellent error correction performance with practical thresholds for both computational and leakage errors, making it a promising platform for scalable fault-tolerant quantum computation.
