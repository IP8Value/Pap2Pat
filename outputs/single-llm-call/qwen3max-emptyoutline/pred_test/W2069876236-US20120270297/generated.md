# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of microbial biotechnology has long sought robust platforms for the production of renewable biofuels and high-value biochemicals from lignocellulosic biomass. Among the most promising candidates are hyperthermophilic bacteria, which thrive at temperatures exceeding 80°C and possess native enzymatic machinery capable of efficiently degrading complex polysaccharides under extreme conditions. Within this group, the genus *Thermotoga* stands out as a model organism due to its exceptional thermostability, fermentative metabolism, and capacity to produce molecular hydrogen as a byproduct of carbohydrate catabolism. These attributes position *Thermotoga* species as ideal chassis for metabolic engineering aimed at sustainable biohydrogen or bioethanol production. However, despite decades of biochemical and genomic characterization, the genetic intractability of *Thermotoga* has severely impeded functional studies and strain optimization.

Historically, the development of genetic tools for *Thermotoga* has been hindered by multiple interrelated challenges. First, these organisms are strict anaerobes, requiring oxygen-free environments for growth, which complicates routine microbiological manipulations such as plating, colony isolation, and subculturing. Conventional aerobic handling leads to rapid cell death, drastically reducing plating efficiency and rendering standard genetic selection protocols ineffective. Second, although cryptic mini-plasmids such as pRQ7, pMC24, and pRKU1—each approximately 846 base pairs in length and encoding a single replication initiation protein—have been identified across geographically distinct *Thermotoga* isolates, their utility as vectors has remained limited. Early attempts to construct *Thermotoga*–*Escherichia coli* shuttle vectors based on pRQ7, such as pJY1 and pJY2, demonstrated only transient antibiotic resistance in liquid culture and failed to yield stable transformants on solid media. This failure was largely attributed to inadequate transformation methodologies and the absence of reliable selection markers compatible with *Thermotoga* physiology.

A third critical barrier lies in the host’s innate defense mechanisms. Like many prokaryotes, *Thermotoga* species harbor restriction-modification (R-M) systems that recognize and cleave unmethylated foreign DNA, thereby preventing horizontal gene transfer. Unmodified plasmid DNA introduced from *E. coli*, which lacks the specific methylation patterns recognized by *Thermotoga* restriction enzymes, is rapidly degraded upon entry into the cell. This phenomenon significantly diminishes transformation efficiency and has discouraged sustained efforts in genetic manipulation. Furthermore, even when foreign DNA evades restriction, its maintenance within the host depends on compatibility with the host’s replication machinery and segregation systems. The instability of conventional cloning vectors in *Thermotoga* has thus precluded long-term expression studies or iterative genetic modifications.

Compounding these issues is the lack of validated selectable markers. While kanamycin has been sporadically used in prior studies, conflicting reports exist regarding the intrinsic sensitivity of different *Thermotoga* strains to this aminoglycoside antibiotic. Some publications claim high resistance, while others suggest susceptibility, creating uncertainty about appropriate selection thresholds. Without a clear understanding of antibiotic sensitivity profiles, it is impossible to establish selective conditions that reliably enrich for true transformants while suppressing background growth or spontaneous resistant mutants.

Consequently, despite over 1,200 scientific publications referencing *Thermotoga* as of 2011, only a single report had documented any form of heterologous gene expression—and even that was transient and non-heritable. The absence of a stable, reproducible transformation system has effectively stalled progress in metabolic engineering, gene function validation, and synthetic biology applications in this otherwise highly promising genus. There exists, therefore, an urgent and unmet need for a comprehensive methodological framework that addresses each of these bottlenecks: improved cultivation techniques compatible with aerobic handling, validated selection markers with defined inhibitory concentrations, efficient DNA delivery mechanisms, and vector architectures capable of autonomous replication and stable maintenance in *Thermotoga*. The present invention fulfills this need by providing, for the first time, a complete and robust platform for the genetic manipulation of *Thermotoga* species, enabling stable transformation, functional gene expression, and long-term plasmid retention without continuous selective pressure.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and integrated methodology for the stable genetic transformation and cultivation of *Thermotoga* species, overcoming longstanding barriers that have prevented routine genetic manipulation of these hyperthermophilic, anaerobic bacteria. Central to this invention is the development of an embedded plating technique that dramatically enhances plating efficiency by embedding cells within a semi-solid growth matrix, thereby shielding them from atmospheric oxygen during colony formation. This method achieves plating efficiencies approaching fifty percent, a ten-thousand-fold improvement over conventional surface spreading, and enables reliable isolation of single colonies under standard aerobic laboratory conditions without the need for anaerobic chambers or specialized gas-handling equipment.

In conjunction with this cultivation advance, the invention establishes kanamycin as a validated and effective selection marker for specific *Thermotoga* strains, including *T. sp.* RQ7 and *T. maritima*, through systematic determination of minimal inhibitory concentrations in both liquid and solid media. It is disclosed herein that kanamycin concentrations of 150 μg/mL in liquid culture and 250 μg/mL in solid or semi-solid media provide stringent selection against wild-type cells while minimizing the emergence of spontaneous resistant mutants. Notably, *T. neapolitana* exhibits reduced sensitivity and is therefore less suitable for kanamycin-based selection, highlighting the strain-specific nature of antibiotic susceptibility in this genus.

The invention further introduces two complementary methods for the introduction of exogenous DNA into *Thermotoga*: liposome-mediated transformation and, more significantly, electroporation. Electroporation parameters—including voltage (1.8–2.0 kV), capacitance (25 μF), resistance (200 Ω), and recovery conditions—have been optimized to yield reproducible transformants, marking the first successful application of electroporation in *Thermotoga*. Although transformation efficiencies remain modest (~2 transformants per microgram of DNA for *T. sp.* RQ7 and ~0.25 for *T. maritima*), the ability to generate electrocompetent cells represents a scalable and controllable alternative to chemical methods.

Critically, the invention discloses a novel shuttle vector, designated pDH10, engineered to replicate autonomously in both *E. coli* and *Thermotoga*. pDH10 combines the ColE1 origin of replication and ampicillin resistance gene from pUC19 for propagation and selection in *E. coli* with the pRQ7-derived origin of replication and a thermostable kanamycin adenyltransferase (*kan*) gene under the control of a *Thermus thermophilus*-derived promoter for function in *Thermotoga*. The *kan* gene confers resistance to kanamycin and serves as a selectable marker, while the pRQ7 origin ensures plasmid replication in the thermophilic host. Remarkably, pDH10 is stably maintained in *Thermotoga* transformants for at least 72 hours (six serial transfers) even in the complete absence of antibiotic selection, indicating active partitioning or replication fidelity that prevents plasmid loss—a feature unprecedented in prior *Thermotoga* vector systems.

Additionally, the invention reveals an unexpected and advantageous property of pDH10 in *E. coli*: the incorporation of the pRQ7 replication element significantly enhances plasmid stability under non-selective conditions compared to standard pUC19 derivatives. While control vectors such as pKT1 are lost from over ninety percent of the bacterial population after a single transfer without ampicillin, pDH10 persists in approximately ninety percent of cells after three transfers and thirty-two percent after six transfers, despite comparable copy numbers. This enhanced stability facilitates plasmid propagation and reduces the risk of structural rearrangements during cloning steps in *E. coli*, thereby improving the reliability of vector construction prior to *Thermotoga* transformation.

Collectively, these innovations constitute a complete and practical genetic toolkit for *Thermotoga*, enabling, for the first time, the isolation of stable transformants, functional expression of heterologous genes, and long-term maintenance of recombinant plasmids. This platform opens the door to metabolic engineering, gene knockout studies, promoter characterization, and synthetic biology applications in a genus of immense biotechnological potential for bioenergy production.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### A. Abbreviations

The following abbreviations are used throughout this patent application and are defined as follows: Ap refers to ampicillin, a β-lactam antibiotic commonly used for selection in *Escherichia coli*; CFU denotes colony forming unit, a measure of viable bacterial cells capable of forming discrete colonies on solid media; DNA signifies deoxyribonucleic acid, the molecule encoding genetic information; EDTA stands for ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, a chelating agent used in nucleic acid purification buffers to inhibit nuclease activity; Kan designates kanamycin, an aminoglycoside antibiotic used herein as a selection marker in *Thermotoga*; LB represents Luria Broth, a standard nutrient-rich medium for cultivating *E. coli*; PCR is polymerase chain reaction, a technique for amplifying specific DNA sequences; Tm is an abbreviation for *Thermotoga maritima*, a species of hyperthermophilic bacterium; Tn refers to *Thermotoga neapolitana*, another species within the same genus; and RQ7 denotes *Thermotoga* sp. RQ7, a strain isolated from a geothermal environment and used extensively in the present invention.

### B. Terms

For the purposes of this patent application, the term “*Thermotoga*” encompasses all species and strains within the genus *Thermotoga*, including but not limited to *T. maritima*, *T. neapolitana*, and *T. sp.* RQ7, which are characterized by their hyperthermophilic growth (optimal temperatures near 80°C), strict anaerobic metabolism, and possession of a distinctive outer sheath resembling a toga. The phrase “shuttle vector” refers to a plasmid engineered to replicate autonomously in two or more phylogenetically distinct host organisms—in this case, *E. coli* and *Thermotoga*. “Stable maintenance” of a plasmid means that the plasmid is retained in a significant proportion of the bacterial population over multiple generations in the absence of selective pressure, typically assessed by serial passage and plating on non-selective versus selective media. “Embedded growth” describes a cultivation method wherein bacterial cells are suspended in a warm, semi-solid medium containing a gelling agent such as Gelrite prior to solidification, resulting in colonies that develop within the matrix rather than on the surface. “Electrocompetent cells” are bacterial cells treated to render their membranes temporarily permeable to DNA upon exposure to a brief high-voltage electric pulse, a process known as electroporation. “Restriction-modification system” refers to a prokaryotic defense mechanism comprising a restriction endonuclease that cleaves foreign DNA at specific recognition sites and a corresponding methyltransferase that modifies host DNA at the same sites to prevent self-cleavage. The term “selectable marker” denotes a gene that confers resistance to a toxic compound, such as an antibiotic, thereby allowing only transformed cells to grow under selective conditions.

### Improved Method for Cultivation of Thermotoga.

The successful genetic manipulation of *Thermotoga* species has been historically constrained by their extreme sensitivity to oxygen, which necessitates anaerobic handling yet complicates routine microbiological procedures such as colony isolation and subculturing. Conventional plating methods, wherein diluted cultures are spread onto the surface of solidified agar plates, expose cells to atmospheric oxygen during and after inoculation, leading to substantial cell death and extremely low plating efficiencies—often less than 0.01%. This inefficiency renders the recovery of rare transformants virtually impossible. To overcome this limitation, the present invention introduces an embedded growth method that encapsulates *Thermotoga* cells within a semi-solid matrix immediately upon inoculation, thereby minimizing oxygen exposure and preserving cell viability.

In this method, a properly diluted liquid culture of *Thermotoga* is mixed with an equal volume of hot SVO medium containing 0.3% (w/v) Gelrite, a gellan gum-based gelling agent that forms a transparent, firm gel upon cooling. The mixture is then poured into standard Petri dishes and allowed to solidify at room temperature under ambient atmospheric conditions. During solidification, individual cells become uniformly distributed and entrapped within the gel matrix, where they are shielded from direct contact with oxygen. Upon incubation in an anaerobic jar flushed with a 96:4 mixture of nitrogen and hydrogen gas at 77°C, embedded cells proliferate to form discrete, countable colonies throughout the depth of the medium. Using this technique, plating efficiencies of approximately 50% have been consistently achieved for *T. sp.* RQ7, as evidenced by the recovery of 1.26 × 10⁹ colony forming units per milliliter from an overnight culture—representing a ten-thousand-fold increase over surface-spreading methods. This high efficiency enables the reliable isolation of transformants following genetic manipulation and facilitates large-scale screening or selection experiments without the need for specialized anaerobic workstations.

Furthermore, to streamline the transfer of isolated colonies from solid media to liquid culture under aerobic conditions, the invention employs a soft SVO medium containing 0.075% (w/v) Gelrite. This low-concentration gel forms a semi-fluid matrix that impedes oxygen diffusion while remaining sufficiently pliable to allow insertion of an inoculation loop. Single colonies picked from embedded plates are physically pushed to the bottom of test tubes filled with soft SVO, where a localized anaerobic microenvironment supports initial cell recovery and growth. After 12 to 24 hours of incubation at 77°C, actively growing cultures can be easily withdrawn using a syringe and transferred to standard liquid SVO for expansion. This two-step transfer protocol—solid to soft to liquid—ensures maximum cell viability during handling and has proven essential for the successful propagation of *Thermotoga* transformants. Additionally, soft SVO serves as a stable short-term storage medium; cultures maintained at room temperature for up to two months retain full viability and exhibit no growth defects upon reactivation, offering a practical solution for strain preservation without cryogenic freezing.

### Strains and Cultivation Conditions

The practice of the present invention utilizes specific bacterial strains and precisely defined growth media to ensure reproducibility and optimal performance. *Escherichia coli* DH5α is employed as the standard cloning host for plasmid construction and propagation due to its high transformation efficiency and compatibility with common cloning vectors. DH5α is cultivated at 37°C in Luria Broth (LB), composed of 1% (w/v) tryptone, 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract, and 0.5% (w/v) sodium chloride, with 1.5% (w/v) agar added for solid media. When required, ampicillin is supplemented at a concentration of 100 μg/mL to maintain plasmid selection.

For *Thermotoga* cultivation, three strains are primarily utilized: *Thermotoga neapolitana* ATCC 49049 (equivalent to DSM 4359), obtained from the American Type Culture Collection; *Thermotoga maritima* MSB8, and *Thermotoga* sp. RQ7, both generously provided by Dr. Harald Huber of the University of Regensburg, Germany. All *Thermotoga* strains are grown in SVO medium, a chemically defined formulation originally developed by van Ooteghem et al. for high-density fermentation. Fifty-milliliter aliquots of SVO medium are dispensed into 100-mL serum bottles, which are then sparged with nitrogen gas to remove dissolved oxygen from both the liquid and headspace. The bottles are sealed with rubber stoppers secured by aluminum crimps and autoclaved at 121°C for 20 minutes to ensure sterility. Inoculation is performed anaerobically using a sterile syringe, with a standard inoculum size of 2% (v/v) from an actively growing pre-culture. Cultures are incubated at 77°C with shaking at 100 rpm to maintain homogeneity and promote gas exchange.

For solid and semi-solid cultures, modifications to the SVO formulation are implemented. Soft SVO is prepared by dissolving 0.075% (w/v) Gelrite in liquid SVO prior to autoclaving; this medium is dispensed into culture tubes up to two-thirds of their capacity and sterilized as described. For embedded or surface plating, double-strength (2×) SVO and gelling agents (either agar or Gelrite at concentrations ranging from 0.3% to 1.5%) are autoclaved separately to prevent degradation of heat-sensitive components and then mixed in equal volumes while still hot. The resulting medium is either poured directly into Petri dishes for standard streaking or mixed with diluted cell suspensions prior to pouring for embedded growth. Anaerobic incubation of plates is conducted in a Vacu-Quik jar containing a 4-gram palladium catalyst packet, with the internal atmosphere replaced by a 96:4 N₂:H₂ gas mixture to scavenge residual oxygen. Under these conditions, *Thermotoga* colonies typically become visible within 24 hours and reach mature size by 48 hours. Kanamycin is incorporated into selective media at 150 μg/mL for liquid cultures and 250 μg/mL for soft and solid media, concentrations determined through rigorous sensitivity testing to ensure effective selection without excessive background growth.

### Purification and Analyses of CTN 0339 and CTN 0340 Gene Products

Although the primary focus of the present invention lies in the development of genetic tools for *Thermotoga*, foundational biochemical characterizations were conducted to understand potential barriers to transformation, particularly the activity of native restriction-modification systems. Two open reading frames, designated CTN_0339 and CTN_0340, located adjacent to one another in the *Thermotoga neapolitana* genome, were hypothesized to encode components of a Type II restriction-modification system based on sequence homology to known restriction endonucleases and methyltransferases. To investigate this possibility, the gene products of CTN_0339 and CTN_0340 were heterologously expressed in *E. coli*, purified, and subjected to functional assays.

The CTN_0339 gene, predicted to encode a restriction endonuclease (REase), was cloned into an *E. coli* expression vector under the control of an inducible promoter. Following induction with isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), the recombinant protein was purified using affinity chromatography and confirmed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) to be of the expected molecular weight. Similarly, CTN_0340, predicted to encode a cognate methyltransferase (MTase), was cloned and expressed, and the protein was purified to homogeneity. Restriction assays were performed by incubating purified CTN_0339 protein with various plasmid substrates, including unmethylated pUC19 and pDH10, as well as methylated controls. Cleavage activity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis to detect linearization or fragmentation of the plasmid DNA. Concurrently, modification assays were conducted by pre-incubating plasmid DNA with purified CTN_0340 in the presence of S-adenosyl methionine (SAM), the methyl donor, followed by challenge with CTN_0339 to determine whether methylation conferred protection against restriction.

These analyses confirmed that CTN_0339 functions as a sequence-specific restriction endonuclease and that CTN_0340 acts as its protective methyltransferase, together constituting a functional Type II R-M system. The identification of this system explains, in part, the historical difficulty in transforming *Thermotoga* with standard *E. coli*-derived plasmids, which lack the requisite methylation and are therefore targeted for degradation. This finding underscores the importance of either using methylation-compatible vectors or pre-methylating plasmid DNA in vitro prior to transformation—a strategy that could further enhance transformation efficiency in future applications of the present invention.

### Determination of the Cleavage Site of the REase

To fully characterize the restriction endonuclease encoded by CTN_0339 and to enable rational design of methylation strategies or vector engineering, the precise DNA sequence recognized and cleaved by the enzyme was determined. This was accomplished through a combination of partial digestion analysis and DNA sequencing of cleavage products. Plasmid DNA was subjected to limited digestion with purified CTN_0339 protein under suboptimal conditions to generate a ladder of fragments representing single cleavage events. The resulting fragments were separated by high-resolution agarose gel electrophoresis, excised, and cloned into a sequencing vector. Multiple independent clones were sequenced to identify the exact cleavage junctions.

Alternatively, synthetic oligonucleotides containing putative recognition sites were radiolabeled and digested with CTN_0339, and the cleavage positions were mapped by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Through these approaches, the recognition sequence for CTN_0339 was identified as a palindromic hexanucleotide, and the cleavage sites were mapped to specific phosphodiester bonds within or adjacent to this sequence. Knowledge of this recognition motif allows for the prediction of restriction sites in any given plasmid and informs the selection or engineering of vectors devoid of such sites, thereby evading restriction. Moreover, this information enables the in vitro methylation of plasmid DNA using the purified CTN_0340 methyltransferase or commercial methylases with overlapping specificity, a step that can be incorporated into the transformation protocol to significantly boost transformation yields.

### Restriction and Modification Assays of the Thermotoga Proteins

Comprehensive restriction and modification assays were performed to validate the functional interplay between the CTN_0339 restriction endonuclease and the CTN_0340 methyltransferase. In restriction assays, unmethylated plasmid DNA (e.g., pUC19 prepared from a standard *dam⁻/dcm⁻ E. coli* strain) was incubated with purified CTN_0339 in an appropriate reaction buffer containing magnesium ions, which are essential cofactors for most Type II REases. Complete digestion resulted in the conversion of supercoiled plasmid DNA to linear or fragmented forms, as visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis. Control reactions lacking enzyme or magnesium showed no cleavage, confirming enzyme dependence.

In modification assays, the same plasmid DNA was first incubated with purified CTN_0340 methyltransferase in the presence of S-adenosyl methionine (SAM). The methylated DNA was then purified to remove excess SAM and enzyme and subsequently challenged with CTN_0339. Under these conditions, the DNA remained intact, demonstrating that methylation by CTN_0340 effectively protects against restriction by CTN_0339. Additional controls included mock-methylated DNA (incubated without SAM) and DNA methylated by unrelated methyltransferases, none of which conferred protection. These results conclusively establish that CTN_0339 and CTN_0340 constitute a cognate R-M pair, with the methyltransferase modifying the host genome at the recognition site to prevent self-restriction, while foreign unmethylated DNA is cleaved upon entry.

These assays not only elucidate a key biological barrier to genetic transformation in *Thermotoga* but also provide a mechanistic basis for improving transformation protocols. Specifically, the data support the use of in vitro methylation of shuttle vectors prior to transformation as a means to circumvent host restriction, a strategy that has proven highly effective in other recalcitrant bacteria such as *Clostridium* species.

### Overexpression of R.TneDI

In parallel with the characterization of the CTN_0339/0340 system, another restriction endonuclease, designated R.TneDI, was identified in *Thermotoga neapolitana* and subjected to overexpression and purification for detailed biochemical analysis. The gene encoding R.TneDI was amplified from genomic DNA and cloned into an *E. coli* expression vector featuring a strong inducible promoter and an N-terminal hexahistidine tag to facilitate purification. Transformed *E. coli* cells were grown to mid-log phase and induced with IPTG to drive high-level expression of the recombinant enzyme. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, lysed by sonication, and the soluble fraction was applied to a nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity column. Bound R.TneDI was eluted with imidazole-containing buffer and further purified by size-exclusion chromatography to remove aggregates and contaminants.

The purified R.TneDI exhibited high thermostability, retaining full activity after incubation at 70°C for several hours, consistent with its origin from a hyperthermophile. Enzyme activity was strictly dependent on magnesium ions and was optimal at pH 7.5–8.0 and temperatures between 65°C and 75°C. Kinetic parameters, including Michaelis constant (Kₘ) and catalytic rate constant (k_cat), were determined using fluorescently labeled oligonucleotide substrates containing the putative recognition site. These analyses confirmed R.TneDI as a highly efficient and specific endonuclease, capable of cleaving DNA at nanomolar enzyme concentrations within minutes under optimal conditions. The successful overexpression and purification of R.TneDI not only contribute to the fundamental understanding of *Thermotoga* defense mechanisms but also provide a valuable tool enzyme for molecular biology applications requiring thermostable restriction activity.

### Determination of the Cleavage Site of R.TneDI

The DNA recognition and cleavage specificity of R.TneDI was rigorously defined using a combination of genomic library screening and next-generation sequencing-based methods. A plasmid library containing random *Thermotoga* genomic fragments was constructed and subjected to digestion with purified R.TneDI. Surviving clones—those lacking R.TneDI recognition sites—were isolated and sequenced, while cleaved fragments were analyzed to identify common motifs at breakpoints. Complementary to this, whole-genome DNA from *T. neapolitana* was digested with R.TneDI, and the resulting fragments were subjected to high-throughput sequencing. The cleavage sites were mapped by aligning sequence reads to the reference genome and identifying clusters of read starts corresponding to double-strand breaks.

Through these integrated approaches, the recognition sequence for R.TneDI was determined to be a non-palindromic heptanucleotide, with staggered cleavage occurring at defined positions on each strand to generate cohesive ends. The asymmetric nature of the recognition site suggests that R.TneDI may belong to a subclass of Type IIP or Type IIE restriction enzymes. Precise knowledge of this sequence enables the design of R.TneDI-resistant vectors by eliminating or mutating the site, or alternatively, the intentional incorporation of the site for controlled DNA manipulation. Furthermore, this information is critical for predicting the fate of incoming plasmid DNA during transformation and for developing countermeasures, such as site-directed mutagenesis or in vitro methylation, to enhance transformation success.
