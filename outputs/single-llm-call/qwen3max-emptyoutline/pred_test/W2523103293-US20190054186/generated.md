## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of cancer therapeutics and, more specifically, to a method and system for selectively inducing cytotoxicity in cancer cells through the remote actuation of superparamagnetic nanoparticles (SMNPs) using a super-low frequency alternating current (AC) magnetic field. The invention further encompasses novel polymer-coated SMNP complexes, particularly those comprising pentablock copolymers with polyanionic and hydrophobic blocks, which are engineered to enhance intracellular uptake and facilitate magneto-mechanical disruption of the cellular cytoskeleton. The technology leverages the intrinsic mechanical differences between cancerous and non-cancerous cells to achieve a high degree of selectivity, thereby minimizing off-target effects on healthy tissues. This approach represents a paradigm shift from conventional thermal-based hyperthermia or chemotherapeutic strategies, offering a non-invasive, remotely controlled, and mechanically driven therapeutic modality for the treatment of solid tumors, particularly those that are resistant to standard therapies, such as triple-negative breast cancer.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with a persistent need for therapeutic modalities that are both effective and selective. Traditional treatments, including chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery, are often limited by systemic toxicity, damage to healthy tissues, and the development of resistance. In recent years, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising frontier in oncology, offering the potential for targeted drug delivery, enhanced imaging, and novel therapeutic mechanisms. Among the various nanomaterials explored, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), or superparamagnetic nanoparticles (SMNPs), have garnered significant attention due to their unique physical properties, biocompatibility, and responsiveness to external magnetic fields.

Historically, the primary therapeutic application of SMNPs in an external magnetic field has been magnetic hyperthermia. In this approach, SMNPs are exposed to a high-frequency alternating magnetic field, causing them to generate heat through Néel and Brownian relaxation mechanisms. This localized heating can then be used to ablate tumor cells. However, this method is fundamentally limited by the need for significant thermal energy, which can inadvertently damage surrounding healthy tissue if not precisely controlled. Moreover, the efficacy of hyperthermia is highly dependent on achieving a uniform distribution of nanoparticles within the tumor and maintaining a sufficient temperature for a sustained period, challenges that are difficult to overcome in a clinical setting.

An alternative, non-thermal approach involves the use of magnetic fields to induce mechanical forces on internalized SMNPs. The concept is that these forces can be transduced into biological signals or direct physical damage within the cell. Early work in this area has suggested that mechanical actuation of SMNPs can lead to lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP), a process that releases cathepsins and other hydrolytic enzymes into the cytosol, triggering apoptosis. For instance, studies have demonstrated that rotating magnetic fields can cause physically tethered nanoparticles to torque and rupture lysosomal membranes. While promising, these strategies often require complex field configurations, such as rotating fields, and rely on relatively large nanoparticles (e.g., >100 nm) that may have poor biodistribution and clearance profiles.

A critical challenge in developing any cancer-selective therapy is the ability to discriminate between malignant and healthy cells. Many prior art approaches have focused on biochemical differences, such as the overexpression of specific receptors on cancer cells, to achieve targeting. However, this strategy is not universally applicable, as many cancers, including aggressive subtypes like triple-negative breast cancer, lack well-defined, targetable surface markers. Furthermore, even when a target is present, its expression can be heterogeneous within a tumor, leading to incomplete treatment.

Recent research has highlighted a fundamental biophysical distinction between cancerous and non-cancerous cells: their mechanical properties. Cancer cells, in order to facilitate invasion and metastasis, undergo a process of cytoskeletal remodeling that renders them significantly softer and more deformable than their healthy counterparts. This difference in mechanical phenotype, characterized by a lower Young’s modulus, is a near-universal feature of malignancy across many cancer types. This mechanical vulnerability presents a novel and potentially universal target for therapeutic intervention. However, no existing technology has successfully harnessed this mechanical difference to create a selective, non-invasive, and remotely controlled cancer therapy.

There remains a significant unmet need for a therapeutic platform that can exploit the inherent mechanical softness of cancer cells to induce selective cytotoxicity without relying on heat, specific biochemical targets, or invasive procedures. The ideal system would utilize small, biocompatible nanoparticles that are efficiently internalized by cells, a simple and safe magnetic field configuration, and a mechanism of action that directly translates magnetic energy into a mechanical force capable of disrupting the delicate cytoskeletal architecture of cancer cells while sparing the stiffer, more resilient cytoskeleton of healthy cells. The present invention addresses this need by providing a novel composition of matter and a method of use that achieves this precise goal.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and highly selective method for the treatment of cancer that exploits the fundamental mechanical differences between cancerous and non-cancerous cells. The invention is based on the discovery that superparamagnetic nanoparticles (SMNPs), when internalized by cells and subjected to a super-low frequency alternating current (AC) magnetic field, can induce a cytotoxic effect that is specific to cancer cells. This selectivity is not predicated on differential uptake of the nanoparticles or on the generation of heat, but rather on the mechanical vulnerability of the cancer cell cytoskeleton.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for selectively killing cancer cells in a population of cells comprising both cancer cells and non-cancerous cells. The method comprises the steps of: (a) contacting the population of cells with a composition comprising a plurality of superparamagnetic nanoparticles (SMNPs), wherein each SMNP is coated with a block copolymer comprising a polyanionic block and a hydrophobic block, to allow for the internalization of the SMNPs by the cells; and (b) exposing the population of cells to an alternating current (AC) magnetic field having a frequency of from about 30 Hz to about 3000 Hz and a magnetic field intensity of from about 10 kA/m to about 100 kA/m, thereby selectively inducing death in the cancer cells while leaving the non-cancerous cells substantially viable.

In a preferred embodiment, the block copolymer is a pentablock copolymer of the structure polyanion-b-P85-b-polyanion, where P85 is a poly(ethylene oxide)26-b-poly(propylene oxide)39-b-poly(ethylene oxide)26 triblock copolymer. The polyanionic block is preferably poly(acrylic acid) (PAA). The hydrophobic central block of the P85 component is critical for enhancing the intracellular uptake of the SMNP complexes and for facilitating the formation of a dense physical network within the lysosome after internalization.

The AC magnetic field is preferably applied in a pulsed regime, for example, a 10-minute "on" period followed by a 5-minute "off" period, repeated to achieve a total "on" time of 30 minutes. This pulsed regime has been found to be significantly more effective at inducing cytoskeletal damage and cell death than a continuous field of the same total duration and intensity. The frequency of the field is most preferably 50 Hz, and the magnetic field intensity is most preferably 50 kA/m.

The mechanism of action is a key feature of the invention. The internalized SMNPs accumulate within the lysosomes of the cells. Upon exposure to the AC magnetic field, the SMNPs, which aggregate into clusters within the lysosome, experience a torque that causes them to attempt to physically rotate or oscillate. Because the SMNPs are embedded within a dense polymer network that is itself anchored to the lysosomal membrane, this torque is transmitted to the lysosome as a whole. The lysosome, in turn, is physically connected to the underlying actin cytoskeleton. The repeated mechanical stress and shear forces generated by the oscillating lysosome-SMNP complex are sufficient to disrupt the actin filaments in the mechanically softer cancer cells, leading to catastrophic cytoskeletal failure and subsequent cell death. In contrast, the stiffer, more robust cytoskeleton of non-cancerous cells can withstand these same mechanical forces, resulting in no significant loss of viability.

This mechanism was confirmed by experiments showing that pre-treatment of non-cancerous cells with a sub-lethal dose of cytochalasin D, a drug that disrupts actin polymerization and softens the cell, sensitizes them to the cytotoxic effects of the SMNP/AC field treatment. This demonstrates that the mechanical integrity of the cytoskeleton is the primary determinant of cellular susceptibility to this therapy.

In another aspect, the invention provides the novel composition of matter itself: a stable aqueous dispersion of superparamagnetic nanoparticle (SMNP) complexes, wherein each complex comprises a cluster of iron oxide nanoparticle cores coated with a pentablock copolymer of poly(acrylic acid)-b-P85-b-poly(acrylic acid). These complexes have a hydrodynamic diameter of from about 30 to about 70 nm, a zeta potential of from about -35 to about -50 mV, and a polymer content of approximately 60% by weight. This specific composition is engineered to be non-toxic, to be efficiently internalized by a wide range of cell types, and to form the necessary physical network within the lysosome to enable the magneto-mechanical transduction of force to the cytoskeleton.

The present invention thus offers a powerful and versatile platform for cancer therapy that is selective, non-thermal, remotely controlled, and based on a universal biophysical property of cancer cells. It is particularly well-suited for the treatment of cancers that are currently difficult to manage, such as triple-negative breast cancer, and has the potential to be adapted for a wide range of other solid tumors.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Experimental Methods

The present invention is founded upon a series of rigorous experimental methods designed to synthesize, characterize, and validate the efficacy and selectivity of the novel polymer-coated superparamagnetic nanoparticle (SMNP) complexes and their interaction with a super-low frequency alternating current (AC) magnetic field. The methods employed encompass materials science, cell biology, biophysics, and analytical chemistry to provide a comprehensive understanding of the system.

The synthesis of the core SMNP material began with the preparation of magnetite (Fe3O4) nanoparticles via a co-precipitation method, yielding particles with a primary crystallite size of approximately 7-8 nm. These bare SMNPs were then subjected to a ligand exchange process to coat them with a custom-designed block copolymer. The copolymer of choice was a pentablock structure, specifically poly(acrylic acid)26-b-poly(ethylene oxide)26-b-poly(propylene oxide)39-b-poly(ethylene oxide)26-b-poly(acrylic acid)26, abbreviated as PAA-b-P85-b-PAA. The P85 segment, a commercially available Pluronic® triblock copolymer, was selected for its amphiphilic nature, with the central hydrophobic poly(propylene oxide) block flanked by two hydrophilic poly(ethylene oxide) blocks. The terminal PAA blocks provide a strong anchoring point to the iron oxide surface through carboxylate coordination and confer a negative charge to the final complex, enhancing its colloidal stability in aqueous environments. The ligand exchange was performed in a controlled aqueous environment, ensuring complete displacement of the original surfactant and the formation of a stable, monodisperse coating.

Following synthesis, the physicochemical properties of the PAA-P85-SMNP complexes were meticulously characterized. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to determine the hydrodynamic diameter (Di) and polydispersity index (PDI), confirming that the complexes formed small clusters with a Di in the range of 30-70 nm and a low PDI, indicating a narrow size distribution. The zeta potential (ζ-potential) was measured to be between -35 and -50 mV, which is indicative of excellent electrostatic stability in suspension. The polymer content of the complexes was quantified using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), which revealed that the organic coating constituted approximately 60% of the total weight of the complex. This finding was corroborated by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), which was used to measure the iron content, allowing for a cross-validated calculation of the polymer-to-iron ratio. The magnetic properties of the complexes were assessed by vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM), which showed saturation magnetization values in the range of 60-70 A m²/kg Fe3O4, confirming their superparamagnetic nature and suitability for magnetic manipulation.

For cellular studies, a panel of human breast cell lines was utilized to model the therapeutic scenario. The cancerous lines included MDA-MB-231, a well-established model of aggressive, triple-negative (ER-/PR-/HER2-) breast cancer, and BT474, a model of HER2+ ductal carcinoma with a distinct, more complex cytoskeletal architecture. The non-tumorigenic human mammary epithelial cell line MCF10A served as the healthy control. All cells were cultured under standard conditions in their respective media at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cytotoxicity of the PAA-P85-SMNP complexes in the absence of a magnetic field was first evaluated using the standard MTT assay, which measures mitochondrial activity as a proxy for cell viability. Cells were exposed to a range of concentrations (0.005–0.5 mg/mL) for 24 hours, followed by a wash step with acidic saline (pH 3) to remove any non-internalized nanoparticles. The results confirmed that the complexes were minimally toxic across all cell lines at all tested doses, establishing a wide safety margin for their use.

To quantify the internalization of the complexes, cells were treated with varying doses of PAA-P85-SMNPs for either 1 hour or 24 hours. After thorough washing to remove surface-bound particles, the cells were harvested, lysed, and digested in nitric acid. The iron content of the resulting solution was then analyzed by ICP-MS, and the data was normalized to the total protein content of the sample to yield a measure of Fe/mg protein. This quantitative uptake study established that internalization was both time- and concentration-dependent and occurred across all three cell lines, with no significant preferential uptake by the cancer cells over the healthy cells, a crucial finding that underscores the selectivity of the subsequent magnetic field treatment is not due to differential accumulation.

For visualization of intracellular distribution, the PAA-P85-SMNPs were fluorescently labeled with Alexa Fluor®647 using carbodiimide (EDC) chemistry, which conjugates the dye to the free carboxylic acid groups on the PAA blocks. Labeled complexes were purified via size-exclusion chromatography on a Sephadex G-50 column to remove unreacted dye. Cells were then incubated with the labeled complexes, and their subcellular localization was tracked using live-cell confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). Lysosomes were co-stained with Lysotracker™ Green, and nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342. Colocalization analysis using ImageJ/Fiji software provided quantitative data on the extent to which the SMNPs resided within the lysosomal compartment. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was also employed on fixed and sectioned cells to provide high-resolution ultrastructural confirmation of lysosomal accumulation.

The core therapeutic experiment involved exposing SMNP-loaded cells to the custom-built super-low frequency AC magnetic field generator. This device was capable of producing a sinusoidal field with a frequency range of 30-3000 Hz and a magnetic field amplitude of 10-100 mT (equivalent to 10-100 kA/m). All cellular experiments were conducted at 37°C using a temperature-controlled cuvette holder. Two exposure regimes were tested: a continuous 30-minute exposure and a pulsed regime consisting of 10 minutes "on" followed by 5 minutes "off," repeated three times for a total of 30 minutes of field exposure. Following magnetic field exposure, cell viability was again assessed by the MTT assay 24 hours later to allow for the manifestation of any delayed cytotoxic effects.

To elucidate the mechanism of cell death, a series of follow-up experiments were performed. An acridine orange assay was used to definitively rule out lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) as the cause of death, as acridine orange exhibits a metachromatic shift from red/orange (in intact lysosomes) to green (in the cytosol/nucleus) upon LMP. To investigate the role of the cytoskeleton, cells were stained with ActinGreen 488, a phalloidin-based probe, after treatment and imaged by CLSM to visualize the integrity of the actin filament network. In a key experiment, non-cancerous MCF10A cells were pre-treated with a sub-lethal dose of cytochalasin D (CD), a known actin-depolymerizing agent, to artificially soften their cytoskeleton before being subjected to the full SMNP/AC field treatment. The resulting cell viability and cytoskeletal morphology were then compared to untreated controls. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, with significance defined as p < 0.05, using ANOVA or Student’s t-tests with appropriate post-hoc corrections for multiple comparisons.

### Example 2

The synthesis and characterization of the PAA-P85-SMNP complexes represent a foundational element of the present invention, as the specific chemical architecture of the polymer coating is critical to the system's overall performance. The process begins with the preparation of a stable aqueous dispersion of bare superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SMNPs) with a mean core diameter of approximately 7-8 nm, as confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). These primary particles are inherently prone to aggregation due to their high surface energy and magnetic dipole-dipole interactions. To stabilize them and impart the desired biological functionality, a ligand exchange reaction is performed. A solution of the PAA-b-P85-b-PAA pentablock copolymer is prepared in deionized water at a concentration that ensures a stoichiometric excess relative to the surface area of the SMNPs. The copolymer is added dropwise to the vigorously stirred SMNP dispersion under an inert atmosphere (e.g., nitrogen) to prevent oxidation of the magnetite to maghemite. The mixture is then allowed to react for a period of 12-24 hours at room temperature, during which the carboxylic acid groups of the terminal PAA blocks displace the original stabilizing ligands (e.g., citrate or oleic acid) on the SMNP surface, forming a strong bidentate or bridging coordination bond with the iron atoms.

The resulting PAA-P85-SMNP complex is a sophisticated nanostructure. The PAA blocks serve as a robust anchor, ensuring the coating remains firmly attached to the particle core even under physiological conditions. The central P85 block, with its hydrophilic PEO corona and hydrophobic PPO core, extends outwards from the particle surface, creating a steric barrier that prevents aggregation and provides a "stealth" effect that can reduce opsonization and prolong circulation time in vivo. More importantly, the hydrophobic PPO segments are believed to play a crucial role in the intracellular fate of the complex. Upon internalization via endocytosis and trafficking to the acidic environment of the lysosome, the PPO blocks are thought to undergo a conformational change, becoming more hydrophobic and interacting with each other and potentially with the lysosomal membrane lipids. This self-association leads to the formation of a dense, cross-linked physical nanogel network within the lysosome, effectively trapping the SMNPs and creating a single, large, mechanically coupled unit.

The physicochemical characterization of these complexes is essential for quality control and to understand their behavior. DLS measurements consistently show a hydrodynamic diameter (Di) of 45 ± 10 nm, indicating that the primary 7-8 nm SMNPs have aggregated into small clusters of 5-20 particles, which is an optimal size for efficient cellular uptake via clathrin-mediated endocytosis. The zeta potential of -45 ± 5 mV confirms a strong negative surface charge, which contributes to the excellent colloidal stability of the dispersion in a variety of buffers, including phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and complete cell culture media, as demonstrated by the absence of significant changes in size or PDI over a 48-hour period. TGA analysis, which measures the weight loss as the sample is heated to 1000°C, reveals that the organic fraction (the polymer coating) constitutes 60 ± 3 wt% of the total complex. This high polymer content is consistent with the thick coating provided by the pentablock copolymer and is a key factor in enabling the formation of the intracellular nanogel. ICP-MS analysis of the iron content independently confirms this composition, as the calculated polymer-to-iron ratio matches the TGA data. The magnetic saturation of the complexes, measured at room temperature, is 65 A m²/kg Fe3O4, which is slightly lower than that of the bare SMNPs due to the diamagnetic contribution of the polymer shell, but remains sufficiently high for effective magnetic manipulation. This detailed characterization establishes that the PAA-P85-SMNP complex is a well-defined, stable, and functional nanomaterial that is primed for its intended biological application.

### Example 3

The in vitro cytotoxicity and quantitative uptake studies of the PAA-P85-SMNP complexes across a panel of human breast cell lines provide critical evidence for the safety and cellular processing of the invention's core component. The cell lines chosen—MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative breast cancer), BT474 (HER2+ ductal carcinoma), and MCF10A (non-tumorigenic epithelial)—represent a spectrum of phenotypes relevant to breast cancer therapy. The initial cytotoxicity assessment, performed via the MTT assay, was designed to establish a baseline of biocompatibility. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 48 hours to ensure they were in a stable, confluent state. They were then exposed to a wide range of PAA-P85-SMNP concentrations, from a very low dose of 0.005 mg/mL up to a high dose of 0.5 mg/mL, for a period of 24 hours. Following this incubation, the cells were washed with an acidic saline solution (pH 3.0), a critical step that effectively removes any nanoparticles that are merely adsorbed to the cell surface but not internalized, ensuring that the subsequent viability measurement reflects only the impact of intracellularly located particles. The cells were then returned to fresh, complete media for an additional 24 hours to allow any potential toxic effects to manifest before the MTT reagent was added. The results of this assay were unequivocal: across all three cell lines and at all concentrations tested, the cell viability remained at or above 95% of the untreated control. This demonstrates that the PAA-P85-SMNP complex itself is non-toxic and that its polymer coating is biocompatible, a prerequisite for any therapeutic agent.

The quantitative uptake study was designed to answer a pivotal question: do the cancer cells take up more nanoparticles than the healthy cells? If so, any observed selectivity in a subsequent treatment could be attributed to this differential accumulation. To address this, cells were seeded at a high density in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere for 72 hours. They were then treated with the same range of PAA-P85-SMNP concentrations (0.005–0.5 mg/mL) for either 1 hour or 24 hours. The 1-hour time point captures the initial rate of internalization, while the 24-hour time point reflects the steady-state level of accumulation. After treatment, the cells were washed three times with PBS and then with the acidic saline to remove all extracellular and surface-bound particles. The cells were then detached using trypsin-EDTA, pelleted, and the cell pellet was resuspended in deionized water. The suspension was subjected to probe sonication to ensure complete lysis of the cells and release of the internalized nanoparticles. The resulting lysate was then digested in concentrated nitric acid at 70°C for 12 hours to completely dissolve the iron oxide cores into Fe³⁺ ions. The digested solution was diluted to a known volume with deionized water and analyzed by ICP-MS to determine the total iron content. This iron content was then normalized to the total protein content of the original cell pellet, which was determined using a standard BCA assay, to yield the final metric of Fe/mg protein.

The data revealed that uptake was both time- and concentration-dependent in all three cell lines. As expected, the amount of iron per milligram of protein increased with both longer exposure time and higher nanoparticle concentration. Crucially, however, there was no statistically significant difference in the uptake efficiency between the cancerous MDA-MB-231 and BT474 cells and the non-cancerous MCF10A cells at any of the tested conditions. At the 24-hour time point and a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL, for example, all three cell lines accumulated approximately 15-18 µg of Fe per mg of protein. This finding is of profound importance because it eliminates differential uptake as a mechanism for the selective cytotoxicity that would be observed in the subsequent magnetic field experiments. It establishes that the selectivity of the invention is an intrinsic property of the treatment itself—the interaction of the internalized nanoparticles with the magnetic field and the subsequent mechanical response of the cell—and not a result of the cancer cells simply taking up more of the "drug."

### Example 4

The investigation into the intracellular distribution of the PAA-P85-SMNP complexes provides a vital link between their cellular uptake and their mechanism of action. Understanding where the nanoparticles reside inside the cell is essential for predicting how they will interact with an external magnetic field and what cellular structures they might affect. To visualize this, the PAA-P85-SMNP complexes were fluorescently labeled with Alexa Fluor®647, a bright, photostable, far-red emitting dye. The labeling was achieved through a standard EDC/NHS coupling chemistry, which activates the carboxylic acid groups on the PAA blocks of the copolymer, allowing them to form stable amide bonds with the hydrazine group on the Alexa Fluor®647 molecule. The reaction was carefully optimized to achieve a high degree of labeling without compromising the colloidal stability of the complexes. The labeled product was then purified using a combination of size-exclusion chromatography (Sephadex G-50) and centrifugal filtration to remove all traces of unreacted dye, which could otherwise produce misleading background fluorescence in the cellular assays.

Cells from the three lines (MDA-MB-231, BT474, MCF10A) were seeded onto 8-well chambered coverglass slides and allowed to adhere and grow for 72 hours. They were then incubated with a specified dose of the Alexa Fluor®647-labeled PAA-P85-SMNP complexes for 24 hours. After this incubation, the cells were thoroughly washed to remove any non-internalized particles. To identify the subcellular compartments, the cells were co-stained with two additional fluorescent probes: Lysotracker™ Green, a weakly basic dye that accumulates in and labels acidic organelles like lysosomes, and Hoechst 33342, a cell-permeable DNA stain that labels the nucleus. The cells were then imaged live using a Zeiss CLSM 710 Spectral Confocal Laser Scanning Microscope, which allows for the simultaneous acquisition of multiple fluorescence channels with high spatial resolution and minimal cross-talk.

The confocal images revealed a clear and consistent pattern of localization. At low concentrations of the SMNP complex (e.g., 0.05 or 0.1 mg/mL), the red fluorescence from the Alexa Fluor®647-labeled nanoparticles was almost entirely colocalized with the green fluorescence from the Lysotracker™, appearing as bright, punctate yellow spots in the merged images. This indicates that the vast majority of the internalized nanoparticles are sequestered within the lysosomal compartment, which is the expected destination for particles taken up via endocytosis. Quantitative colocalization analysis using the Colocalization Threshold tool in ImageJ/Fiji confirmed this, showing a Pearson’s correlation coefficient of greater than 0.8, corresponding to a colocalization percentage of approximately 80%. However, a striking change was observed at a high concentration of the SMNP complex (0.5 mg/mL). Under these conditions, while a significant amount of the nanoparticles remained in the lysosomes, a substantial fraction of the red fluorescence was now diffusely distributed throughout the cytoplasm, outside of the Lysotracker™-positive compartments. The colocalization percentage dropped dramatically to around 30%. This suggests that the lysosomal capacity for sequestering the nanoparticles is finite, and at high doses, the system becomes overloaded, leading to the escape of some particles into the cytosol. This observation is important for defining the therapeutic window, as the mechanism of action described in the invention is predicated on the nanoparticles being confined within the lysosome, where they can form a mechanically coupled unit. The high-dose, cytosolic distribution may represent a different, less efficient, or even non-selective mode of action. To complement the optical data, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on fixed and sectioned cells. The TEM images provided ultrastructural confirmation, clearly showing clusters of electron-dense SMNPs enclosed within the double-membrane structure of lysosomes, further validating the confocal microscopy findings.

### Example 5

The central and most innovative aspect of the present invention is the demonstration of selective cancer cell killing through the application of a super-low frequency AC magnetic field to cells that have internalized PAA-P85-SMNP complexes. This experiment was designed to test the core hypothesis: that the mechanical forces generated by the actuated nanoparticles can be selectively lethal to cancer cells. Cells were seeded in specialized 96-well strip plates that are compatible with the magnetic field generator and allowed to adhere for 3 days. They were then treated with PAA-P85-SMNP complexes at concentrations ranging from 0.05 to 0.5 mg/mL for 24 hours. After a thorough wash with acidic saline to remove non-internalized particles, the plates were placed into the temperature-controlled holder of the custom AC magnetic field generator, which maintained the samples at a constant 37°C. The cells were then exposed to a 50 Hz AC magnetic field at either 50 or 100 kA/m intensity, using either a continuous 30-minute exposure or a pulsed regime (10 min on, 5 min off, for a total of 30 min of field exposure).

The results were both remarkable and highly selective. In the cancerous MDA-MB-231 and BT474 cells, a significant reduction in viability was observed at the very low concentration of 0.05 mg/mL of PAA-P85-SMNP complexes, regardless of the field strength or exposure regime. This effect was not dose-dependent in the classical sense, as increasing the concentration to 0.5 mg/mL did not lead to a further decrease in viability. This plateau effect suggests that a threshold number of internalized nanoparticles is sufficient to trigger the cytotoxic mechanism, and exceeding this threshold does not amplify the effect. Most importantly, the non-cancerous MCF10A cells, which had internalized an equivalent amount of nanoparticles as shown in the quantitative uptake study, showed no decrease in viability whatsoever under any of the magnetic field conditions tested. Their viability remained at 100%, indistinguishable from the control cells that received no nanoparticles and no field exposure. This stark contrast is the hallmark of the invention's selectivity.

A second key finding was the superior efficacy of the pulsed field regime over the continuous regime. In both MDA-MB-231 and BT474 cells, the pulsed exposure caused a significantly greater reduction in cell viability than the continuous exposure of the same total "on" time. For example, in MDA-MB-231 cells treated with 0.1 mg/mL of nanoparticles, the continuous field resulted in ~100% viability, while the pulsed field reduced viability to ~50%. This suggests that the "off" periods in the pulsed regime are not merely a rest period but are functionally important, perhaps allowing for stress relaxation or the reorganization of the cytoskeleton, which makes it more vulnerable to the next cycle of force application. The difference in sensitivity between the two cancer cell lines was also notable. The BT474 cells, which grow in complex, multi-layered colonies, were more sensitive to the treatment than the MDA-MB-231 cells, which grow as a more dispersed monolayer. This may be related to the structural role of the cytoskeleton in maintaining the integrity of the colony, where damaging the basal cells can have a cascading effect on the entire structure. Control experiments confirmed that exposure to the magnetic field alone (in the absence of nanoparticles) or to the nanoparticles alone (in the absence of the field) caused no significant toxicity in any of the cell lines, proving that the cytotoxic effect is a direct result of the synergistic interaction between the two components.

### Example 6

Having established the selective cytotoxic effect, the next critical step was to elucidate the underlying mechanism of cell death. Two primary hypotheses were considered: (1) that the actuated nanoparticles generate enough local heat to kill the cells (a non-thermal hyperthermia effect), and (2) that they cause mechanical disruption of the lysosomal membrane, leading to lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP) and the release of cytotoxic enzymes. Both of these mechanisms have been proposed in the literature for other magnetic nanoparticle systems. The present invention, however, operates through a distinct and novel pathway.

The heating hypothesis was readily dismissed. Theoretical calculations based on the size of the nanoparticles (7-8 nm) and the parameters of the AC field (50 Hz, 50 kA/m) predict negligible heat generation, as the Néel and Brownian relaxation times are far too long at this low frequency to produce significant energy dissipation. This was confirmed experimentally by measuring the temperature of a PAA-P85-SMNP dispersion during field exposure, which showed no detectable increase above the ambient 37°C. Furthermore, the observed cellular effects, such as cytoskeletal disruption, are morphologically distinct from the coagulative necrosis typically seen in thermal ablation.

To definitively test the LMP hypothesis, two complementary methods were employed. First, live-cell confocal microscopy was performed on cells co-stained with Lysotracker™ Green and Alexa Fluor®647-labeled PAA-P85-SMNP complexes. If LMP were occurring, the Lysotracker™ dye would leak out of the lysosomes, causing a diffuse, dim green fluorescence throughout the cytoplasm instead of the bright, punctate pattern. No such change was observed in any of the cell lines after magnetic field exposure; the Lysotracker™ signal remained bright and punctate and perfectly colocalized with the SMNPs. Second, a more robust and quantitative assay using acridine orange was performed. Acridine orange is a metachromatic dye that fluoresces red/orange when concentrated in the acidic lysosome but shifts to green when in the neutral pH of the cytosol. Hydrogen peroxide, a known inducer of LMP, was used as a positive control and caused a dramatic loss of red/orange fluorescence and a gain of green fluorescence. In stark contrast, cells treated with PAA-P85-SMNP complexes and exposed to the pulsed AC field retained their strong, punctate red/orange acridine orange signal, identical to the untreated control cells. This conclusive evidence rules out LMP as the mechanism of death.

With heating and LMP eliminated, the focus shifted to the cytoskeleton. The hypothesis was that the SMNP-loaded lysosomes, acting as a single, large mechanical actuator, exert shear and torque forces on their anchoring points to the actin cytoskeleton. To test this, cells were treated with the full therapeutic protocol (nanoparticles + pulsed field) and then fixed and stained with ActinGreen 488 to visualize the F-actin network. The confocal images revealed a dramatic and selective disruption of the actin cytoskeleton in the cancerous MDA-MB-231 and BT474 cells. The normally dense, well-organized actin stress fibers and cortical networks were fragmented and disorganized. In sharp contrast, the actin cytoskeleton of the non-cancerous MCF10A cells remained completely intact and undisturbed. This direct visual evidence strongly supports the proposed mechanism of magneto-mechanical cytoskeletal disruption.

### Example 7

The most compelling evidence for the proposed mechanism of action—selective cytoskeletal disruption based on mechanical cell properties—comes from a set of experiments designed to artificially manipulate the mechanical stiffness of the cells. The hypothesis predicts that if the mechanical integrity of the cytoskeleton is the key determinant of susceptibility, then softening a healthy cell should make it vulnerable to the treatment, while stiffening a cancer cell should make it resistant. The former prediction was tested using cytochalasin D (CD), a fungal metabolite that caps the barbed ends of actin filaments, preventing their polymerization and thereby disrupting the actin network and reducing the cell's mechanical stiffness.

In this experiment, non-cancerous MCF10A cells were pre-treated with a sub-lethal concentration of CD (100 nM) for 1 hour. This dose was carefully chosen to be below the threshold for causing significant cell death on its own but sufficient to measurably soften the cells, as has been documented in the literature using atomic force microscopy. The CD-treated MCF10A cells were then subjected to the standard therapeutic protocol: incubation with PAA-P85-SMNP complexes (0.1 mg/mL) for 24 hours, followed by exposure to the pulsed AC magnetic field (50 Hz, 50 kA/m). The results were striking. The MCF10A cells, which were completely resistant to the treatment in their normal state, now showed a significant reduction in viability, dropping to approximately 25% after the combined treatment. Confocal microscopy of their actin cytoskeleton confirmed a level of damage comparable to that seen in the cancerous cells after the same treatment. This experiment provides a direct causal link between cytoskeletal mechanical integrity and cellular susceptibility to the magneto-mechanical therapy.

Conversely, the cancerous cells (MDA-MB-231 and BT474) were also treated with the same sub-lethal dose of CD. Interestingly, this pre-treatment appeared to sensitize them to the continuous field regime, which was previously less effective. This suggests that by further weakening their already compromised cytoskeleton, the lower magnitude of force generated by the continuous field became sufficient to cause lethal damage. This finding reinforces the idea that the force generated by the system is on the order of the mechanical strength of the cytoskeleton, and small changes in that strength can tip the balance between survival and death. Together, these experiments with cytochalasin D provide powerful, mechanistic proof that the invention works by exploiting the inherent mechanical vulnerability of the cancer cell cytoskeleton, and that this vulnerability can be a tunable parameter for therapeutic effect.

### Example 8

The final set of experiments and observations serves to integrate the various findings into a coherent and comprehensive model of the invention's mechanism of action and to highlight its potential advantages and applications. The data collectively support a model where the PAA-P85-SMNP complex is internalized by cells via endocytosis and trafficked to the lysosome. Within the acidic lysosomal environment, the hydrophobic PPO blocks of the P85 segment self-associate, forming a dense, cross-linked physical nanogel that entraps the SMNPs and anchors the entire complex to the lysosomal membrane. When exposed to a super-low frequency AC magnetic field, the magnetic moment of the SMNP cluster attempts to align with the rapidly oscillating field direction. This generates a torque on the cluster. Because the cluster is embedded in the rigid nanogel and anchored to the membrane, this torque is transmitted to the lysosome as a whole, causing it to physically oscillate or "wobble" within the cytoplasm. The lysosome is physically connected to the actin cytoskeleton via linker proteins. The repeated mechanical stress and shear forces from this oscillation are applied to these connection points. In the mechanically soft cancer cells, whose actin network is less robust and more dynamic, these forces are sufficient to cause catastrophic failure of the cytoskeleton, leading to loss of cell shape, impaired cellular functions, and ultimately, cell death. In the stiffer, more resilient non-cancerous cells, the same forces are easily absorbed and dissipated by the stronger cytoskeleton, resulting in no damage.

The superiority of the pulsed field regime can be explained by the viscoelastic nature of the cytoskeleton. During the "on" period, stress is applied and the cytoskeleton deforms. During the "off" period, the cytoskeleton can partially relax, but not fully recover. When the field is turned back on, the stress is reapplied to a structure that is already in a weakened state, leading to cumulative damage over multiple cycles. This is analogous to metal fatigue, where repeated cyclic loading causes failure at stresses far below the material's ultimate tensile strength.

The implications of this invention are significant. It provides a truly selective cancer therapy that is not based on a specific molecular marker, making it potentially applicable to a wide range of cancers, including those that are currently "undruggable." It is non-thermal, avoiding the collateral damage associated with hyperthermia. It is remotely controlled, allowing for precise spatial and temporal targeting. The use of small, biocompatible nanoparticles with a well-defined polymer coating offers favorable pharmacokinetics and safety. The system is also highly tunable; the polymer composition, nanoparticle size, and field parameters (frequency, intensity, pulse pattern) can all be optimized for different cancer types or clinical scenarios. In conclusion, the present invention represents a groundbreaking advance in the field of cancer nanomedicine, offering a powerful new tool to combat this devastating disease.
