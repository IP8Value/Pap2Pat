# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The complement system is a critical component of the innate immune response, functioning as a cascade of proteolytic enzymes that recognize and eliminate pathogens, clear immune complexes, and modulate adaptive immunity. Complement activation proceeds through three primary pathways: the classical pathway (CP), the lectin pathway (LP), and the alternative pathway (AP). While all three converge at the level of C3 cleavage, the AP is unique in its capacity for spontaneous, continuous low-level activation in the fluid phase, termed “tick-over,” and its role in amplifying complement responses on microbial surfaces or damaged host cells. Central to AP activity is the cleavage of the third complement component, C3, into C3b—a covalently reactive fragment that binds to surfaces and serves as the scaffold for the assembly of both C3 and C5 convertases. These enzymatic complexes, C3bBb and (C3b)₂Bb, respectively, drive the amplification loop of the AP and initiate the terminal pathway leading to the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), which lyses target cells.

Dysregulation of the complement system, particularly overactivation of the AP, has been implicated in a growing number of pathological conditions, including paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome, age-related macular degeneration, ischemia-reperfusion injury, autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus, and various forms of glomerulonephritis. Consequently, therapeutic inhibition of the complement cascade—especially at the level of C3 or C3b—has emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate tissue damage in these diseases. However, broad-spectrum inhibition of complement carries significant risks, including increased susceptibility to infections and impaired clearance of immune complexes. Therefore, there is a compelling need for therapeutics that selectively target pathogenic complement activation while preserving protective immune functions.

Historically, complement inhibitors have included natural regulatory proteins such as soluble complement receptor 1 (sCR1), recombinant factor H (fH), and engineered variants thereof. More recently, small molecules, peptides, and monoclonal antibodies have been developed to interfere with specific steps in the cascade. For instance, compstatin, a cyclic peptide, binds to C3 and prevents its cleavage by C3 convertase, thereby blocking all downstream complement activation. While effective, such agents inhibit both physiological and pathological complement activity, potentially compromising host defense. Other approaches have targeted upstream components of the AP, such as factor D or factor B, using monoclonal antibodies or small-molecule inhibitors. Although these strategies offer greater pathway specificity, they do not directly address the central amplification node represented by C3b.

A more refined therapeutic approach involves targeting only the activated forms of C3—namely C3b, iC3b, and C3c—while sparing the abundant circulating precursor, C3. This distinction is crucial because native C3 constitutes approximately 1.2 mg/mL in human serum, whereas its cleavage products are present at very low levels under normal conditions (less than 1% of total C3). Antibodies that bind exclusively to C3b and its derivatives would therefore exhibit favorable pharmacokinetics, reduced off-target effects, and enhanced specificity for sites of active complement deposition. Moreover, such antibodies could be engineered to selectively inhibit the AP without affecting the CP or LP, which rely on distinct molecular architectures for their convertases despite sharing C3b as a common component.

Prior efforts to generate C3b-selective antibodies have faced challenges due to the high structural similarity between C3 and C3b. The conversion of C3 to C3b involves proteolytic removal of the anaphylatoxin domain (C3a) and a dramatic conformational rearrangement, particularly in the macroglobulin (MG) domains, which exposes new epitopes. However, many of these neoepitopes are either cryptic, transient, or shared with other complement fragments, making it difficult to isolate antibodies with true selectivity. Phage display technology offers a powerful solution to this problem by enabling the selection of binders under stringent conditions, including the use of fluid-phase competitors to subtract cross-reactive clones. Using this method, it is possible to enrich for antibodies that recognize only the conformationally distinct surface of C3b, thereby achieving unprecedented specificity.

One notable example of a naturally occurring C3b-binding protein is Complement Receptor of the Immunoglobulin superfamily (CRIg), which is expressed on tissue-resident macrophages and mediates the phagocytosis of C3b-opsonized particles. Structural studies have revealed that CRIg binds to a face of C3b opposite the thioester-containing domain (TED), engaging residues primarily from the β-chain. Importantly, CRIg recognizes C3b, iC3b, and C3c but not native C3, demonstrating that selective recognition of activated complement fragments is biologically feasible. However, CRIg-based therapeutics face limitations in terms of half-life, manufacturability, and potential immunogenicity when used as a recombinant protein.

Thus, despite advances in complement biology and therapeutic development, there remains a significant unmet need for a fully human, high-affinity monoclonal antibody that selectively targets C3b and its cleavage products with exquisite specificity, inhibits the alternative pathway without compromising the classical or lectin pathways, and exhibits favorable drug-like properties for clinical use. The invention described herein addresses this need by providing a novel antibody, designated S77, that fulfills all these criteria through a unique mechanism of action rooted in structural recognition of a conformation-dependent epitope formed only after C3 activation.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel monoclonal antibody, designated S77, that specifically binds to activated cleavage products of the human complement component C3—namely C3b, iC3b, and C3c—but does not bind to native C3. This selectivity arises from the antibody’s recognition of a conformational epitope that is formed only upon the structural rearrangement of C3 during its conversion to C3b, a process that involves the release of the anaphylatoxin domain and reorientation of the macroglobulin (MG) domains. The S77 antibody is a fully human IgG1 molecule derived from a synthetic phage display library through a multi-round selection process that included counter-selection against native C3 to eliminate cross-reactive clones. Subsequent affinity maturation yielded a variant with sub-nanomolar binding affinity for C3b and its derivatives, while maintaining strict specificity for the activated forms.

The S77 antibody exerts potent inhibitory effects on the alternative pathway (AP) of complement activation by interfering with multiple critical interactions involving C3b. Specifically, S77 binds to an epitope located at the interface of the MG6 and MG7 domains of the C3b α-chain, a region that becomes accessible only after C3 activation. This binding sterically blocks the interaction of C3b with factor B (fB), thereby preventing the formation of the AP C3 convertase (C3bBb). Additionally, S77 inhibits the binding of C5 to C3b multimers, thus preventing the assembly and activity of the AP C5 convertase ((C3b)₂Bb). As a result, S77 effectively suppresses both the amplification loop and the terminal pathway of the AP. Importantly, S77 does not accelerate the decay of pre-formed C3 convertase, distinguishing its mechanism from that of natural regulators like CR1.

Furthermore, S77 inhibits the cofactor activity of complement regulators factor H (fH) and soluble complement receptor 1 (sCR1) by preventing their access to C3b, although the S77 epitope does not directly overlap with the known binding sites for these regulators. This indirect inhibition occurs through steric hindrance, as the large size of the IgG molecule occludes adjacent functional regions on C3b. Despite these broad effects on C3b interactions, S77 exhibits remarkable pathway selectivity: it potently inhibits AP-mediated complement activation in human serum but has no effect on the classical pathway (CP), even though the CP C5 convertase also contains C3b. This selectivity is attributed to the structural context of C3b within the CP convertase, where residual C5 binding may occur via the C4b subunit, rendering the complex resistant to S77 inhibition.

The crystal structure of the S77 Fab fragment in complex with C3b, solved at 3.1-Å resolution, reveals the molecular basis for its specificity and function. The antibody engages a solvent-accessible surface area of approximately 1930 Å², with the heavy chain complementarity-determining regions (CDRs)—particularly CDR-H3—making the dominant contribution. A key interaction involves Phe-898 of C3b, which inserts into a hydrophobic pocket formed by CDR-H3 and CDR-L2 of the Fab. Critically, His-897 of human C3b forms two hydrogen bonds with the antibody backbone, a feature that explains the lack of cross-reactivity with non-human primate C3b, where this residue is substituted by phenylalanine. This species-specific interaction underscores the precision of the S77-C3b interface and supports the use of humanized models for preclinical evaluation.

Therapeutically, S77 offers several advantages over existing complement inhibitors. By targeting only the activated forms of C3, which constitute less than 1% of total serum C3, S77 avoids sequestration by the vast pool of native C3, resulting in improved pharmacokinetics and reduced dosing requirements. Its exclusive inhibition of the AP preserves the CP and LP, maintaining essential immune surveillance functions. Moreover, as a fully human antibody, S77 is expected to exhibit low immunogenicity in patients. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising S77, methods of treating AP-mediated diseases using S77, and diagnostic applications based on its selective detection of C3b deposits in tissues or fluids.

In summary, the S77 antibody represents a breakthrough in complement-targeted therapy, combining high specificity, potent inhibitory activity, pathway selectivity, and favorable drug properties. Its development demonstrates the power of structure-guided antibody engineering and provides a new tool for the treatment of a wide range of inflammatory and autoimmune disorders driven by dysregulated alternative pathway activation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Definitions

As used herein, the term “C3” refers to the native, uncleaved form of the third complement component, a ~185 kDa glycoprotein composed of an α-chain and a β-chain linked by a disulfide bond. The term “C3b” denotes the major cleavage product of C3 generated by C3 convertase, consisting of the C3 α’-chain and β-chain, wherein the anaphylatoxin domain (C3a) has been removed and the thioester bond exposed, enabling covalent attachment to surfaces. “iC3b” refers to the inactivated form of C3b, produced by factor I-mediated cleavage in the presence of cofactors such as factor H or CR1, resulting in further fragmentation of the α’-chain. “C3c” is a soluble fragment derived from iC3b, containing the core of the α’-chain and the β-chain, and is typically released into the fluid phase. “Hydrolyzed C3” or “iC3” describes the spontaneously activated form of C3 that occurs in the fluid phase due to tick-over, structurally resembling C3b but lacking covalent binding capability.

The term “S77” refers to the monoclonal antibody described herein, including its full-length IgG form, Fab fragment, and any antigen-binding derivatives or variants that retain the ability to specifically bind C3b, iC3b, and C3c with high affinity and selectivity over native C3. The term “alternative pathway (AP)” denotes the complement activation cascade initiated by spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 or direct deposition of C3b on activator surfaces, leading to the formation of C3 and C5 convertases that amplify complement activity. The “classical pathway (CP)” refers to the complement cascade triggered by antigen-antibody complexes, involving C1q, C1r, C1s, C4, and C2, culminating in the formation of C4b2a (C3 convertase) and C4b2a3b (C5 convertase).

“Binding specificity” means the ability of an antibody to bind to a target antigen with significantly higher affinity than to related or unrelated molecules, as determined by assays such as ELISA, surface plasmon resonance, or competitive binding. “Inhibition” refers to the reduction or prevention of a biological or biochemical activity, such as convertase formation, cofactor activity, or substrate cleavage, as measured by standardized functional assays. “Fully human antibody” indicates an antibody whose variable and constant regions are derived entirely from human germline sequences, minimizing the risk of immunogenicity in human subjects.

### MODES OF CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The invention may be practiced in various forms, including the administration of the S77 antibody as a therapeutic agent, its use in diagnostic assays to detect complement activation, or its incorporation into research tools for studying complement biology. In therapeutic applications, S77 is formulated as a pharmaceutical composition comprising the antibody in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, such as phosphate-buffered saline, and may be administered intravenously, subcutaneously, or via other routes depending on the disease indication. The dosage regimen is tailored to achieve sufficient serum concentrations to neutralize C3b generated at sites of AP activation, typically ranging from 1 to 20 mg/kg body weight, administered weekly or biweekly.

For diagnostic purposes, S77 can be labeled with detectable moieties such as fluorophores, enzymes, or radionuclides and used in immunoassays to quantify C3b in serum, plasma, or tissue sections. Due to its inability to bind native C3, S77 provides a highly specific readout of complement activation, distinguishing it from total C3 measurements. In research settings, S77 serves as a precise tool to dissect the role of C3b in cellular and molecular processes, including phagocytosis, inflammation, and immune complex clearance.

The antibody may also be engineered to enhance its properties, such as extending half-life through Fc modifications (e.g., YTE mutation), reducing effector function by mutating the Fc region to minimize FcγR binding, or converting it to a bispecific format to engage additional targets. However, the core antigen-binding specificity for the C3b MG6-MG7 interface must be preserved in all variants to maintain the functional profile described herein.

### Example 1

The generation of the S77 antibody began with the screening of a human synthetic phage antibody library displaying bivalent Fab fragments on M13 phage. The library was constructed using oligonucleotide-directed mutagenesis to introduce diversity in the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the heavy chain, while maintaining a fixed human IgG1 framework. To isolate clones that bind C3b but not native C3, a subtractive panning strategy was employed. Nunc 96-well plates were coated with purified human C3b at 10 μg/mL and blocked with bovine serum albumin. The phage library was pre-incubated with a 500 nM concentration of soluble human C3 to absorb and remove phage displaying Fabs that cross-react with native C3. The pre-cleared phage mixture was then added to the C3b-coated plates and allowed to bind for 2–3 hours at room temperature. After extensive washing with phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20, bound phage were eluted with 50 mM HCl and 500 mM NaCl, neutralized with Tris base, and amplified in E. coli XL-1 Blue cells. This cycle was repeated for four rounds with increasing stringency in wash conditions to enrich for high-affinity, C3b-selective binders.

Following the final round, individual clones were screened by ELISA for binding to C3b and lack of binding to C3. One clone, designated YW144.2.45, exhibited strong C3b binding and negligible C3 reactivity. The variable heavy (VH) and light (VL) chain genes of this clone were PCR-amplified and cloned into mammalian expression vectors LPG3 and LPG4, respectively, which encode the human IgG1 constant regions. The resulting full-length IgG was transiently expressed in HEK293F cells and purified using protein A affinity chromatography. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis revealed a dissociation constant (K_D) of 160 nM for C3b, confirming moderate affinity.

To improve binding kinetics, affinity maturation was performed by constructing three separate combinatorial libraries targeting CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3 of both the heavy and light chains. Selected positions within these CDRs were randomized using degenerate codons, and the libraries were subjected to six additional rounds of phage panning under high-stringency conditions, including shorter incubation times and inclusion of excess C3 in the binding step. From eight reformatted IgG candidates, one variant, YW144.2.45.S77 (hereafter S77), demonstrated a 10-fold improvement in affinity, with a K_D of approximately 16 nM for C3b by SPR. Sequence analysis revealed seven amino acid substitutions in the heavy chain CDRs and two in CDR-L3 of the light chain. ELISA confirmed that S77 retained strict selectivity for C3b over C3, with only minimal binding to C3 at very high antibody concentrations, likely due to trace amounts of hydrolyzed C3 in the preparation. Further SPR studies using the Fab fragment of S77 showed high-affinity binding (1.2–2.2 nM) to C3b, iC3, iC3b, and C3c, but no detectable binding to C3dg, establishing its specificity for the larger C3 cleavage products.

### CRIg Variants

While the present invention focuses on the S77 antibody, it is instructive to compare its properties with those of CRIg, a naturally occurring C3b receptor. CRIg, expressed on Kupffer cells and other tissue macrophages, binds C3b, iC3b, and C3c via an immunoglobulin-like domain and mediates phagocytosis of opsonized particles. Recombinant soluble CRIg (sCRIg) has been explored as a therapeutic inhibitor of the AP. However, sCRIg differs from S77 in several key aspects. First, CRIg binds primarily to the β-chain of C3b, whereas S77 engages the α-chain, specifically the MG6 and MG7 domains. Second, structural studies show that CRIg and S77 bind to overlapping but non-identical regions on the same face of C3b—the side opposite the TED domain—suggesting convergent evolution toward a functional hotspot for convertase regulation. Third, while both inhibit AP convertase activity, CRIg has been reported to decay pre-formed C3 convertase, whereas S77 does not, indicating distinct mechanisms of action. Finally, as a recombinant protein, CRIg may face challenges in manufacturing, stability, and half-life compared to a monoclonal antibody like S77. Thus, S77 represents a superior therapeutic candidate with enhanced drug-like properties and a well-defined mechanism of selective inhibition.

### Example 3

The crystal structure of the S77 Fab fragment in complex with C3b was determined to elucidate the molecular basis of its specificity. Crystals were grown by hanging-drop vapor diffusion at 19°C using a 1:1 mixture of protein solution (10 mg/mL C3b-S77 complex in 50 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris, pH 7.8) and mother liquor (10% PEG 4000, 0.2 M MgCl₂, 100 mM Na-HEPES, pH 7.0). The crystals belonged to space group C2 with unit cell parameters a = 216.4 Å, b = 180.4 Å, c = 154.6 Å, β = 115.73°, and diffracted to 3.1-Å resolution. The structure was solved by molecular replacement using the C3b coordinates from the C3b-CRIg complex (PDB 2ICF) as a search model. Refinement yielded a model with R_cryst of 21.7% and R_free of 28.3%.

The asymmetric unit contained two C3b-S77 complexes, which superimposed with minimal deviation, confirming the robustness of the binding interface. The Fab buried approximately 1930 Å² of solvent-accessible surface on C3b, with the majority of contacts mediated by the heavy chain CDRs: CDR-H1 (210 Å²), CDR-H2 (180 Å²), and CDR-H3 (360 Å²). Light chain CDRs contributed an additional 150 Å². Key interactions included hydrophobic packing of C3b Phe-898 into a pocket formed by CDR-H3 and CDR-L2, and hydrogen bonding between C3b His-897 and the backbone atoms of Ser-31 (CDR-H1) and Leu-97 (CDR-H3). Comparison with the structure of native C3 revealed that the MG6 and MG7 domains undergo a ~15° rotation relative to each other upon C3 activation, creating the composite epitope recognized by S77. In native C3, this rotation does not occur, and modeling showed that S77 would sterically clash with the MG6 domain, explaining the lack of binding. This conformational selectivity is analogous to that of CRIg but achieved through a distinct structural mechanism.

### Example 4

Functional assays demonstrated that S77 inhibits multiple steps in the AP cascade. In ELISA-based binding assays, S77 blocked the interaction of factor B with C3b in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC₅₀ of approximately 50 nM. Consequently, S77 prevented the formation of the C3 convertase (C3bBb) in both fluid-phase and solid-phase assays. However, when added to pre-formed C3 convertase, S77 did not accelerate its decay, unlike sCR1, which rapidly dissociates the complex. This indicates that S77 acts prophylactically by preventing convertase assembly rather than therapeutically by dismantling existing complexes.

S77 also inhibited the binding of C5 to C3b-coated surfaces, with an IC₅₀ of ~100 nM, thereby preventing C5 cleavage and MAC formation. In a zymosan-based C5 convertase assay, S77 reduced C5a generation by over 90% at 1 μM concentration. Furthermore, S77 interfered with the cofactor activity of both factor H and sCR1 in factor I-mediated cleavage of C3b to iC3b, as shown by reduced iC3b formation on SDS-PAGE. Although the S77 epitope does not overlap with the known fH/CR1 binding site (residues 727–768 of the α’NT), steric occlusion by the large IgG molecule likely prevents regulator access.

Critically, S77 selectively inhibited the AP but not the CP. In hemolytic assays using C1q-depleted serum (to isolate AP activity), S77 reduced lysis of rabbit erythrocytes by >80%. In contrast, in fB-depleted serum reconstituted to assess CP activity, S77 had no effect on lysis of antibody-sensitized sheep erythrocytes. This pathway selectivity is therapeutically advantageous, as it preserves CP-mediated immune functions such as pathogen opsonization and immune complex clearance.

### Example 5

Species cross-reactivity studies revealed that S77 is highly specific for human C3b and does not inhibit complement activation in serum from baboon, cynomolgus, or rhesus monkeys. Sequencing of rhesus C3b identified a single amino acid difference within the S77 epitope: His-897 in human C3b is replaced by Phe in rhesus. Structural modeling showed that this substitution abolishes the two hydrogen bonds formed between His-897 and the S77 Fab backbone, and introduces a hydrophobic residue into a polar environment, destabilizing the interface. This finding explains the lack of cross-reactivity and underscores the precision of the S77-C3b interaction. It also implies that preclinical efficacy studies must be conducted in humanized models or non-human primates engineered to express human C3b. Nevertheless, this species specificity enhances the safety profile of S77, as it minimizes off-target effects in non-human systems and supports the development of a truly human-selective therapeutic.
