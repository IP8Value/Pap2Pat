# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Decision support systems have become integral to a wide array of industries, including finance, healthcare, telecommunications, and retail, where predictive analytics are used to inform strategic decisions. These systems typically rely on machine learning models trained on historical data to classify or predict outcomes based on input features. Common applications include credit risk assessment, customer churn prediction, fraud detection, and hospital readmission forecasting. In each of these domains, the goal is to develop models that not only achieve high predictive accuracy but also maintain stable performance over time while utilizing as few features as possible—thereby enhancing interpretability, reducing computational overhead, and minimizing data collection costs. The capacity of a model to capture complex, non-linear relationships among features often correlates strongly with its generalization ability and robustness across diverse datasets.

In classical machine learning, ensemble methods such as boosting and bagging have emerged as powerful techniques for improving predictive performance by combining multiple base learners. Among these, boosting algorithms like AdaBoost iteratively train weak classifiers—typically simple decision stumps—on reweighted versions of the training data, placing greater emphasis on misclassified instances in subsequent rounds. The final prediction is derived from a weighted majority vote of all constituent models. This approach has proven highly effective in practice, particularly when applied to tree-based models such as those implemented in XGBoost or LightGBM. However, despite their success, classical ensembles are fundamentally constrained by the representational power of their underlying learners and the feature spaces they operate within.

Quantum machine learning (QML) offers a promising avenue for transcending some of these limitations by leveraging the principles of quantum mechanics—such as superposition, entanglement, and interference—to construct models capable of exploring exponentially larger hypothesis spaces than their classical counterparts. One prominent class of QML models is the Quantum Support Vector Machine (QSVM), which maps classical data into a high-dimensional quantum Hilbert space via a parameterized quantum circuit known as a *feature map*. Once encoded, kernel evaluations between data points are performed through measurements of quantum state overlaps, enabling classification using standard support vector machine frameworks adapted to quantum kernels. The theoretical advantage of QSVM lies in its ability to induce decision boundaries that are inaccessible to classical kernels due to the unique geometric structure of quantum state space.

Early experimental implementations of QSVM, such as the work by Havlíček et al., demonstrated feasibility on superconducting quantum processors by encoding data into multi-qubit states and evaluating quantum kernels through repeated circuit executions. Subsequent studies explored variations in feature map design, regularization strategies, and hardware-aware optimizations to improve empirical performance. For instance, Park et al. introduced parameterized shallow unitary transformations with rotational gates and explicit regularization terms, reporting modest gains over classical SVMs on small-scale benchmarks. Wu et al. conducted comparative evaluations across multiple quantum computing platforms—including IBM Quantum, Google’s TensorFlow Quantum, and Amazon Braket—and found that QSVMs generally matched but did not consistently surpass the performance of classical baselines like SVM and XGBoost on real-world datasets. Meanwhile, Glick et al. investigated symmetry-induced quantum kernels for problems exhibiting group-theoretic structures, suggesting potential for quantum advantage under specific data conditions.

Despite these advances, practical demonstrations of quantum advantage in supervised learning remain elusive. A key challenge lies in the sensitivity of QML models to noise, limited qubit coherence times, and the difficulty of optimizing variational circuits—a problem exacerbated by the so-called "barren plateaus" in the loss landscape. Moreover, most existing QML architectures require significant domain expertise in quantum physics for effective deployment, limiting accessibility for data scientists without specialized training. Consequently, there is a pressing need not only to enhance the predictive capabilities of quantum models but also to automate their design and tuning processes to facilitate broader adoption.

Prior attempts to integrate boosting concepts into quantum settings have been largely conceptual or restricted to specific hardware paradigms. Neven et al. proposed an adiabatic quantum boosting framework using D-Wave annealers, employing single-level decision trees as weak classifiers within an Ising-model formulation. Other works by Schuld et al. and Abbas et al. examined quantum ensembles primarily from the perspective of computational speedup, focusing on parallel evaluation of multiple quantum classifiers rather than performance enhancement through architectural diversity. These approaches, while theoretically intriguing, do not directly address the core objective of improving generalization accuracy through adaptive model composition—a hallmark of successful classical boosting.

Crucially, the independence of different QSVMs—arising from distinct feature map choices—presents a unique opportunity for ensemble construction. Unlike classical models where diversity must be artificially induced (e.g., through random subsampling or stochastic depth), quantum models naturally yield heterogeneous decision functions when initialized with different unitary encodings. This intrinsic variability suggests that an ensemble of QSVMs, each employing a unique feature map, could collectively explore a richer set of decision boundaries than any individual model. However, realizing this potential requires an automated mechanism to select optimal feature maps at each boosting iteration while avoiding redundancy and ensuring progressive exploration of the model space.

Furthermore, current QML workflows often rely on manually curated feature maps inspired by physical models such as the transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian. While physically motivated, these constructions may impose unnecessary constraints or overlook more effective encodings tailored to specific data distributions. An automated search over a library of candidate feature maps—combined with hyperparameter optimization for kernel parameters and regularization—could uncover novel architectures that outperform conventional designs without requiring user intervention.

In summary, while quantum machine learning holds theoretical promise for enhanced modeling capacity, its practical utility in real-world decision support systems remains limited by performance parity with classical methods, lack of automation, and insufficient integration with established ensemble learning principles. The present invention addresses these gaps by introducing a novel boosting framework specifically designed for quantum support vector machines, featuring automated model selection, dynamic exclusion of previously used feature maps, and rigorous validation protocols to ensure consistent performance gains across diverse datasets.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel ensemble learning method for quantum support vector machines (QSVMs) that significantly enhances predictive performance on binary classification tasks by systematically combining multiple quantum classifiers with diverse feature encodings. Unlike conventional boosting approaches that rely on weak learners such as decision stumps, the disclosed method employs full-capacity QSVMs as base estimators and introduces a structured search mechanism to automatically select the optimal quantum feature map, rotation scaling factor, and regularization parameter at each boosting iteration. This innovation enables the ensemble to progressively explore distinct regions of the quantum Hilbert space, thereby constructing more complex and accurate decision boundaries than achievable by any single QSVM.

Central to the invention is an iterative boosting algorithm that begins with uniformly weighted training samples and, at each step, performs a comprehensive grid search over a predefined library of Pauli-based feature maps—each corresponding to a unique unitary transformation for data encoding—alongside tunable hyperparameters including the Pauli rotation multiplier (α ∈ (0, 2]) and the SVM regularization constant (C ∈ [1, 100]). The best-performing model on a held-out validation set is selected, and its associated feature map is permanently excluded from future iterations to enforce architectural diversity and prevent convergence to redundant solutions. Classifier weights are then computed using the standard AdaBoost formula αₘ = log((1 − errₘ)/errₘ), where errₘ denotes the weighted training error of the m-th estimator, and sample weights are updated to emphasize previously misclassified instances.

The algorithm incorporates robust early stopping criteria to ensure computational efficiency and numerical stability: termination occurs if (1) the current estimator achieves perfect classification on the training set (error ≤ 0), (2) the estimator performs no better than random guessing (error ≥ 0.5 for binary classification), or (3) a maximum number of estimators is reached. The final ensemble aggregates predictions via a weighted majority vote, with weights proportional to each classifier’s confidence as measured by its αₘ value. Notably, the method is not restricted to classification; it can be extended to regression tasks by adapting the loss function and prediction aggregation scheme accordingly.

Empirical validation across synthetic datasets—including XOR, moons, and circles patterns generated via scikit-learn—demonstrates that the boosted QSVM consistently outperforms both single QSVMs and classical baselines such as SVM with RBF/linear kernels and XGBoost. On the circles dataset, the ensemble achieves median test accuracy of 100%, surpassing all comparators. For more challenging topologies like moons, where individual QSVMs struggle, the ensemble yields an average accuracy improvement of 7.5% over single models, with mean ensemble sizes reaching 3.8 classifiers—indicating active utilization of multiple diverse learners. Even on XOR, a problem inherently suited to linear separation in transformed spaces, a 4.2% gain is observed, underscoring the method’s adaptability.

Critically, the invention automates the entire model selection and training pipeline, eliminating the need for manual feature map design or physics-informed ansatz construction. By decoupling model architecture from domain-specific quantum knowledge, the system becomes accessible to general data scientists while simultaneously enabling discovery of non-intuitive, high-performing quantum encodings. The exclusion mechanism ensures that each boosting round contributes meaningfully to the ensemble’s representational capacity, avoiding stagnation and promoting exploration of the quantum kernel landscape. Furthermore, the use of validation-based hyperparameter tuning at every iteration guarantees that each added classifier is optimized for generalization, not just training fit.

This approach represents a fundamental departure from prior art in quantum ensemble learning, which either focused on computational speedup through parallelism or employed weak quantum classifiers in restricted hardware settings. Instead, the present invention prioritizes predictive accuracy through intelligent composition of strong, diverse quantum learners, grounded in proven classical boosting principles yet uniquely adapted to the structural properties of quantum models. The result is a scalable, automated, and high-performance quantum machine learning framework suitable for deployment in industrial decision support systems seeking a competitive edge through next-generation AI technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a computer-implemented method and system for constructing an ensemble of quantum support vector machines (QSVMs) through an adaptive boosting procedure that dynamically selects and combines diverse quantum feature maps to enhance classification accuracy. The method operates on classical input data and leverages near-term quantum processors or simulators to evaluate quantum kernels, thereby integrating quantum computational advantages into a robust, automated machine learning pipeline. Below, the invention is described in detail with reference to its components, operational workflow, mathematical foundations, and implementation considerations.

The method begins with the preparation of classical input data comprising a set of N labeled examples {(x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), ..., (x_N, y_N)}, where each x_i ∈ ℝ^d is a d-dimensional feature vector and y_i ∈ {−1, +1} denotes the corresponding binary class label. Following standard machine learning practice, the dataset is partitioned into three disjoint subsets: a training set used for iterative model fitting, a validation set employed for hyperparameter selection during each boosting round, and a test set reserved exclusively for final performance evaluation. In exemplary embodiments, equal-sized splits (e.g., 50 observations per subset for a total of 150) are used, though alternative ratios may be adopted depending on data availability and application requirements.

Data encoding into the quantum domain is achieved through a feature map Φ(x): ℝ^d → ℋ, which embeds each classical input x into a quantum state |Φ(x)⟩ residing in a 2ⁿ-dimensional Hilbert space ℋ, where n denotes the number of qubits. In the preferred embodiment, the feature map is constructed as a parameterized quantum circuit composed of single-qubit Hadamard gates followed by Pauli rotation gates, specifically:

|Φ(x)⟩ = U_Φ(x)|0⟩^⊗n,

where U_Φ(x) = ∏_{k=1}^{K} exp(−i α P_k(x) θ_k),

with P_k(x) representing a tensor product of Pauli operators (I, X, Y, Z) acting on subsets of qubits, θ_k denoting data-dependent rotation angles derived from input features, and α ∈ (0, 2] serving as a global scaling factor for rotation magnitudes. For two-dimensional input data (d = 2), a library of candidate feature maps is pre-defined based on combinations of Pauli strings, such as ZZ, XX, YY, XY, etc., each inducing a distinct nonlinear transformation of the input space. This library constitutes the search space for model selection during boosting.

The core of the invention is an iterative boosting algorithm, herein referred to as Boosted QSVM, which proceeds as follows. Initially, all training samples are assigned uniform weights w_i^(1) = 1/N. At iteration m (starting from m = 1), the algorithm performs a grid search over the joint space of (1) available feature maps (excluding those already selected in prior iterations), (2) α values sampled from (0, 2], and (3) regularization parameters C ∈ [1, 100]. For each candidate configuration, a QSVM is trained by first encoding the training data into quantum states using the specified feature map, computing the quantum kernel matrix K_ij = |⟨Φ(x_i)|Φ(x_j)⟩|² via quantum circuit sampling or simulation, and then solving the dual-form SVM optimization problem:

min_{α} (1/2) ∑_{i,j} α_i α_j y_i y_j K_ij − ∑_i α_i,

subject to 0 ≤ α_i ≤ C and ∑_i α_i y_i = 0.

The resulting classifier f_m(x) = sign(∑_i α_i y_i k(x_i, x) + b) is evaluated on the validation set, and the configuration yielding the highest validation accuracy is selected as the m-th estimator. This model’s feature map is then removed from the candidate pool to ensure architectural diversity in subsequent rounds.

Following model selection, the algorithm checks for early termination conditions. If the training error err_m = ∑_{i: f_m(x_i) ≠ y_i} w_i^(m) is zero (perfect classification), the loop terminates as further boosting is unnecessary. Conversely, if err_m ≥ 0.5—indicating performance no better than random guessing—the estimator is discarded and boosting halts to prevent degradation. Additionally, a user-defined maximum number of estimators M_max (e.g., M_max = 10) serves as a hard stop to control computational cost.

If none of the stopping criteria are met, the estimator weight α_m is computed as α_m = log((1 − err_m)/err_m), reflecting the classifier’s confidence. Sample weights are then updated according to:

w_i^(m+1) = w_i^(m) × exp(−α_m y_i f_m(x_i)),

and normalized to sum to one. This reweighting increases the influence of misclassified samples in the next iteration, guiding the ensemble to focus on difficult regions of the input space.

After termination, the final ensemble prediction for a new input x is given by:

F(x) = sign(∑_{m=1}^M α_m f_m(x)),

where M denotes the actual number of selected estimators (M ≤ M_max). This weighted majority vote aggregates the decisions of all constituent QSVMs, with higher-confidence models contributing more strongly to the outcome.

The invention’s novelty lies in several key mechanisms. First, unlike classical boosting that assumes weak learners, the method uses full-strength QSVMs whose expressive power varies with feature map choice. Second, the dynamic exclusion of used feature maps enforces exploration of orthogonal decision boundaries, mitigating redundancy and promoting coverage of the hypothesis space. Third, per-iteration hyperparameter tuning on a validation set ensures that each added model is optimized for generalization, not overfitting. Fourth, the entire process is fully automated, requiring no manual intervention in feature map design—thus democratizing access to quantum-enhanced modeling.

Implementation may occur on quantum hardware (e.g., IBM Quantum System One with superconducting qubits) or classical simulators for development and benchmarking. Kernel evaluations involve executing the feature map circuit for pairs of inputs and estimating the fidelity |⟨Φ(x_i)|Φ(x_j)⟩|² through repeated measurements; error mitigation techniques may be applied to counteract noise in physical devices. Classical components—including weight updates, grid search orchestration, and SVM optimization—are executed on conventional CPUs or GPUs using libraries such as scikit-learn.

Experimental results validate the efficacy of the approach. On synthetic datasets (XOR, moons, circles), Boosted QSVM achieves statistically significant improvements over single QSVMs: 4.2% and 7.5% average accuracy gains on XOR and moons, respectively, with ensemble sizes adapting to problem difficulty (mean M = 1.1 for circles, 3.8 for moons). On circles, median test accuracy reaches 100%, exceeding classical SVM and XGBoost. These findings confirm that the ensemble actively leverages architectural diversity to overcome limitations of individual quantum models.

The method is extensible to multiclass classification via one-vs-rest or error-correcting output codes, and to regression by replacing the classification loss with squared error and using weighted averaging instead of voting. Hyperparameter grids may be expanded to include deeper circuits, alternative encoding strategies (e.g., amplitude or angle embedding), or adaptive α schedules. Future enhancements could incorporate Bayesian optimization for efficient search or integrate with quantum neural networks for end-to-end training.

In conclusion, the disclosed invention provides a principled, automated, and high-performance framework for quantum ensemble learning, bridging the gap between theoretical quantum advantage and practical machine learning deployment. By synergistically combining boosting theory with quantum kernel methods, it unlocks new pathways for building accurate, stable, and interpretable predictive models in the era of noisy intermediate-scale quantum computing.
