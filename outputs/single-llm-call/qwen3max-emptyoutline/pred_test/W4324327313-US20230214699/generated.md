# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### 1. Technical Field

The present invention relates generally to the field of quantum computing and, more specifically, to methods and systems for the efficient quantum simulation of stochastic processes. The invention provides a novel approach to representing and simulating trajectories of stochastic processes, such as Brownian motion and fractional Brownian motion, on a quantum computer. This approach, termed “analog simulation,” leverages the spectral properties of these processes and the quantum Fourier transform to achieve a significant reduction in computational resources—specifically, in circuit depth and qubit count—compared to conventional “digital” simulation methods that directly quantize classical sampling algorithms. The resulting efficient quantum simulation is then integrated with quantum amplitude estimation techniques to create end-to-end quantum algorithms for estimating specific properties of stochastic processes, such as time-averaged quantities, which are relevant in applications including financial derivative pricing and the statistical analysis of anomalous diffusion in physical systems. The invention thus resides at the intersection of quantum information science, computational mathematics, and applied probability theory.

### 2. Description of Related Art

Stochastic processes are fundamental mathematical models used to describe the evolution of random phenomena over time across a wide range of disciplines, including physics, engineering, biology, and finance. A central task in the application of these models is the estimation of expected values of functions of the process, often referred to as Monte Carlo integration. For example, in computational finance, the price of a complex derivative security is typically the expected value of its future payoff under a risk-neutral measure, which is a function of the underlying asset’s stochastic price path. In physics, one might be interested in the expected mean-squared displacement of a particle undergoing anomalous diffusion. Classically, such expectations are estimated using Monte Carlo methods, which involve generating a large number of independent sample paths of the stochastic process and averaging the function of interest over these samples. The precision of this estimate scales as the inverse square root of the number of samples, meaning that to achieve an additive error of ε, a classical Monte Carlo method requires Θ(1/ε²) samples.

Quantum computing offers a potential paradigm shift for this problem through the use of quantum amplitude estimation (QAE) and its variants. These quantum algorithms can, in principle, estimate the expectation value of a random variable with a precision that scales as the inverse of the number of quantum queries, i.e., Θ(1/ε), thereby providing a quadratic speedup over the classical Monte Carlo rate. This speedup has generated significant interest, particularly in the context of financial applications like option pricing. However, to leverage this quantum advantage, a critical prerequisite is the ability to coherently simulate the underlying stochastic process on a quantum computer. This means preparing a quantum state that encodes the probability distribution of the entire trajectory of the process in a way that is compatible with the unitary operations required by QAE.

The standard approach to this quantum simulation task, which we refer to as digital simulation, involves taking a classical algorithm that can sample a trajectory of the process and compiling it into a reversible quantum circuit. This is done by replacing the classical random bits used in the sampling algorithm with quantum superposition states (e.g., |+⟩ states) and implementing the classical logic with quantum gates, typically Toffoli gates. The output of this quantum circuit is a state where the computational basis of a set of registers directly encodes a specific trajectory (v₁, v₂, ..., v_T), and the amplitude of that basis state is proportional to the square root of the probability of that trajectory. While this approach is straightforward and always possible in principle, it suffers from two major practical drawbacks that threaten its viability for achieving a real-world quantum advantage. First, the process of compiling a classical sampling algorithm into a fault-tolerant quantum circuit can result in a prohibitively large number of quantum gates, especially for complex stochastic processes. Second, and perhaps more critically, the quantum amplitude estimation algorithm requires this simulation circuit to be executed in a coherent sequence a number of times proportional to 1/ε. This means the total circuit depth—the primary determinant of runtime on a fault-tolerant quantum computer due to the overhead of quantum error correction—becomes the product of the depth of the single simulation circuit and the number of repetitions, O(depth_of_simulation / ε). Recent analyses have shown that for many practical problems, such as the pricing of certain financial derivatives, this total depth may be so large that the quadratic speedup in sample complexity is completely negated by the immense clock-speed overhead of quantum error correction, rendering the quantum approach impractical even on future hardware.

To address this challenge, researchers have sought more efficient ways to perform the quantum simulation step. One known technique uses quantum walks to accelerate the simulation of certain Markovian processes, which can lead to a speedup in the mixing time of the underlying random walk. Another line of work focuses on the efficient loading of specific, simple probability distributions, such as the Gaussian distribution, into a quantum register. However, these methods are either limited to a narrow class of processes or do not provide a general framework for simulating the full trajectory of a complex, correlated stochastic process.

A key insight is that any quantum speedup in the simulation of the stochastic process itself will compound with the quadratic speedup from amplitude estimation, potentially leading to a more robust and practical end-to-end quantum advantage. Therefore, the central problem in the field has become the development of highly gate-efficient, low-depth quantum circuits for simulating the trajectories of important stochastic processes. The related art lacks a general method for achieving this, particularly for non-Markovian processes with long-range correlations, such as fractional Brownian motion, which are of significant interest in modern finance and physics. The present invention directly addresses this gap by introducing a fundamentally new representation and simulation paradigm—the analog simulation—which exploits the inherent spectral structure of a broad class of stochastic processes to achieve a simulation complexity that is polylogarithmic in the number of time steps, T, thereby offering a path to a practical quantum advantage.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system and method for the efficient quantum simulation of a class of stochastic processes, including Brownian motion (BM) and fractional Brownian motion (fBM), and their subsequent use in quantum Monte Carlo algorithms for estimating specific properties of these processes. The core innovation is the introduction of a novel “analog representation” of a stochastic process trajectory, which stands in contrast to the conventional “digital representation.” In the digital representation, the entire trajectory is stored in the computational basis of a quantum register, requiring a number of qubits linear in the number of time steps, T. In the analog representation, the value of the process at a given time is encoded in the amplitude of a quantum state, while the time index is stored in the computational basis. This representation leverages the exponential nature of Hilbert space, allowing a T-step trajectory to be represented with only O(log T) qubits.

The invention describes a method for producing an approximate coherent analog representation of a stochastic process. This coherent representation is a superposition over all possible trajectories, where each trajectory is encoded in the analog form and is entangled with a garbage register that holds the randomness used to generate it. This coherence is essential for compatibility with quantum amplitude estimation. The method for generating this representation is based on a quantum spectral algorithm. It exploits the well-known fact that BM and fBM admit a Fourier series representation (the Wiener series) where the frequency components are independent Gaussian random variables with variances that decay according to a specific power law determined by the Hurst parameter H.

The quantum algorithm proceeds in two main stages. First, it prepares a quantum state in the Fourier (frequency) domain whose amplitudes are distributed as independent Gaussians with the correct, diminishing variances. This is achieved through a novel “data loading the data loader” technique, which recursively applies efficient quantum data loading circuits to prepare the necessary distribution of rotation angles. Second, it applies the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) to this state, which efficiently maps the frequency-domain representation to the time-domain analog representation of the process trajectory. The QFT is implemented in a circuit of depth polylogarithmic in T, and the state preparation in the Fourier domain is shown to require resources that are polynomial in the inverse of the desired precision and polylogarithmic in T.

The invention further provides a method for using this efficient analog simulation within an end-to-end quantum algorithm to estimate specific properties of the stochastic process. Because the analog representation stores the process values in amplitudes, it is not straightforward to extract arbitrary functions of the trajectory. However, the invention identifies that certain time-averaged quantities, which are inner products between the process trajectory and a known test function, can be efficiently extracted as amplitudes of the final quantum state. By combining the analog simulation with quantum amplitude estimation, the invention provides a method to estimate these quantities with a total computational cost of O(polylog(T) / ε^c), where the exponent c is a constant between 3/2 and 2 that depends on the Hurst parameter of the fBM. This represents a significant improvement over the classical Monte Carlo cost of O(T / ε²) and is incomparable to, but in many regimes superior to, the standard black-box quantum Monte Carlo cost of O(T / ε).

Two concrete applications of this general framework are detailed. The first is a method for pricing a variance swap, a financial derivative whose payoff is a function of the realized variance of an underlying asset. The method assumes the asset's volatility follows an fBM and provides a quantum algorithm that is exponentially faster in T than classical methods. The second is a method for performing a statistical test to distinguish between different diffusive regimes of a particle, such as normal diffusion (BM) versus anomalous diffusion (fBM with H ≠ 1/2). The method uses the analog simulation to efficiently compute the time-averaged mean-squared displacement, a key statistic for this test. The invention also discusses generalizations of the core method to other classes of stochastic processes, such as Lévy processes, and outlines the potential for achieving genuine white-box quantum speedups by applying the method to post-selected subsets of trajectories for which no closed-form analytical solutions exist.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Example Method

The present invention is now described in detail with reference to a specific, exemplary method for the quantum simulation of fractional Brownian motion (fBM) and its application to quantum Monte Carlo estimation. The method is structured around the core concept of the analog representation and the quantum spectral algorithm. The input to the method is a description of the fBM, which includes its drift (which may be set to zero without loss of generality for the purposes of this simulation), its variance, and its Hurst parameter H ∈ (0, 1]. The Hurst parameter is a critical characteristic that governs the nature of the correlations between the increments of the process: for H = 1/2, the process is standard Brownian motion with independent increments; for H > 1/2, the process exhibits long-range positive correlations (persistence); and for H < 1/2, it exhibits long-range negative correlations (anti-persistence). The method also takes as input the desired number of time steps, T, and the target precision, ε, for the simulation.

The first step of the method is to leverage the spectral, or Fourier series, representation of fBM. It is a classical result that a sample path of fBM on the interval [0, 1] can be expressed as a stochastic Fourier series. Specifically, the value of the process at time t, denoted B_H(t), can be written as a sum over sine and cosine terms, where the coefficients of these terms are independent Gaussian random variables. The variance of the k-th coefficient in this series is not constant but decays as a power law, specifically as k^(-2H-1). This spectral decomposition is the foundation of classical spectral methods for simulating fBM, which involve drawing a finite number of these Gaussian coefficients, applying an inverse discrete Fourier transform (via the Fast Fourier Transform, or FFT), and obtaining a discretized sample path. The classical FFT has a time complexity of O(T log T).

The quantum method of the present invention begins by truncating this infinite series to a finite number of terms, L. The choice of L is a crucial parameter that balances accuracy and resource cost. The truncation error, which is the difference between the true fBM path and its truncated series approximation, can be rigorously bounded. It is shown that to achieve an ε-approximate simulation in the L² norm, it suffices to choose L = O(ε^(-1/(2H))). This is a key result, as it demonstrates that for processes with a higher Hurst parameter (H closer to 1), far fewer terms are needed to achieve a given precision, making the simulation more efficient. For standard Brownian motion (H=1/2), this simplifies to L = O(1/ε).

The next step is to prepare a quantum state that encodes these truncated Fourier coefficients. The goal is to prepare a state of the form |ψ_fourier⟩ = (1/Z) ∑_{k=1}^L (a_k / k^(H+1/2)) |k⟩, where a_k are independent N(0,1) Gaussian random variables, and Z is a normalization constant. Preparing a state with amplitudes drawn from a specific, non-uniform distribution is a non-trivial task in quantum computing. The invention solves this problem through a sophisticated recursive procedure. The method utilizes a known class of logarithmic-depth quantum circuits called “data loaders.” A data loader is a parametrized circuit that, given a set of classical rotation angles, can prepare a quantum state whose amplitudes correspond to a given vector. The key insight is that if the desired output state is a random vector (in this case, a Gaussian vector with decaying variances), then the input angles to the data loader are themselves random variables drawn from a specific joint distribution.

A direct approach of trying to load this joint distribution of angles would be infeasible due to the high correlations between the angles. The invention overcomes this by a two-stage process. First, it is observed that preparing a state with i.i.d. Gaussian amplitudes (i.e., all variances equal) is much simpler. For this special case, the induced distribution on the data loader angles is a product distribution—they are independent. This is a consequence of the rotational symmetry of the high-dimensional Gaussian distribution, which aligns perfectly with the binary tree structure of the data loader circuit. The distribution of each individual angle can be derived in closed form and is related to the Beta and Gamma distributions. Therefore, the first stage of the method prepares a set of independent quantum registers, each encoding a sample from the correct marginal distribution for a single angle.

The second stage of the method uses these prepared angle registers as control inputs to a second, “outer” data loader circuit. This outer circuit is designed to take the state with i.i.d. Gaussian amplitudes and convert it into a state with the desired decaying variances. This is achieved by a clever trick involving reversible arithmetic. Specifically, the method prepares an auxiliary state of the form ∑_{l} (1/l^(H+1/2)) |l⟩ and then uses a series of controlled addition operations to effectively multiply the i.i.d. Gaussian amplitudes by the decaying factor 1/k^(H+1/2). This two-layered approach—“data loading the data loader”—is the technical heart of the state preparation subroutine and allows for the efficient creation of the required Fourier-domain state with a gate complexity of O(L + polylog(T)).

Once the state |ψ_fourier⟩ is prepared, the method proceeds to the third step: applying the quantum Fourier transform (QFT). The QFT is a unitary transformation that maps a state from the frequency domain to the time domain. A quantum circuit for the QFT on a register of size T can be implemented with a depth of O((log T)²) and a number of gates that is also polylogarithmic in T. This is an exponential speedup over the classical FFT, which has a linear dependence on T. The output of the QFT is the state |ψ_time⟩ = (1/Z) ∑_{t=1}^T B_H(t) |t⟩, which is precisely the analog representation of a single trajectory of the fBM. The value of the process at time t is stored in the amplitude of the basis state |t⟩.

To make this simulation compatible with quantum amplitude estimation for Monte Carlo tasks, the method must produce a *coherent* superposition over all possible trajectories. This is achieved by ensuring that the randomness used to generate the Gaussian coefficients a_k is kept in a separate, entangled “garbage” register. The full state prepared by the method is therefore of the form |S⟩ = ∑_{trajectories} √p(v) |ψ_v⟩ |g_v⟩, where |ψ_v⟩ is the analog representation of a specific trajectory v, and |g_v⟩ is the garbage state containing the random seeds that generated it. Tracing out the garbage register yields a mixed state whose diagonal elements in the time basis correspond to the correct probability distribution of the process.

Finally, to estimate a property of the fBM, the method integrates this simulation with a quantum amplitude estimation (QAE) algorithm. The property to be estimated must be a quantity that can be expressed as an amplitude in the final quantum state. The invention identifies that time-averaged quantities of the form (1/T) ∑_t f(t) B_H(t) are ideal candidates, as they correspond to the inner product ⟨f|ψ_time⟩, where |f⟩ is a quantum state encoding the known test function f(t). The QAE algorithm is used to estimate this amplitude with high precision. The total runtime of the end-to-end algorithm is dominated by the cost of the simulation oracle, which is called O(1/ε) times by QAE. Since the oracle has a cost of O(polylog(T) + poly(ε^(-1/(2H)))), the total cost is O(polylog(T) / ε^c) where c = 1 + 1/(2H). This completes the description of the example method.

### Description of a Computing System

The methods of the present invention are implemented on a specialized quantum computing system. This system comprises a hybrid classical-quantum architecture, where a classical controller manages and orchestrates the execution of a quantum processing unit (QPU). The QPU is a fault-tolerant quantum computer capable of executing a universal set of quantum gates, including Clifford gates (such as Hadamard, CNOT, and Phase gates) and at least one non-Clifford gate (such as the T-gate), which are necessary for universal quantum computation. The QPU is equipped with a sufficient number of logical qubits to carry out the algorithms described herein, with the exact number scaling as O(L + log T), where L is the number of terms in the truncated Fourier series and T is the number of time steps in the simulated process. The system also includes a large pool of ancilla qubits to facilitate intermediate computations, such as the unary-to-binary conversion and the implementation of multi-controlled gates.

The classical controller is a high-performance computing system that performs several critical functions. First, it pre-processes the input parameters of the problem, such as the Hurst parameter H, the number of time steps T, and the desired precision ε, to compute the necessary internal parameters of the quantum algorithm, most notably the truncation number L = O(ε^(-1/(2H))). Second, it compiles the high-level quantum algorithm into a low-level sequence of physical or logical quantum gates that can be executed on the QPU. This compilation process involves generating the specific sequences of rotation angles for the data loader circuits, constructing the QFT circuit, and integrating the entire simulation oracle with the QAE routine. The compilation must be optimized to minimize the total circuit depth, as this is the primary factor determining the runtime on a fault-tolerant machine.

The quantum memory of the system is organized into several distinct registers to hold the different components of the quantum state. A primary register of size log T qubits is used to store the time index in the analog representation. A second, larger register of size L qubits is used during the state preparation phase to hold the Fourier coefficients in a unary or binary encoding. Additional registers are allocated for the garbage state, which stores the random seeds and the intermediate angle parameters for the data loader. The system also includes a dedicated register for the test function |f⟩, which may be prepared by a separate state preparation subroutine if it is not a simple uniform or indicator function.

The communication between the classical controller and the QPU is bidirectional. The controller sends the compiled quantum circuit to the QPU for execution. After the QPU completes the QAE subroutine, it returns a measurement outcome to the controller. The controller then performs classical post-processing on this outcome, which typically involves a simple rescaling and rounding operation to convert the raw amplitude estimate into the final estimate of the desired property of the stochastic process (e.g., the price of a variance swap or the value of a test statistic for anomalous diffusion). The system is designed to be modular, allowing the core analog simulation subroutine to be easily integrated with different QAE variants (e.g., iterative QAE for lower depth) and different state preparation routines for various test functions f(t).

### Additional Considerations

The invention, as described, provides a powerful framework for the quantum simulation of a specific class of stochastic processes. Several additional considerations and potential extensions are worth noting. First, while the primary examples focus on fBM, the core quantum spectral method is applicable to any stochastic process that admits a Karhunen-Loève (K-L) expansion with a rapidly decaying spectrum of eigenvalues. The K-L theorem is a generalization of the Fourier series for stochastic processes, stating that any process can be represented as a sum of orthogonal functions weighted by uncorrelated random variables. The efficiency of the analog simulation hinges on the decay rate of these eigenvalues; a rapid decay (e.g., polynomial or exponential) implies that only a few terms are needed for a good approximation, leading to a low L and hence a low resource cost. This suggests that the method could be extended to other important processes in physics and finance that have well-behaved K-L expansions.

Second, the invention discusses the distinction between a black-box quantum speedup and a white-box quantum speedup. The current applications, such as the variance swap pricing, are black-box speedups because they are compared against the classical Monte Carlo method. However, for these specific cases, closed-form analytical solutions exist, meaning that the best classical algorithm is not Monte Carlo but a direct formula evaluation, which is far faster. To achieve a true white-box speedup, the method must be applied to problems where no such closed-form solution exists. The invention proposes that a promising avenue is to consider post-selected expectations, where the average is taken only over a subset of trajectories that satisfy a certain condition (e.g., a barrier option that only pays out if the asset price crosses a certain threshold). Such post-selection typically destroys the analytical tractability of the problem, forcing a classical solver to resort to Monte Carlo, while the quantum algorithm can incorporate the post-selection as a simple conditional rotation within the amplitude estimation framework, largely preserving its efficiency. This is a key direction for future practical applications.

Third, the invention outlines a generalization of the method to Lévy processes, which are a broader class of stochastic processes that include jumps in addition to continuous diffusion. The method for Lévy processes relies on their representation as a stochastic integral over a Lévy noise, which has a flat power spectrum. The simulation is performed by embedding the discrete integration operator (a Toeplitz matrix) into a circulant matrix, which can be diagonalized by the QFT. However, the resulting simulation is incoherent, meaning it cannot be directly used with amplitude estimation. This highlights a limitation of the current approach and suggests that developing a coherent analog simulation for jump processes is a significant open challenge.

Finally, it is important to consider the practical implications of the resource estimates. The polylogarithmic dependence on T is a dramatic theoretical improvement, but the constants hidden in the O-notation and the polynomial dependence on 1/ε may be large. A detailed resource estimation for a specific, real-world problem instance (e.g., pricing a specific exotic option with realistic market parameters) would be necessary to conclusively determine if the method offers a practical advantage on foreseeable fault-tolerant quantum hardware. Nonetheless, the invention provides a clear and promising pathway towards overcoming the primary bottleneck—the high circuit depth of the simulation oracle—that has plagued previous proposals for quantum-enhanced Monte Carlo methods.
