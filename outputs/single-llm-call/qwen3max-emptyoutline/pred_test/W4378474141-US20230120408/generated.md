# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

### 1. Field of the Invention

The present invention relates generally to the field of superconducting materials and, more particularly, to methods for fabricating wafer-scale magnesium diboride (MgB₂) thin films with high critical temperature (Tc), low surface roughness, and controlled kinetic inductance, as well as to superconducting microdevices formed from such films. The invention further encompasses the integration of MgB₂ thin films onto silicon substrates using a buffer layer to prevent deleterious interfacial reactions, and the use of a boron capping layer during post-deposition annealing to suppress magnesium evaporation and maintain stoichiometry. The resulting MgB₂ thin films are suitable for high-frequency microwave applications, including but not limited to superconducting resonators, photon detectors, parametric amplifiers, and quantum computing circuits, operating at temperatures significantly higher than those required for conventional niobium-based superconductors.

### 2. Description of the Related Art

Magnesium diboride (MgB₂) is a binary intermetallic compound that exhibits superconductivity at a remarkably high critical temperature (Tc) of approximately 39 K, which was first reported in 2001. This Tc is nearly twice that of conventional low-temperature superconductors such as niobium (Nb, Tc ≈ 9.2 K) and significantly higher than that of many high-temperature cuprate superconductors when measured on an absolute scale. The relatively high Tc, combined with its simple crystal structure, low cost of raw materials, and absence of toxic elements, has made MgB₂ a promising candidate for a wide range of superconducting applications, including power transmission cables, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) magnets, and, more recently, microwave and quantum devices. However, the practical implementation of MgB₂ in thin-film form—particularly for microelectronic and photonic applications—has been hindered by significant materials science and fabrication challenges.

One of the primary obstacles in MgB₂ thin film fabrication is the inherent volatility and high reactivity of elemental magnesium. Magnesium has a low melting point (650 °C) and a high vapor pressure at elevated temperatures, which leads to substantial loss of magnesium during high-temperature processing steps that are typically required to crystallize the MgB₂ phase. This magnesium evaporation results in off-stoichiometric films, degraded superconducting properties, and increased surface roughness. Furthermore, magnesium readily oxidizes upon exposure to ambient air or trace oxygen, forming insulating magnesium oxide (MgO) that disrupts the superconducting pathways and degrades device performance. These issues are exacerbated in thin-film geometries, where the surface-to-volume ratio is large, and even minor contamination or stoichiometric deviation can have a pronounced effect.

Various deposition techniques have been explored to overcome these challenges, including pulsed laser deposition (PLD), molecular beam epitaxy (MBE), and hybrid physical–chemical vapor deposition (HPCVD). While these methods have produced MgB₂ films with high Tc, they often suffer from poor scalability, limited compatibility with standard semiconductor manufacturing processes, or difficulty in achieving uniformity over large areas. Magnetron sputtering, in contrast, is a widely adopted, industry-standard technique that offers excellent scalability, uniformity, and compatibility with existing microfabrication infrastructure. However, conventional in-situ sputtering of MgB₂ at elevated temperatures has consistently yielded films with low Tc (typically below 22 K) due to oxidation, small grain size, and magnesium loss. Post-annealing of co-sputtered Mg-B precursor films at room temperature has shown promise in improving Tc, but this approach often results in excessive surface roughness (greater than 10 nm root mean square, or rms) due to uncontrolled magnesium migration and evaporation during annealing.

To address magnesium evaporation, researchers have attempted to use capping layers during annealing. Tantalum (Ta) has been used as a capping material due to its high melting point and chemical inertness toward Mg and B at temperatures below 800 °C. However, tantalum tends to crack above 700 °C, compromising its protective function, and must be removed after annealing to access the underlying MgB₂ for device characterization and patterning—a process that introduces additional complexity and risk of contamination. Alternative capping strategies using boron have been proposed, leveraging boron’s high melting point (2076 °C) and the fact that its surface oxide (B₂O₃) melts at approximately 450 °C to form a viscous, self-healing liquid layer that effectively seals the film and prevents magnesium out-diffusion. Although boron-based capping appears advantageous, prior art has not demonstrated a robust, reproducible process for integrating this approach into a wafer-scale fabrication flow compatible with silicon substrates.

Substrate selection presents another critical challenge. Sapphire (Al₂O₃) has been commonly used for MgB₂ growth due to its hexagonal crystal structure and near-perfect lattice match (<0.1% mismatch with 30° rotation) with MgB₂, which promotes epitaxial growth and high Tc. However, sapphire is expensive and, more importantly, oxygen readily diffuses from the sapphire substrate into the MgB₂ film during annealing because magnesium has a lower oxidation enthalpy than aluminum. This interfacial reaction forms a non-superconducting interdiffusion layer that degrades the film’s stoichiometry and superconducting properties, particularly in thin films (<100 nm). Silicon carbide (SiC) offers a better lattice match and has yielded the highest reported Tc values for MgB₂, but it is also costly and incompatible with standard silicon CMOS processing. Silicon, the most ubiquitous and cost-effective substrate in microelectronics, reacts with magnesium to form magnesium silicide (Mg₂Si), a non-superconducting compound that destroys the integrity of the MgB₂ layer. To mitigate this, buffer layers such as silicon nitride (SiNₓ) have been employed, but prior efforts have not systematically optimized the buffer thickness, stress, and deposition method to ensure pinhole-free coverage and long-term stability during high-temperature annealing.

Beyond materials integration, the utility of MgB₂ in microwave and quantum devices depends critically on the ability to control key electromagnetic parameters, particularly kinetic inductance (Lk) and internal quality factor (Qi). Kinetic inductance arises from the inertia of Cooper pairs in a superconductor and is inversely proportional to the superfluid density. In MgB₂, which is a two-gap superconductor with σ and π bands contributing differently to superconductivity, Lk can be tuned by controlling the film’s resistivity, which in turn is governed by stoichiometry and microstructure. Conventional superconductors like NbN or TiN rely on reactive sputtering in nitrogen atmospheres, where precise control of gas partial pressures is required to tune resistivity—a process that is notoriously sensitive to chamber conditions and difficult to reproduce. In contrast, MgB₂ offers a unique advantage: resistivity can be controlled by simply adjusting the Mg-to-B ratio in the precursor film, without the need for reactive gases. However, prior art has not demonstrated a systematic, scalable method for achieving a broad, reproducible range of Lk values in wafer-scale MgB₂ films.

Moreover, the internal quality factor of superconducting resonators—critical for low-loss microwave applications—is often limited by dielectric losses from interfaces, substrates, or surface oxides rather than intrinsic superconducting losses. While early MgB₂ resonators exhibited Qi values on the order of 10⁴ at 4.5 K, these were still dominated by extrinsic dielectric losses, and the dependence of Qi on device geometry (e.g., center conductor width) had not been fully leveraged to isolate and mitigate loss mechanisms. Additionally, the two-gap nature of MgB₂ introduces complex temperature-dependent behavior in the kinetic inductance and surface impedance, which has not been adequately modeled or exploited in device design.

In summary, despite the theoretical promise of MgB₂, the prior art lacks a comprehensive, manufacturable process for producing wafer-scale MgB₂ thin films that simultaneously achieve high Tc, low surface roughness, controlled stoichiometry, compatibility with silicon substrates, and tunable electromagnetic properties. The absence of such a process has prevented the widespread adoption of MgB₂ in commercial superconducting electronics and quantum technologies. The present invention addresses these longstanding deficiencies by introducing a novel fabrication methodology that integrates room-temperature co-sputtering, boron capping, silicon nitride buffering, and optimized rapid thermal annealing into a unified, scalable platform for high-performance MgB₂ microdevices.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a method for fabricating high-quality, wafer-scale magnesium diboride (MgB₂) thin films with enhanced superconducting properties, including a critical temperature (Tc) of at least 32 K, surface roughness below 1 nm root mean square (rms), and tunable kinetic inductance in the range of 5 to 50 pH per square (pH/□). The invention further provides superconducting microdevices, such as coplanar waveguide (CPW) resonators, fabricated from these films, which exhibit internal quality factors (Qi) exceeding 10⁴ at temperatures up to 4.5 K and are suitable for high-frequency microwave and quantum applications.

The method comprises depositing a magnesium-boron (Mg-B) composite precursor film onto a silicon substrate coated with a silicon nitride (SiNₓ) buffer layer by radio frequency (RF) magnetron sputtering at room temperature. The silicon nitride buffer layer, having a thickness of at least 30 nm and deposited by low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD), prevents the formation of magnesium silicide (Mg₂Si) at the interface between the MgB₂ film and the silicon substrate. During deposition, a low RF substrate bias of approximately 15 W is applied to reduce the surface roughness of the as-deposited precursor film to between 0.34 and 0.45 nm rms without inducing excessive heating or etching that would compromise stoichiometric control.

Following deposition, a boron capping layer, having a thickness of tens of nanometers, is applied onto the Mg-B precursor film. This boron cap serves a dual function: it acts as a diffusion barrier to prevent magnesium evaporation during subsequent high-temperature annealing, and its surface oxide (B₂O₃) forms a viscous, crack-free liquid layer at annealing temperatures above 450 °C, thereby sealing the film and maintaining stoichiometry. The capped structure is then subjected to rapid thermal annealing at a temperature below the melting point of magnesium (650 °C)—preferably between 550 °C and 620 °C—for a duration sufficient to fully convert the Mg-B precursor into crystalline MgB₂ while minimizing surface roughening and interfacial reactions.

In an alternative embodiment, the precursor film is formed by alternating layers of magnesium and boron, each deposited to achieve an overall atomic ratio of two boron atoms to one magnesium atom. When annealed under the boron capping layer, this layered structure promotes large-grain MgB₂ formation with a Tc as high as 37.2 K, albeit with increased surface roughness, which may be acceptable for devices with small active areas.

The invention further provides a method for tuning the kinetic inductance of the resulting MgB₂ films by controlling the magnesium-to-boron ratio in the precursor film. By reducing the RF sputtering power applied to the magnesium target relative to the boron target, the resistivity of the as-deposited precursor—and consequently the kinetic inductance of the post-annealed MgB₂ film—can be systematically increased. This enables the fabrication of films with kinetic inductance values ranging from 5 to 50 pH/□ for a nominal thickness of approximately 40 nm, and potentially higher values for thinner films.

After annealing, the boron capping layer is removed by argon ion milling, and the MgB₂ film is patterned into microdevices using standard photolithography and etching techniques. To protect the MgB₂ from oxidation during fabrication, a titanium-gold (Ti/Au) bilayer is employed as a tri-functional layer: it acts as a passivation layer during lithography, a hardmask during etching, and electrical contact pads after selective removal. The resulting devices demonstrate high critical current density (Jc), low microwave losses, and nonlinear kinetic inductance suitable for advanced applications such as parametric amplifiers, frequency multipliers, and kinetic inductance-based qubits.

In summary, the invention overcomes the historical limitations of MgB₂ thin film fabrication by providing a scalable, reproducible, and CMOS-compatible process that yields high-Tc, smooth, and electrically tunable superconducting films on silicon wafers, thereby enabling the immediate implementation of MgB₂ in next-generation superconducting electronics and quantum technologies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Technical Description

The present invention is directed to a comprehensive fabrication process for producing high-performance magnesium diboride (MgB₂) thin films on silicon substrates, along with the superconducting microdevices derived therefrom. The process is engineered to address the fundamental challenges associated with MgB₂ synthesis—namely, magnesium volatility, oxidation sensitivity, substrate incompatibility, and the need for precise stoichiometric and microstructural control—while leveraging industry-standard deposition and patterning tools to ensure scalability and manufacturability.

The foundation of the invention lies in the strategic use of a silicon nitride (SiNₓ) buffer layer deposited on a high-resistivity silicon wafer. Silicon is chosen as the substrate due to its ubiquity, low cost, mechanical robustness, and compatibility with existing semiconductor foundry processes. However, direct contact between MgB₂ and silicon is thermodynamically unfavorable, as magnesium readily reacts with silicon to form magnesium silicide (Mg₂Si), a non-superconducting compound that degrades the film’s integrity and superconducting properties. To circumvent this, a buffer layer of silicon nitride is deposited prior to MgB₂ film formation. The silicon nitride layer is deposited by low-pressure chemical vapor deposition (LPCVD), a highly commercialized and controllable process that yields films with low stress (50–150 MPa tensile) and excellent uniformity. Critically, the thickness of the silicon nitride buffer is selected to be at least 30 nm, which has been empirically determined to be sufficient to prevent pinhole formation and ensure complete isolation of the MgB₂ layer from the underlying silicon. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) cross-sectional analysis confirms the absence of interfacial reactions and the maintenance of a sharp, clean interface between the silicon nitride and the MgB₂ film after annealing.

The MgB₂ film itself is formed via a two-step process: (1) room-temperature co-sputtering of a magnesium-boron (Mg-B) composite precursor film, and (2) post-deposition rapid thermal annealing under a boron capping layer. The precursor film is deposited using RF magnetron sputtering from separate elemental magnesium and boron targets. Boron sputtering is performed at a fixed RF power of 580 W, while the magnesium sputtering power is varied between 50 W and 250 W to control the Mg-to-B atomic ratio. The deposition is carried out at a base pressure of 1–2 mTorr in an argon atmosphere. Crucially, the entire deposition is conducted at room temperature to avoid premature magnesium loss or reaction. To achieve the ultra-smooth surface morphology required for high-yield microdevice fabrication, a low RF substrate bias of 15 W is applied during sputtering. This bias enhances adatom mobility without causing significant resputtering or heating, thereby reducing the root mean square (rms) surface roughness of the as-deposited precursor film from 1.74 nm to between 0.34 and 0.45 nm. This level of smoothness is unprecedented for MgB₂ precursor films and is essential for minimizing scattering losses in microwave devices.

Immediately following precursor deposition, a boron capping layer of tens of nanometers in thickness is sputtered onto the film. This capping layer is the key innovation that enables high-temperature annealing without magnesium evaporation. During annealing, the surface of the boron cap oxidizes to form boron trioxide (B₂O₃), which has a melting point of 450 °C. Above this temperature, B₂O₃ becomes a viscous liquid that flows to seal any microcracks or voids, creating a hermetic, self-healing barrier that traps magnesium within the film. This mechanism is far superior to solid capping layers such as tantalum, which crack under thermal stress and fail to provide continuous protection. Moreover, boron is chemically compatible with MgB₂; potential byproducts such as MgB₄ or MgB₇ are thermodynamically less stable than MgB₂ and, if formed, are dielectric and do not interfere with superconducting measurements.

The capped structure is then annealed in a rapid thermal processor (RTP) with a controlled ramp rate of 1 °C per second for co-sputtered films or up to 33 °C per second for layered precursors. The annealing temperature is carefully maintained below 650 °C—the melting point of magnesium—to prevent catastrophic evaporation. Typical annealing conditions involve holding the sample at 600 °C for 2 minutes, which is sufficient to drive the reaction Mg + 2B → MgB₂ to completion while preserving surface smoothness. Under these conditions, the resulting MgB₂ films exhibit a critical temperature (Tc,0) of 32 K, with a transition width of less than 1 K, and a surface roughness of 0.476 nm rms over a 100 mm diameter wafer. In an alternative embodiment, where the precursor consists of alternating stoichiometric layers of Mg and B, the same annealing process induces massive interdiffusion and grain growth, yielding a Tc,0 of 37.2 K—the highest ever reported for sputtered MgB₂—albeit with increased roughness due to void formation.

A critical aspect of the invention is the ability to tune the electromagnetic properties of the MgB₂ film, particularly its kinetic inductance (Lk). Kinetic inductance is a key parameter in superconducting microwave circuits, as it determines the resonant frequency, nonlinearity, and sensitivity of devices such as resonators and detectors. In MgB₂, Lk is directly proportional to the sheet resistance, which can be controlled by adjusting the Mg-to-B ratio in the precursor film. By reducing the RF power to the magnesium target, the amount of magnesium incorporated into the film is decreased, leading to a higher resistivity in the as-deposited state and, after annealing, a higher Lk in the superconducting state. This method enables precise, reproducible tuning of Lk across a wide range—from 5 to 50 pH/□ for ~40 nm thick films—without the need for reactive gases or complex chamber conditioning, a significant advantage over NbN or TiN systems.

Following annealing, the boron cap is removed by argon ion milling, a gentle etching process that does not damage the underlying MgB₂. The film is then patterned into microdevices using a maskless photolithography system (e.g., Heidelberg MLA 150) and a tri-functional titanium-gold (Ti/Au) bilayer. The Ti/Au layer serves three purposes: (1) it passivates the MgB₂ surface during lithography, preventing oxidation; (2) it acts as a hardmask during ion milling to define device features; and (3) after selective removal, the remaining Au regions serve as low-resistance electrical contact pads. Device features as small as 0.5 µm have been successfully demonstrated, indicating high process fidelity.

The resulting superconducting microdevices, particularly CPW resonators, exhibit excellent microwave performance. Internal quality factors (Qi) of at least 10⁴ are achieved at 4.5 K, with values reaching 4×10⁴ at temperatures below 1.5 K. The Qi is observed to decrease with narrower center conductor widths, confirming that losses are dominated by dielectric interfaces rather than superconducting surface resistance. The kinetic inductance is consistently measured between 22 and 28 pH/□, with alpha parameters (participation ratio) ranging from 0.84 to 0.95, indicating strong superconducting response. Temperature-dependent frequency shift measurements confirm the two-gap nature of MgB₂, with the larger gap contributing approximately 12% to the total response, consistent with theoretical expectations.

In conclusion, the technical description of the invention encompasses a holistic, wafer-scale fabrication platform that integrates materials engineering, process optimization, and device physics to unlock the full potential of MgB₂ for superconducting electronics. Every step—from buffer layer deposition to final device patterning—is designed to be robust, reproducible, and compatible with industrial manufacturing, thereby bridging the gap between laboratory discovery and commercial application.

## CONCLUSION

The present invention establishes a transformative methodology for the wafer-scale production of high-performance magnesium diboride (MgB₂) thin films, overcoming decades of materials science challenges that have impeded the practical adoption of this promising superconductor. By integrating a silicon nitride buffer layer, room-temperature co-sputtering with optimized substrate biasing, a self-sealing boron capping layer, and controlled rapid thermal annealing, the invention achieves an unprecedented combination of high critical temperature (up to 37.2 K), ultra-low surface roughness (below 0.5 nm rms), and tunable kinetic inductance (5–50 pH/□) on cost-effective silicon substrates. This process is not only scientifically rigorous but also industrially viable, leveraging standard semiconductor fabrication tools and techniques to ensure scalability, reproducibility, and compatibility with existing microelectronics infrastructure.

The resulting MgB₂ thin films enable the fabrication of superconducting microdevices with figures of merit that rival or exceed those of conventional niobium-based technologies, while operating at significantly higher temperatures—thereby reducing cryogenic overhead and expanding the range of feasible applications. The demonstrated control over stoichiometry, microstructure, and electromagnetic properties opens new avenues for designing advanced microwave and quantum devices, including high-sensitivity photon detectors, low-noise parametric amplifiers, and kinetic inductance-based qubits. Moreover, the invention provides a foundational platform for future studies of two-gap superconductivity in engineered thin-film systems, offering a testbed for refining theoretical models and exploring novel quantum phenomena.

In sum, the invention represents a paradigm shift in superconducting thin-film technology, transforming MgB₂ from a laboratory curiosity into a manufacturable, high-performance material ready for immediate deployment in next-generation scientific instruments, communication systems, and quantum computers. The comprehensive process disclosed herein sets a new standard for superconducting film fabrication and paves the way for the widespread integration of MgB₂ into commercial and research applications worldwide.
