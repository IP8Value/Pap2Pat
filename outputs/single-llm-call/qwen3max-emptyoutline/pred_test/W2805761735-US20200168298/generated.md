# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of computational genomics and, more specifically, to systems and methods for performing allelic decomposition of highly polymorphic and structurally variant genes using high-throughput sequencing (HTS) data. The invention provides a novel combinatorial framework capable of reconstructing the exact sequence content of each copy of a gene of interest—even in the presence of complex genomic alterations such as partial or full gene duplications and deletions, hybridizations with highly homologous pseudogenes, single nucleotide variants (SNVs), and short insertions or deletions (indels). This framework is particularly suited for the accurate genotyping of pharmacogenes—genes that regulate drug metabolism, transport, and response—whose allelic composition directly influences clinical decision-making regarding drug selection and dosage. The invention further encompasses a practical implementation of this framework in the form of a software tool, herein referred to as Aldy, which operates on standard HTS data formats such as SAM/BAM files and leverages curated databases of known star-alleles and structural variants to infer both known and novel genotypes with high precision, speed, and minimal computational resource requirements.

## BACKGROUND

The advent of high-throughput sequencing (HTS) technologies has revolutionized genomic research and holds immense promise for clinical applications, particularly in the realm of personalized medicine. In principle, HTS data should enable the precise determination of the sequence composition of every copy of any gene within an individual’s genome. However, from a computational standpoint, this goal remains elusive for many functionally critical genes due to their inherent genomic complexity. A significant subset of these genes—especially those involved in pharmacogenomics—are characterized by high levels of polymorphism, the presence of multiple gene copies, and close physical proximity to highly homologous pseudogenes. These pseudogenes, while non-functional, share extensive sequence similarity with their functional counterparts and frequently participate in recombination events, leading to the formation of hybrid or fusion alleles that combine segments of the functional gene with those of the pseudogene. Furthermore, these genes are often subject to structural variations, including whole-gene or partial-gene deletions and duplications, which alter not only copy number but also the internal architecture of individual gene copies.

Traditional genotyping approaches, such as array-based panels (e.g., Affymetrix DMET+ or Illumina ADME assays), are limited in scope as they can only interrogate a predefined set of known variants. Consequently, they fail to detect rare or personal variants that may have significant functional consequences, thereby compromising the accuracy of pharmacogenomic predictions. While HTS offers a more comprehensive alternative, existing computational tools for analyzing HTS data are ill-equipped to handle the challenges posed by structurally complex genes. Current structural variation detection tools—such as VariationHunter, HYDRA, Socrates, Barnacle, Delly, Pindel, and GASVPro—are primarily designed to identify large-scale genomic rearrangements in uniquely mappable regions of the genome. They typically report only the approximate genomic loci of structural variants without reconstructing the precise sequence content of the altered regions. Moreover, these tools often rely exclusively on uniquely mapped reads and are therefore ineffective in repetitive or homologous regions where mapping ambiguity is high—a common scenario for pharmacogenes like CYP2D6.

Similarly, copy number alteration detection tools aim to estimate the number of copies of a particular gene but operate under the simplifying assumption that duplications or deletions affect the entire gene uniformly. They do not attempt to reconstruct the exact sequence of each gene copy, nor do they account for partial gene alterations or hybridization events with pseudogenes. Specialized genotyping tools such as Cypiripi and Astrolabe (formerly Constellation) have been developed for specific pharmacogenes like CYP2D6, but they are constrained by their inability to model structural variations beyond simple copy number changes or to interpret data from targeted capture sequencing platforms such as PGRNseq, which exhibit highly non-uniform coverage. Even when these tools can process HTS data, they often fail to resolve ambiguous mappings or to phase distant variants, leading to inaccurate or incomplete genotype calls.

The star-allele nomenclature system, widely used in pharmacogenomics, defines a star-allele as a specific sequence variant of a gene that differs from the canonical “wild-type” sequence (*1) by a defined set of mutations. Major star-alleles are distinguished by gene-disrupting mutations that alter the protein product or its activity, while minor star-alleles represent extensions of major alleles through the addition of neutral (non-functional) mutations. Accurate genotyping thus requires not only the identification of all present mutations but also the correct assignment of these mutations to individual gene copies and the determination of whether the resulting allele corresponds to a known star-allele or represents a novel configuration. No existing computational method integrates read alignment, mutation detection, copy number estimation, structural variant reconstruction, and star-allele assignment into a unified framework capable of performing full allelic decomposition under these complex conditions.

Accordingly, there exists a critical unmet need for a robust, accurate, and computationally efficient method that can leverage HTS data—whether from whole-genome sequencing or targeted capture panels—to perform complete allelic decomposition of structurally variant and polymorphic genes. Such a method must be capable of resolving mapping ambiguities, detecting and characterizing hybrid alleles formed through recombination with pseudogenes, estimating both whole and partial gene copy numbers, and assigning both known and novel star-allele labels based on the reconstructed sequence content of each gene copy. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a novel combinatorial optimization framework that overcomes the limitations of prior art and enables precise, clinically actionable genotyping of even the most challenging pharmacogenes.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive computational framework and associated systems for performing allelic decomposition of structurally complex and highly polymorphic genes using high-throughput sequencing (HTS) data. The core innovation lies in a novel combinatorial optimization formulation that integrates multiple layers of genomic analysis—including read alignment, mutation detection, copy number estimation, structural variant characterization, and star-allele assignment—into a unified pipeline capable of reconstructing the exact sequence content of each copy of a target gene. This framework is implemented in a software tool, herein named Aldy, which accepts standard HTS data in SAM/BAM format and a curated gene-specific database containing information about known star-alleles, pseudogenes, exon-intron boundaries, and documented structural variations.

The method begins with the alignment of HTS reads to a reference genome and the identification of all single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and short indels within the target gene region. Subsequently, the system estimates the aggregate copy number profile across each exon and intron of the gene and its associated pseudogenes by normalizing read coverage and accounting for non-uniformity inherent in targeted capture data. Using this copy number profile, the invention employs an integer linear programming (ILP) formulation—the Copy Number Estimation Problem (CNEP)—to infer the most parsimonious combination of known structural configurations (e.g., whole gene copies, partial deletions/duplications, and hybrid fusions with pseudogenes) that best explains the observed coverage data.

Following structural variant estimation, the system identifies the major star-allele of each gene copy by solving another ILP—the Major Star-Allele Identification Problem (MSAIP)—which selects a set of known major star-alleles whose defining gene-disrupting mutations collectively match the observed mutational profile while respecting copy number constraints. If no feasible solution exists using known alleles, the system infers the presence of novel major star-alleles by minimizing the number of additional mutations or structural changes required to explain the data.

Finally, the genotype is refined through the Genotype Refining Problem (GRP), a quadratic integer programming formulation that assigns neutral mutations to each major star-allele to define minor star-alleles. This step resolves ambiguities arising from shared gene-disrupting mutations among multiple major alleles by leveraging the co-occurrence patterns of neutral variants, thereby enabling the distinction between otherwise indistinguishable allelic configurations. The GRP also facilitates the discovery of novel minor star-alleles by extending known major alleles with observed neutral mutations in the most parsimonious manner.

The invention is particularly effective for pharmacogenes such as CYP2D6, CYP2A6, CYP2C9, and DPYD, which are prone to structural rearrangements and pseudogene-mediated hybridization. It supports both whole-genome sequencing and targeted capture data (e.g., from the PGRNseq platform), operates with minimal computational overhead (processing high-coverage samples in under one minute on a standard laptop), and demonstrates superior accuracy compared to existing tools, with validation rates exceeding 99% across multiple datasets. By providing a complete, accurate, and rapid solution for allelic decomposition, the present invention enables reliable pharmacogenomic profiling for clinical decision support, paving the way for truly personalized drug therapy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### CYP2D6

The cytochrome P450 2D6 (CYP2D6) gene serves as a paradigmatic example of the genomic complexity that the present invention is designed to address. Located on chromosome 22q13.1, CYP2D6 is one of the most important pharmacogenes, responsible for the metabolism of approximately 20–25% of clinically prescribed drugs, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, beta-blockers, and opioids. Its clinical significance necessitates precise genotyping to guide drug selection and dosing, yet its genomic architecture presents formidable analytical challenges. CYP2D6 resides within a cluster of highly homologous genes and pseudogenes, most notably CYP2D7 and CYP2D8, which share over 95% sequence identity across exonic regions. This extreme homology facilitates frequent non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR), leading to a wide spectrum of structural variants, including whole-gene deletions (*5), duplications/multiplications (e.g., *1xN, *2xN), and hybrid alleles formed by fusion or gene conversion events between CYP2D6 and CYP2D7 (e.g., *13, *36, *68).

Hybrid alleles may involve the replacement of one or more exons or introns of CYP2D6 with the corresponding segment from CYP2D7, resulting in chimeric genes that retain partial CYP2D6 coding sequence but may lack critical functional domains or contain pseudogene-derived sequences that disrupt enzyme activity. For instance, the *13 allele arises from a gene conversion event where exon 9 of CYP2D6 is replaced by the homologous exon from CYP2D7, introducing a premature stop codon. Similarly, the *36 allele involves the replacement of exons 3–6, while *68 results from a fusion where the 5′ end of CYP2D6 is joined to the 3′ end of CYP2D7. These structural alterations cannot be detected by conventional SNP-based genotyping or by tools that assume uniform gene structure.

Moreover, CYP2D6 exhibits extensive allelic diversity, with over 100 star-alleles documented in public databases such as the Human Cytochrome P450 Allele Nomenclature Database and PharmGKB. These alleles are defined by combinations of SNVs and indels that may be gene-disrupting (e.g., nonsense mutations, splice-site variants, frameshifts) or neutral (e.g., synonymous SNPs). Critically, many major star-alleles share identical gene-disrupting mutations, making them indistinguishable without additional phasing information provided by neutral variants. For example, *2, *29, and *33 all carry the same set of functional mutations but differ in their neutral SNP profiles.

The present invention overcomes these challenges by modeling CYP2D6 and its pseudogenes as a composite genomic locus composed of discrete regions—specifically, 19 exons and 18 introns (n=37 total regions). Each possible structural configuration (e.g., wild-type CYP2D6, CYP2D7, or any documented hybrid) is represented as a vector v of length 2n (accounting for both CYP2D6 and CYP2D7 regions), where each entry indicates the number of copies of that region present in the configuration. The system then uses integer programming to find the combination of such vectors that best fits the observed per-region copy number profile derived from HTS data. This approach explicitly accounts for partial gene alterations and hybrid structures, enabling the reconstruction of complex genotypes such as *4/*4x2, *1/*36+*10, or *5/*68+*4, which would be misclassified or unresolved by prior methods. By integrating structural variant detection with star-allele assignment, the invention provides a complete and accurate characterization of CYP2D6 diplotypes, essential for predicting metabolic phenotypes ranging from poor to ultrarapid metabolizer status.

### HTS Data

The present invention is designed to operate on high-throughput sequencing (HTS) data generated by next-generation sequencing platforms, particularly those producing short-read data such as Illumina HiSeq, NovaSeq, or MiSeq instruments. The primary input format is the Sequence Alignment/Map (SAM) or its binary counterpart (BAM), which contains aligned sequencing reads along with metadata regarding mapping quality, insert size, and base quality scores. The invention is compatible with both whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data and targeted capture sequencing data, such as that produced by the PGRNseq platform, which enriches for exonic and flanking regions of 84 pharmacogenes of interest.

Targeted capture data, while cost-effective and high-coverage (typically averaging 500–600× per chromosome for PGRNseq), presents unique challenges due to non-uniform coverage resulting from biases in probe hybridization efficiency, GC content, and repetitive elements. The invention addresses this by implementing a coverage normalization procedure that compares the sample’s coverage profile to a reference sample processed with the same sequencing protocol, thereby correcting for systematic technical artifacts. This normalized coverage is then used to estimate the true biological copy number of each genomic region within the target gene locus.

The invention does not impose strict requirements on the upstream read alignment pipeline; it accepts any valid SAM/BAM file covering the gene of interest. However, optimal performance is achieved when reads are aligned using best-practice workflows that include local realignment around indels (e.g., via the Genome Analysis Toolkit, GATK) to improve the accuracy of small variant calling. The system is robust to variations in read length (typically 100–150 bp) and insert size (typically 300–500 bp), though it acknowledges inherent limitations imposed by these parameters—particularly the inability to phase variants separated by distances greater than the insert size or to unambiguously map reads originating from regions of perfect sequence identity between a gene and its pseudogene that exceed the combined length of the read pair.

Crucially, the invention leverages the depth of coverage provided by HTS to statistically resolve mapping ambiguities. Even in homologous regions where individual reads cannot be confidently assigned to CYP2D6 or CYP2D7, the aggregate coverage profile across the entire gene locus provides sufficient signal to infer structural configurations through combinatorial optimization. This data-driven approach allows the system to function effectively on both WGS (with average coverage of 30–50×) and targeted capture data (with coverage >500×), demonstrating consistent accuracy across sequencing modalities. The flexibility to process diverse HTS data types ensures broad applicability in both research and clinical settings, where sequencing strategies may vary based on cost, throughput, and diagnostic requirements.

### Alignment/Read Mapping

The initial step in the allelic decomposition pipeline involves the alignment of raw sequencing reads to a reference human genome, typically GRCh37/hg19 or GRCh38/hg38. While the present invention does not mandate a specific aligner, it is optimized for use with widely adopted short-read mappers such as BWA-MEM or Bowtie2, which produce SAM/BAM output compliant with community standards. The alignment process maps each read to its most likely genomic origin based on sequence similarity, assigning a mapping quality score that reflects the confidence in the placement. Reads that map equally well to multiple locations—common in regions of high homology such as the CYP2D6/CYP2D7 locus—are either randomly assigned or flagged as multi-mapped, depending on the aligner’s settings.

To enhance the accuracy of downstream variant calling, the invention recommends preprocessing the aligned reads using established best practices, particularly those outlined by the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK). This includes base quality score recalibration (BQSR) to correct systematic errors in base calling and, critically, local realignment around insertion-deletion (indel) sites. Local realignment adjusts the alignment of reads in the vicinity of indels to minimize mismatches caused by misplacement, thereby improving the sensitivity and specificity of indel detection. This step is especially important for pharmacogenes like CYP2D6, where functionally relevant indels (e.g., the 138delT in *6) must be accurately identified.

The invention accepts the final BAM file as input, regardless of the specific alignment or preprocessing pipeline used, provided that the file contains reads covering the entire genomic region of the target gene, including upstream and downstream flanking sequences necessary for structural variant detection. The system does not perform its own read alignment but instead relies on the alignment information embedded in the BAM file, including the CIGAR string (which describes how the read aligns to the reference, including matches, mismatches, insertions, and deletions) and the mate-pair information for paired-end reads. This design choice ensures compatibility with existing bioinformatics workflows and allows users to leverage their preferred alignment strategies while still benefiting from the invention’s advanced genotyping capabilities.

Notably, the invention incorporates mechanisms to handle regions of ambiguous mappability. Specifically, it identifies segments where the reference gene and its pseudogene share perfect sequence identity over a length exceeding the sum of the read length and insert size (e.g., the region spanning exons 7–8 in CYP2D6/CYP2D7). Reads originating from such regions cannot be unambiguously assigned to either locus and are therefore excluded from copy number estimation to prevent bias. However, because these regions are identical in sequence, their exclusion does not impede the detection of structural breakpoints, which must occur in divergent regions. This selective masking strategy balances the need for accurate copy number inference with the realities of short-read sequencing limitations.

### Sequence Variant Calling

Following read alignment, the invention proceeds to identify all single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and short insertions or deletions (indels) present within the target gene region. This variant calling step is performed internally by the system and does not require external variant callers such as GATK HaplotypeCaller or FreeBayes, although it can utilize pre-called variants if provided. The internal variant caller operates directly on the aligned reads in the BAM file, scanning each genomic position within the gene locus for evidence of non-reference alleles.

For each candidate variant site, the system calculates the allele fraction—the proportion of reads supporting the alternate allele relative to the total coverage at that position. To account for potential mapping errors or sequencing artifacts, only variants supported by a minimum number of high-quality reads (e.g., base quality ≥ Q30, mapping quality ≥ Q20) are retained. Additionally, the system applies strand bias filters to exclude variants that appear predominantly on reads from only one DNA strand, which may indicate technical artifacts rather than true biological variation.

A critical aspect of variant calling in the context of the present invention is the estimation of the copy number of each variant, denoted as mutcn(m) for a mutation m. Unlike standard diploid variant callers that assume two copies per locus, the invention recognizes that structurally variant genes may have more than two copies, and that variants may be present on only a subset of those copies. Therefore, mutcn(m) is computed by normalizing the number of reads supporting mutation m by the expected coverage at that locus, which is derived from the previously estimated regional copy number profile. For example, if a genomic region has an estimated copy number of 3 and 150 out of 300 reads support a particular SNV, the mutcn(m) would be approximately 1.5, suggesting the variant is present on one or two of the three gene copies.

The system distinguishes between gene-disrupting mutations and neutral mutations based on annotations provided in the gene-specific database. Gene-disrupting mutations include nonsynonymous SNVs that alter amino acid sequence, nonsense mutations that introduce premature stop codons, frameshift indels, and splice-site variants that affect mRNA processing. Neutral mutations are primarily synonymous SNVs or variants in non-coding regions that do not impact protein function. This classification is essential for subsequent steps, as gene-disrupting mutations define major star-alleles, while neutral mutations refine them into minor star-alleles.

Importantly, the variant calling module is designed to be sensitive to low-frequency variants that may arise from partial gene duplications or mosaic structural alterations. By integrating variant evidence with copy number information, the system avoids false negatives that might occur if variants were called under a strict diploid assumption. This comprehensive approach ensures that all relevant mutational data are captured and appropriately weighted in the downstream combinatorial optimization steps, enabling accurate reconstruction of complex allelic architectures.

### Detecting Structural Variants

The detection of structural variants (SVs) is a cornerstone of the present invention, enabling the resolution of complex genotypes that elude conventional genotyping methods. The system focuses on three primary classes of SVs relevant to pharmacogenes: (i) whole-gene deletions or duplications, (ii) partial-gene deletions or duplications affecting one or more contiguous exons/introns, and (iii) hybrid alleles formed by recombination between the functional gene and its pseudogene (via fusion or gene conversion events).

The SV detection pipeline begins by segmenting the target gene locus into discrete regions—each corresponding to an exon or intron—based on annotations in the gene database. For each region r, the system computes an estimated copy number cn[r] by averaging the normalized coverage across all positions within r, excluding ambiguously mappable segments as previously described. This yields a vector cn of length n (where n is the total number of regions in the gene), representing the aggregate copy number profile across the locus.

This profile is then compared against a library of known structural configurations, each encoded as a configuration vector v of length n(d+1), where d is the number of highly homologous pseudogenes (e.g., d=1 for CYP2D6 with CYP2D7). Each entry v[i] in the vector indicates how many copies of region i (from either the gene or pseudogene) are present in that configuration. For example, a wild-type CYP2D6 copy is represented as [1,1,…,1,0,0,…,0] (n ones followed by n zeros), while a CYP2D6-CYP2D7 hybrid with exon 9 converted from CYP2D7 would have a zero in the position corresponding to CYP2D6 exon 9 and a one in the corresponding CYP2D7 position.

The system formulates the Copy Number Estimation Problem (CNEP) as an integer linear program (ILP) that seeks non-negative integer coefficients z_i representing the number of copies of each configuration v_i such that the linear combination Σ z_i v_i best approximates the observed copy number vector cn. The objective is to minimize the sum of absolute deviations between observed and predicted copy numbers across all regions. To ensure biological plausibility, the system imposes a parsimony constraint: among all solutions that achieve the minimal deviation, it selects those with the smallest total number of gene/pseudogene copies (i.e., minimal Σ z_i).

This combinatorial approach allows the system to simultaneously infer copy number and structural architecture. For instance, a sample with cn = [2,2,2,1,1,2,…] might be explained by one wild-type CYP2D6 copy plus one hybrid copy missing exons 4–5, rather than two wild-type copies with stochastic coverage fluctuations. The use of a curated database of known SVs ensures that only biologically plausible configurations are considered, avoiding the combinatorial explosion that would result from enumerating all possible vectors.

In cases where the observed copy number profile cannot be fully explained by known configurations, the system flags the possibility of novel structural variants. However, in its current implementation, it defaults to the closest matching known configuration, prioritizing robustness over speculative novelty. Future versions may incorporate de novo SV discovery by expanding the configuration library to include all theoretically possible breakpoints, particularly in genes where recombination hotspots are well characterized.

### Coverage Normalization

Accurate estimation of regional copy numbers is fundamental to the invention’s ability to detect structural variants, yet HTS data—especially from targeted capture platforms like PGRNseq—exhibit significant non-uniformity in coverage due to technical biases. To address this, the invention implements a sophisticated coverage normalization procedure that transforms raw read depth into a biologically meaningful copy number scale.

The normalization process begins by selecting a reference sample sequenced using the same protocol (e.g., PGRNseq v.1 or v.2) and known to have a diploid (copy number = 2) genotype for the target gene. The reference coverage profile is smoothed using a moving average or kernel density estimator to reduce noise, then used to compute a position-specific normalization factor that corrects for probe-specific hybridization efficiency and other systematic biases. For each position i in the sample, the normalized coverage cns(i) is calculated as:

cns(i) = (sample_coverage(i) / reference_coverage(i)) × 2

This scaling ensures that regions with true diploid copy number yield cns(i) ≈ 2, while deviations indicate copy number gains or losses. The system further refines this estimate by masking regions of low mappability or high GC content that are prone to coverage artifacts, and by applying a segmentation algorithm (e.g., circular binary segmentation) to group adjacent positions with similar copy number into larger regions, thereby improving statistical power.

For whole-genome sequencing data, where coverage is more uniform but lower in depth, the invention uses a genome-wide median coverage as the reference baseline, adjusted for local GC content using LOESS regression. This approach maintains compatibility across sequencing modalities while preserving the sensitivity needed to detect subtle copy number changes.

The resulting normalized coverage profile serves as the foundation for all downstream analyses, including regional copy number estimation, structural variant detection, and variant copy number calculation. By decoupling biological signal from technical noise, coverage normalization enables the invention to achieve high accuracy even in challenging datasets with extreme coverage variability, a key advantage over methods that assume uniform coverage or rely solely on relative depth comparisons.

### Major Star-Allele Identification

Once structural configurations and regional copy numbers have been inferred, the invention proceeds to assign major star-alleles to each gene copy. This step is formalized as the Major Star-Allele Identification Problem (MSAIP), an integer linear program that selects a multiset of known major star-alleles whose combined mutational profile best matches the observed set of gene-disrupting mutations.

The input to MSAIP is the set M of all gene-disrupting mutations detected in the sample, along with their estimated copy numbers mutcn(m). The system first filters the database of known major star-alleles to exclude any allele that (i) contains a gene-disrupting mutation not observed in M, or (ii) has a structural configuration not supported by the CNEP solution. The remaining candidate alleles form the set A = {a_1, …, a_t}.

MSAIP introduces integer variables p_i representing the number of copies of allele a_i in the final genotype. For each mutation m ∈ M, the system enforces the constraint that the total number of allele copies harboring m must be at least one (to ensure all observed mutations are explained) and as close as possible to mutcn(m). The objective function minimizes the sum of absolute residuals |mutcn(m) − Σ_{i: m ∈ gdm(a_i)} p_i| across all m ∈ M, where gdm(a_i) is the set of gene-disrupting mutations defining allele a_i.

To enhance accuracy, the system optionally incorporates read-backed phasing information. Reads that span multiple mutation loci provide direct evidence of co-occurrence on the same DNA molecule. If a read covers two gene-disrupting mutations m1 and m2, any major star-allele that includes m1 but not m2 (or vice versa) is eliminated from consideration, as it would contradict the observed haplotype. This filter is particularly powerful for resolving ambiguities between alleles that share some but not all functional mutations.

In cases where MSAIP yields no feasible solution—indicating the presence of novel major star-alleles—the system constructs a hypothetical allele by taking the union of all observed gene-disrupting mutations and assigning it a provisional label (e.g., *n+1). This novel allele is then carried forward to the genotype refining step, where its neutral mutation profile will be characterized. The high penalty (η = 100,000) applied to modifications of gene-disrupting mutations in the GRP objective ensures that novel major alleles are only invoked when absolutely necessary, preserving the integrity of the star-allele nomenclature system.

### Genotype Refining

The final step in the allelic decomposition pipeline is genotype refining, which resolves ambiguities in major star-allele assignment and defines minor star-alleles by incorporating neutral mutations. This step addresses a critical limitation of prior methods: many major star-alleles share identical sets of gene-disrupting mutations but differ in their neutral SNP profiles (e.g., CYP2D6*2A vs. *2B). Without phasing information, these alleles are indistinguishable based on functional mutations alone.

The Genotype Refining Problem (GRP) is formulated as a quadratic integer program that extends each major star-allele identified in MSAIP into a minor star-allele by assigning observed neutral mutations. The input includes the set of major alleles from MSAIP, the full set of observed mutations (both gene-disrupting and neutral), and their copy numbers mutcn(m). The system considers all known minor star-alleles in the database as candidate extensions, as well as the possibility of novel minor alleles formed by adding or removing neutral mutations from known major alleles.

For each major allele b and each candidate minor allele a that extends b, the system defines binary variables x_{a,b} indicating whether a is selected as the refinement of b. Additional binary variables e_{a,b,m} and f_{a,b,m} track whether mutation m is retained or added, respectively, in the refined allele. The objective function minimizes a weighted sum of three components: (i) the deviation between observed and predicted mutation copy numbers (|F_m|), (ii) the penalty for missing known neutral mutations (α = 2), and (iii) the penalty for adding novel neutral mutations (β = 1). A very high penalty (η = 100,000) is applied to any modification of gene-disrupting mutations, ensuring that major allele definitions remain intact unless MSAIP was infeasible.

Constraints enforce that each major allele is extended by exactly one minor allele, that all gene-disrupting mutations are preserved, and that mutation copy numbers are not over-called (i.e., the total number of allele copies containing m does not exceed mutcn(m)). The quadratic nature of the objective arises from interactions between mutation assignments across different alleles, reflecting the combinatorial complexity of phasing neutral variants in a multi-copy context.

By solving GRP, the system produces a complete diplotype call consisting of minor star-alleles (e.g., *1/*2A, *4M/*41), along with confidence scores based on the objective value. In cases of residual ambiguity—such as when multiple minor allele combinations yield identical scores—all equally likely solutions are reported. This transparency is crucial for clinical interpretation, where uncertain calls may warrant orthogonal validation.

## Complexity

The computational problems underlying the present invention—namely the Copy Number Estimation Problem (CNEP) and the Major Star-Allele Identification Problem (MSAIP)—are formally NP-hard, as demonstrated by reductions from the Closest Vector Problem (CVP), a well-known NP-hard problem in computational geometry. This theoretical hardness stems from the combinatorial explosion in the number of possible structural configurations and allele combinations as the number of genomic regions and known star-alleles increases. However, in practice, the invention achieves remarkable efficiency through several key strategies.

First, the use of curated databases restricts the search space to biologically plausible configurations and alleles, dramatically reducing the dimensionality of the integer programs. For CYP2D6, for example, the number of known hybrid configurations is on the order of tens, not millions, making the ILP tractable. Second, the invention leverages state-of-the-art integer programming solvers such as Gurobi or SCIP, which employ advanced techniques like branch-and-bound, cutting planes, and heuristic search to find optimal or near-optimal solutions rapidly. Third, the problems exhibit favorable structure in many real-world instances—for example, when no structural variants are present, the configuration matrix becomes unimodular, allowing polynomial-time solution via linear programming relaxation.

Empirically, the invention processes high-coverage PGRNseq samples (600×) in under 10 seconds and consumes less than 100 MB of memory on a standard laptop, making it suitable for clinical deployment. This efficiency is orders of magnitude better than competing tools like Cypiripi, which can take up to an hour on similar data. The quadratic nature of the Genotype Refining Problem (GRP) is mitigated by the relatively small number of candidate minor alleles per major allele and the sparsity of neutral mutations.

The primary practical limitations arise not from algorithmic complexity but from inherent constraints of short-read sequencing technology. Regions of perfect sequence identity between a gene and its pseudogene longer than the insert size cannot be resolved, leading to unavoidable ambiguities in breakpoint localization or copy number estimation. Similarly, variants separated by distances greater than the insert size cannot be phased, potentially resulting in multiple equally valid genotype solutions. These limitations are fundamental to the data modality and not specific to the invention; however, the system is designed to gracefully handle them by reporting all plausible solutions and flagging low-confidence calls. Future integration with long-read or barcoded sequencing data could alleviate these issues, and the modular architecture of the invention facilitates such enhancements without requiring a complete redesign.

## Systems

The present invention is embodied in a software system, Aldy, implemented in a combination of Python and C++ for optimal performance and usability. The system is designed as a command-line tool that accepts standard input formats (BAM files and gene database JSON/YAML files) and produces structured output in JSON, VCF, or human-readable text formats, facilitating integration into clinical reporting pipelines.

The core computational engine consists of three main modules corresponding to the key steps of the pipeline: (1) a coverage analysis module that performs read counting, normalization, and segmentation; (2) an integer programming module that solves CNEP and MSAIP using the Gurobi solver; and (3) a genotype refinement module that implements the GRP quadratic program. These modules are orchestrated by a workflow manager that handles data flow, error checking, and parallelization where applicable.

The system includes a comprehensive database schema for storing gene-specific knowledge, including genomic coordinates, exon-intron structures, pseudogene mappings, lists of known major and minor star-alleles with their defining mutations, and documented structural variants with their breakpoint coordinates. The database is extensible, allowing users to add newly discovered alleles or structural configurations without modifying the core code. Pre-built databases are provided for ten key pharmacogenes (CYP2D6, CYP2A6, CYP2C8, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4, CYP3A5, CYP4F2, DPYD, TPMT), with support for additional genes via user-defined configurations.

Hardware requirements are minimal: the system runs on any modern x86-64 processor with 2 GB RAM and 100 MB disk space, though higher memory is recommended for very high-coverage samples. It is compatible with Linux, macOS, and Windows operating systems and can be deployed locally or in cloud environments. Docker containers and Conda packages are provided for easy installation and reproducibility.

The system outputs a detailed report for each sample, including the inferred diplotype (with minor star-allele labels), supporting evidence (read counts for key mutations, coverage plots), confidence metrics (objective scores from CNEP, MSAIP, and GRP), and flags for novel alleles or ambiguous calls. This rich output supports both automated interpretation in clinical decision support systems and manual review by geneticists or pharmacologists.

### DISCUSSION

The present invention represents a significant advance in the computational genotyping of structurally complex pharmacogenes, addressing long-standing challenges that have hindered the clinical adoption of HTS for pharmacogenomic testing. By integrating structural variant detection with star-allele assignment in a unified combinatorial framework, the invention achieves unprecedented accuracy in allelic decomposition, as demonstrated across multiple independent datasets encompassing diverse sequencing technologies and ethnic backgrounds.

In head-to-head comparisons with existing tools, the invention correctly genotyped 100% of CYP2D6 samples in a cohort of 96 PGRNseq v.2-sequenced Coriell trios, outperforming Astrolabe (68% concordance) and Cypiripi (59% concordance). It also resolved complex structural events—including hybrid alleles like *68+*4 and partial duplications—that were entirely missed by competing methods. On whole-genome sequencing data from the Platinum Genomes project, the invention maintained high accuracy (92% concordance with validated calls) despite lower coverage, and exhibited perfect Mendelian consistency across family trios, a critical validation metric absent in prior studies.

The invention’s ability to discover novel alleles further enhances its utility. In GeT-RM samples, it identified previously unreported major star-alleles such as a CYP2D6*10-like variant with c.77G>A and novel DPYD alleles combining features of *4, *5, and *6. It also revealed inconsistencies in commercial genotyping panels, which misclassified alleles like CYP2D6*35 and *45 as *2 due to missing key SNPs, underscoring the limitations of array-based approaches.

From a clinical perspective, the invention’s speed (<1 minute per sample) and low resource requirements make it ideal for point-of-care implementation, where rapid turnaround is essential for treatment decisions. Its support for targeted capture data aligns with current trends in cost-effective pharmacogenomic screening, while its compatibility with WGS ensures future-proofing as sequencing costs decline.

Limitations remain, primarily tied to short-read sequencing constraints. Ambiguities in regions of perfect homology or between distant variants cannot be fully resolved without additional data (e.g., long reads or family phasing). However, the invention transparently reports all plausible solutions, enabling informed clinical judgment. Future work will integrate barcoded sequencing for haplotype resolution and expand the database to cover additional pharmacogenes and population-specific variants.

In summary, the present invention bridges the gap between the theoretical potential of HTS and the practical demands of clinical pharmacogenomics, providing a robust, accurate, and scalable solution for allelic decomposition that promises to improve drug safety and efficacy worldwide.
