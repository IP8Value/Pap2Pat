# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention pertains to the field of metabolic engineering and biotechnology, specifically to the development of genetically modified strains of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* for the high-level production of L-ornithine. More particularly, the invention relates to a modular pathway rewiring (MPR) strategy that enables the coordinated optimization of multiple metabolic modules—comprising L-ornithine consumption, L-ornithine biosynthesis, and precursor supply—to overcome native regulatory constraints, subcellular compartmentalization barriers, and carbon flux limitations inherent in wild-type yeast. The invention further encompasses methods for engineering subcellular trafficking, relocating biosynthetic pathways across cellular compartments, attenuating the Crabtree effect through modulation of glucose uptake and respiratory chain activity, and fine-tuning feedback regulation via leaky auxotrophy to achieve industrially relevant titers of L-ornithine. The resulting engineered microbial cell factories are capable of producing L-ornithine at concentrations exceeding 1 gram per liter in batch cultures and over 5 grams per liter in fed-batch fermentations, thereby establishing a robust platform for the sustainable bioproduction of this non-proteinogenic amino acid and its derivatives, which find applications in pharmaceuticals, nutraceuticals, agrochemicals, and as precursors for polyamines, proline, and other high-value metabolites.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

L-Ornithine is a non-essential, non-proteinogenic amino acid that plays a central role in the urea cycle and serves as a key metabolic intermediate in the biosynthesis of L-arginine, L-proline, glutamate, and polyamines such as putrescine and spermidine. Due to its physiological functions—including ammonia detoxification, stimulation of growth hormone secretion, enhancement of wound healing, and support of immune function—L-ornithine has gained significant commercial interest in the health and wellness industry. Industrially, it is also a valuable precursor for the synthesis of pharmaceuticals, including antiviral agents and cardiovascular therapeutics, as well as for the production of biodegradable polymers like poly(γ-glutamic acid) derivatives. Traditionally, L-ornithine has been produced by chemical synthesis or extracted from natural sources, but these methods are often environmentally taxing, energy-intensive, and yield racemic mixtures requiring costly separation. In contrast, microbial fermentation offers a sustainable, stereospecific, and scalable alternative.

Among microbial hosts, *Corynebacterium glutamicum* has been extensively engineered for L-ornithine overproduction, leveraging its natural capacity for amino acid excretion and well-characterized metabolic pathways. However, despite its success, *C. glutamicum* faces limitations in genetic tractability, tolerance to industrial process conditions, and the ability to utilize complex feedstocks such as lignocellulosic hydrolysates. In this context, the eukaryotic microorganism *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* presents a compelling alternative host due to its robustness, safety status (GRAS), advanced genetic tools, and compatibility with existing industrial fermentation infrastructure. Nevertheless, the native metabolism of *S. cerevisiae* is not naturally predisposed to L-ornithine accumulation. The endogenous L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway is tightly regulated by feedback inhibition and transcriptional repression mediated by L-arginine, and the pathway is compartmentalized between the mitochondria and cytosol, creating transport bottlenecks. Moreover, under standard aerobic conditions with high glucose concentrations, *S. cerevisiae* exhibits the Crabtree effect—a phenomenon where excess carbon is diverted to ethanol production via aerobic fermentation rather than complete oxidation through the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, thereby limiting the availability of α-ketoglutarate, the key precursor for L-ornithine biosynthesis.

Prior attempts to engineer *S. cerevisiae* for L-ornithine production have been largely unsuccessful or yielded only marginal improvements. Conventional strategies such as complete deletion of ornithine-consuming enzymes (e.g., ornithine carbamoyltransferase encoded by *ARG3*) result in L-arginine auxotrophy, necessitating costly supplementation and complicating large-scale cultivation. Furthermore, efforts to enhance flux through the mitochondrial L-ornithine pathway by overexpressing native enzymes have been hampered by insufficient precursor supply, inefficient intercompartmental metabolite transport, and the lack of coordination between upstream and downstream metabolic modules. While pathway re-localization has emerged as a promising strategy in metabolic engineering—enabling the concentration of enzymes and intermediates, avoidance of competing reactions, and mitigation of toxic intermediate accumulation—its application to L-ornithine biosynthesis in yeast has not been systematically explored. Similarly, although attenuation of the Crabtree effect through modulation of glucose uptake or respiratory chain activity has been shown to improve yields of TCA-derived products, its integration into a comprehensive L-ornithine production platform remains unaddressed.

Thus, there exists a critical need for a holistic and modular approach to rewire the metabolism of *S. cerevisiae* in a manner that simultaneously addresses regulatory control, subcellular compartmentalization, and carbon flux distribution. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel MPR strategy that decomposes the complex L-ornithine biosynthetic network into three functionally distinct modules—consumption, synthesis, and precursor supply—and applies targeted, sequential, and synergistic genetic interventions to each module. This approach not only overcomes the intrinsic limitations of yeast metabolism but also establishes a generalizable framework for the production of other amino acid-derived compounds in eukaryotic microbial cell factories.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides genetically engineered strains of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* and associated methods for the high-yield production of L-ornithine through a modular pathway rewiring (MPR) strategy. The invention is based on the systematic decomposition of the L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway into three functional modules: (1) an L-ornithine consumption module, (2) an L-ornithine synthesis module, and (3) an α-ketoglutarate precursor supply module. Each module is independently optimized through precise genetic modifications, and the resulting perturbations are integrated in a coordinated manner to achieve synergistic enhancement of L-ornithine flux.

In the first aspect, the invention employs a “leaky” L-arginine auxotrophy strategy to modulate the expression of ornithine carbamoyltransferase (*ARG3*), thereby reducing—but not eliminating—L-ornithine conversion to L-citrulline. This is achieved by replacing the native *ARG3* promoter with weaker, conditionally regulated promoters such as those of *HXT1* or *KEX2*, which maintain basal L-arginine biosynthesis sufficient for cell growth while alleviating feedback repression on the L-ornithine pathway. Additionally, deletion of *CAR2*, encoding ornithine aminotransferase, minimizes the futile transamination of L-ornithine to glutamate-γ-semialdehyde, further stabilizing intracellular L-ornithine pools.

In the second aspect, the invention addresses the challenge of subcellular compartmentalization by implementing three complementary strategies for pathway engineering. First, the native mitochondrial L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway is enhanced through overexpression of key enzymes (*ARG5,6*, *ARG7*, *ARG8*, and *ARG2*) and mitochondrial transporters (*ORT1* for ornithine export and *AGC1* for glutamate import), significantly improving flux through the acetylated derivative cycle. Second, cytosolic glutamate biosynthesis is reinforced by overexpressing *GDH1*, encoding NADPH-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase, to ensure adequate supply of the immediate precursor to the mitochondrial pathway. Third, and most effectively, the entire L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway is re-localized to the cytosol by introducing a synthetic heterologous pathway composed of genes from *Escherichia coli* (*argAEC*, *argBEc*) and *Corynebacterium glutamicum* (*argJCg*, *argCCg*, *argDCg*). This cytosolic pathway bypasses mitochondrial transport limitations and achieves superior L-ornithine production compared to engineered endogenous routes.

In the third aspect, the invention overcomes the Crabtree effect—a major bottleneck in *S. cerevisiae* metabolism—by optimizing carbon flux toward α-ketoglutarate. This is accomplished through three integrated strategies: (i) overexpression of TCA cycle and anaplerotic enzymes (*PDA1*, *PYC2*, *CIT1*, *ACO2*, *IDP1*) to enhance α-ketoglutarate synthesis; (ii) overexpression of alternative NADH oxidases (*HaAOX1* from *Hansenula anomala* and *NDI1*) to increase respiratory capacity and TCA cycle flux; and (iii) attenuation of glucose uptake by overexpressing a stabilized mutant form of the hexose transporter regulator *MTH1-ΔT*, which downregulates hexose transporter expression and shifts metabolism from fermentative to respiratory, thereby eliminating ethanol production and dramatically increasing L-ornithine yield.

Finally, the invention further enhances L-ornithine titers by engineering the urea cycle through overexpression of *CAR1*, encoding arginase, which converts L-arginine to urea and L-ornithine. This not only recycles L-arginine back into the L-ornithine pool but also reduces intracellular L-arginine levels, thereby mitigating feedback inhibition on the biosynthetic pathway. The combined implementation of all these strategies in a single strain results in an L-ornithine titer of 1,041 mg/L in shake-flask cultures—a 23-fold improvement over baseline strains—and up to 5.1 g/L in fed-batch fermentations, demonstrating the industrial viability of the platform.

The invention thus provides a comprehensive, modular, and scalable solution for the microbial production of L-ornithine in *S. cerevisiae*, with broad applicability to the biosynthesis of other amino acid-derived chemicals.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to the development of engineered *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* strains capable of high-level production of L-ornithine through a modular pathway rewiring (MPR) approach. This strategy involves the rational decomposition of the complex metabolic network leading to L-ornithine into three distinct functional modules—L-ornithine consumption, L-ornithine biosynthesis, and α-ketoglutarate precursor supply—each of which is subjected to targeted genetic interventions. The modular design allows for independent optimization and subsequent integration of beneficial perturbations, thereby enabling a systematic and synergistic enhancement of metabolic flux toward the desired product.

The L-ornithine consumption module encompasses enzymatic reactions that degrade or divert L-ornithine away from accumulation. In wild-type *S. cerevisiae*, L-ornithine synthesized in the mitochondria is transported to the cytosol, where it serves as a substrate for ornithine carbamoyltransferase (Arg3p), encoded by *ARG3*, in the L-arginine biosynthetic pathway. This reaction not only consumes L-ornithine but also subjects the entire upstream pathway to stringent feedback inhibition and transcriptional repression by L-arginine. Complete deletion of *ARG3* abolishes L-arginine biosynthesis, rendering the strain auxotrophic and requiring exogenous supplementation—a condition that is economically and operationally undesirable for industrial fermentation. To circumvent this limitation, the invention employs a “leaky” auxotrophy strategy wherein the native *ARG3* promoter is replaced with weaker, constitutive or conditionally regulated promoters such as *HXT1* (encoding a low-affinity glucose transporter) or *KEX2* (encoding a protein-processing protease). These promoters drive reduced but sufficient expression of *ARG3* to maintain minimal L-arginine biosynthesis for cell growth, while simultaneously alleviating the repressive effects of L-arginine on the L-ornithine pathway. Experimental data demonstrate that strains with the *KEX2* promoter driving *ARG3* expression (designated M1b) produce 42 mg/L of L-ornithine, a 76% increase over the *HXT1*-promoter strain (M1a, 24 mg/L), with only a 30% reduction in intracellular L-arginine, indicating effective decoupling of growth from pathway repression. Furthermore, deletion of *CAR2*, encoding ornithine aminotransferase, which catalyzes the transamination of L-ornithine to glutamate-γ-semialdehyde, provides an additional incremental improvement by blocking this competing catabolic route, yielding strain M1c with 45 mg/L L-ornithine.

The L-ornithine biosynthesis module involves the conversion of α-ketoglutarate to L-ornithine via a five-step mitochondrial pathway known as the acetylated derivative cycle. This cycle begins with the acetylation of L-glutamate by N-acetylglutamate synthase (Arg2p), followed by phosphorylation, reduction, acetylation, and finally deacetylation to regenerate L-ornithine and recycle the acetyl group. A key challenge in engineering this pathway is its strict compartmentalization within the mitochondria, which necessitates efficient transport of precursors (e.g., L-glutamate) into the organelle and products (e.g., L-ornithine) out to the cytosol. The invention addresses this through multiple strategies. First, overexpression of the mitochondrial biosynthetic enzymes *ARG5,6* (bifunctional N-acetylglutamate kinase/N-acetylglutamyl-phosphate reductase), *ARG7* (N-acetylornithine aminotransferase), and *ARG8* (N-acetylornithine deacetylase) in the M1c background (yielding M1cM2f) increases L-ornithine titer to 59 mg/L. Further overexpression of *ARG2* (N-acetylglutamate synthase) in strain M1cM2g boosts production to 80 mg/L, suggesting that acetyl-CoA availability or Arg2p activity may be limiting. Second, enhancing mitochondrial transport by overexpressing *ORT1* (mitochondrial ornithine exporter) and *AGC1* (mitochondrial glutamate importer/aspartate-glutamate exchanger) in strains M1cM2h and M1cM2k, respectively, elevates titers to 115 mg/L and 149 mg/L, underscoring the rate-limiting nature of subcellular trafficking. Third, reinforcing cytosolic glutamate supply by overexpressing *GDH1* (NADPH-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase)—the dominant glutamate biosynthetic enzyme under ammonia-rich conditions—in strain M1cM2l increases L-ornithine to 173 mg/L. Attempts to relocate glutamate biosynthesis into the mitochondria by targeting *GDH1* or *GDH2* (NADH-dependent isoform) were unsuccessful, likely due to cofactor imbalance or unfavorable mitochondrial metabolite concentrations. Most significantly, the invention introduces a synthetic cytosolic L-ornithine pathway by expressing heterologous genes: *argAEC* and *argBEc* from *E. coli*, and *argJCg*, *argCCg*, and *argDCg* from *C. glutamicum*. This chimeric pathway, assembled in strain M1cM2q, functions independently of mitochondrial transport and achieves 192 mg/L L-ornithine, demonstrating the superiority of cytosolic re-localization for this application.

The α-ketoglutarate precursor supply module governs the conversion of glucose to α-ketoglutarate via glycolysis and the TCA cycle. In *S. cerevisiae*, this flux is severely constrained by the Crabtree effect, wherein high glucose uptake rates trigger aerobic fermentation and ethanol production, diverting carbon away from the TCA cycle. The invention combats this through three complementary approaches. First, overexpression of key enzymes in the pyruvate-to-α-ketoglutarate segment—including pyruvate dehydrogenase (*PDA1*), pyruvate carboxylase (*PYC2*), citrate synthase (*CIT1*), aconitase (*ACO2*), and isocitrate dehydrogenase (*IDP1*)—in strain M1cM2qM3a increases L-ornithine to 245 mg/L. A phospho-mimetic mutant *PDA1(S313A)*, designed to bypass inhibitory phosphorylation, provides only marginal additional benefit. Second, overexpression of alternative NADH oxidases—*HaAOX1* from *H. anomala* and endogenous *NDI1*—enhances respiratory capacity and TCA cycle flux, yielding 258 mg/L and 278 mg/L L-ornithine in strains M1cM2qM3c and M1cM2qM3d, respectively. Third, and most effectively, overexpression of a truncated, stabilized variant of the hexose transporter regulator *MTH1-ΔT* downregulates hexose transporter expression, reduces glucose uptake rate from 2.2 to 0.2 g/g/h, abolishes ethanol production, and elevates L-ornithine titer to 778 mg/L in strain M1cM2qM3e. Although this strategy reduces growth rate, it proves invaluable for strain evaluation in shake-flask cultures. In industrial settings, the same metabolic shift can be achieved through glucose-limited fed-batch fermentation, as demonstrated by a 5.1 g/L titer with strain M1cM2qM3a.

Finally, the invention incorporates urea cycle engineering by overexpressing *CAR1* (arginase) in the high-performing strain M1cM2qM3e, generating strain M1dM2qM3e. This modification converts residual L-arginine back to L-ornithine and urea, further reducing feedback inhibition and increasing the final titer to 1,041 mg/L. The invention thus provides a fully integrated, multi-layered metabolic engineering platform that transforms *S. cerevisiae* into a highly efficient L-ornithine producer, with clear utility in industrial biotechnology.

## EXAMPLES

### Strain Construction of Ornithine-Overproducing Strains

The construction of L-ornithine-overproducing *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* strains was executed through a systematic, modular pathway rewiring (MPR) strategy, wherein the metabolic network from glucose to L-ornithine was partitioned into three functional modules: Module 1 (L-ornithine consumption), Module 2 (L-ornithine biosynthesis), and Module 3 (α-ketoglutarate precursor supply). All engineered strains were derived from the parental strain *S. cerevisiae* CEN.PK 113-11C (MATa SUC2 MAL2-8c his3Δ1 ura3-52), which served as a genetically stable and well-characterized chassis for metabolic engineering. Genetic modifications were implemented using established molecular biology techniques, including homologous recombination, CRISPR-Cas9-mediated genome editing, and plasmid-based expression, guided by the DNA assembler and modular pathway engineering (MOPE) methodologies. Promoter replacements, gene deletions, and gene overexpressions were performed sequentially, with each module optimized independently before integration into a unified strain architecture. Strain nomenclature followed a standardized format (e.g., M1aM2bM3c), where the alphanumeric suffixes denote the specific engineering strategy applied to each module, facilitating traceability and combinatorial analysis. For instance, Module 1 strategies included promoter swaps for *ARG3* (M1a: *HXT1* promoter; M1b: *KEX2* promoter) and deletion of *CAR2* (M1c); Module 2 strategies encompassed overexpression of native mitochondrial enzymes (M2f, M2g), mitochondrial transporters (M2h, M2k), cytosolic glutamate biosynthesis (M2l), and heterologous cytosolic pathway assembly (M2q); Module 3 strategies involved TCA cycle enhancement (M3a, M3b), respiratory chain engineering (M3c, M3d), and glucose uptake modulation (M3e). All constructs were verified by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing to ensure correct genomic integration and absence of off-target mutations. The resulting strain library, comprising over twenty distinct genotypes, enabled a comprehensive evaluation of individual and combinatorial genetic perturbations, ultimately leading to the identification of synergistic interactions that maximized L-ornithine production. Cultivation was carried out in defined minimal medium (Delft medium) under controlled aerobic conditions in shake flasks, with L-ornithine titers quantified using validated ninhydrin colorimetric assays and confirmed by GC–MS. This structured strain construction pipeline not only ensured reproducibility and scalability but also provided a robust foundation for iterative optimization and industrial translation.

### L-Arginine Leaky Auxotroph Enables L-Ornithine Overproduction

To enable L-ornithine overproduction without imposing a strict L-arginine auxotrophy, the invention implements a “leaky” auxotroph strategy by fine-tuning the expression of ornithine carbamoyltransferase, encoded by the *ARG3* gene. In wild-type *S. cerevisiae*, Arg3p catalyzes the conversion of L-ornithine to L-citrulline in the cytosol, a step essential for L-arginine biosynthesis but detrimental to L-ornithine accumulation. Moreover, the presence of L-arginine triggers potent feedback inhibition of N-acetylglutamate synthase (Arg2p) and transcriptional repression of the entire *ARG* gene cluster, effectively shutting down L-ornithine synthesis. Complete deletion of *ARG3* eliminates this consumption route but renders the strain unable to synthesize L-arginine, necessitating exogenous supplementation—a condition that is impractical for large-scale fermentation due to cost, contamination risk, and regulatory complexity. To circumvent these drawbacks, the native promoter of *ARG3* was replaced with two alternative, weaker promoters: the *HXT1* promoter, which is active under high-glucose conditions but drives low-level expression, and the *KEX2* promoter, which exhibits constitutive but low activity. Strain M1a, harboring the *HXT1* promoter driving *ARG3*, produced 24 mg/L of L-ornithine, demonstrating that partial attenuation of Arg3p activity is sufficient to initiate product accumulation. Strain M1b, with the *KEX2* promoter, achieved a 76% higher titer of 42 mg/L, indicating superior decoupling of L-arginine biosynthesis from pathway repression. Intracellular metabolite analysis revealed that L-arginine levels in M1b were only reduced by approximately 30% compared to the control, confirming that basal L-arginine synthesis was maintained for cell viability. It is hypothesized that the modest reduction in L-arginine triggered a compensatory upregulation of the L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway via the general amino acid control transcription factor Gcn4p, which activates *ARG* gene expression under amino acid starvation conditions. To further stabilize the L-ornithine pool, the *CAR2* gene, encoding ornithine aminotransferase, was deleted in the M1b background to generate strain M1c. Car2p catalyzes the transamination of L-ornithine to glutamate-γ-semialdehyde, a reaction that can drain L-ornithine under certain metabolic conditions. Although the initial impact of *CAR2* deletion was modest—increasing the titer to 45 mg/L—the modification was retained as a foundational element for subsequent pathway optimization, anticipating that its benefit would become more pronounced as L-ornithine concentrations rose. This leaky auxotrophy approach thus represents a critical innovation, balancing the dual objectives of minimizing L-ornithine consumption and maintaining cellular fitness, thereby enabling sustained overproduction without nutritional supplementation.

### Pathway Re-Localization and Subcellular Trafficking Engineering Elevates L-Ornithine Synthesis

The compartmentalization of L-ornithine biosynthesis in *S. cerevisiae*—with key steps occurring in the mitochondria and precursor/product exchange requiring specific transporters—presents a significant barrier to high-level production. To overcome this, the invention employs a multi-pronged strategy involving both enhancement of the native mitochondrial pathway and radical re-localization of the entire pathway to the cytosol. Initial efforts focused on boosting flux through the endogenous mitochondrial acetylated derivative cycle by overexpressing the core biosynthetic enzymes *ARG5,6*, *ARG7*, and *ARG8* in the M1c background, yielding strain M1cM2f with a 59 mg/L L-ornithine titer. Further overexpression of *ARG2*, encoding N-acetylglutamate synthase, in strain M1cM2g increased production to 80 mg/L, suggesting that the acetylation step is a key control point. Recognizing that metabolite transport across the mitochondrial membrane may be rate-limiting, the invention then targeted mitochondrial carriers: overexpression of *ORT1*, encoding the mitochondrial ornithine exporter, in strain M1cM2h elevated titers to 115 mg/L, while co-overexpression of *AGC1*, encoding the glutamate uniporter/aspartate-glutamate exchanger, in strain M1cM2k further increased production to 149 mg/L, highlighting the critical role of internal trafficking in pathway efficiency. Concurrently, cytosolic glutamate biosynthesis was reinforced by overexpressing *GDH1* (NADPH-dependent glutamate dehydrogenase), the dominant enzyme under ammonia-rich conditions, resulting in strain M1cM2l with 173 mg/L L-ornithine. Attempts to relocate glutamate biosynthesis into the mitochondria by targeting *GDH1* or *GDH2* (NADH-dependent isoform) were unsuccessful, likely due to cofactor imbalance or suboptimal mitochondrial metabolite concentrations. The most transformative intervention was the complete re-localization of the L-ornithine pathway to the cytosol. A synthetic operon was constructed comprising *argAEC* and *argBEc* from *Escherichia coli* and *argJCg*, *argCCg*, and *argDCg* from *Corynebacterium glutamicum*—organisms known for efficient amino acid production. This heterologous pathway, assembled in strain M1cM2q, bypassed all mitochondrial transport requirements and achieved 192 mg/L L-ornithine, surpassing all mitochondrial engineering strategies. Functional validation was confirmed by complementation of an L-arginine auxotroph with disrupted *ORT1*, proving that the cytosolic pathway could supply sufficient L-ornithine for growth. This re-localization not only eliminated transport bottlenecks but also concentrated pathway enzymes and intermediates in a single compartment, minimizing diversion into competing reactions and maximizing catalytic efficiency. Thus, pathway re-localization and subcellular trafficking engineering represent pivotal innovations that collectively elevate L-ornithine synthesis to industrially relevant levels.

### ‘Crabtree Negative’ S. cerevisiae Construction Enables Efficient Carbon Channeling to L-Ornithine

The Crabtree effect—the tendency of *S. cerevisiae* to produce ethanol aerobically under high glucose conditions—diverts substantial carbon flux away from the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, severely limiting the supply of α-ketoglutarate, the essential precursor for L-ornithine biosynthesis. To redirect carbon toward L-ornithine, the invention implements a three-tiered strategy to attenuate the Crabtree effect and enhance respiratory metabolism. First, the TCA cycle flux was amplified by overexpressing key enzymes in the pyruvate-to-α-ketoglutarate segment: pyruvate dehydrogenase (*PDA1*), pyruvate carboxylase (*PYC2*), citrate synthase (*CIT1*), aconitase (*ACO2*), and isocitrate dehydrogenase (*IDP1*). In strain M1cM2qM3a, this intervention increased L-ornithine titer to 245 mg/L. A phospho-mimetic mutant *PDA1(S313A)*, designed to evade inhibitory phosphorylation, provided only marginal additional benefit (264 mg/L in M1cM2qM3b), suggesting that other regulatory nodes dominate flux control. Second, the respiratory capacity was enhanced by overexpressing alternative NADH oxidases: *HaAOX1* from *Hansenula anomala* and the endogenous *NDI1* (NADH dehydrogenase). *HaAOX1* overexpression alone (M1cM2qM3c) yielded 258 mg/L L-ornithine, while combined overexpression with *NDI1* (M1cM2qM3d) further increased production to 278 mg/L, demonstrating that alleviating NADH accumulation stimulates TCA cycle activity. Third, and most effectively, glucose uptake was attenuated by overexpressing a truncated, stabilized variant of the hexose transporter regulator *MTH1-ΔT*. This mutation interferes with Mth1p degradation, leading to downregulation of hexose transporter expression and a dramatic reduction in glucose uptake rate—from 2.2 to 0.2 g/g/h. In strain M1cM2qM3e, this metabolic shift abolished ethanol production entirely and elevated L-ornithine titer to 778 mg/L, a fourfold increase over the parent strain. Although this strategy reduced the maximum specific growth rate, it proved invaluable for strain evaluation in shake-flask cultures. For industrial application, the same metabolic phenotype was achieved through glucose-limited fed-batch fermentation with strain M1cM2qM3a, yielding 5.1 g/L L-ornithine and 56 g/L dry cell weight. Notably, direct attenuation of α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (*KGD2* deletion) was counterproductive, decreasing L-ornithine by 90% and exacerbating ethanol production, underscoring the importance of balanced TCA cycle flux. Collectively, these interventions transform *S. cerevisiae* into a “Crabtree-negative” phenotype, enabling efficient carbon channeling from glucose to α-ketoglutarate and, ultimately, to high-level L-ornithine production.

### Example 5

Building upon the cumulative optimizations of Modules 1, 2, and 3, the final stage of strain engineering integrates urea cycle modulation to further amplify L-ornithine titers. In *S. cerevisiae*, the urea cycle enzyme arginase, encoded by *CAR1*, catalyzes the hydrolysis of L-arginine to urea and L-ornithine. While this reaction is typically associated with nitrogen catabolism, it presents a strategic opportunity to recycle L-arginine—accumulated as a byproduct of residual Arg3p activity—back into the L-ornithine pool. Moreover, by reducing intracellular L-arginine concentrations, *CAR1* overexpression mitigates the feedback inhibition and transcriptional repression that constrain the L-ornithine biosynthetic pathway. To exploit this, the *CAR1* gene was overexpressed in the high-performing strain M1cM2qM3e (which already incorporates leaky *ARG3* expression, *CAR2* deletion, cytosolic pathway re-localization, and *MTH1-ΔT*-mediated Crabtree attenuation), resulting in strain M1dM2qM3e. This final modification yielded a remarkable L-ornithine titer of 1,041 mg/L in shake-flask batch cultures, representing a 34% increase over the parent strain and a 23-fold improvement over the initial Module 1-optimized strain M1c. The success of this intervention is further supported by the known “epi-arginase” regulatory mechanism, wherein Arg3p and Car1p form a one-to-one complex that inhibits OTC activity while preserving arginase function, thereby preventing a futile cycle between L-ornithine and L-arginine. Overexpression of *CAR1* likely enhances the efficiency of this complex, ensuring that any residual L-arginine biosynthesis is immediately reversed to L-ornithine. This final engineering step exemplifies the power of the modular pathway rewiring approach, where each layer of optimization—consumption control, pathway re-localization, carbon flux redirection, and cycle engineering—builds synergistically upon the last. The resulting strain, M1dM2qM3e, not only achieves gram-per-liter titers in simple batch cultures but also demonstrates the feasibility of producing L-ornithine in *S. cerevisiae* at levels competitive with traditional bacterial platforms. Furthermore, the strategies employed—particularly cytosolic pathway re-localization and Crabtree effect attenuation—are broadly applicable to the production of other amino acid-derived compounds, establishing a versatile platform for industrial biotechnology.
