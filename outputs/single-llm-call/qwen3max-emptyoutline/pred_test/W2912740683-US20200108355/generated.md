# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Fouling represents a pervasive and economically burdensome challenge across numerous industrial sectors that rely on fluid-handling systems, particularly those involving membrane-based separation technologies. In the context of water treatment, fouling is broadly defined as the undesirable accumulation of materials—ranging from inorganic precipitates to biological entities—on the surfaces of membranes or other system components. This phenomenon manifests in several distinct forms: mineral scaling due to the precipitation of sparingly soluble salts such as calcium carbonate or barium sulfate; colloidal or particulate fouling arising from suspended solids like silt, clay, or humic substances; organic fouling driven by macromolecules including proteins, polysaccharides, lipids, and natural organic matter; and biofouling, which involves the adhesion, proliferation, and metabolic activity of microorganisms on membrane surfaces. Among these, biofouling has emerged as one of the most recalcitrant and operationally disruptive forms, especially in pressure-driven membrane processes such as reverse osmosis (RO), nanofiltration (NF), ultrafiltration (UF), and microfiltration (MF).

Biofouling initiates when planktonic microorganisms encounter a suitable surface under favorable environmental conditions—typically in the presence of nutrients, appropriate temperature, and neutral pH—and transition into a sessile lifestyle by adhering to the surface. This initial attachment is often facilitated by the presence of conditioning films composed of organic molecules, such as natural organic matter, which serve as both a nutrient source and an adhesive intermediary. Once attached, bacteria begin to proliferate and secrete extracellular polymeric substances (EPS)—a complex matrix of polysaccharides, proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids—that encase the microbial community and form a structured biofilm. The development of this biofilm is not a passive aggregation but a highly regulated process governed by intercellular communication mechanisms known as quorum sensing (QS). Through QS, bacterial populations coordinate gene expression in response to cell density, enabling synchronized behaviors such as EPS production, motility suppression, and enhanced resistance to antimicrobial agents.

The consequences of biofouling in membrane systems are severe and multifaceted. The biofilm layer acts as an additional hydraulic resistance, significantly reducing water permeability and consequently diminishing the volumetric flux through the membrane. To compensate for this loss in performance, operators must increase transmembrane pressure, leading to elevated energy consumption and operational costs. Moreover, the persistent presence of biofilms necessitates frequent chemical or physical cleaning cycles, which not only incur direct expenses but also accelerate membrane degradation, thereby shortening service life and increasing replacement frequency. In extreme cases, irreversible fouling can render membranes non-functional, requiring complete system shutdown for remediation.

Traditional strategies to mitigate biofouling have largely focused on feedwater pretreatment or post-fouling intervention. Common pretreatment methods include chlorination, ozonation, and ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, all aimed at reducing the viable microbial load in the influent stream. However, these approaches carry significant drawbacks. Chlorine, while effective as a biocide, reacts with organic constituents in water to form disinfection byproducts (DBPs)—notably trihalomethanes and haloacetic acids—that are carcinogenic and capable of permeating certain membranes, thereby compromising product water quality. Ozonation, though powerful in oxidizing organic matter, generates assimilable organic carbon (AOC), a readily biodegradable substrate that paradoxically promotes bacterial regrowth downstream. UV treatment, while avoiding chemical residuals, offers only transient microbial inactivation without residual protection, allowing rapid re-colonization once the water exits the irradiation zone.

Alternative biological control strategies have explored the disruption of QS signaling pathways using quorum quenching agents or enzymes that degrade autoinducer molecules such as N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs). While promising in laboratory settings, these approaches face scalability challenges, instability under industrial operating conditions, and limited efficacy against diverse microbial consortia. Similarly, the use of bacteriophages—viruses that specifically infect and lyse bacteria—has shown potential in controlled environments like membrane bioreactors, but their narrow host range, susceptibility to environmental stressors, and regulatory uncertainties hinder widespread adoption.

Physical and chemical cleaning protocols—ranging from backwashing and air sparging to the application of acids, alkalis, surfactants, chelating agents, and enzymatic cleaners—are routinely employed to restore membrane performance. Yet, these methods often fail to eradicate the foundational layers of biofilm, and the residual dead biomass can serve as a scaffold for rapid microbial recolonization. Furthermore, aggressive cleaning regimens can compromise membrane integrity over time, exacerbating long-term performance decline.

In light of these limitations, considerable research effort has been directed toward the development of intrinsically antifouling membranes—materials engineered at the molecular or structural level to resist microbial adhesion and biofilm formation from the outset. One prominent strategy involves surface modification through polymer blending, grafting, coating, or incorporation of antimicrobial additives. These modifications aim to alter key surface properties known to influence bacterial attachment, including hydrophilicity, surface charge, roughness, and chemical functionality. Hydrophilic surfaces, for instance, tend to form a tightly bound hydration layer that acts as a physical and energetic barrier to microbial adhesion. Similarly, negatively charged surfaces can electrostatically repel the predominantly anionic cell envelopes of most bacteria, thereby reducing initial colonization.

Natural polymers have garnered increasing interest as sustainable, biocompatible modifiers for membrane fabrication. Among these, arabic gum (AG)—a branched polysaccharide exuded by Acacia senegal and related species—has demonstrated notable potential. AG is composed of a complex mixture of neutral sugars (e.g., arabinose, galactose) and acidic residues (e.g., glucuronic and rhamnuronic acids), conferring it with amphiphilic character, high water solubility, and an abundance of hydroxyl and carboxyl functional groups. Prior studies have shown that incorporating AG into polyethersulfone (PES) membranes enhances hydrophilicity, porosity, and pure water flux while exhibiting resistance to fouling by model organisms such as Escherichia coli. However, these investigations have been limited in scope, focusing primarily on a single bacterial strain and failing to assess performance against a broader spectrum of clinically and environmentally relevant pathogens, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative species.

Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria differ fundamentally in cell wall architecture, which influences their interaction with surfaces. Gram-positive bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus, possess a thick peptidoglycan layer embedded with teichoic acids that impart a strong negative surface charge. Gram-negative bacteria, including E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, feature a thinner peptidoglycan layer overlaid by an outer membrane rich in lipopolysaccharides (LPS), which also confer net negative charge but with different spatial distribution and chemical composition. Consequently, an effective antifouling membrane must demonstrate broad-spectrum resistance across this taxonomic divide—a criterion that has not been rigorously evaluated in prior AG-modified membrane studies.

Moreover, the relationship between additive concentration and membrane performance is rarely linear. While low to moderate loadings of hydrophilic modifiers often enhance porosity and flux by accelerating phase inversion during membrane casting, excessive concentrations can increase dope solution viscosity, retard solvent–non-solvent exchange, and induce additive agglomeration, ultimately degrading membrane morphology and performance. Thus, identifying an optimal loading window is critical to balancing antifouling efficacy with structural integrity and hydraulic efficiency.

Given these gaps in the current state of the art, there exists a compelling need for a novel class of polysulfone (PS)-based membranes that integrate arabic gum in a precisely controlled manner to achieve simultaneous improvements in hydrophilicity, surface charge, porosity, and—most critically—broad-spectrum resistance to biofouling by diverse bacterial strains. Such membranes would represent a significant advancement over existing commercial and experimental alternatives by offering a sustainable, non-biocidal, and durable solution to one of the most persistent challenges in membrane technology.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel polysulfone (PS)-based composite membrane incorporating arabic gum (AG) as a functional additive, wherein the membrane exhibits significantly enhanced resistance to biofouling by both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial species, improved hydrophilicity, increased negative surface charge, optimized porosity, and elevated pure water flux compared to unmodified PS membranes and commercially available counterparts. The invention further encompasses methods for fabricating these membranes via phase inversion, wherein AG is uniformly dispersed within the PS casting solution at concentrations ranging from 0.1 wt.% to 7 wt.%, with optimal performance observed at loadings of 3 wt.% and 7 wt.%.

In one aspect, the invention relates to a composite membrane comprising a polysulfone matrix and arabic gum homogeneously distributed therein at a concentration of at least 0.1 wt.% and up to 7 wt.% relative to the total weight of the membrane-forming solution. The arabic gum is derived from natural exudates of Acacia species and consists of a branched polysaccharide containing neutral monosaccharides and acidic uronic acid residues, which collectively impart amphiphilic character and ionizable functional groups to the membrane structure.

In another aspect, the composite membrane exhibits a water contact angle of no greater than 65 degrees, preferably no greater than 55 degrees, and most preferably approximately 48 degrees when measured with a 2 µL droplet, indicating a highly hydrophilic surface that resists bacterial adhesion through the formation of a stable hydration layer. Concurrently, the membrane demonstrates a zeta potential of at least −35 mV at neutral pH (pH 7), and more preferably at least −40 mV, due to the dissociation of carboxylic acid groups present in the arabic gum component, thereby generating strong electrostatic repulsion against negatively charged bacterial cells.

In a further aspect, the membrane possesses a total porosity of at least 75%, with an average pore size ranging from 30 nm to 40 nm, and a pure water flux exceeding 250 L/m²·h·bar under standard testing conditions (1 bar pressure, 25°C). These structural and transport properties are achieved through the modulation of thermodynamic and kinetic parameters during phase inversion, wherein the hydrophilic nature of AG accelerates solvent–non-solvent exchange, promoting the formation of interconnected macrovoids and a porous surface layer without inducing defects or delamination.

Critically, the membrane of the invention demonstrates exceptional antifouling performance against a panel of clinically and environmentally relevant bacterial strains, including Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive), and Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Gram-negative). When exposed to bacterial suspensions at concentrations of 2.4 × 10⁷ CFU/mL for durations of 10 minutes or up to 24 hours, the AG-modified membranes exhibit near-complete inhibition of bacterial colonization, as evidenced by field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) showing minimal to no adherent cells. In contrast, unmodified PS membranes and commercial microfiltration membranes display extensive biofilm coverage under identical conditions.

The antifouling mechanism is non-biocidal and anti-adhesive in nature. Unlike antimicrobial membranes that kill bacteria through leaching agents or contact-killing chemistries, the present invention prevents the initial attachment of microbial cells by synergistically combining enhanced surface hydrophilicity and negative charge. This dual-action approach avoids the generation of resistant mutants, eliminates the risk of toxic leachates, and ensures long-term stability, as the active components are integral to the membrane matrix rather than surface coatings susceptible to wear or delamination.

The invention also includes a method of manufacturing the composite membrane, comprising the steps of: (a) dissolving polysulfone in N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc) to form a homogeneous dope solution at a concentration of 16 wt.% PS; (b) adding arabic gum to the dope solution at a predetermined concentration between 0.1 wt.% and 7 wt.%; (c) subjecting the mixture to sonication and mechanical stirring to ensure uniform dispersion; (d) degassing the solution to remove entrapped air; (e) casting the solution onto a glass substrate using a doctor blade with a gap height of 200 µm at a casting speed of 3 m/min; and (f) immersing the cast film in a coagulation bath of deionized water to induce phase inversion and membrane solidification. The resulting membrane is then washed and stored in aqueous medium until use.

The membranes of the invention are particularly suited for applications in water purification, wastewater treatment, seawater desalination, and biomedical filtration, where resistance to biofouling is paramount to maintaining efficiency, reducing operational costs, and ensuring consistent product quality. By addressing the root cause of biofouling—initial bacterial adhesion—rather than merely treating its symptoms, the present invention offers a durable, sustainable, and scalable solution to a longstanding industrial challenge.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a novel class of composite membranes fabricated from polysulfone (PS) and arabic gum (AG), designed to overcome the persistent problem of biofouling in membrane-based separation processes. The membranes are engineered to exhibit superior antifouling properties through intrinsic surface modifications that deter microbial adhesion without relying on biocidal agents, thereby ensuring long-term performance, environmental compatibility, and operational safety. The following detailed description sets forth the composition, structure, fabrication methodology, and functional characteristics of the invented membranes, supported by empirical data demonstrating their efficacy across a spectrum of bacterial contaminants.

The base polymer used in the invention is polysulfone, a high-performance engineering thermoplastic known for its excellent mechanical strength, thermal stability, chemical resistance, and film-forming capability. Polysulfone with a molecular weight of approximately 35 kDa is employed, dissolved in N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAc)—a polar aprotic solvent that effectively solvates PS chains—to form a casting solution at a concentration of 16 wt.% polymer. This concentration is selected to balance solution viscosity for castability with the ability to form defect-free asymmetric membranes during phase inversion. To this base solution, arabic gum is introduced as a functional additive at varying loadings: 0.1 wt.%, 1 wt.%, 3 wt.%, 5 wt.%, and 7 wt.% relative to the total weight of the casting solution. A control membrane containing 0 wt.% AG is also prepared for comparative analysis.

Arabic gum, a natural exudate obtained from Acacia senegal and related species, is a complex, branched heteropolysaccharide composed of a backbone of β(1→3)-linked D-galactopyranosyl units with side chains containing L-arabinofuranose, L-rhamnose, and D-glucuronic acid residues. The presence of glucuronic and rhamnuronic acids imparts a significant number of carboxylic acid functional groups, which are partially ionized under neutral and alkaline conditions, contributing to the molecule’s polyanionic character. Additionally, the abundance of hydroxyl groups renders AG highly hydrophilic and water-soluble. These physicochemical attributes are leveraged in the present invention to modify the surface and bulk properties of PS membranes during fabrication.

The fabrication process begins with the preparation of the dope solution. Polysulfone is first dissolved in DMAc under continuous mechanical stirring at ambient temperature until a clear, viscous solution is obtained. Arabica gum, in powder form, is then added incrementally to aliquots of this solution corresponding to the desired AG loading levels. The mixtures are subjected to probe sonication for one hour using a Q500 sonicator while simultaneously being stirred on a digital hotplate stirrer. This dual-action treatment ensures thorough deagglomeration and uniform dispersion of AG throughout the polymeric matrix, preventing localized clustering that could compromise membrane homogeneity. Following sonication, the solutions are degassed under vacuum for two hours to eliminate entrapped air bubbles, which could otherwise lead to pinhole defects or surface irregularities in the final membrane.

The degassed solutions are then cast onto clean glass plates using a Labcoat Master casting system equipped with a doctor blade. The casting parameters are tightly controlled: a knife gap height of 200 µm and a casting speed of 3 meters per minute are maintained to ensure consistent film thickness and surface smoothness. Immediately after casting, the glass plate is immersed vertically into a coagulation bath filled with deionized water at room temperature. This immersion triggers the phase inversion process, wherein the water (non-solvent) diffuses into the cast film while DMAc (solvent) diffuses out, leading to the thermodynamic destabilization of the polymer solution and subsequent precipitation of the PS/AG composite into a solid, porous membrane. The rate of this solvent–non-solvent exchange is critically influenced by the hydrophilicity of AG; higher AG content accelerates demixing, promoting the formation of a more open and interconnected pore structure.

After precipitation, the nascent membranes are removed from the glass substrate, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to remove residual solvent, and stored in water until characterization or testing. This storage protocol prevents membrane drying, which could collapse pores and alter surface properties.

Morphological analysis via field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) reveals profound structural differences between AG-modified and unmodified membranes. Surface images show that neat PS membranes (0 wt.% AG) exhibit a relatively dense skin layer with sparse, small pores. In contrast, membranes containing AG display increasingly porous surfaces as AG loading increases, with M4 (3 wt.% AG) and M6 (7 wt.% AG) showing abundant, well-defined pores without visible defects such as cracks or pinholes. Cross-sectional views further illustrate that AG incorporation induces the formation of elongated finger-like macrovoids extending from the surface into the substructure, surrounded by a sponge-like matrix. These macrovoids are larger and more numerous in membranes with 3–5 wt.% AG, consistent with accelerated phase separation. At 7 wt.% AG, a slight reduction in macrovoid size is observed, attributed to increased solution viscosity retarding demixing kinetics—a phenomenon well-documented in polymer/additive systems.

Quantitative characterization confirms these qualitative observations. Membrane porosity, determined gravimetrically, increases from approximately 61% for neat PS to a maximum of 76% for M4 (3 wt.% AG), representing a 24% enhancement. Porosity slightly declines to 73% at 7 wt.% AG, corroborating the viscosity-mediated suppression of instantaneous demixing at high additive loadings. Average pore size, calculated using the Guerout-Elford-Ferry equation based on pure water permeation data, follows a similar trend: rising from 24 nm (neat PS) to 39 nm (M4), then decreasing to 28 nm (M6). These structural enhancements directly translate into improved hydraulic performance. Pure water flux measurements, conducted in a dead-end stirred cell at 1 bar and 25°C, show that M4 achieves a flux of 265 L/m²·h·bar—more than double that of the control (120 L/m²·h·bar). Even at 7 wt.% AG, flux remains elevated at 210 L/m²·h·bar, underscoring the robustness of the design.

Surface properties are equally transformative. Contact angle measurements using a 2 µL water droplet demonstrate a progressive increase in hydrophilicity with AG content: from 87° for neat PS to 48° for M6 (7 wt.% AG), a 45% reduction. This pronounced wettability arises from the orientation of AG’s hydrophilic moieties toward the membrane-water interface during phase inversion, creating a surface enriched in hydroxyl and carboxyl groups. Concurrently, zeta potential analysis across a pH range of 3–10 reveals that AG-modified membranes exhibit significantly more negative surface charges than controls. At pH 7, the zeta potential of M6 reaches −42 mV, compared to −20 mV for neat PS—a 110% increase in magnitude. This enhanced negative charge stems from the dissociation of carboxylic acid groups in AG, which remain ionized across a wide pH spectrum (pKa ~3–4).

These combined surface traits—high hydrophilicity and strong negative charge—underpin the membrane’s exceptional antifouling performance. Biofouling tests were conducted using four bacterial strains: Staphylococcus aureus (Gram-positive), and Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Gram-negative). Membranes were disinfected, equilibrated in buffer, and immersed in bacterial suspensions (2.4 × 10⁷ CFU/mL) for either 10 minutes or 24 hours. Post-exposure, samples were fixed, dried, sputter-coated, and imaged via FESEM.

Results are unequivocal. Commercial PS membranes (PM UP150) and neat PS controls show dense, confluent layers of adherent bacteria across all strains and exposure times. In stark contrast, AG-modified membranes—particularly M4 and M6—exhibit near-complete absence of bacterial cells. Even after 24 hours of incubation with E. coli, M6 shows no appreciable colonization, whereas the control is fully covered. Notably, bacterial morphology remains intact on AG membranes, with no signs of lysis or deformation, confirming a non-biocidal, anti-adhesive mechanism. The few S. aureus cells occasionally observed on M6 are attributed to minor AG agglomeration at high loadings, creating localized hydrophobic patches, but overall coverage remains negligible (<5% surface area).

The anti-adhesion effect is explained by two synergistic phenomena. First, the hydrophilic surface binds a tightly associated layer of water molecules, creating an energetic barrier that prevents bacterial approach and attachment—the so-called “hydration layer” effect. Second, the strong negative surface charge electrostatically repels the negatively charged bacterial envelopes: teichoic acids in Gram-positives and lipopolysaccharides in Gram-negatives both carry net negative charges at neutral pH. Together, these forces drastically reduce the probability of initial adhesion, the critical first step in biofilm formation.

Importantly, the invention avoids the pitfalls of conventional antifouling strategies. No toxic leachates are produced, as AG is non-toxic and immobilized within the matrix. No DBPs are formed, as no oxidants are used. Resistance development is unlikely, as the mechanism is physical rather than biochemical. And performance is durable, as the active components are bulk-integrated, not surface-coated.

In summary, the PS/AG composite membranes of the present invention represent a paradigm shift in antifouling membrane design—offering a sustainable, effective, and scalable solution to biofouling through intelligent material engineering. By harnessing the natural properties of arabic gum to tune surface chemistry and morphology, the invention delivers membranes that are not only high-performing but inherently resistant to the most challenging form of fouling in water treatment systems.
