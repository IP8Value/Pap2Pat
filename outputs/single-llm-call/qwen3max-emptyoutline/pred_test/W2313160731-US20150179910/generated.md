# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of thermal energy systems, and more particularly to novel organic compounds suitable for use as heat transfer fluids (HTFs) in high-temperature industrial applications, including concentrated solar power (CSP) plants, thermal energy storage systems, and other processes requiring efficient and stable thermal transport media. The invention further encompasses synthetic methodologies for producing these compounds, wherein the synthesis is conducted under resource-efficient conditions—specifically, through one-pot sequential reactions combining etherification and Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling transformations, often under solvent-minimized or solventless conditions. The resulting aryl ether-based compounds exhibit a unique combination of thermal stability, elevated specific heat capacity, low viscosity, wide liquidus temperature range, and oxidative resistance at elevated temperatures, thereby addressing critical limitations of conventional HTFs such as mineral oils, synthetic aromatics, silicone oils, and molten salts. The technical domain thus spans chemical engineering, materials science, sustainable chemistry, and renewable energy technology, with particular emphasis on the molecular design and green synthesis of functional organic fluids optimized for thermophysical performance in energy conversion and storage infrastructure.

## BACKGROUND ART

Heat transfer fluids play a pivotal role in numerous industrial and energy-related processes by facilitating the transport, storage, and exchange of thermal energy. In concentrated solar power (CSP) systems, for instance, HTFs absorb solar radiation at elevated temperatures and convey this thermal energy to a working fluid that drives a turbine or to a thermal storage medium for later use. The efficiency, safety, cost-effectiveness, and environmental impact of such systems are heavily dependent on the physicochemical properties of the HTF employed. Historically, a variety of substances have been utilized as HTFs, each with distinct advantages and drawbacks. Water and steam, while possessing excellent thermal properties such as high specific heat capacity and thermal conductivity, are limited to relatively low operating temperatures due to their critical point and high vapor pressure at elevated temperatures, necessitating high-pressure containment systems that increase capital and operational costs. Mineral oils and petroleum-derived hydrocarbons have been widely used in mid-temperature ranges (up to approximately 300°C), but they suffer from thermal instability, oxidative degradation, flammability, and relatively poor heat capacity compared to water. Synthetic organic fluids—such as diphenyl oxide/biphenyl eutectics (e.g., Dowtherm A), alkylated aromatics, and polyalkylene glycols—offer improved thermal stability and higher boiling points, yet they remain susceptible to decomposition above 350°C, often releasing volatile and potentially toxic byproducts. Moreover, many synthetic HTFs exhibit high viscosity at ambient temperatures, requiring preheating or auxiliary pumping systems, which reduces overall system efficiency.

Inorganic alternatives, such as molten nitrate salts (typically a mixture of sodium nitrate and potassium nitrate), are employed in CSP plants operating between 290°C and 565°C due to their high thermal stability, non-flammability, and reasonable heat capacity. However, these salts have significant disadvantages: they solidify at relatively high temperatures (melting point ~220–240°C), necessitating continuous heating during standby to prevent freezing and potential damage to piping; they are corrosive to metallic components, requiring expensive alloy construction; and their thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity, while acceptable, are not optimal. Liquid metals such as sodium offer exceptional thermal conductivity and high boiling points but pose severe safety hazards due to reactivity with air and water, limiting their practical deployment. Silicone-based oils provide good thermal stability and low toxicity but are costly and exhibit lower specific heat capacities than desired.

A critical challenge in HTF development lies in achieving a balanced set of properties: a high specific heat capacity (to maximize energy storage per unit mass), high thermal conductivity (to enhance heat transfer rates), low viscosity (to minimize pumping power), a wide liquid temperature range (low melting point and high boiling/decomposition point), high flash and fire points (for safety), chemical and oxidative stability at operating temperatures, low environmental impact, and cost-effectiveness. Conventional HTFs typically excel in only a subset of these criteria. For example, water has excellent heat capacity and conductivity but poor high-temperature operability; molten salts are stable at high temperatures but freeze easily; synthetic organics offer moderate performance across several parameters but degrade over time and lack sufficient heat capacity.

Furthermore, the synthesis of candidate HTF molecules has traditionally relied on multi-step organic transformations involving stoichiometric reagents, hazardous solvents, and extensive purification protocols—processes that are not only economically burdensome but also environmentally unsustainable. Chromatographic purification using silica gel and large volumes of organic solvents generates significant waste, contradicting principles of green chemistry. The need for inert atmospheres (e.g., nitrogen or argon) in many catalytic reactions further increases complexity and cost. Consequently, there exists a long-felt but unmet need for both novel HTF compounds that overcome the thermophysical limitations of existing fluids and synthetic routes that align with sustainability goals by minimizing solvent use, avoiding chromatography where possible, enabling one-pot operations, and functioning under ambient conditions.

Prior art includes various attempts to design aromatic ethers as HTFs due to their inherent thermal stability conferred by the ether linkage and aromatic rings. However, these efforts have largely focused on simple diaryl or alkyl aryl ethers without structural elaboration to enhance heat capacity or suppress crystallization. Moreover, their syntheses typically involve Williamson ether synthesis under anhydrous conditions with strong bases, followed by separate functionalization steps, resulting in low atom economy and high E-factors. Cross-coupling methodologies such as Suzuki-Miyaura reactions have been used to construct biaryl motifs in advanced materials, but their integration with ether-forming reactions in a single pot—especially under solventless or minimally solvated conditions—has not been systematically explored for HTF development. The concurrent execution of phase-transfer-catalyzed etherification and palladium-catalyzed C–C bond formation represents a significant methodological gap in the prior art, particularly when aimed at producing structurally complex, high-performance HTFs with minimal environmental footprint.

Thus, despite decades of research, no commercially available HTF simultaneously offers high specific heat capacity (>1.3 J/g·K), thermal stability beyond 300°C in air, low viscosity at ambient temperatures, a melting point below 0°C, and a synthesis pathway that is scalable, solvent-efficient, and chromatography-free. The present invention addresses this deficiency by introducing a new class of extended benzyl aryl ethers—specifically, biphenyl-containing benzyl alkyl/aryl ethers and their derivatives—that exhibit superior thermophysical profiles and can be prepared via innovative one-pot sequential protocols that significantly reduce solvent consumption and purification burden, thereby advancing both performance and sustainability in thermal energy systems.

## DISCLOSURE OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel organic compounds specifically designed for use as high-performance heat transfer fluids (HTFs) in industrial thermal systems, particularly those operating at elevated temperatures up to 300°C or higher. These compounds are characterized as substituted benzyl ethers containing extended aromatic architectures, including biphenyl moieties, optionally functionalized with hydroxymethyl or alkoxy groups, and linked to alkyl or aryl alcohol-derived chains. The core structural motif consists of a benzyl ether linkage (–CH₂–O–R) where the benzyl group is substituted with one or more aryl rings—preferably phenyl or substituted phenyl groups—introduced via palladium-catalyzed cross-coupling, and R represents a linear or branched alkyl chain (e.g., n-pentyl, 2-phenylethyl) or an arylalkyl group. In preferred embodiments, the compounds are bis-ether derivatives formed by etherification of diols derived from reduction of formyl-substituted biphenyl ethers, resulting in molecules with two benzyl ether functionalities connected through a central biphenyl core. These structural features collectively confer enhanced specific heat capacity, suppressed crystallinity (maintaining liquidity over a broad temperature range), and exceptional thermal oxidative stability.

A key aspect of the invention is the synthetic methodology employed to produce these HTF candidates. The invention discloses a one-pot sequential reaction protocol that integrates phase-transfer-catalyzed (PTC) etherification with Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling, thereby enabling the direct conversion of bromobenzyl halides and alcohols into complex aryl ether products in a single reaction vessel without intermediate isolation or extensive purification. In certain embodiments, the etherification step is conducted under solventless conditions using ground potassium hydroxide as base and a quaternary ammonium salt (e.g., benzyltributylammonium bromide, BTBAC) as phase-transfer catalyst at temperatures up to 75°C. Following completion of ether formation, the same reaction mixture is subjected to Suzuki-Miyaura coupling by addition of an arylboronic acid, a palladium catalyst (e.g., Pd(PPh₃)₂Cl₂ or Pd(PPh₃)₄), a phosphine ligand if necessary, a mild aqueous base (e.g., Na₂CO₃), and a minimal amount of co-solvent (e.g., dioxane or THF) to facilitate the cross-coupling while maintaining overall solvent economy. Notably, the entire sequence can be performed under ambient atmosphere, eliminating the need for inert gas purging—a significant advantage for scalability and cost reduction.

The resulting compounds exhibit a specific heat capacity of at least 1.30 J/g·K at 300 K, as determined experimentally and corroborated by group contribution estimation methods. Thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) demonstrates that these fluids remain stable up to at least 300°C in air, with minimal weight loss indicative of oxidative or thermal decomposition. Their melting points are sufficiently low—often below room temperature—due to molecular asymmetry and steric hindrance imparted by the biphenyl and alkyl substituents, which frustrate crystalline packing and ensure liquid state across the operational range of many CSP and industrial heating systems. Density measurements confirm a linear decrease with increasing temperature up to 90°C, consistent with predictable thermal expansion behavior required for system design. Furthermore, the compounds display low viscosity relative to conventional synthetic HTFs, reducing pumping power requirements.

The invention thus comprises both the novel chemical entities themselves and the integrated synthetic process for their preparation. The HTFs of the invention are non-corrosive, non-aqueous, and free of heavy metals or halogenated solvents, enhancing their environmental and safety profile. By combining molecular design for superior thermophysical properties with a green, one-pot synthesis that minimizes solvent use and avoids chromatographic purification in many cases, the invention provides a holistic solution to longstanding challenges in thermal energy technology. The disclosed compounds are particularly suited for use in next-generation CSP plants, industrial waste heat recovery systems, and high-temperature process heating applications where reliability, efficiency, and sustainability are paramount.

## MODE(S) FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The invention may be carried out through the following detailed modes, which describe both the synthesis of the novel heat transfer fluid compounds and their characterization for thermophysical performance. The synthetic strategy is centered on a one-pot sequential protocol that begins with the etherification of a bromobenzyl halide with an alcohol under phase-transfer catalysis, followed in situ by a Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling reaction to install extended aromatic systems. This approach maximizes atom economy, minimizes solvent consumption, and reduces purification steps, aligning with green chemistry principles while delivering structurally complex HTF candidates with tailored properties.

In a first mode of carrying out the invention, a bromobenzyl bromide (e.g., 4-bromobenzyl bromide or 3-bromobenzyl bromide) is combined with an alcohol (e.g., n-pentanol, 2-phenylethanol) and ground potassium hydroxide in the presence of a phase-transfer catalyst such as benzyltributylammonium bromide (BTBAC). The reaction is conducted under solventless conditions at a temperature between 60°C and 80°C, preferably 75°C, for a duration of 12 to 48 hours, depending on the reactivity of the alcohol and the physical state (solid or liquid) of the reactants. It has been discovered that this etherification proceeds efficiently even when one or both reactants are solids, provided sufficient mixing and thermal energy are applied. Upon completion, as monitored by thin-layer chromatography or NMR spectroscopy, the crude benzyl ether product is obtained in sufficient purity for direct use in the subsequent coupling step without chromatographic purification in many cases. For instance, when 2-phenylethanol is employed, the resulting 4-bromobenzyl 2-phenylethyl ether precipitates upon addition of water and can be isolated by filtration in high purity.

In a second mode, the crude or purified benzyl ether containing a bromoaryl group is subjected to Suzuki-Miyaura cross-coupling in the same reaction vessel. To this end, an arylboronic acid (e.g., 4-methoxyphenylboronic acid, 3-formyl-4-methoxyphenylboronic acid), a palladium catalyst such as tetrakis(triphenylphosphine)palladium(0) [Pd(PPh₃)₄] or dichlorobis(triphenylphosphine)palladium(II) [Pd(PPh₃)₂Cl₂], additional triphenylphosphine if needed, and an aqueous solution of sodium carbonate are added to the reaction mixture. A minimal volume of a water-miscible organic co-solvent such as 1,4-dioxane or tetrahydrofuran (THF)—typically 2–5 mL per mmol of substrate—is introduced to create a biphasic system that facilitates the cross-coupling while maintaining overall low solvent usage. The reaction is heated to 70–90°C for 12–24 hours under ambient atmosphere. This modification overcomes the limitations observed when using palladium on carbon (Pd/C) under strictly solventless conditions, which led to low yields with electron-deficient boronic acids due to catalyst deactivation in air. The use of well-defined phosphine-ligated palladium complexes in a minimally solvated biphasic system enables high-yielding coupling even with challenging substrates, affording biphenyl-containing benzyl ethers such as 5g and 5h in yields exceeding 70%.

In a third mode, functional group interconversion is performed to further enhance thermophysical properties. Specifically, formyl groups introduced via coupling with formyl-substituted boronic acids are reduced to hydroxymethyl groups using sodium borohydride in a mixture of methanol and dioxane (or THF) at 0°C to room temperature. The resulting benzylic alcohols (e.g., 6a, 6b) are isolated either by extraction after aqueous workup—sufficiently pure for direct use—or by silica gel chromatography if minor impurities persist. These alcohols are then subjected to a second etherification with another equivalent of bromobenzyl bromide under the original PTC conditions (BTBAC, KOH, 75–80°C) to yield bis-benzyl ether derivatives (e.g., 7a, 7b). Although this final step is currently performed separately to allow full characterization of the diether products, the methodology is compatible with further one-pot extension in future implementations.

The resulting compounds are characterized by standard analytical techniques: melting points are determined using a calibrated apparatus; NMR spectroscopy (¹H at 400 MHz, ¹³C at 100 MHz) confirms structure and purity; and elemental analysis validates composition. Critically, thermophysical properties are evaluated to assess HTF suitability. Specific heat capacity (Cp) is measured via differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and found to be at least 1.30 J/g·K at 300 K—for example, compound 2d exhibits an experimental Cp of 1.35 J/g·K. Group contribution methods (e.g., Kolsk’s three-level model) are used to estimate Cp as a function of temperature, showing good agreement with experimental data for simpler ethers and providing predictive capability for extended structures. Melting and boiling points are estimated using Marrero and Gani’s group contribution model and validated experimentally where possible; extended ethers consistently remain liquid at room temperature due to conformational disorder. Thermal stability is assessed by TGA under air, revealing decomposition onset temperatures above 300°C for compounds such as 8b, confirming suitability for high-temperature operation. Density is measured across 20–90°C, showing linear thermal expansion with coefficients suitable for engineering calculations.

The HTFs of the invention are deployed in thermal systems by charging them into closed-loop circulation circuits equipped with pumps, heat exchangers, and storage tanks. Their low viscosity ensures manageable pressure drops, while high Cp reduces mass flow requirements for a given thermal load. Oxidative stability up to 300°C eliminates the need for inert blanketing in many applications, simplifying system design. The absence of water prevents corrosion and freezing issues, and the organic nature allows compatibility with standard elastomers and metals used in industrial piping.

Thus, the invention is carried out through an integrated approach of molecular design, sustainable synthesis, and rigorous thermophysical validation, delivering a new generation of heat transfer fluids that outperform conventional options in both performance and environmental metrics.
