# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD AND INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular pharmacology and signal transduction, specifically to novel peptide-based inhibitors that selectively modulate the activity of Src family kinases by targeting a functional signaling complex formed between the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit and Src kinase. More particularly, the invention provides engineered peptides derived from defined structural domains of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit that bind to and inhibit Src kinase with high potency and selectivity, thereby disrupting pathological signaling cascades initiated through the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src receptor complex without broadly suppressing global Src activity. The invention further encompasses cell-permeable formulations of these peptides, achieved through conjugation to cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) moieties, which enable targeted delivery to the plasma membrane compartment where the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex resides.

The industrial applicability of the invention spans multiple therapeutic and diagnostic domains. In the pharmaceutical industry, the disclosed peptides—particularly the optimized variant designated herein as “NaKtide” and its cell-permeable derivative “pNaKtide”—represent a new class of signal-selective kinase modulators with potential utility in treating diseases driven by aberrant activation of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src signaling axis. Such conditions include, but are not limited to, cardiac hypertrophy, heart failure, renal fibrosis, certain forms of cancer, and other proliferative or inflammatory disorders wherein cardiotonic steroids (e.g., ouabain) or endogenous ligands trigger pathological signaling through this receptor complex. Unlike conventional broad-spectrum Src inhibitors such as PP2, which non-selectively suppress all Src-dependent pathways and thereby induce significant off-target toxicities, the peptides of the present invention exhibit functional selectivity by preferentially inhibiting only the pool of Src that is physically associated with Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase at the plasma membrane. This spatial and mechanistic specificity enhances therapeutic index and reduces adverse effects.

Moreover, the invention has significant utility in basic and applied research. The peptides serve as precise molecular tools for dissecting the role of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex in cellular physiology and disease models, enabling researchers to interrogate this specific signaling node without perturbing other Src-mediated functions such as integrin signaling, growth factor receptor transactivation, or cytoskeletal dynamics. Additionally, the design principles underlying NaKtide—namely, the identification of an endogenous regulatory domain within a transmembrane pump that allosterically controls a cytosolic kinase—open new avenues for the development of “receptor-targeted” kinase inhibitors across other multi-protein signaling platforms. From a biotechnology perspective, the successful coupling of NaKtide to HIV-Tat and penetratin CPPs demonstrates a generalizable strategy for engineering subcellularly targeted therapeutics, with implications for drug delivery beyond kinase inhibition. The invention thus holds substantial commercial value in drug discovery, precision medicine, and molecular diagnostics, offering a platform technology for developing next-generation, context-dependent modulators of intracellular signaling networks.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Historically, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase was viewed exclusively as an ion transporter responsible for maintaining electrochemical gradients across the plasma membrane by actively pumping three sodium ions out of the cell and two potassium ions into the cell per molecule of ATP hydrolyzed. This canonical function is essential for cellular homeostasis, neuronal excitability, and renal electrolyte balance. However, over the past two decades, accumulating evidence has revealed that the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase also serves as a scaffold for organizing multi-protein signaling complexes, thereby functioning as a receptor for cardiotonic steroids such as ouabain. Upon binding of ouabain to the extracellular domain of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit, a conformational change is transmitted across the membrane, leading to the activation of associated non-receptor tyrosine kinases, most notably Src. This activated Src then initiates downstream signaling cascades—including the Ras/Raf/MEK/ERK pathway—that regulate gene expression, cell growth, proliferation, and survival. Critically, this signaling function is distinct from the ion-pumping activity of the pump and can occur even when ion transport is partially inhibited.

Despite this paradigm shift, therapeutic strategies targeting the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src signaling axis have remained underdeveloped due to a lack of selective molecular tools. Conventional approaches have relied on small-molecule Src kinase inhibitors such as PP2, which competitively bind to the ATP-binding pocket of Src and related kinases. While effective in blocking Src catalytic activity in vitro, these inhibitors suffer from poor selectivity, affecting numerous other kinases and disrupting essential physiological processes. For instance, systemic administration of pan-Src inhibitors has been associated with immunosuppression, impaired wound healing, and gastrointestinal toxicity in preclinical models. Moreover, because only a fraction of cellular Src is engaged in the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase complex—estimates suggest approximately 25% in renal epithelial cells—the use of global Src inhibitors leads to unnecessary suppression of non-pathological Src functions, thereby limiting their clinical utility.

Prior attempts to develop more selective inhibitors have focused on disrupting protein–protein interactions (PPIs) rather than catalytic sites. However, PPIs are traditionally considered "undruggable" due to their large, flat interfaces and lack of deep pockets suitable for small-molecule binding. Peptide-based inhibitors offer a promising alternative, as they can mimic key interaction motifs and achieve high affinity and specificity. Nevertheless, early efforts to target the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src interface were hampered by incomplete mapping of the interaction domains and poor cellular delivery of candidate peptides. Although it was known that the large cytoplasmic C-terminal domain (CD3) of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit binds and inhibits Src, the precise region responsible for this interaction remained undefined. Furthermore, full-length CD3 is too large for practical therapeutic use and lacks cell permeability.

Compounding these challenges, existing cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) technologies often result in non-specific intracellular distribution, sequestering cargo in endosomal compartments rather than delivering it to the intended site of action—here, the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane. For example, conjugation to penetratin, a commonly used CPP, frequently leads to accumulation in cytoplasmic vesicles, rendering the inhibitor ineffective against membrane-localized targets. Thus, there existed a critical unmet need for a rationally designed, membrane-targeted peptide inhibitor that could specifically disrupt the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex with minimal off-target effects.

The present invention addresses these longstanding limitations by first identifying the minimal Src-interacting domain within the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit through systematic domain truncation and binding assays. Building upon this structural insight, the inventors synthesized overlapping peptides spanning this domain and identified a single 20-amino acid sequence—designated NaKtide—that potently and selectively inhibits Src. Crucially, when fused to the HIV-Tat CPP, NaKtide (as pNaKtide) not only gains efficient cellular entry but also preferentially localizes to the plasma membrane, where it effectively competes with endogenous Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase for Src binding. This spatial targeting enables pNaKtide to act as a functional antagonist of ouabain-induced signaling, blocking pathological ERK activation and cardiac myocyte hypertrophy without interfering with growth factor–mediated ERK signaling. These features collectively represent a significant advance over the prior art, providing the first truly selective pharmacological tool for modulating the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src signaling complex.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel peptide compositions and methods for selectively inhibiting the Src kinase activity associated with the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit, thereby modulating signal transduction pathways initiated through the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src receptor complex. The invention is based on the discovery that a specific 20-amino acid sequence within the N-domain of the cytoplasmic region of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit—herein designated as “NaKtide”—binds directly to Src kinase and functions as a potent and selective inhibitor. Unlike conventional ATP-competitive Src inhibitors, NaKtide operates through an allosteric mechanism by disrupting the physical interaction between Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Src, thereby preventing ouabain-induced conformational activation of Src without globally suppressing basal Src activity.

In one aspect, the invention provides an isolated peptide comprising the amino acid sequence of NaKtide, which corresponds to residues 389–408 of the porcine Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit (or the equivalent sequence in human or other mammalian orthologs). This peptide exhibits an IC₅₀ of approximately 70 nM for Src inhibition in vitro and demonstrates at least 40-fold selectivity for Src over the closely related Src family kinase Lyn, with no detectable inhibition of serine/threonine kinases such as PKC at concentrations up to 10 μM. A control peptide, designated C1, comprising residues 369–388 of the same domain, shows no inhibitory activity and serves as a negative control for experimental validation.

In another aspect, the invention provides cell-permeable derivatives of NaKtide, generated by covalent conjugation to a cell-penetrating peptide (CPP) moiety. Preferably, the CPP is derived from the HIV-Tat protein (residues 48–57: YGRKKRRQRRR), resulting in a fusion peptide designated “pNaKtide.” Surprisingly, conjugation to HIV-Tat not only confers efficient cellular uptake but also enhances the intrinsic inhibitory potency of NaKtide, reducing the IC₅₀ to approximately 4 nM in vitro. Moreover, pNaKtide exhibits unique subcellular localization, accumulating predominantly at the plasma membrane—the precise site of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex—thereby maximizing its functional efficacy as a targeted inhibitor. Alternative CPPs, such as penetratin, may be used but result in less favorable distribution, with predominant sequestration in intracellular vesicles.

The invention further provides methods of using NaKtide or pNaKtide to antagonize ouabain-induced signaling. Specifically, pNaKtide blocks the formation of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex in a dose-dependent manner, as demonstrated by fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assays, and abolishes ouabain-triggered activation of downstream effectors including ERK1/2. Critically, pNaKtide does not inhibit ERK activation induced by insulin-like growth factor (IGF-1), confirming its selectivity for the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase–dependent signaling arm. This functional selectivity translates into therapeutic efficacy in cellular models of disease: pNaKtide completely prevents ouabain-induced hypertrophic growth in neonatal rat cardiac myocytes at low micromolar concentrations, without affecting basal cell physiology.

Additionally, the invention encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising NaKtide or pNaKtide in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, as well as kits for research or diagnostic use containing the peptide alongside appropriate controls and instructions. The peptides may be formulated for parenteral, oral, or localized delivery and are contemplated for use in treating conditions characterized by pathological activation of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src pathway, including cardiac hypertrophy, heart failure, renal fibrosis, and certain cancers. The invention thus represents a paradigm-shifting approach to kinase inhibition—moving from catalytic site blockade to spatially restricted disruption of disease-specific signaling complexes—offering improved safety, efficacy, and mechanistic precision over existing therapies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENT

### Binding of Src to Na+/K+-ATPase Forms a Functional Signaling Complex

The foundation of the present invention lies in the elucidation of a direct and functionally significant interaction between the cytoplasmic domain of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit and the non-receptor tyrosine kinase Src. Contrary to the long-held view of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase as merely an ion pump, it is now established that this transmembrane protein serves as a scaffold for assembling a multi-component signaling complex that includes Src kinase as a core constituent. This complex is not static; rather, it functions as a dynamic receptor whose conformational state is modulated by extracellular ligands such as cardiotonic steroids (e.g., ouabain). Under basal conditions, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit maintains Src in an autoinhibited, closed conformation through direct physical interaction, thereby suppressing its kinase activity. Upon ouabain binding to the extracellular face of the pump, a conformational wave propagates through the transmembrane helices to the cytoplasmic domains, leading to the release of Src from its autoinhibitory constraints and subsequent activation. This activated Src then phosphorylates downstream substrates, initiating cascades such as the Ras/MAPK pathway that culminate in changes in gene expression, cell growth, and survival.

The interaction between Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Src occurs primarily through the large cytoplasmic loop connecting transmembrane segments M4 and M5, commonly referred to as the CD3 domain. This domain comprises both the phosphorylation (P) domain and the nucleotide-binding (N) domain, with the latter being more solvent-exposed and thus accessible to cytosolic proteins like Src. Through a series of GST pull-down assays using progressively truncated fragments of the CD3 domain, the inventors mapped the minimal Src-binding region to a segment within the N-domain, specifically residues 379–594 (designated ND). Further refinement localized the interaction to the N-terminal portion of ND, residues 379–475 (ND2), and ultimately to an even smaller fragment, residues 379–435 (ND1). Notably, the complementary fragment ND1R (residues 436–594) showed negligible binding, confirming that the Src-interaction motif is confined to the ND1 subdomain. Structural modeling based on the crystal structure of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase indicates that ND1 is surface-exposed and does not participate in ATP coordination, making it an ideal candidate for protein–protein interactions.

This physical association is not merely structural but is functionally consequential. Co-immunoprecipitation experiments from native tissues and cultured cells confirm that endogenous Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Src exist in a complex under physiological conditions. Moreover, fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis in live cells expressing fluorescently tagged Src and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase demonstrates close proximity (<10 nm) consistent with direct binding. Disruption of this complex—either by ouabain treatment or by competitive peptides—leads to measurable increases in Src autophosphorylation at Tyr-418, a hallmark of activation. Thus, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex represents a bona fide signaling receptor whose integrity is essential for maintaining Src in a quiescent state, and whose perturbation drives pathological signaling. The identification of ND1 as the critical interaction module provides the molecular basis for the rational design of targeted inhibitors described herein.

### Functional Characterization of Src-Interacting Na+/K+-ATPase Using RNA Interference Assay

To validate the physiological relevance of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src signaling complex and to establish a cellular model for testing inhibitors, the inventors employed RNA interference (RNAi) to knock down endogenous Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 expression in pig kidney proximal tubule LLC-PK1 cells. A stable knockdown cell line, designated TCN23-19, was generated and characterized. As expected, TCN23-19 cells exhibited significantly reduced levels of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 protein compared to parental LLC-PK1 cells. Importantly, this reduction correlated with elevated basal activity of both Src and its downstream effector ERK1/2, as measured by phosphorylation-specific antibodies. This observation is consistent with the model wherein Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase acts as a tonic inhibitor of Src; loss of the pump removes this brake, leading to constitutive Src activation.

The TCN23-19 cell line served as a critical tool for assessing the selectivity of candidate inhibitors. When treated with the cell-permeable NaKtide derivative pNaKtide, LLC-PK1 cells showed only modest (~10%) and statistically insignificant inhibition of basal Src activity, whereas TCN23-19 cells exhibited significantly greater suppression (>20%). This differential effect strongly suggests that pNaKtide preferentially targets the pool of Src that is normally restrained by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. In the absence of the pump, more Src is free in the cytosol, but paradoxically, this “free” Src appears more susceptible to inhibition by pNaKtide—likely because the peptide can access a larger fraction of the kinase population that is no longer shielded within the complex. Alternatively, the conformational state of Src in the absence of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase may render it more vulnerable to peptide inhibition.

Further confirmation of the Src-dependence of observed effects was obtained using SYF cells—mouse embryonic fibroblasts genetically deficient in Src, Yes, and Fyn—and their Src-rescued counterparts (SYF + Src). In SYF cells, neither basal nor stimulated ERK1/2 activity was affected by pNaKtide, demonstrating that the peptide’s action requires the presence of Src family kinases. In contrast, SYF + Src cells responded to pNaKtide with significant ERK1/2 inhibition, mirroring the response seen in wild-type cells. These genetic complementation experiments provide compelling evidence that the biological effects of pNaKtide are mediated specifically through Src and not through off-target mechanisms.

Collectively, these RNAi and knockout studies underscore the functional interdependence of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and Src and provide a robust cellular framework for evaluating the potency and selectivity of NaKtide-based inhibitors. They confirm that the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex is not an artifact of overexpression systems but a physiologically relevant signaling unit whose modulation has predictable consequences on kinase activity and downstream signaling.

### Example III

In this example, the inventors synthesized four overlapping 20-mer peptides spanning the entire ND1 domain (residues 379–435) of the porcine Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase α1 subunit to identify the minimal sequence responsible for Src inhibition. Based on the crystal structure of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, peptides 1 (residues 369–388) and 2 (389–408) were predicted to reside in unstructured loops, whereas peptides 3 (409–428) and 4 (429–448) were located in regions with potential α-helical character. Each peptide was tested in an in vitro Src kinase assay wherein purified recombinant Src was incubated with increasing concentrations of peptide prior to addition of ATP and measurement of autophosphorylation at Tyr-418 by Western blot.

Peptide 3 emerged as the most potent inhibitor, achieving nearly complete suppression of Src activity at 1 μM, with an IC₅₀ of approximately 70 nM. Peptide 4 showed partial inhibition, while peptides 1 and 2 had no effect even at 10 μM. Sequence alignment confirmed that peptide 3 corresponds to residues 389–408 of the mature α1 subunit (accounting for signal peptide cleavage), and this sequence was designated “NaKtide.” Peptide 1, lacking activity, was designated “C1” and used as a negative control in all subsequent experiments.

To assess selectivity, NaKtide was tested against Lyn, another Src family kinase. While NaKtide did inhibit Lyn, the IC₅₀ was ~2.5 μM—over 35-fold higher than for Src—indicating significant preference for Src. In contrast, neither NaKtide nor C1 affected PKC activity in a PepTag-based assay up to 10 μM, confirming lack of cross-reactivity with serine/threonine kinases. Mechanistically, NaKtide’s inhibition was unaffected by varying ATP concentrations (0.1–2 mM), ruling out competition with ATP and supporting an allosteric mode of action. These data established NaKtide as a potent, selective, and non-ATP-competitive Src inhibitor derived from an endogenous regulatory domain of Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase.

### Example IV

To overcome the inherent cell impermeability of NaKtide, the inventors conjugated it to cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs). Two constructs were generated: HIV-Tat-NaKtide (pNaKtide) and penetratin-NaKtide (AP-NaKtide), along with corresponding control peptides (pC1 and AP-C1). In vitro kinase assays revealed that pNaKtide exhibited enhanced potency, with an IC₅₀ of ~4 nM—nearly 20-fold lower than unconjugated NaKtide—suggesting that the Tat moiety may stabilize the active conformation of the peptide or improve solubility. AP-NaKtide showed similar enhancement.

Cellular uptake and localization were assessed using FITC-labeled peptides in LLC-PK1 cells. Confocal microscopy showed that pNaKtide rapidly entered cells within 30–60 minutes and accumulated predominantly at the plasma membrane, colocalizing with endogenous Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. In stark contrast, AP-NaKtide was largely trapped in intracellular vesicles, with minimal plasma membrane presence. This differential localization is critical: because the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex resides at the plasma membrane, only pNaKtide is positioned to effectively compete for Src binding. Functional assays confirmed this: pNaKtide, but not AP-NaKtide, blocked ouabain-induced ERK activation. Thus, the choice of CPP dictates not only delivery efficiency but also subcellular targeting, with HIV-Tat uniquely enabling membrane-localized action.

### Example V

The ability of pNaKtide to disrupt the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase/Src complex was directly tested using FRET. LLC-PK1 cells were co-transfected with Src-ECFP (donor) and YFP-α1 (acceptor). In untreated cells, significant FRET efficiency (~15%) confirmed close proximity of Src and Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Upon treatment with pNaKtide, FRET decreased in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC₅₀ of ~1 μM for complex disruption—higher than the in vitro IC₅₀ due to cellular barriers. This demonstrates that pNaKtide competes with endogenous Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase for Src binding, preventing stable complex formation. Control pC1 had no effect. These data provide direct visual evidence that pNaKtide functions as a molecular wedge at the plasma membrane.

### Example VI

The functional selectivity of pNaKtide was demonstrated by comparing its effects on ouabain- versus IGF-1–induced ERK activation. In LLC-PK1 cells and primary cardiac myocytes, 1 μM pNaKtide completely abolished ouabain-triggered ERK phosphorylation but had no effect on IGF-1–stimulated ERK activation. In contrast, the pan-Src inhibitor PP2 blocked both pathways. This proves that pNaKtide specifically targets the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase–dependent arm of Src signaling, sparing growth factor receptor pathways. This selectivity is therapeutically crucial, as it avoids the toxicities associated with global Src inhibition.

### Example VII

The therapeutic potential of pNaKtide was validated in a model of cardiac hypertrophy. Neonatal rat cardiac myocytes were pretreated with pNaKtide and then exposed to ouabain for 48 hours. Ouabain induced significant cell enlargement, a hallmark of hypertrophy. pNaKtide blocked this effect in a dose-dependent manner, with complete inhibition at 2 μM. Cell volume and number were quantified by Coulter counting, confirming that pNaKtide specifically prevents pathological growth without affecting basal myocyte size. This establishes pNaKtide as a promising candidate for treating cardiac remodeling in heart failure and hypertension.
