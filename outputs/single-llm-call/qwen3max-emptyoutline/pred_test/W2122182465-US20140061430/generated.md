# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to solar energy systems and, more specifically, to an image-based sun position sensor integrated with a high-precision dual-axis solar tracking system for use in photovoltaic (PV) and high-concentration photovoltaic (HCPV) applications. The invention provides a novel optical configuration comprising a self-designed reflecting Cassegrain telescope coupled with a digital imaging device, such as a high-resolution webcam, and an embedded image processing algorithm that enables accurate and stable determination of the sun’s position under varying atmospheric conditions—including partial cloud cover, haze, and low irradiance environments. This system is particularly suited for deployment in HCPV systems, which require extremely precise alignment with direct sunlight due to their narrow acceptance angles, but it is also applicable to conventional flat-plate or single-axis PV systems seeking enhanced energy yield through improved tracking fidelity. The invention further encompasses methods for real-time image acquisition, noise filtering, edge detection, and centroid calculation of the solar disk to generate corrective feedback signals for actuating a dual-axis solar tracker, thereby ensuring optimal orientation of the photovoltaic receiver toward the sun throughout the day. By overcoming the limitations inherent in traditional bar-shadow and four-quadrant photodiode-based sun sensors—such as sensitivity to photodetector mismatch, reduced performance under diffuse lighting, and susceptibility to environmental degradation—the present invention establishes a robust, cost-effective, and highly accurate solution for maximizing solar energy capture across diverse geographic and climatic conditions.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE RELATED ART

Solar energy harvesting has long been recognized as a critical component in the global transition toward sustainable and renewable power sources. Among the various technologies employed to harness solar radiation, photovoltaic (PV) systems have seen widespread adoption due to their modularity, scalability, and declining cost per watt. However, the efficiency of these systems is inherently limited by the fixed orientation of conventional flat-plate panels, which results in suboptimal exposure to incident solar irradiance over the course of a day. To address this limitation, solar tracking systems have been developed to dynamically reorient PV modules so that they remain perpendicular to the sun’s rays, thereby increasing energy output by up to 50% compared to static installations, depending on latitude and local weather patterns. In high-concentration photovoltaic (HCPV) systems, where sunlight is focused onto small, high-efficiency multi-junction cells using optical concentrators, the requirement for precise alignment becomes even more stringent; deviations as small as a few tenths of a degree can lead to significant losses in power generation due to the extremely narrow acceptance angle of the concentrator optics.

Historically, two primary approaches have been employed for sun tracking: open-loop and closed-loop control strategies. Open-loop systems rely on astronomical algorithms to predict the sun’s position based on time, date, and geographic coordinates, without any real-time feedback from the environment. While computationally simple and immune to sensor failure, these systems are vulnerable to cumulative errors arising from mechanical drift, installation misalignment, and atmospheric refraction effects, which can degrade tracking accuracy over time. In contrast, closed-loop systems utilize physical sensors to detect the actual position of the sun and provide continuous feedback to the tracking mechanism, enabling real-time correction of pointing errors. Among the most common closed-loop sensors are bar-shadow type photosensors, which consist of a central opaque shadow bar surrounded by four photodiodes positioned at the cardinal directions (north, south, east, west). Under ideal conditions, when the sensor is perfectly aligned with the sun, the shadow bar casts no shadow on the photodiodes, and the differential signals between opposing pairs (e.g., east-west and north-south) approach zero. The tracking controller adjusts the azimuth and elevation axes until these differences fall below predefined thresholds, thereby achieving alignment.

Despite their simplicity and low cost, bar-shadow sensors suffer from several well-documented drawbacks. First, they exhibit poor performance under cloudy or overcast conditions, where diffuse skylight dominates over direct beam radiation, leading to ambiguous or erroneous signal differentials that prevent accurate sun localization. Second, manufacturing tolerances and aging effects often result in characteristic mismatches among the four photodiodes—differences in spectral response, responsivity, or dark current—which introduce systematic biases into the tracking error signal. These mismatches cannot be fully compensated through calibration and tend to worsen over time, especially in harsh outdoor environments. Third, the spatial resolution of such sensors is inherently limited by the physical dimensions of the shadow bar and photodiode array, making them unsuitable for applications requiring sub-degree accuracy, such as HCPV systems.

To mitigate these issues, alternative designs based on four-quadrant light sensors have been proposed. These sensors replace the discrete photodiodes with a monolithic quadrant photodetector, improving uniformity and reducing inter-element mismatch. Some implementations incorporate a pinhole aperture to project a small solar image onto the detector surface, enhancing angular resolution. While this approach offers modest improvements in consistency and linearity, it remains fundamentally constrained by the same limitations as bar-shadow sensors: dependence on direct beam irradiance, vulnerability to low-light conditions, and inability to distinguish the true solar disk from bright sky patches or reflections. Consequently, the power output of PV systems employing such sensors still degrades significantly during periods of partial cloud cover or early morning/late evening operation when solar elevation is low and atmospheric scattering is high.

In response to these challenges, researchers have explored image-based sun sensing techniques that leverage digital imaging technology to capture and analyze the actual solar disk. Early implementations in aerospace applications utilized charge-coupled device (CCD) cameras equipped with narrowband filters to detect the sun against the dark background of space, achieving accuracies better than 0.01°. Terrestrial adaptations have included systems combining commercial webcams with polarizing filters or multiple-aperture optical heads to enhance contrast and reduce glare. One such system demonstrated a tracking accuracy of 0.1° and exhibited greater immunity to varying weather conditions compared to photodiode-based sensors. However, these existing image-based solutions often lack sufficient optical magnification to resolve fine details of the solar disk, particularly when using low-cost, off-the-shelf imaging hardware. Without adequate image scale, pixel-level quantization errors and digitization noise can significantly impair the precision of centroid estimation, especially when the solar image occupies only a few pixels on the sensor array. Furthermore, many prior art systems do not incorporate advanced image processing pipelines capable of robustly segmenting the sun from complex backgrounds containing clouds, glare, or other bright objects, leading to false detections or unstable tracking behavior.

Optical design also plays a crucial role in the performance of image-based sun sensors. Conventional refractive telescopes, while effective at magnification, suffer from chromatic aberration and thermal instability, which can distort the solar image and introduce pointing errors. Reflective telescopes, such as the Cassegrain configuration, offer superior optical performance by eliminating chromatic effects and providing compact form factors. However, standard Cassegrain designs are typically optimized for astronomical observation rather than real-time solar tracking, and their integration with consumer-grade imaging devices presents significant engineering challenges related to focal length, field of view, and mechanical packaging. Prior attempts to adapt such telescopes for solar tracking have either failed to achieve sufficient resolution or resulted in bulky, expensive assemblies incompatible with commercial PV installations.

Moreover, the computational algorithms used to extract sun position from digital images have historically been simplistic, often relying on thresholding or centroid-of-brightness methods that assume a uniform, circular solar disk. These assumptions break down under real-world conditions where the sun may be partially obscured by clouds, distorted by atmospheric turbulence, or contaminated by lens flare and sensor blooming. Advanced edge detection and geometric fitting techniques—such as the three-point circle method—are rarely implemented in embedded tracking controllers due to perceived computational complexity, despite their potential to improve accuracy and robustness.

In summary, while the concept of image-based sun tracking has been recognized in the literature, no existing solution adequately addresses the combined requirements of high angular resolution, environmental robustness, cost-effectiveness, and mechanical compactness necessary for widespread deployment in terrestrial PV and HCPV systems. There remains a critical need for an integrated sun position sensor that combines a purpose-built optical system with a sophisticated yet efficient image processing pipeline to deliver sub-0.1° tracking accuracy under all operational conditions, including low irradiance and partial occlusion. The present invention fulfills this need by introducing a novel reflecting Cassegrain telescope specifically engineered for solar imaging, coupled with a high-resolution webcam and a proprietary image analysis algorithm that enables reliable, high-precision sun localization regardless of ambient lighting conditions.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a high-accuracy, image-based sun position sensor and associated solar tracking system designed to overcome the limitations of conventional photodiode-based and rudimentary image-based sun sensors. The core innovation lies in the integration of a custom-designed reflecting Cassegrain telescope with a digital imaging device and a specialized image processing algorithm, resulting in a compact, robust, and highly precise sun tracking solution suitable for both high-concentration photovoltaic (HCPV) and standard photovoltaic (PV) applications. The reflecting Cassegrain telescope is engineered to produce a clear, enlarged, and well-defined image of the solar disk on the image sensor plane, thereby enabling accurate determination of the sun’s center even under challenging atmospheric conditions such as partial cloud cover, haze, or low solar elevation. Unlike refractive optical systems, the all-reflective design eliminates chromatic aberration and ensures thermal stability, while the inclusion of a right-angle prism and an adjustable eyepiece allows for a compact physical footprint—approximately 10 centimeters in length—facilitating easy mounting on existing solar tracking structures without adding significant weight or wind load.

The digital imaging component of the system employs a commercially available high-resolution webcam, selected for its balance of image quality, frame rate, and cost-effectiveness. In preferred embodiments, the webcam features a resolution of at least 2,304 × 1,536 pixels, although lower-resolution variants may be used in cost-sensitive applications with appropriate trade-offs in tracking accuracy. The combination of the telescope’s magnification (adjustable between 5× and 15×, with 15× being optimal) and the high pixel density of the image sensor yields a system resolution of 0.0017 degrees per pixel, which forms the foundation for sub-0.1° tracking precision. Critically, the system does not rely on direct beam irradiance alone; instead, it leverages the full spatial information contained in the solar image, allowing it to maintain functionality even when the sun is partially obscured or when diffuse light dominates.

The image processing algorithm constitutes a key aspect of the invention, enabling robust and accurate extraction of the sun’s position from raw image data. The algorithm begins by converting the input RGB image into the HSL (Hue, Saturation, Lightness) color space, which decouples brightness from chromatic information and facilitates more reliable thresholding under variable lighting. A dynamic binarization step isolates the solar disk by applying a lightness threshold that effectively distinguishes the sun from background clouds and sky. Subsequently, edge detection is performed using the Sobel operator to identify the boundary of the solar disk with high fidelity. The central coordinates of the sun are then computed using a geometric three-point circle method: three non-collinear points are selected from the detected edge, and the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the chords formed by these points is calculated to determine the circle’s center. This method is repeated across multiple point triplets, and the final centroid is determined by statistical consensus—specifically, the coordinate pair that appears most frequently among all computed centers—thereby mitigating the effects of noise, partial occlusion, or edge irregularities.

The resulting sun position coordinates are compared against the center of the image frame, which corresponds to the optical axis of the tracking system. Any deviation generates an error signal that is fed to a dual-axis actuator controller, which adjusts the azimuth and elevation angles of the solar tracker to minimize the offset. The system incorporates a configurable threshold window (e.g., 5 or 10 pixels) to prevent unnecessary actuation due to minor fluctuations or sensor noise, thereby enhancing stability without compromising accuracy. Experimental validation has demonstrated tracking errors of less than 0.04° in both X and Y coordinates under a 5-pixel threshold setting, with consistent performance maintained even when more than half of the solar disk is shadowed.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, self-contained sun tracking solution that achieves unprecedented levels of accuracy and reliability through the synergistic combination of purpose-built optics, high-resolution imaging, and intelligent image processing. It eliminates the photodetector mismatch and low-light sensitivity issues of traditional sensors, operates effectively in diverse weather conditions, and is mechanically compatible with standard solar tracking infrastructure. The system is particularly advantageous for HCPV applications, where tracking precision directly impacts energy yield, but it also offers substantial benefits for conventional PV systems seeking to maximize daily energy harvest. By enabling consistent, high-fidelity sun tracking without reliance on subsidies or ideal weather, the invention supports the broader economic viability and scalability of solar power as a competitive energy source.

## DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is best understood through a detailed description of its preferred embodiments, which illustrate the integration of optical, mechanical, electronic, and algorithmic components into a unified sun tracking system. The system comprises three principal subsystems: (1) an image-based sun position sensor incorporating a self-designed reflecting Cassegrain telescope and a digital imaging device; (2) a dual-axis solar tracker actuated by precision motors; and (3) an embedded tracking controller executing a specialized image processing algorithm to compute sun position and generate corrective commands. Each of these subsystems is described in turn, with emphasis on their novel features and synergistic interactions.

The image-based sun position sensor is the cornerstone of the invention. Its optical assembly is based on a modified Cassegrain telescope configuration, chosen for its reflective nature, compactness, and ability to produce high-contrast images free from chromatic aberration. The telescope consists of two concave primary mirrors, a convex secondary mirror, a right-angle prism, and an adjustable eyepiece. Incident sunlight first strikes the primary concave mirror, which reflects the light toward the convex secondary mirror. The secondary mirror then redirects the converging beam through the right-angle prism, which folds the optical path by 90 degrees to reduce the overall length of the assembly. Finally, the light passes through the eyepiece, which serves both to further magnify the image and to focus it onto the image sensor plane of the attached webcam. This folded design allows the entire optical train to be housed within a tube approximately 10 centimeters long, making it lightweight and easily mountable on standard solar tracker structures without requiring significant structural reinforcement.

The selection of reflective optics over refractive elements is deliberate and critical to the system’s performance. Refractive lenses suffer from wavelength-dependent focal shifts (chromatic aberration), which can blur the solar image and introduce positional errors, especially under broadband solar illumination. Moreover, temperature changes can cause refractive indices to vary, leading to thermal defocus. In contrast, the all-mirror design of the Cassegrain telescope is achromatic and thermally stable, ensuring consistent image quality across a wide range of operating temperatures. The mirrors are coated with high-reflectivity aluminum or dielectric coatings to maximize throughput while minimizing absorption-induced heating.

The eyepiece is designed to be interchangeable or adjustable, allowing the user to select a magnification level between 5× and 15× depending on the application requirements. Higher magnification increases the size of the solar image on the sensor, improving centroid resolution but reducing the field of view and potentially increasing sensitivity to tracker jitter. Lower magnification offers a wider field of view, which may be beneficial during initial acquisition or in high-wind conditions. Through empirical testing, a 15× magnification was found to provide the optimal balance for high-precision tracking, yielding a system resolution of 0.0017 degrees per pixel when paired with a high-resolution webcam (e.g., Logitech C920 with 2,304 × 1,536 pixel resolution).

The digital imaging device is a standard USB webcam, selected for its availability, low cost, and compatibility with embedded computing platforms. While scientific-grade CCD or CMOS sensors could offer superior performance, the use of a commercial webcam significantly reduces system cost and complexity, aligning with the goal of practical, scalable deployment. The webcam is mounted directly to the eyepiece housing, with its image sensor positioned precisely at the focal plane of the optical system. No additional filters are strictly required, as the image processing algorithm is robust to variations in intensity and color; however, in some embodiments, a neutral density (ND) filter or infrared (IR) cut filter may be added to prevent sensor saturation or to reduce thermal loading on the optical components.

The dual-axis solar tracker is a conventional mechanical structure capable of independent rotation about azimuth (horizontal) and elevation (vertical) axes. It is driven by stepper motors or servo motors equipped with high-resolution encoders, enabling precise angular positioning. The tracker is designed to support the photovoltaic receiver (e.g., HCPV module or flat panel) as well as the sun position sensor, which is co-aligned with the receiver to ensure that both share the same line of sight. The mechanical design emphasizes rigidity and low backlash to minimize pointing errors due to structural flexure or gear play.

The embedded tracking controller is implemented on a microprocessor or single-board computer (e.g., Raspberry Pi, Arduino with co-processor, or industrial PLC) and executes the core image processing algorithm in real time. The algorithm operates in a continuous loop: first, it acquires a new image frame from the webcam; second, it processes the image to locate the sun; third, it computes the tracking error; and fourth, it sends corrective commands to the motor drivers if the error exceeds a predefined threshold.

The image processing pipeline begins with color space conversion. The raw RGB image is transformed into the HSL (Hue, Saturation, Lightness) color model using the standard conversion formulae. The lightness channel (L) is isolated because it represents the perceived brightness of each pixel independent of its color, making it more suitable for thresholding under variable lighting conditions. A binary image is then generated by applying a lightness threshold: pixels with L values above the threshold are set to white (indicating potential sun pixels), and those below are set to black. The threshold value is selected empirically to maximize the completeness of the solar disk while minimizing inclusion of background noise; in practice, a range of thresholds may be tested, and the one yielding the most circular and contiguous region is chosen.

Next, edge detection is performed using the Sobel operator, a gradient-based method that highlights regions of high spatial frequency corresponding to object boundaries. The resulting edge map contains the outline of the solar disk, even if partially obscured by clouds. From this edge map, the algorithm selects multiple sets of three non-collinear points. For each set, it calculates the center of the circle passing through the three points using the three-point circle method. Given three points (x₁, y₁), (x₂, y₂), and (x₃, y₃), the center (x, y) is computed as:

x = (b₂c₁ − b₁c₂) / (a₂b₁ − a₁b₂)  
y = (a₂c₁ − a₁c₂) / (a₁b₂ − a₂b₁)

where  
a₁ = x₁ − x₂, a₂ = x₁ − x₃  
b₁ = y₁ − y₂, b₂ = y₁ − y₃  
c₁ = (x₂² − x₁² + y₂² − y₁²)/2  
c₂ = (x₃² − x₁² + y₃² − y₁²)/2

This computation is repeated for numerous point triplets distributed around the detected edge. The final sun center is determined by identifying the coordinate pair that occurs most frequently among all computed centers—a voting mechanism that effectively rejects outliers caused by noise, partial occlusion, or edge fragmentation.

The computed sun center (x_sun, y_sun) is then compared to the center of the image frame (x_center, y_center), which corresponds to the optical axis of the tracking system. The differences Δx = x_sun − x_center and Δy = y_sun − y_center represent the angular tracking errors in the horizontal and vertical directions, respectively. These errors are converted to motor commands using a proportional control law or a more sophisticated PID controller. However, to prevent excessive actuation due to minor fluctuations, a deadband or threshold window is applied: if |Δx| and |Δy| are both less than a preset number of pixels (e.g., 5 or 10), no correction is issued. This threshold is carefully chosen to balance accuracy and stability; experimental results show that a 5-pixel threshold yields tracking errors below 0.04° in both axes, while a 10-pixel threshold results in slightly higher but still acceptable errors (0.07° and 0.05°, respectively).

The system has been rigorously tested using a sun image simulator that replicates realistic solar trajectories and atmospheric conditions. The simulator employs a high-resolution camera with ND400 and IR filters to capture authentic solar images, which are then projected onto a screen to serve as a dynamic target for the tracking system. Tests under partial occlusion—where up to 60% of the solar disk is shadowed—demonstrate that the tracking accuracy remains within 0.04°, confirming the algorithm’s robustness to incomplete solar images. Furthermore, the system performs reliably under low-irradiance conditions where traditional sensors fail, owing to its reliance on spatial image features rather than absolute intensity levels.

In field deployment, the sun position sensor is mounted on the solar tracker such that its optical axis is parallel to the normal vector of the photovoltaic receiver. Initialization involves a coarse alignment using GPS-derived sun position estimates, followed by fine-tuning via the image-based feedback loop. Once operational, the system requires no recalibration and maintains high accuracy over extended periods, as it is immune to photodetector drift and environmental degradation.

In conclusion, the preferred embodiment of the invention represents a significant advancement in solar tracking technology, combining innovative optical design, accessible imaging hardware, and intelligent software to deliver a high-performance, low-maintenance, and economically viable solution for maximizing solar energy harvest across a wide range of applications and environmental conditions.
