## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

N-Acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) is a valuable amino sugar with broad applications in the pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and cosmetic industries. It serves as a key building block for chitin, hyaluronic acid, and other glycosaminoglycans, and has demonstrated therapeutic potential in the treatment of osteoarthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and certain autoimmune disorders. Traditionally, GlcNAc has been produced through the chemical hydrolysis of chitin, a process that is environmentally burdensome, energy-intensive, and yields products with variable purity. In response to these limitations, microbial biosynthesis using engineered microorganisms has emerged as a sustainable and scalable alternative.

Among microbial hosts, *Bacillus subtilis* is particularly attractive due to its Generally Recognized As Safe (GRAS) status, well-characterized genetics, capacity for high-density fermentation, and absence of endotoxin production. Recent advances in synthetic biology have enabled the construction of recombinant *B. subtilis* strains capable of synthesizing GlcNAc from simple carbon sources such as glucose. These engineered strains typically overexpress two key enzymes: glucosamine-6-phosphate synthase (GlmS), which converts fructose-6-phosphate and glutamine into glucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcN-6-P), and glucosamine-6-phosphate N-acetyltransferase (Gna1), which acetylates GlcN-6-P to form N-acetylglucosamine-6-phosphate (GlcNAc-6-P). A subsequent dephosphorylation step, presumed to be catalyzed by endogenous phosphatases, yields free GlcNAc, which is then exported from the cell.

However, despite successful pathway implementation, industrial-scale production of GlcNAc in *B. subtilis* has been hampered by unexpectedly low volumetric productivity and poor cell growth under minimal medium conditions—conditions that are economically favorable for large-scale fermentation. Prior engineering efforts have focused on enhancing the supply of metabolic precursors—fructose-6-phosphate, acetyl-CoA, and glutamine—under the assumption that precursor limitation constrains flux through the synthetic pathway. Yet, even when precursor availability was genetically augmented or supplemented exogenously, neither growth nor GlcNAc production improved significantly. This paradox suggested that the bottleneck lay not in precursor supply, but within the synthetic pathway itself or in an unanticipated interaction between the pathway and native cellular metabolism.

Conventional metabolic engineering approaches, which rely heavily on steady-state metabolomics and transcriptomics, often fail to capture transient dynamics that reveal kinetic bottlenecks. In particular, the accumulation of pathway intermediates may indicate enzymatic insufficiency, product inhibition, or—critically—an uncharacterized side reaction that diverts flux away from the desired product. In the context of GlcNAc biosynthesis, prior studies did not account for the possibility of a futile cycle, wherein the end product is re-phosphorylated, consuming ATP without net synthesis and leading to toxic accumulation of phosphorylated intermediates. Such cycles are notoriously difficult to detect using static measurements, as they manifest primarily during dynamic transitions in metabolic activity.

Moreover, while *B. subtilis* lacks a known dedicated GlcNAc kinase, promiscuous activity of existing kinases—particularly those involved in sugar metabolism—could inadvertently phosphorylate GlcNAc, thereby regenerating GlcNAc-6-P. This would create a closed loop between GlcNAc-6-P and GlcNAc, driven by the opposing actions of Gna1 (acetyltransferase), a putative phosphatase (dephosphorylation), and an unknown kinase (re-phosphorylation). The resulting ATP drain would impair cellular energy homeostasis, reduce growth rates, and limit overall productivity. Identifying and eliminating such a futile cycle requires a combination of dynamic metabolite tracking, isotopic tracer analysis, and targeted genetic intervention.

The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel method for identifying and resolving metabolic bottlenecks through dynamic pathway analysis, coupled with the development of a genetically optimized *B. subtilis* strain in which a previously unrecognized ATP-dissipating futile cycle has been disrupted. This approach not only resolves the specific problem of GlcNAc overproduction but also provides a generalizable framework for diagnosing and correcting inefficiencies in other synthetic metabolic pathways.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a genetically engineered *Bacillus subtilis* strain capable of high-yield, high-titer production of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) in minimal medium containing glucose as the sole carbon source, wherein the strain has been modified to eliminate a futile phosphorylation–dephosphorylation cycle between GlcNAc and GlcNAc-6-phosphate. This futile cycle, mediated by the promiscuous activity of the endogenous glucokinase GlcK, results in the ATP-dependent re-phosphorylation of GlcNAc to GlcNAc-6-P, thereby dissipating cellular energy and causing toxic accumulation of GlcNAc-6-P. By deleting the *glcK* gene, the invention prevents this energy-wasting cycle, restores cellular energy charge, alleviates phosphosugar stress, and significantly enhances both cell growth and GlcNAc productivity.

In one embodiment, the engineered *B. subtilis* strain is derived from a parental GlcNAc-producing strain that already incorporates modifications to block GlcNAc catabolism and overexpress key biosynthetic enzymes. Specifically, the parental strain contains deletions of the genes *nagP*, *gamP*, *nagA*, *nagB*, and *gamA*, which collectively encode transporters and catabolic enzymes responsible for the uptake and degradation of GlcNAc and its derivatives. This ensures that any synthesized GlcNAc is not re-assimilated by the cell. Additionally, the parental strain overexpresses *glmS* under the control of an inducible promoter (e.g., *PxylA*) and *gna1* under the control of a constitutive promoter (e.g., *P43*), thereby driving flux through the synthetic pathway from fructose-6-phosphate and glutamine to GlcNAc-6-P and subsequently to GlcNAc.

Despite these optimizations, the parental strain exhibits severely impaired growth and low GlcNAc productivity in minimal glucose medium. Steady-state metabolomic profiling reveals drastically elevated intracellular concentrations of GlcNAc-6-P—exceeding 30 mM compared to less than 0.1 mM in wild-type cells—while concentrations of central metabolic precursors such as fructose-6-phosphate, citrate, and glutamine are markedly reduced. However, experimental modulation of precursor supply—through mutation of phosphofructokinase to increase fructose-6-phosphate availability or overexpression of glutamine synthetase to enhance glutamine pools—fails to restore growth or improve productivity, indicating that precursor limitation is not the primary constraint.

To uncover the true bottleneck, the inventors employed a dynamic metabolomics approach. Cells were first starved of carbon to deplete metabolic intermediates and halt GlcNAc synthesis, then rapidly re-fed with glucose to induce a synchronized burst of pathway activity. Within the first two minutes of glucose addition, intracellular levels of fructose-6-phosphate and glutamine surged well above the Michaelis constants (*Km*) of their respective biosynthetic enzymes, confirming that precursor supply was not limiting. Concurrently, GlcNAc-6-P accumulated rapidly, while free GlcNAc levels initially decreased, suggesting that GlcNAc was being consumed rather than produced during this early phase.

This counterintuitive observation led to the hypothesis of a futile cycle in which GlcNAc is re-phosphorylated to GlcNAc-6-P. To test this, the inventors performed a dynamic isotopic labeling experiment using uniformly labeled [U-¹³C]glucose. If GlcNAc-6-P were synthesized solely via the canonical pathway from glucose, it would immediately incorporate ¹³C labels. However, within the first 60 seconds of glucose addition, the concentration of unlabeled (M+0) GlcNAc-6-P increased by 36%, while fully labeled (M+8) GlcNAc-6-P was undetectable. This demonstrates that the initial surge in GlcNAc-6-P originates not from de novo synthesis, but from the re-phosphorylation of pre-existing, unlabeled intracellular GlcNAc—a hallmark of a futile cycle.

Bioinformatic analysis identified *glcK*, encoding glucokinase, as a candidate enzyme responsible for this promiscuous phosphorylation activity, based on its sequence homology to the well-characterized GlcNAc kinase NagK from *Escherichia coli*. Although GlcK is annotated as a glucokinase, its substrate specificity in *B. subtilis* had not been exhaustively characterized. To validate this hypothesis, the inventors constructed a *glcK* deletion strain (BSGNK) derived from the parental GlcNAc producer. In this strain, the dynamic labeling experiment showed complete abolition of M+0 GlcNAc-6-P formation and a rapid rise in M+8 GlcNAc-6-P, confirming that GlcK is the primary enzyme responsible for the futile re-phosphorylation of GlcNAc.

Under steady-state cultivation in minimal glucose medium, the BSGNK strain exhibited dramatic improvements in physiological and production metrics. Intracellular GlcNAc-6-P concentration was reduced from 33.71 mM in the parental strain to 0.06 mM—comparable to wild-type levels—thereby alleviating phosphosugar stress. The specific growth rate more than doubled, and the volumetric GlcNAc productivity increased from 9.20 mg·L⁻¹·h⁻¹ to over 20 mg·L⁻¹·h⁻¹. The GlcNAc yield on glucose rose by 2.3-fold to 147.5 mg·g⁻¹, and the biomass yield on glucose similarly improved to 138.3 mg·g⁻¹. Critically, the cellular energy charge increased from 0.68 to 0.81, confirming that eliminating the ATP-dissipating cycle restored energy homeostasis.

The invention thus encompasses a *B. subtilis* strain comprising: (i) deletions of *nagP*, *gamP*, *nagA*, *nagB*, and *gamA*; (ii) heterologous expression of *glmS* under an inducible promoter and *gna1* under a constitutive promoter; and (iii) a deletion of *glcK*. The strain is capable of producing GlcNAc at a yield of at least 140 mg per gram of glucose consumed in minimal medium with glucose as the sole carbon source. The invention also includes methods of producing GlcNAc by culturing this strain under aerobic conditions in a bioreactor containing minimal salts medium and glucose, followed by recovery of GlcNAc from the culture broth.

Furthermore, the invention provides a general method for identifying metabolic bottlenecks in engineered pathways, comprising: (a) performing steady-state metabolomic profiling of a production strain and comparing it to a wild-type control; (b) inducing a rapid metabolic transition (e.g., by carbon source addition after starvation); (c) monitoring the temporal dynamics of pathway intermediates and products; (d) using isotopic tracers to distinguish between de novo synthesis and interconversion of existing metabolites; and (e) validating hypothesized enzymatic activities through targeted gene deletions. This methodology is applicable to any synthetic pathway where unexpected intermediate accumulation or poor growth is observed.

The promoters used in the invention are not limited to *PxylA* and *P43*; other inducible or constitutive promoters functional in *B. subtilis* may be employed, such as *Pveg*, *Pspac*, *Ptet*, or *Plac*. Similarly, the *glmS* and *gna1* genes may be codon-optimized for *B. subtilis* expression or derived from heterologous sources, provided they retain enzymatic activity. The *glcK* deletion may be complete or partial, as long as kinase activity toward GlcNAc is abolished. Complementary strategies, such as overexpression of a specific GlcNAc-6-P phosphatase or engineering of GlcNAc export systems, may be combined with the *glcK* deletion to further enhance performance.

The culture conditions for GlcNAc production include temperatures between 30°C and 37°C, pH maintained between 6.5 and 7.5, and dissolved oxygen levels above 20% saturation. The minimal medium comprises inorganic salts, a nitrogen source (e.g., ammonium chloride), and glucose at concentrations ranging from 1 to 50 g·L⁻¹. GlcNAc is recovered from the culture supernatant by filtration, ion exchange chromatography, crystallization, or other standard purification techniques.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Construction of GlcNAc-Producing *B. subtilis* Strains**

The parental GlcNAc-producing strain BSGN was constructed as previously described. Briefly, the *B. subtilis* 168 genome was modified by markerless deletion of *nagP*, *gamP*, *nagA*, *nagB*, and *gamA* to block GlcNAc catabolism. The *glmS* gene from *B. subtilis* was placed under the control of the xylose-inducible promoter *PxylA* and integrated into the chromosome, while the *GNA1* gene from *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* was expressed from the constitutive promoter *P43* on a stable plasmid. Strain BSGN-Pfk* was generated by introducing the Arg252Ala mutation into the native *pfk* gene using a mazF-based markerless editing system, as detailed in the Methods section. Strain BSGN-GS was created by constructing a DNA multimer plasmid co-expressing *GNA1* and *glnA* (glutamine synthetase) under *P43*, and transforming it into BSGN. Strain BSGNK was derived by deleting the *glcK* gene via homologous recombination using a disruption cassette amplified from a *B. subtilis* Δ*glcK* donor strain.

**Example 2: Physiological Characterization in Minimal Glucose Medium**

All strains were cultivated in M9 minimal medium with 2 g·L⁻¹ glucose at 37°C and 300 rpm. Growth was monitored by optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀). GlcNAc concentration in the supernatant was measured by HPLC. The specific growth rate of BSGN was 0.12 h⁻¹, approximately one-fifth that of the wild-type strain (0.60 h⁻¹). BSGN-Pfk* and BSGN-GS exhibited similar growth rates to BSGN, confirming that precursor supply was not limiting. In contrast, BSGNK displayed a specific growth rate of 0.27 h⁻¹, more than double that of BSGN. GlcNAc volumetric productivity in BSGNK reached 21.5 mg·L⁻¹·h⁻¹, compared to 9.2 mg·L⁻¹·h⁻¹ in BSGN. The final GlcNAc titer after 24 hours was 516 mg·L⁻¹ for BSGNK versus 220 mg·L⁻¹ for BSGN.

**Example 3: Steady-State Metabolomics**

Cells were harvested at mid-exponential phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5) and metabolites extracted using cold acetonitrile/methanol/water. UHPLC-MS/MS analysis revealed that GlcNAc-6-P concentration in BSGN, BSGN-Pfk*, and BSGN-GS ranged from 20 to 34 mM, whereas in wild-type and BSGNK, it was approximately 0.06 mM. Fructose-6-P, citrate, and glutamine were depleted in all production strains relative to wild-type, but restoration of these pools in BSGN-GS did not improve growth, indicating that their depletion was a consequence, not a cause, of the metabolic imbalance.

**Example 4: Dynamic Metabolite Profiling and Isotopic Tracer Analysis**

Exponentially grown cells were washed and resuspended in M9 medium without carbon source for 30 minutes, then pulsed with 2 g·L⁻¹ [U-¹³C]glucose. Samples were taken at 0, 30, 60, and 120 seconds. In BSGN, M+0 GlcNAc-6-P increased by 36% within 60 seconds, while M+8 remained undetectable. In BSGNK, no M+0 accumulation occurred, and M+8 GlcNAc-6-P rose steadily, confirming de novo synthesis. Glycolytic intermediates responded normally in both strains, indicating intact central metabolism.

**Example 5: Energy Charge Measurement**

Cellular energy charge was calculated as ([ATP] + 0.5[ADP]) / ([ATP] + [ADP] + [AMP]). Triplicate measurements showed an energy charge of 0.68 ± 0.03 in BSGN and 0.81 ± 0.04 in BSGNK, demonstrating improved energy homeostasis upon *glcK* deletion.

**Example 6: Scale-Up Fermentation**

BSGNK was cultivated in a 5-L bioreactor with controlled pH (7.0), temperature (37°C), and dissolved oxygen (>30%). Fed-batch operation with glucose feeding achieved a final GlcNAc titer of 12.3 g·L⁻¹, a yield of 148 mg·g⁻¹ glucose, and a productivity of 0.85 g·L⁻¹·h⁻¹, demonstrating industrial feasibility.

These examples illustrate the superior performance of the *glcK*-deleted strain and validate the role of the futile cycle in limiting GlcNAc production. The invention thus provides a robust platform for the commercial biosynthesis of GlcNAc and a powerful diagnostic tool for metabolic engineering.
