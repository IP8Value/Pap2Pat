# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present disclosure relates generally to the field of imaging systems and computer vision, and more particularly to thermal management techniques for three-dimensional (3D) stacked image sensor architectures that integrate vision processing units in close physical proximity to image sensors. The invention specifically addresses the problem of thermal noise degradation in image fidelity caused by heat generated from near-sensor computational workloads, and provides novel runtime mechanisms for dynamically managing sensor temperature to balance energy efficiency, computational performance, and image quality requirements. The disclosed technology enables high-performance, energy-efficient vision systems through intelligent thermal regulation policies that respond to both continuous vision processing needs and on-demand high-fidelity image capture requirements, thereby facilitating the practical deployment of 3D stacked sensor architectures in applications ranging from wearable life-loggers and dashboard cameras to augmented reality systems and autonomous navigation platforms.

## BACKGROUND

Imaging and vision systems have become increasingly sophisticated, enabling computing devices to sense and interpret real-world visual environments for a wide array of applications including object detection and tracking, spatial mapping for augmented reality, and driverless navigation assistance. However, these capabilities come at a significant energy cost due to the high data rates required to transfer pixel information from image sensors to computational units. Traditional imaging architectures, where sensors are physically separated from processing units by interconnects such as ribbon cables, suffer from substantial bottlenecks in energy efficiency and processing performance. State-of-the-art convolutional neural networks (ConvNets) implemented on application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) can consume over one watt of power to achieve desirable performance levels of 30 frames per second with low-resolution QVGA frames, with power consumption increasing dramatically for higher resolution inputs and faster frame rates. This energy inefficiency has motivated the development of three-dimensional stacked integrated circuit architectures that enable near-sensor processing by layering the image sensor, vision processor unit (VPU), and memory within the same package.

While 3D stacked architectures offer compelling advantages in terms of reduced data movement and improved energy efficiency, they introduce a critical challenge related to thermal management. Image sensors are inherently sensitive to temperature variations, which generate thermal noise in the form of dark current and read noise that degrades image quality. This thermal noise not only produces less aesthetically pleasing images for human viewing but also significantly reduces the accuracy of computer vision applications. The problem is exacerbated in poor lighting conditions, where sensors must operate at high exposure times and ISO settings to adequately capture scenes, thereby increasing their vulnerability to thermal noise. Despite the existence of various dynamic thermal management (DTM) mechanisms developed for general-purpose processors, these traditional approaches fail to account for the specific imaging requirements of near-sensor processing systems. Conventional DTM techniques focus primarily on maintaining processor temperatures below thermal design power (TDP) thresholds without considering the transient imaging needs that arise in vision applications.

Previous research has documented the thermal and noise implications of stacked-sensor processing, but existing solutions have been limited to design-time approaches such as static partitioning of computational workloads between sensor and host processors. These static solutions cannot adapt to the dynamic nature of real-world imaging scenarios where fidelity requirements may change rapidly based on environmental conditions, application demands, or user interactions. Furthermore, there has been a lack of comprehensive end-to-end modeling frameworks that simultaneously characterize the energy, thermal, and noise implications of near-sensor processing workloads. This gap in understanding has hindered the development of effective runtime thermal management strategies specifically tailored to the unique requirements of 3D stacked imaging systems.

The thermal characteristics of 3D stacked sensors present both challenges and opportunities for innovative thermal management. While the coarse thermal time constant of sensor packages governs the overall trajectory toward steady-state temperatures over periods of tens of seconds, the removal of near-sensor processing power results in an immediate and significant reduction in transient junction temperature on the order of milliseconds. For example, in a 2.5-watt system, sensor temperature can drop by approximately 13 degrees Celsius within 20 milliseconds when processing is deactivated. This rapid temperature response characteristic provides a unique opportunity for dynamic thermal management that can leverage brief processing interruptions to achieve on-demand high-fidelity image captures while maintaining overall system energy efficiency during continuous vision processing operations.

Existing thermal management techniques for multi-core processors, such as stop-and-go and heat-and-run approaches, provide inspiration for addressing thermal challenges in near-sensor processing systems. However, these traditional mechanisms require significant modification to accommodate the specific requirements of imaging applications. Unlike general-purpose computing where thermal management primarily concerns preventing hardware damage or performance throttling, imaging systems must balance thermal considerations against image fidelity requirements that can vary dramatically based on lighting conditions, exposure settings, and application-specific needs. The development of thermal management strategies that can dynamically adapt to these varying requirements represents a critical advancement in enabling practical deployment of energy-efficient 3D stacked imaging architectures.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution for thermal management in three-dimensional stacked image sensor architectures that integrate vision processing units in close proximity to image sensors. The invention addresses the fundamental challenge that near-sensor processing, while offering substantial energy efficiency benefits through reduced data movement, simultaneously generates heat that degrades image fidelity through increased thermal noise. The core innovation lies in the recognition that sensor temperature exhibits distinct thermal time constants: a coarse package-level time constant governing steady-state behavior over tens of seconds, and a fine-grained junction-level time constant enabling rapid temperature changes on the order of milliseconds when processing power is modulated.

Based on this insight, the invention introduces a runtime thermal management framework called Stagioni that implements two novel temperature-aware scheduling policies specifically designed for near-sensor processing systems. The first policy, termed "stop-capture-go," temporarily suspends near-sensor processing to allow for on-demand high-fidelity image captures and resumes processing after the capture is complete. This approach leverages the rapid temperature drop characteristic of sensor junctions to quickly achieve the lower temperatures required for high-quality image acquisition while minimizing the duration of processing interruption. The second policy, called "seasonal migration," occasionally shifts processing workloads to a thermally isolated far-sensor processing unit during periods requiring high-fidelity capture, thereby maintaining continuous computational performance while allowing the sensor to cool down.

The invention establishes three fundamental design principles for effective sensor thermal management in near-sensor processing systems. First, situational temperature regulation requires that sensor temperature be maintained within thresholds that correspond to the specific fidelity needs of the current vision task and environmental conditions. Second, on-demand fidelity provision necessitates the ability to rapidly reduce sensor temperature to desired capture levels within latency constraints dictated by application requirements. Third, system power minimization is achieved through optimal duty cycling between near-sensor processing and cooling modes, balancing energy savings against switching overhead and performance requirements.

The thermal management framework operates through a runtime controller that continuously monitors system conditions and dynamically adjusts processing schedules based on ambient temperature, lighting conditions, camera settings, and application-specific fidelity requirements. The controller implements adaptive thermal boundaries that automatically adjust to changing environmental conditions, ensuring that temperature management policies remain effective across diverse operating scenarios. When high-fidelity image capture is requested, either through application demand or environmental triggers, the controller immediately initiates appropriate cooling mechanisms to achieve the required temperature reduction within specified latency constraints.

Experimental validation demonstrates that the disclosed thermal management mechanisms achieve substantial system power reductions ranging from 22% to 53% compared to traditional far-sensor processing architectures, with minimal performance impact. The stop-capture-go policy provides the lowest power consumption by operating exclusively on the near-sensor VPU while accepting controlled frame drops during cooling periods. The seasonal migration policy offers a balanced approach that maintains continuous processing performance by migrating workloads to far-sensor units during cooling phases, achieving moderate power savings with negligible performance overhead. Both policies demonstrate smooth adaptation to varying ambient temperatures and lighting conditions, automatically adjusting thermal boundaries and duty cycles to maintain optimal system performance across diverse operating environments.

The invention represents a significant advancement in enabling practical deployment of energy-efficient 3D stacked imaging architectures by providing the first runtime solution specifically designed for stacked sensor thermal management. By addressing the critical trade-off between energy efficiency and image fidelity that has previously limited adoption of near-sensor processing, the disclosed technology unlocks the potential for sophisticated vision applications in power-constrained environments such as wearable devices, automotive systems, and mobile platforms. The framework's adaptability to varying application requirements and environmental conditions ensures broad applicability across diverse imaging scenarios, from continuous object detection in life-logging applications to high-fidelity capture for critical decision-making in automotive safety systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive thermal management framework for three-dimensional stacked image sensor architectures that integrate vision processing units in close physical proximity to image sensors. The detailed implementation encompasses both hardware architectural considerations and software runtime mechanisms that work in concert to optimize the trade-offs between energy efficiency, computational performance, and image fidelity. The foundation of the invention rests upon a thorough characterization of the thermal behavior of 3D stacked sensor systems, which reveals critical insights into the relationship between processing power, sensor temperature, and image quality.

The thermal modeling framework developed as part of this invention employs resistance-capacitance (RC) network models to accurately simulate the temperature dynamics of stacked sensor architectures. The model accounts for the vertical stacking of sensor, DRAM, and vision processing unit (VPU) layers, connected through through-silicon vias, with heat dissipation occurring primarily through the package substrate to the printed circuit board. The component values for the RC model are derived through a combination of analytical calculations based on semiconductor fabrication guidelines from the International Technology Roadmap for Semiconductors (ITRS), empirical measurements from commercial image sensors, and manufacturer-provided package thermal characteristics. The model validation demonstrates exceptional accuracy, with predictions matching real-world measurements within 0.1% error margins across various power dissipation levels and operating conditions.

A key insight from the thermal characterization is the distinction between two different thermal time constants governing sensor temperature behavior. The package-level thermal time constant, determined by the thermal capacitance of the entire chip package, governs the trajectory toward steady-state temperatures over periods of tens of seconds. However, the junction-level thermal time constant, associated with the sensor die itself, enables rapid temperature changes on the order of milliseconds when processing power is modulated. Specifically, experimental measurements confirm that removing near-sensor processing power can reduce sensor temperature by approximately 13 degrees Celsius within 20 milliseconds in a 2.5-watt system. This rapid temperature response characteristic forms the basis for the invention's novel thermal management policies.

The image fidelity implications of temperature variations are thoroughly characterized through empirical studies using commercial image sensors under controlled thermal conditions. The experiments demonstrate that thermal noise becomes particularly problematic above specific temperature thresholds, with the exact threshold depending on camera settings such as exposure time and ISO gain. In low-light conditions requiring high exposure and ISO settings, sensors become thermally sensitive even at relatively modest temperatures around 52 degrees Celsius. The thermal noise manifests as increased pixel intensity variance and mean shifts due to dark current effects, visibly degrading image quality and substantially impairing computer vision task accuracy. Neural network classification accuracy degrades measurably with increasing thermal noise, confirming the critical importance of temperature management for reliable vision system performance.

The Stagioni runtime thermal management framework implements two primary scheduling policies that leverage the characterized thermal behavior to optimize system performance. The stop-capture-go policy operates by temporarily halting near-sensor processing during periods requiring high-fidelity image capture. Unlike traditional stop-and-go thermal management that might gate the entire sensor pipeline, this policy specifically targets the near-sensor VPU and DRAM components while maintaining basic sensor functionality for image capture. The policy is governed by situational temperature thresholds that account for current lighting conditions, camera settings, and application requirements. When the sensor temperature approaches the upper bound defined by current fidelity needs, processing is suspended for a duration sufficient to achieve the required temperature reduction. The minimal stop time is typically equivalent to one frame duration (approximately 33 milliseconds at 30 fps), which provides adequate cooling while minimizing the impact on visual continuity.

The seasonal migration policy provides an alternative approach that maintains continuous processing performance by migrating computational workloads between near-sensor and far-sensor processing units. This policy operates through a hysteresis-based temperature control mechanism with two distinct thresholds: a high temperature boundary (T_high) that triggers migration to far-sensor processing, and a low temperature boundary (T_low) that enables return to near-sensor processing. The T_high threshold is dynamically calculated based on current fidelity requirements and latency constraints, while the T_low threshold is optimized to balance switching frequency against duty cycle efficiency. The migration process is implemented with minimal overhead through pre-copy techniques that prepare the target processing unit before actual workload transfer, resulting in switching latencies of approximately 100 microseconds—negligible compared to typical frame capture times.

The runtime controller implements three core design principles that guide thermal management decisions. Situational temperature regulation ensures that sensor temperature remains within bounds appropriate for current vision task requirements and environmental conditions. The controller continuously monitors ambient temperature, lighting conditions, and camera settings to dynamically adjust thermal thresholds. On-demand fidelity provision enables rapid temperature reduction when high-quality image capture is required, leveraging the characterized relationship between power reduction and temperature drop. The controller calculates the achievable temperature reduction within specified latency constraints using the formula T_jump^latency = T_jump × (e^(-t_latency/RC_die)), where T_jump represents the maximum possible temperature reduction and RC_die is the junction-level thermal time constant.

System power minimization is achieved through optimal duty cycle management between near-sensor processing (NSP) and cooling (CAP) modes. The average system power is modeled as P_avg = d × P_NSP^system + (1 - d) × P_CAP^system + f_switch × E_switch, where d represents the duty cycle, f_switch is the switching frequency, and E_switch is the energy per switch. The controller optimizes this equation by adjusting duty cycle parameters based on current power profiles and fidelity requirements. The duty cycle varies widely depending on the interplay between application power consumption and fidelity demands, ranging from 100% (continuous NSP mode) when thermal boundaries exceed steady-state temperatures, to less than 50% when high fidelity requirements necessitate frequent cooling periods.

The implementation architecture supports multiple deployment scenarios, including integration as a dynamically linked library, runtime operating system service, or dedicated hardware controller. In the preferred embodiment, Stagioni operates as a runtime OS service residing on the near-sensor processor, enabling the main system-on-chip (SoC) to enter sleep states during near-sensor processing periods. The controller interfaces with both near-sensor and far-sensor processing units through standard communication protocols, implementing producer-consumer queues for synchronization during workload migration. High-fidelity capture requests are handled through immediate mode switching, with the controller prioritizing temperature reduction over processing continuity when critical image quality is required.

The thermal management framework demonstrates exceptional adaptability to varying operating conditions. Ambient temperature changes automatically adjust the duty cycle through their effect on steady-state temperature trajectories, with higher ambient temperatures reducing the proportion of time spent in energy-efficient NSP mode. Lighting condition changes trigger automatic adjustment of thermal boundaries based on the corresponding changes in camera settings and fidelity requirements. The controller smoothly transitions between different operational modes as environmental conditions change, maintaining optimal system performance across diverse scenarios from bright outdoor conditions to dimly lit indoor environments.

Experimental validation using FPGA-based emulation platforms confirms the effectiveness of the disclosed thermal management mechanisms. The evaluation employs realistic workloads representative of life-logging applications, combining continuous vision tasks (object detection and image classification) with occasional high-fidelity image captures triggered by event detection. The results demonstrate system power reductions of 22-53% compared to traditional far-sensor processing architectures, with the exact savings depending on specific power profiles and fidelity requirements. The stop-capture-go policy achieves the lowest power consumption by eliminating far-sensor processing overhead entirely, while the seasonal migration policy provides a balanced approach that maintains continuous processing performance with moderate power savings.

The framework's performance overhead is carefully managed through architectural optimizations. Seasonal migration incurs negligible performance impact due to the minimal switching latency of 100 microseconds, which is orders of magnitude smaller than typical frame capture times. Stop-capture-go accepts controlled performance degradation through frame drops during cooling periods, with the actual impact scaling linearly with the duty cycle. At high duty cycles (above 90%), the effective frame rate reduction is minimal (less than 10%), while lower duty cycles result in more substantial performance impacts that must be balanced against energy savings requirements.

The invention's comprehensive approach to thermal management addresses the critical barrier that has previously limited the adoption of energy-efficient 3D stacked imaging architectures. By providing runtime mechanisms that dynamically balance energy efficiency, computational performance, and image fidelity requirements, the disclosed technology enables practical deployment of near-sensor processing systems across diverse application domains. The framework's adaptability to varying environmental conditions and application requirements ensures robust performance across real-world operating scenarios, making it suitable for deployment in power-constrained mobile devices, automotive safety systems, wearable computing platforms, and other applications where energy efficiency and image quality must be carefully balanced.
