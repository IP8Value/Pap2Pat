# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of neuroscience has long recognized the central role of neurons in information processing and transmission within the central nervous system. However, over recent decades, a growing body of evidence has revealed that glial cells, particularly astrocytes, are not merely passive support structures but active participants in brain function, including synaptic modulation, metabolic regulation, and intercellular signaling. Among the most significant discoveries in this evolving understanding is the recognition that astrocytes possess secretory capabilities akin to those observed in classical endocrine and neuroendocrine cells. These secretory functions are mediated through the regulated release of bioactive molecules—termed gliotransmitters—such as glutamate, ATP, D-serine, neuropeptide Y (NPY), and atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP). The release of these gliotransmitters occurs via Ca²⁺-dependent exocytotic mechanisms, thereby positioning astrocytes as integral components of neuron–glia communication networks essential for normal brain physiology and plasticity.

Historically, the secretory machinery of astrocytes was thought to be limited to small synaptic-like vesicles, which store and release small-molecule gliotransmitters in a manner analogous to neuronal synaptic vesicles. However, accumulating evidence has demonstrated the presence of another class of secretory organelles in astrocytes: large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs). These LDCVs resemble the secretory granules found in neuroendocrine and endocrine cells and are capable of storing and releasing larger signaling molecules, including peptides and proteins. Despite their structural similarity to canonical secretory granules, the precise molecular identity and functional significance of LDCVs in astrocytes have remained poorly defined, largely due to the absence of definitive molecular markers that could unequivocally classify them as bona fide secretory granules.

In parallel, the intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling pathways that govern regulated exocytosis in secretory cells have been extensively studied. A key mechanism involves the generation of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP₃) through the activation of phospholipase C (PLC), which subsequently triggers the release of Ca²⁺ from intracellular stores. Traditionally, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) has been regarded as the primary IP₃-sensitive Ca²⁺ reservoir in most cell types. However, recent studies in neuroendocrine and other secretory cells have challenged this paradigm by demonstrating that secretory granules themselves serve as major IP₃-sensitive intracellular Ca²⁺ stores. These granules not only contain high concentrations of Ca²⁺—often exceeding 40 mM—but also harbor all three isoforms of the IP₃ receptor (IP₃R), which are localized to the granule membrane. Notably, the IP₃R channels in secretory granules exhibit significantly higher sensitivity to IP₃ compared to their ER counterparts, enabling granules to respond to lower concentrations of IP₃ and thereby initiate Ca²⁺-dependent exocytosis even in the absence of extracellular Ca²⁺ influx.

This emerging model of secretory granules as dynamic, IP₃-responsive Ca²⁺ stores has profound implications for understanding regulated secretion in non-neuronal cells, including astrocytes. Yet, until now, it has remained unclear whether astrocytic LDCVs possess the molecular machinery required to function as such Ca²⁺ stores. Specifically, the presence of canonical secretory granule marker proteins—such as chromogranin B (CGB) and secretogranin II (SgII)—and the localization of IP₃R isoforms within astrocytic LDCVs had not been conclusively established. Without this critical evidence, the functional classification of astrocytic LDCVs as true secretory granules—and their potential role in IP₃-mediated Ca²⁺ signaling—remained speculative.

Moreover, the physiological relevance of such a mechanism in astrocytes extends beyond basic cell biology. Dysregulation of Ca²⁺ signaling in astrocytes has been implicated in a range of neurological disorders, including glioblastoma, where IP₃R-mediated Ca²⁺ release plays a critical role in tumor cell proliferation, migration, and survival. Additionally, the release of growth factors such as fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2) from astrocytes is known to depend on IP₃-mediated intracellular Ca²⁺ mobilization. Thus, identifying the subcellular source of this Ca²⁺—particularly whether it originates from secretory granules—has direct therapeutic implications. If astrocytic secretory granules indeed function as the dominant IP₃-sensitive Ca²⁺ store, they represent a novel and previously underappreciated target for modulating astrocyte-mediated signaling in both health and disease.

Prior art in the field has not addressed this gap. While some studies have reported the presence of granin proteins or IP₃Rs in astrocytes, none have provided ultrastructural evidence linking these molecules specifically to LDCVs, nor have they demonstrated co-localization with astrocyte-specific markers to confirm cellular identity. Furthermore, no prior work has systematically quantified the distribution of these proteins in secretory granules versus control organelles (e.g., mitochondria) to establish specificity. Consequently, the prevailing view in the art has been that astrocytes, while capable of regulated secretion, lack the full complement of secretory granule machinery seen in classical secretory cells. This misconception has hindered the development of targeted interventions aimed at modulating astrocytic secretion through granule-specific pathways.

The present invention arises from the unexpected discovery that astrocytic LDCVs are, in fact, genuine secretory granules, as evidenced by the specific and abundant localization of two well-established secretory granule marker proteins—chromogranin B and secretogranin II—within these vesicles. Moreover, the invention discloses for the first time that all three isoforms of the IP₃ receptor (IP₃R1, IP₃R2, and IP₃R3) are present in the membranes of astrocytic secretory granules, thereby endowing these organelles with the capacity to function as major IP₃-sensitive intracellular Ca²⁺ stores. This finding fundamentally redefines the understanding of Ca²⁺ dynamics in astrocytes and establishes a new paradigm in which secretory granules—not the endoplasmic reticulum—serve as the primary trigger for Ca²⁺-dependent exocytosis of gliotransmitters. The implications of this discovery are far-reaching, enabling the development of novel diagnostic tools, therapeutic agents, and research methodologies centered on astrocytic secretory granules as a distinct and targetable cellular compartment.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THIS INVENTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive and definitive characterization of large dense-core vesicles (LDCVs) in astrocytes as bona fide secretory granules, equipped with the molecular machinery necessary to function as major IP₃-sensitive intracellular Ca²⁺ stores. This conclusion is based on rigorous ultrastructural and immunological analyses demonstrating the specific localization of canonical secretory granule marker proteins—chromogranin B (CGB) and secretogranin II (SgII)—within astrocytic LDCVs, as well as the presence of all three isoforms of the inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP₃R1, IP₃R2, and IP₃R3) in the membranes of these organelles. Critically, these findings are corroborated by double-labeling experiments that confirm the astrocytic identity of the cells under study through co-localization with glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), an intermediate filament protein exclusively expressed in astrocytes. Together, these data establish a novel and heretofore unrecognized functional architecture within astrocytes, wherein secretory granules serve not only as storage compartments for gliotransmitters but also as dynamic regulators of intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling.

The invention begins with the identification of LDCVs in human astrocytes using transmission electron microscopy. These vesicles, measuring 300–400 nm in diameter (with some exceeding 400 nm), are morphologically distinct from the smaller, translucent synaptic-like vesicles also present in astrocytes. LDCVs are characterized by an electron-dense core, though some appear partially transparent, possibly reflecting stages of maturation, cargo loading, or recent endocytic recycling. Quantitative analysis reveals that LDCVs are relatively sparse, with 0–4 vesicles typically observed per 6 µm² of astrocytic cytoplasm, and are preferentially localized in cellular processes rather than in the cell body—a distribution that may facilitate localized signaling at sites of neuron–astrocyte interaction.

To determine the molecular identity of these LDCVs, the invention employs high-resolution immunogold electron microscopy using affinity-purified polyclonal antibodies against CGB and SgII. Both proteins are members of the granin family, which are universally recognized as hallmark markers of secretory granules in neuroendocrine and endocrine cells. The results demonstrate that CGB- and SgII-specific gold particles are densely localized within the lumen of LDCVs, with negligible labeling observed in mitochondria—a control organelle known to lack granin proteins. Quantitative assessment confirms that the density of CGB labeling is 12.38 gold particles per µm² of secretory granule area, compared to only 0.37 per µm² in mitochondria, a value consistent with background. Similarly, SgII labeling yields 10.31 particles per µm² in granules versus 0.20 in mitochondria. These statistically significant differences provide unambiguous evidence that CGB and SgII are enriched in LDCVs, thereby confirming their identity as secretory granules.

Furthermore, the invention validates the astrocytic origin of these secretory granules through double immunogold labeling experiments. Using monoclonal anti-GFAP antibody conjugated to 10-nm gold particles and anti-CGB or anti-SgII antibodies conjugated to 15-nm gold particles, the invention demonstrates that GFAP labeling is strictly confined to intermediate filaments—cytoskeletal structures unique to astrocytes—while CGB or SgII labeling remains restricted to LDCVs. This dual labeling not only confirms the cellular identity of the granule-containing cells as astrocytes but also rules out contamination from neuronal or other glial cell types. The consistency of these findings across multiple human tissue samples (n = 5) and hundreds of analyzed organelles underscores the robustness and reproducibility of the invention.

A central aspect of the invention is the demonstration that astrocytic secretory granules contain all three isoforms of the IP₃ receptor. Using isoform-specific polyclonal antibodies raised against unique C-terminal peptides of IP₃R1, IP₃R2, and IP₃R3, the invention shows that each receptor isoform is localized to the limiting membrane of secretory granules, consistent with their known topology as transmembrane Ca²⁺ channels. No labeling is detected in mitochondrial membranes, confirming the specificity of the signal. Quantitative analysis reveals that IP₃R1, IP₃R2, and IP₃R3 are present at densities of 0.872, 0.586, and 0.719 gold particles per µm of granule membrane, respectively, compared to background levels of ≤0.035 in mitochondria. These values are comparable to those reported in neuroendocrine chromaffin cells, reinforcing the functional homology between astrocytic secretory granules and those of classical secretory cells.

The presence of IP₃Rs in secretory granules has profound functional implications. In secretory cells, granule-resident IP₃Rs are known to be 6–7-fold more sensitive to IP₃ than ER-resident receptors, enabling granules to release Ca²⁺ in response to subthreshold IP₃ concentrations that would not activate the ER. This heightened sensitivity allows secretory granules to act as the first responders in IP₃-mediated Ca²⁺ signaling, providing the initial Ca²⁺ microdomain necessary to trigger vesicle fusion and exocytosis. The invention posits that the same mechanism operates in astrocytes: upon stimulation of Gq-coupled receptors (e.g., by neurotransmitters or inflammatory mediators), PLC activation generates IP₃, which rapidly binds to granule IP₃Rs, causing localized Ca²⁺ release from the granule lumen. This Ca²⁺, in concert with granule-stored gliotransmitters such as NPY, ATP, or glutamate, facilitates the fusion of the granule membrane with the plasma membrane, resulting in the regulated release of signaling molecules into the extracellular space.

Importantly, the invention recognizes that astrocytic secretory granules are not merely passive storage depots but active signaling hubs. The granule lumen contains high concentrations of Ca²⁺—up to 40 mM—bound by CGB and SgII, which function as high-capacity, low-affinity Ca²⁺ buffers. At the acidic intragranular pH (~5.5), these granins bind directly to IP₃Rs and potentiate channel activity, increasing both open probability and mean open time by up to 42-fold. This molecular coupling ensures that IP₃-induced Ca²⁺ release from granules is both rapid and robust. In contrast, the ER, which maintains a neutral pH (~7.4), lacks this potentiation mechanism, as chromogranin A does not bind IP₃Rs at physiological pH and CGB binding is markedly weakened. Thus, the invention establishes a biochemical and biophysical rationale for why secretory granules—not the ER—serve as the primary IP₃-sensitive Ca²⁺ store in astrocytes.

The invention further contemplates that astrocytic secretory granules may also contain components of the exocytotic machinery, such as SNARE proteins (e.g., syntaxin, SNAP-25, VAMP) and Ca²⁺ sensors like synaptotagmin, based on homology with neuroendocrine cells. Although not directly demonstrated herein, this inference is supported by prior reports of SNARE-dependent gliotransmitter release and the presence of synaptotagmin in astrocytic vesicles. The co-localization of IP₃Rs, Ca²⁺, gliotransmitters, and exocytotic proteins within a single organelle creates a self-contained unit capable of autonomous stimulus-secretion coupling, independent of global cytosolic Ca²⁺ waves.

From a therapeutic standpoint, the invention opens new avenues for targeting astrocyte-mediated signaling in neurological diseases. For example, in glioblastoma, where IP₃R-mediated Ca²⁺ signaling drives tumor progression, selective inhibition of granule IP₃Rs could suppress pathological secretion without disrupting ER-dependent housekeeping functions. Similarly, in conditions involving aberrant gliotransmission—such as epilepsy, chronic pain, or neuroinflammation—modulators of granule Ca²⁺ release could offer more precise control over astrocytic output. The invention thus provides the foundational knowledge required to develop granule-specific pharmacological agents, diagnostic probes, and gene-editing strategies.

In summary, the invention redefines the secretory biology of astrocytes by establishing that their LDCVs are authentic secretory granules endowed with IP₃-sensitive Ca²⁺ release capability. This paradigm shift has broad implications for neuroscience, cell biology, and drug discovery, and represents a significant advance over the prior art, which failed to recognize the full secretory potential of astrocytes.

### EXAMPLES

The invention is exemplified through a series of experimental procedures conducted on human astrocytes derived from surgically resected temporal lobe tissue. All tissue samples were obtained with informed consent under Institutional Review Board approval (IRB #0806-006-246, Seoul National University Hospital). Immediately following surgical removal, tissue fragments (~1 mm³) were fixed in 0.1% glutaraldehyde and 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 2 hours at 4°C to preserve ultrastructure and antigenicity. After fixation, samples were postfixed in 1% osmium tetroxide on ice for 2 hours, dehydrated through an ethanol gradient, and embedded in Epon 812 resin. Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut and collected on Formvar/carbon-coated nickel grids for electron microscopy.

For immunogold labeling, grids were treated with 3% sodium metaperiodate for 40 minutes to enhance antigen retrieval, followed by blocking in buffer A (PBS, pH 8.2, containing 4% normal goat serum, 1% bovine serum albumin, 0.1% Tween 20, and 0.1% sodium azide) for 1 hour. Primary antibodies—affinity-purified rabbit polyclonals against CGB, SgII, IP₃R1, IP₃R2, or IP₃R3—were diluted in buffer B (buffer A with 1% normal goat serum) and applied to grids for 3 hours at room temperature in a humidified chamber. Antibody specificity was confirmed by preabsorption controls (excess purified antigen abolished labeling), omission of primary antibody (no signal), and use of preimmune serum (background levels only). Secondary detection employed 15-nm gold-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG for rabbit primaries and 10-nm gold-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG for mouse anti-GFAP (clone G-A-5, Sigma-Aldrich).

Double-labeling experiments combined mouse anti-GFAP (10-nm gold) with rabbit anti-CGB or anti-SgII (15-nm gold), with sequential incubation and stringent washing between steps to prevent cross-reactivity. All grids were counterstained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before imaging on a JEOL JEM-1011 electron microscope.

Quantitative analysis involved counting gold particles over secretory granules and mitochondria in 20–26 micrographs per condition, derived from 5 independent tissue samples. Granule and mitochondrial areas or membrane lengths were measured using ImageJ software. Statistical significance was assessed by Student’s t-test, with p < 0.001 considered highly significant.

These methods yielded reproducible, high-resolution data confirming the specific enrichment of CGB, SgII, and all three IP₃R isoforms in astrocytic secretory granules, thereby validating the core claims of the invention.

### Results

The experimental results unequivocally demonstrate that large dense-core vesicles in human astrocytes are authentic secretory granules. Electron microscopy revealed LDCVs with diameters of 300–400 nm, often located in cellular processes. Immunogold labeling showed dense accumulation of CGB and SgII within LDCV lumens, with particle densities of 12.38 and 10.31 per µm², respectively, versus background levels (<0.4) in mitochondria. Double labeling confirmed astrocytic identity via GFAP-positive intermediate filaments adjacent to CGB/SgII-positive granules.

All three IP₃R isoforms were detected on granule membranes: IP₃R1 (0.872 particles/µm), IP₃R2 (0.586/µm), and IP₃R3 (0.719/µm), with mitochondrial labeling indistinguishable from background. These findings mirror those in chromaffin cells, confirming functional homology.

### Discussion

These results redefine astrocyte biology by establishing secretory granules as major IP₃-sensitive Ca²⁺ stores. This mechanism explains how astrocytes achieve localized, rapid gliotransmitter release independent of ER Ca²⁺. The presence of granin proteins and IP₃Rs in granules creates a self-sufficient secretory unit, with implications for understanding brain signaling and developing therapies for glioblastoma, epilepsy, and neuroinflammation. The invention thus provides a foundational advance with broad scientific and clinical relevance.
