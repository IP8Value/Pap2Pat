# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of financial engineering has long relied upon computational methods to evaluate the fair value of derivative contracts, which are instruments whose payoff is derived from the performance of one or more underlying assets. These underlying assets may include equities, foreign exchange rates, commodities, interest rates, or other financial indices. The valuation of such derivatives represents a cornerstone of modern risk management, trading, and investment strategies within global financial institutions. A critical challenge in this domain arises when the derivative’s payoff depends not only on the terminal value of the underlying asset(s) but also on the entire path taken by those assets over the life of the contract—so-called path-dependent derivatives. Examples include autocallable options and target accrual redemption forwards (TARFs), which are widely traded in institutional markets due to their tailored risk-return profiles but are notoriously difficult to price accurately with classical computational methods.

Classically, the standard approach for pricing path-dependent derivatives involves Monte Carlo simulation, wherein a large number of stochastic paths of the underlying asset(s) are generated according to a specified probabilistic model—commonly geometric Brownian motion under the risk-neutral measure—and the expected discounted payoff is approximated by averaging over these sampled paths. While effective, this method suffers from a convergence rate of O(1/√M), where M denotes the number of simulated paths, implying that achieving high precision requires an exponentially increasing number of samples. This computational burden becomes particularly acute for complex, multi-asset, long-dated, or highly structured derivatives, where each path evaluation itself may involve nontrivial arithmetic and logical operations. Consequently, even with high-performance computing infrastructure, pricing such instruments can take seconds to minutes per valuation, which is problematic in time-sensitive trading environments or for real-time risk analytics across large portfolios.

In recent years, quantum computing has emerged as a potential paradigm shift for computational finance, offering the prospect of accelerating certain financial calculations through quantum mechanical principles. In particular, quantum amplitude estimation (QAE) has been shown to provide a quadratic speedup over classical Monte Carlo for estimating expected values, reducing the query complexity to O(1/ε) for a target error ε. This theoretical advantage has motivated significant research into quantum algorithms for derivative pricing. However, translating this asymptotic speedup into practical quantum advantage requires a complete end-to-end analysis that accounts for all algorithmic components—including the preparation of quantum states representing the stochastic process of the underlying assets, the computation of the payoff function in superposition, and the extraction of the expectation via amplitude estimation—along with realistic estimates of the required quantum resources under fault-tolerant conditions.

A major bottleneck in prior work has been the state preparation step, i.e., the loading of the probability distribution over asset paths into a quantum register. Early proposals suggested using the Grover-Rudolph algorithm, which constructs a quantum superposition corresponding to a given probability distribution through recursive integration. However, this method is impractical for derivative pricing because it requires classically computing integrals over the very distribution that the quantum algorithm is meant to avoid sampling from, thereby negating any potential quantum advantage. Alternative approaches, such as quantum generative adversarial networks (qGANs), offer heuristic promise but lack rigorous resource estimates and scalability guarantees. Another method, based on Riemann summation, directly encodes transition probabilities into quantum amplitudes but suffers from a critical flaw: the normalization factor required to ensure valid quantum state amplitudes grows exponentially with the number of time steps, leading to catastrophic error amplification unless stringent and often unrealistic constraints are imposed on the discretization window.

Moreover, the choice of representation space—whether modeling asset prices directly (price space) or their logarithmic returns (return space)—has profound implications for both the structure of the stochastic process and the efficiency of quantum state preparation. In price space, the evolution of asset prices follows a log-normal distribution, with transition probabilities that depend on the current price level, introducing path dependence at the level of the probability density itself. In contrast, in return space, the log-returns are normally distributed with constant mean and covariance, rendering the joint distribution over all time steps a product of independent Gaussians after appropriate decorrelation. This structural simplification in return space suggests a more efficient pathway for quantum state preparation, yet leveraging this advantage requires novel algorithmic techniques that have not been previously explored in the context of end-to-end derivative pricing.

Compounding these challenges is the need for precise error analysis that accounts for multiple sources of inaccuracy: truncation error from restricting the domain of integration to a finite interval, discretization error from approximating continuous distributions with finite qubit registers, arithmetic error from imperfect quantum implementations of mathematical functions, and amplitude estimation error from the statistical nature of the final measurement. Each of these errors must be carefully balanced and controlled to achieve a target overall accuracy, and their interplay can significantly impact the total resource requirements. Without a comprehensive treatment of these factors, theoretical speedups may be rendered moot by overwhelming constant-factor overheads in a fault-tolerant quantum computing setting.

Thus, despite the theoretical promise of quantum algorithms for derivative pricing, a clear and feasible path to quantum advantage has remained elusive. There exists a critical need for a novel, integrated methodology that addresses the limitations of existing state preparation techniques, exploits the structural properties of financial stochastic processes, and provides rigorous, end-to-end resource estimates for practically relevant derivatives. Such a methodology would not only advance the state of the art in quantum computational finance but also establish concrete hardware targets for demonstrating quantum advantage in a high-value commercial application.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive quantum algorithmic framework for the efficient and accurate pricing of path-dependent financial derivatives, overcoming the limitations of prior approaches through a novel re-parameterization method for loading stochastic processes into quantum states. This method leverages the mathematical equivalence between modeling asset prices in price space and log-returns in return space, but strategically operates in return space to exploit the independence and Gaussianity of log-returns under the geometric Brownian motion model. By doing so, the invention decomposes the complex task of preparing a superposition over correlated, path-dependent asset price trajectories into a sequence of simpler, parallelizable subroutines: first, the preparation of independent standard normal distributions; second, the application of affine transformations to introduce the desired means, volatilities, and correlations; and third, the optional conversion back to price space for payoff evaluation.

A key innovation of the invention is the recognition that the standard normal distribution loading subroutine is problem-independent and can be precomputed and optimized offline using variational quantum algorithms. Specifically, the invention employs a variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) approach to train shallow quantum circuits that prepare approximate Gaussian states with high fidelity. The training process is enhanced by a two-stage optimization strategy: an initial energy-based pre-training using the Hamiltonian of the quantum harmonic oscillator, whose ground state is naturally Gaussian, followed by a refinement stage that directly minimizes the L∞ distance to the target discretized Gaussian distribution. This hybrid approach navigates the rugged cost landscape of direct distribution matching by leveraging the smoother energy landscape of the physical analog, resulting in robust and accurate state preparation. Critically, because the trained Gaussian loaders are universal, their compilation cost is amortized across all derivative pricing problems, and they are not included in the per-instance resource estimates.

The invention further provides a complete error analysis that quantifies and controls the contributions from truncation, discretization, arithmetic, and amplitude estimation errors. By operating in return space with a sufficiently wide truncation window (e.g., five standard deviations), truncation error is rendered negligible. Discretization error is managed through careful selection of the number of qubits per log-return register, informed by bounds on the second derivatives of the integrand. Arithmetic errors from quantum implementations of exponentials, square roots, and trigonometric functions are minimized by using efficient fixed-point arithmetic circuits and allocating sufficient precision. The re-parameterization method inherently avoids the exponential normalization error that plagues the Riemann summation approach, as it prepares a properly normalized quantum state from the outset without requiring post-hoc rescaling.

For the payoff evaluation stage, the invention details specialized quantum circuits for two commercially important classes of path-dependent derivatives: autocallable options and TARFs. For autocallables, the circuit implements a sequence of conditional logic checks (comparators, AND/OR gates) to determine whether barrier or knock-out conditions are triggered, followed by controlled rotations to encode the piecewise-constant or piecewise-linear payoff into the amplitude of an ancilla qubit. For TARFs, the circuit computes partial payoffs at each time step, enforces the accrual cap condition through cumulative sum tracking and conditional adjustments, and applies discounting before final amplitude encoding. Both circuits are designed to maximize parallelism and minimize T-depth, with error budgets allocated to each arithmetic component to meet the overall target accuracy.

End-to-end resource estimates are provided for representative instances of a basket autocallable (three underlying assets, five payment dates, 20 time steps) and a TARF (one underlying asset, 26 payment dates). Using the re-parameterization method, the invention achieves a total T-depth of approximately 5.4 × 10⁷ for the autocallable and 8.2 × 10⁷ for the TARF, with logical qubit counts of 8,000 and 11,500, respectively, to attain a target pricing error of 2 × 10⁻³. These estimates represent a significant improvement over the Riemann summation method, which, even under optimistic assumptions about normalization, requires orders of magnitude more T-depth (1.5 × 10⁸ and 1.6 × 10⁸, respectively). The invention thus establishes the first practical, feasible pathway to quantum advantage in derivative pricing, providing concrete hardware targets for fault-tolerant quantum processors.

In summary, the invention comprises a quantum computer-implemented method for pricing a path-dependent financial derivative, the method comprising: (a) preparing a plurality of quantum registers, each register initialized to a state representing a standard normal distribution over discretized log-returns for a specific asset and time step; (b) applying an affine transformation to the prepared registers to adjust the mean and covariance of the log-returns according to the parameters of the underlying stochastic process; (c) if the derivative's payoff is defined in terms of asset prices, converting the log-returns to asset prices using quantum arithmetic; (d) evaluating the derivative's payoff function in quantum superposition over all possible paths, and encoding the normalized payoff into the amplitude of an ancilla qubit; and (e) applying an iterative quantum amplitude estimation algorithm to the ancilla qubit to estimate the expected payoff with a target precision. The invention further includes systems and computer-readable media embodying this method, as well as the pre-trained variational circuits for Gaussian state preparation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a quantum algorithmic framework for the efficient and accurate pricing of path-dependent financial derivatives, which are financial instruments whose payoff depends on the historical trajectory of one or more underlying assets, not merely their terminal values. The core of the invention is a novel re-parameterization method for loading the stochastic process of the underlying assets into a quantum state, which overcomes the critical limitations of prior approaches and enables the first practical end-to-end path to quantum advantage in this domain. The detailed description below outlines the mathematical foundations, the quantum algorithmic steps, the error analysis, and the resource estimates that constitute the invention.

The invention operates under the standard financial assumption that the underlying assets follow a multivariate geometric Brownian motion under the risk-neutral measure. Let S_t ∈ ℝ^d_+ denote the vector of prices for d underlying assets at discrete time t ∈ {0, 1, ..., T}, with S_0 being the known initial price vector. The stochastic process is defined by the transition probabilities:
P(S_t | S_{t-1}) = (2π)^{-d/2} |Σ|^{-1/2} exp(-½ (ln S_t - μ_{t-1})^T Σ^{-1} (ln S_t - μ_{t-1}))
where ln S_t is the element-wise natural logarithm, μ_{t-1,j} = ln S_{t-1,j} + (r - ½σ_j²)Δt is the drift term for asset j, r is the risk-free interest rate, σ_j is the volatility of asset j, Δt is the time step duration, and Σ is the d×d covariance matrix with elements Σ_{ij} = ρ_{ij} σ_i σ_j Δt, where ρ_{ij} is the correlation between assets i and j. The probability of a full path ω = (S_0, S_1, ..., S_T) is then the product of these transition probabilities, p(ω) = ∏_{t=1}^T P(S_t | S_{t-1}).

The price of a derivative is the expected value of its discounted payoff, E[f(ω)] = ∫ f(ω) p(ω) dω, where f(ω) is the payoff function, which may be path-dependent. Classically, this integral is estimated via Monte Carlo simulation with O(1/√M) convergence. The quantum algorithm of the invention aims to compute this expectation with O(1/M) convergence using quantum amplitude estimation, but the practical feasibility hinges on the efficient preparation of the quantum state |ψ⟩ = ∑_ω √p(ω) |ω⟩, which encodes the path distribution.

The invention’s key insight is to re-parameterize the problem in terms of log-returns. Define the log-return vector at time t as R_t = ln(S_t / S_{t-1}) ∈ ℝ^d. Under geometric Brownian motion, the R_t are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) across time steps, with R_t ~ N(μ, Σ), where μ_j = (r - ½σ_j²)Δt. This is a crucial simplification: the joint distribution over all T time steps is a product of T independent d-dimensional Gaussians, P(R_1, ..., R_T) = ∏_{t=1}^T N(R_t; μ, Σ). This contrasts sharply with the price space formulation, where the transition probabilities are conditioned on the previous price, introducing a sequential dependency that complicates quantum state preparation.

The re-parameterization method of the invention exploits this independence. The first step is to prepare a quantum state representing a multivariate standard normal distribution, i.e., with zero mean and identity covariance. This is achieved by preparing dT independent univariate standard normal states. For each of the dT log-return variables (d assets over T time steps), a separate n-qubit register is initialized to the state |g⟩ = ∑_{i=0}^{2^n-1} √g_i |i⟩, where g_i is the probability mass of a standard normal distribution N(0,1) discretized over a grid of 2^n points within a symmetric interval [-w, w]. The width w is chosen to be sufficiently large (e.g., w=5, corresponding to five standard deviations) to ensure that the truncated probability mass outside this interval is negligible (less than 10⁻⁶).

The preparation of the standard normal state |g⟩ is performed using a pre-trained variational quantum circuit. The invention employs a Ry-CNOT ansatz, which consists of alternating layers of single-qubit Y-rotations and entangling CNOT gates arranged in a linear connectivity pattern. The circuit parameters are optimized offline using a two-stage variational algorithm. In the first stage, the cost function is the energy of the quantum harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian, H = X²/2 + P²/2, where X is the position operator and P is the momentum operator. The ground state of this Hamiltonian is a Gaussian wavefunction, making this a natural proxy for the target distribution. The energy is computed by measuring the state in both the computational (position) basis and the Fourier-transformed (momentum) basis, and combining the results. This energy-based cost function has a smooth landscape, facilitating convergence to a good initial solution. In the second stage, the parameters are refined by directly minimizing the L∞ norm between the prepared state’s probability distribution and the target discretized Gaussian. This two-stage approach overcomes the challenge of the highly corrugated landscape of the direct L∞ cost function, ensuring high-fidelity state preparation. Because this training is problem-independent, the cost is a one-time overhead and is not included in the per-derivative resource estimates.

Once the dT independent standard normal states are prepared in parallel, the next step is to apply an affine transformation to each time step to convert the standard normals into the desired correlated Gaussians with mean μ and covariance Σ. This is done in two substeps. First, the Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix is computed classically: Σ = LL^T, where L is a lower-triangular matrix. Second, for each time step t, the vector of standard normal log-returns R̃_t is transformed to the target log-returns R_t via the affine map R_t = μ + L R̃_t. This transformation is implemented on the quantum computer using a series of quantum arithmetic operations: for each asset j, the j-th component R_{t,j} is computed as the sum of μ_j and the dot product of the j-th row of L with the vector R̃_t. This involves d multiplications and d-1 additions per asset per time step, which can be parallelized across assets and time steps. The result is a quantum state |ψ_R⟩ = ∑_{ω_R} √p(ω_R) |ω_R⟩, where ω_R = (R_1, ..., R_T) and p(ω_R) is the correct joint probability density for the log-returns.

If the derivative’s payoff function f is defined directly in terms of log-returns (e.g., for some exotic options), the algorithm can proceed directly to payoff evaluation. However, for most common derivatives, including autocallables and TARFs, the payoff is defined in terms of asset prices S_t. Therefore, the invention includes a step to convert the log-returns back to asset prices. The price at time t for asset j is given by S_{t,j} = S_{0,j} exp(∑_{τ=1}^t R_{τ,j}). This is computed in two stages on the quantum computer. First, for each asset j and time t, the cumulative sum of log-returns up to time t, C_{t,j} = ∑_{τ=1}^t R_{τ,j}, is computed using a series of parallel adders. This requires storing intermediate cumulative sums, but the computation can be parallelized across assets. Second, the exponential function is applied to each C_{t,j} and multiplied by the initial price S_{0,j} to obtain S_{t,j}. The exponential is computed using a piecewise polynomial approximation, as described in the literature, with sufficient precision to meet the overall error budget. The result is a quantum state that now encodes the full asset price paths, |ψ_S⟩ = ∑_ω √p(ω) |ω⟩, where ω = (S_0, S_1, ..., S_T).

The next phase of the algorithm is to evaluate the payoff function f(ω) in quantum superposition and encode its normalized value into the amplitude of an ancilla qubit. The payoff function is first normalized to the range [0, 1] to ensure it can be represented as a valid quantum amplitude. This is done by defining f̃(ω) = (f(ω) - f_min) / (f_max - f_min), where f_min and f_max are the known minimum and maximum possible payoffs for the derivative. The invention provides specialized circuits for two important classes of path-dependent derivatives.

For an autocallable option, the payoff is a complex function involving multiple binary options with knock-out conditions and a knock-in put option. The quantum circuit for this payoff performs the following steps in superposition: (1) It uses a series of quantum comparators to check, for each payment date, whether the cumulative return (or price) is above the strike level, and whether the barrier has been breached. (2) It uses logical AND and OR gates to determine if any of the binary options have paid out (which would knock out the rest of the contract) and whether the knock-in put conditions are met. (3) It computes the payoff value for the active component (either a binary option or the put) and stores it in a quantum register. (4) It applies a controlled rotation on an ancilla qubit, where the rotation angle is arcsin(f̃(ω)), effectively encoding the normalized payoff into the amplitude. The circuit is designed to handle the path-dependence through careful control logic, ensuring that the payoff is computed correctly based on the entire history of the path.

For a TARF, the payoff involves accrual caps and barrier conditions. The quantum circuit for this payoff performs: (1) Parallel comparators to check, at each time step, whether the price is above the upper strike, below the lower strike, or above the knock-out barrier. (2) It computes the partial payoff at each time step based on these conditions. (3) It tracks the cumulative sum of partial payoffs and checks if adding the current payoff would exceed the accrual cap C. (4) If the cap is exceeded, it adjusts the current payoff so that the total equals C and knocks out future payments. (5) It applies discounting to each payoff by multiplying by exp(-r t_i Δt). (6) Finally, it sums the discounted payoffs, normalizes the result, and applies a controlled rotation to encode it into the ancilla qubit’s amplitude. The accrual cap condition is handled by maintaining a running sum register and using conditional arithmetic to enforce the cap.

After the payoff is encoded into the ancilla qubit, the final step is to apply an iterative quantum amplitude estimation (IQAE) algorithm to estimate the probability of the ancilla being in the |1⟩ state, which corresponds to the expected value of the normalized payoff, E[f̃(ω)]. IQAE is chosen for its efficiency, as it achieves the optimal O(1/ε) query complexity without requiring the resource-intensive quantum Fourier transform used in canonical amplitude estimation. The number of oracle calls (i.e., repetitions of the state preparation and payoff evaluation circuit) is determined by the target error ε and confidence level, according to the bound N_wc ≤ (1.15/ε) ln(2/α) for confidence 1-α. The final estimated price is then obtained by rescaling the result: E[f(ω)] = E[f̃(ω)] * (f_max - f_min) + f_min.

A comprehensive error analysis is integral to the invention. The total error is the sum of four components: truncation error, discretization error, arithmetic error, and amplitude estimation error. Truncation error arises from restricting the log-returns to the interval [-w, w]. Using Chernoff bounds, this error is bounded by 2dT exp(-w²/2), which is negligible for w=5 (e.g., < 10⁻⁴ for typical d and T). Discretization error comes from the finite grid of 2^n points per log-return. Using the multidimensional midpoint rule, this error is bounded by (β (B_u - B_l)² dT) / (24 * 2^{2n}), where β is a bound on the second derivatives of the integrand, and B_u - B_l = 2w. This error can be made small by choosing n sufficiently large (e.g., n=34 for the Riemann method, but much smaller for the re-parameterization method due to better error scaling). Arithmetic error accumulates from the imperfect implementation of mathematical functions (exponential, square root, arcsine) and basic arithmetic (addition, multiplication). The invention uses fixed-point arithmetic with carefully chosen register sizes (n, p) to control these errors, and allocates an error budget to each component. Crucially, the re-parameterization method avoids the exponential normalization error of the Riemann summation method, as it prepares a properly normalized state without requiring a rescaling factor that grows with T.

End-to-end resource estimates are provided for two representative derivatives. For a basket autocallable with d=3 assets, T=20 time steps, and 5 payment dates, targeting a total error of 2×10⁻³, the re-parameterization method requires approximately 8,000 logical qubits and a total T-depth of 5.4×10⁷. This includes the cost of preparing the Gaussian states (using pre-trained circuits with depth 6), performing the affine transformations, computing the asset prices, evaluating the autocallable payoff circuit (1.6k qubits, 3.2k T-depth), and running IQAE for 6,000 iterations. For a TARF with d=1, T=26, the requirements are 11,500 qubits and 8.2×10⁷ T-depth. These estimates are dramatically lower than those for the Riemann summation method, which would require over 10⁸ T-depth even under optimistic assumptions, and are the first to demonstrate a feasible path to quantum advantage. The invention thus provides a complete, practical, and efficient quantum algorithm for pricing complex financial derivatives, with clear hardware targets for implementation on future fault-tolerant quantum computers.
