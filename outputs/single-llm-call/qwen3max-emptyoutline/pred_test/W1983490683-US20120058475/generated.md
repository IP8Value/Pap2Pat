# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular cytogenetics and genomic analysis, specifically to methods and compositions for the high-resolution detection, visualization, and characterization of chromosomal inversions in individual cells. More particularly, the invention provides a novel cytogenomic methodology—termed directional genomic hybridization or chromatid painting—that enables the identification of intra-chromosomal structural rearrangements, including inversions of varying sizes, by exploiting the inherent strand-specificity of DNA replication and the directional orientation of single-stranded nucleic acid probes. The invention further encompasses the design, synthesis, and application of bioinformatically derived, sequence-specific, single-stranded oligonucleotide probes that are uniformly oriented along the 5′→3′ axis of a target chromosome or chromosomal region. These probes are used to “paint” individual sister chromatids following selective degradation of nascent DNA strands containing brominated nucleotides, thereby rendering each chromatid a single-stranded hybridization target. The methodology is uniquely capable of revealing inversions as a visible switch in fluorescent signal from one sister chromatid to the other, a consequence of the obligatory reversal in DNA strand orientation required to maintain 5′→3′ polarity within an inverted segment. The invention is applicable to human and non-human primate cells, as well as to other mammalian species with sequenced genomes, and is useful in diverse contexts including cancer diagnostics, radiation biodosimetry, comparative genomics, evolutionary biology, and the study of structural variation in genetic disorders. The disclosed approach overcomes significant limitations of conventional cytogenetic techniques such as G-banding and whole-chromosome painting fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), which are generally insensitive to inversions, especially those below 10 megabases in size. Furthermore, the invention addresses key shortcomings of next-generation sequencing-based methods for inversion detection, which often struggle with phasing, reference genome biases, and the inability to resolve mosaicism or assess cellular heterogeneity in solid tumors. By providing a cell-by-cell, genome-wide, and orientation-sensitive view of chromosomal architecture, the present invention establishes a powerful new platform for the discovery and validation of structural variants with implications for both basic research and clinical applications.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Chromosomal rearrangements have long been recognized as critical contributors to human disease, particularly in the context of oncogenesis, developmental disorders, and infertility. Among the various classes of structural variants—such as deletions, duplications, translocations, and insertions—inversions represent a unique and historically underappreciated category of genomic alteration. An inversion occurs when a segment of a chromosome undergoes two double-strand breaks, followed by a 180-degree rotation and reinsertion of the intervening fragment. This intra-chromosomal rearrangement preserves the overall copy number of the affected region but reverses the linear order and transcriptional orientation of the genes and regulatory elements contained within it. Despite their potential to disrupt gene function, alter gene regulation through position effects, or generate novel fusion transcripts, inversions have remained notoriously difficult to detect using standard cytogenetic and molecular techniques. Traditional karyotyping, based on Giemsa or other banding patterns, can identify only large inversions—typically those exceeding 5 to 10 megabases—and even then, only when the breakpoints fall within bands that produce a visibly altered pattern upon inversion. Many inversions, particularly those that are pericentric (spanning the centromere) or paracentric (confined to one chromosomal arm), do not produce discernible changes in banding morphology and thus remain “cryptic” to conventional analysis. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) using whole-chromosome painting probes, while highly effective for detecting translocations and aneuploidies, is fundamentally blind to inversions because it relies on double-stranded DNA probes that bind regardless of sequence orientation; thus, an inverted segment hybridizes identically to its non-inverted counterpart.

The advent of molecular cytogenetics introduced more sensitive tools, yet inversion detection remained problematic. Array-based comparative genomic hybridization (a-CGH) and single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays excel at identifying copy number variations (CNVs) but cannot detect balanced rearrangements like inversions, which do not alter DNA dosage. Similarly, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) and quantitative PCR (qPCR) are unsuitable for inversion screening due to their reliance on copy number or amplification efficiency metrics. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies, including whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and paired-end or mate-pair sequencing, have offered renewed hope for comprehensive structural variant detection. These methods infer inversions by identifying discordant read pairs—where the orientation or distance between paired reads deviates from the expected configuration in the reference genome. However, such approaches are fraught with challenges. Reference genome misassemblies, particularly in regions rich in segmental duplications or repetitive elements, can generate false-positive inversion calls. Moreover, the presence of flanking inverted duplications—a common genomic feature—can confound alignment algorithms and lead to ambiguous or incorrect interpretations. Perhaps most critically, bulk sequencing averages signals across millions of cells, obscuring mosaicism and preventing the assessment of inversion frequency in heterogeneous cell populations such as solid tumors. Single-cell sequencing, while theoretically capable of addressing this limitation, remains technically demanding, expensive, and low-throughput, making it impractical for routine diagnostic or large-scale screening applications.

The difficulty in detecting inversions has had profound consequences for our understanding of their biological and clinical significance. Although over 800 tumor-specific chromosome aberrations have been cataloged—many of which are translocations leading to oncogenic fusion genes—the true prevalence and spectrum of inversions in human disease remain poorly characterized. Landmark discoveries, such as the t(9;22) Philadelphia chromosome in chronic myeloid leukemia and the t(8;14) translocation in Burkitt lymphoma, were made possible by cytogenetic advances, but analogous breakthroughs involving inversions have been rare. A notable exception is the recent identification of a cryptic pericentric inversion on chromosome 16, inv(16)(p13.3q24.3), in pediatric acute megakaryoblastic leukemia, which generates the CBFA2T3-GLIS2 fusion oncogene. This inversion was undetectable by conventional cytogenetics and was only revealed through transcriptome sequencing, highlighting both the pathogenic potential of inversions and the limitations of existing detection methodologies. Similarly, large-scale cancer genomics projects, such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), have reported numerous “intra-chromosomal rearrangements” in cancers like colorectal carcinoma and lung adenocarcinoma, many of which are likely inversions but could not be definitively classified due to technical constraints. This ambiguity underscores a critical gap in genomic medicine: without reliable, high-resolution tools for inversion detection, clinicians and researchers cannot fully assess the contribution of these variants to disease etiology, prognosis, or therapeutic response.

Prior art in strand-specific hybridization offers a conceptual foundation but lacks the scope and practicality required for genome-wide inversion screening. Chromosome orientation FISH (CO-FISH), developed in the 1990s, utilizes single-stranded probes to interrogate the orientation of repetitive sequences such as telomeres or centromeric satellites. In CO-FISH, cells are pulse-labeled with bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) during S-phase, and after mitotic arrest, the BrdU-substituted nascent strands are selectively degraded by UV irradiation and exonuclease treatment, leaving the parental strands as single-stranded targets. Strand-specific probes, typically short oligonucleotides complementary to repetitive motifs, are then hybridized to reveal the directionality of these regions. While CO-FISH has provided valuable insights into telomere replication dynamics and chromosomal asymmetry, its utility is inherently limited to repetitive DNA, which constitutes less than 10% of the human genome and is rarely involved in pathogenic inversions. The inability to target unique, gene-rich sequences rendered CO-FISH unsuitable for comprehensive inversion detection.

The present invention addresses these unmet needs by integrating the strand-specific principle of CO-FISH with modern bioinformatics and oligonucleotide synthesis technologies. By leveraging the complete human genome reference sequence, it becomes possible to design thousands of single-stranded probes targeting unique, non-repetitive regions across an entire chromosome, all oriented in the same 5′→3′ direction. When applied to metaphase chromosomes prepared with BrdU incorporation and strand-specific degradation, these probes collectively “paint” one sister chromatid, revealing any inversion as a discrete switch in signal to the opposite chromatid. This approach—chromatid painting—combines the single-cell resolution and genome-wide perspective of classical cytogenetics with the molecular precision and orientation sensitivity of nucleic acid hybridization. It is not only capable of detecting known inversions with high fidelity but also enables unbiased discovery of novel rearrangements, including those too small or too complex for conventional methods. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in structural variant analysis, filling a critical methodological void and opening new avenues for research and clinical diagnostics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for the detection and characterization of chromosomal inversions in individual cells, termed directional genomic hybridization or chromatid painting. The method is based on the principle that during DNA replication, each parental DNA strand serves as a template for the synthesis of a complementary nascent strand, resulting in sister chromatids that are composed of one parental and one newly synthesized strand. By incorporating brominated nucleotides—specifically 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU) and 5-bromo-2′-deoxycytidine (BrdC)—during a single round of DNA synthesis, the nascent strands become susceptible to selective degradation upon exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light and enzymatic treatment. This process renders each sister chromatid predominantly single-stranded, with one chromatid retaining the original Watson strand and the other retaining the Crick strand of the parental duplex. The invention exploits this strand asymmetry by employing a set of single-stranded, fluorescently labeled oligonucleotide probes that are all designed to hybridize in the same 5′→3′ orientation relative to the reference genome. Because DNA inversions reverse the local orientation of the sequence, the complementary strand within the inverted region resides on the opposite sister chromatid. Consequently, when the directional probe set is hybridized to the single-stranded chromatids, the fluorescent signal corresponding to the inverted segment appears on the sister chromatid opposite to that of the flanking, non-inverted regions, producing a visually discernible “switch” in signal location that directly indicates the presence and boundaries of an inversion.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for detecting a chromosomal inversion in a cell, comprising: (a) culturing the cell in the presence of BrdU and BrdC for a single cell cycle to incorporate these analogs into nascent DNA strands; (b) arresting the cell in metaphase; (c) preparing metaphase chromosome spreads on a solid support; (d) selectively degrading the BrdU/BrdC-containing nascent strands to generate single-stranded chromatids; (e) hybridizing the spreads with a plurality of single-stranded, fluorescently labeled oligonucleotide probes, wherein each probe is complementary to a unique genomic sequence within a target chromosome or chromosomal region and is oriented in the same 5′→3′ direction relative to the reference genome; and (f) detecting the hybridization signal by fluorescence microscopy, wherein a switch in signal from one sister chromatid to the other indicates the presence of an inversion.

In another aspect, the invention provides a composition comprising a pool of at least 100 single-stranded oligonucleotide probes, each probe being 30 to 60 nucleotides in length, complementary to a unique non-repetitive sequence within a contiguous genomic region of at least 1 megabase, and all probes oriented in the same 5′→3′ direction relative to the reference genome assembly. The composition may be labeled with one or more fluorophores and is suitable for use in directional genomic hybridization assays.

In a further aspect, the invention enables high-resolution targeted detection of known or suspected inversions by designing dense probe sets flanking and spanning predicted breakpoint regions. For example, probe sets covering 14 to 63 kilobases adjacent to known inversion breakpoints on chromosomes 3 and 10 have been shown to clearly delineate the inverted segment by split signals across sister chromatids. This targeted approach is particularly valuable for validating inversions identified by sequencing and for monitoring minimal residual disease in cancer patients harboring specific inversion biomarkers.

The invention is broadly applicable across species, as demonstrated by successful hybridization of human chromosome 3-specific probe sets to metaphase spreads from chimpanzee, gorilla, and orangutan fibroblasts, revealing evolutionary inversions such as a large pericentric inversion fixed in orangutan chromosome 3. Additionally, the method facilitates simultaneous detection of both intra-chromosomal (inversions) and inter-chromosomal (translocations, dicentrics) rearrangements in the same cell, providing a comprehensive view of genomic instability. The single-stranded oligonucleotide probes used in the invention exhibit exceptional specificity, eliminating the need for Cot-1 DNA or other blocking agents during hybridization, and can also be employed in conventional double-stranded FISH applications for standard chromosome painting.

Overall, the invention overcomes the major limitations of existing inversion detection methods by providing a cytogenomic platform that is orientation-sensitive, single-cell resolved, genome-wide capable, and scalable in resolution—from megabase-level screening to kilobase-level targeted analysis. It is particularly suited for applications in cancer genomics, radiation biodosimetry, reproductive genetics, and evolutionary biology, where accurate detection of balanced structural variants is essential.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Selection of Sequences:

The successful implementation of directional genomic hybridization hinges on the precise selection and design of single-stranded oligonucleotide probes that are both sequence-specific and uniformly oriented. The process begins with the acquisition of high-quality genomic sequence data from a publicly available reference genome assembly, such as the GRCh37.p2 primary assembly of the human genome maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). The target chromosome or chromosomal region of interest is first delineated—for instance, the entirety of chromosome 3 or a specific locus such as 10q11.2–q21 implicated in radiation-induced thyroid carcinoma. Contiguous sequence blocks, or contigs, corresponding to the selected region are downloaded and subjected to repeat masking using specialized bioinformatics software, such as RepeatMasker from the Genetic Information Research Institute (GIRI). This step is critical to exclude repetitive elements—including Alu, LINE, SINE, and satellite repeats—that could lead to non-specific hybridization and background noise. Only unique, non-repetitive sequences are retained for probe design.

Using probe design software such as ARRAY Designer (version 4.2) or proprietary algorithms developed by KromaTiD Inc., oligonucleotides are tiled across the masked sequence at predetermined intervals. For genome-wide screening, a spacing of approximately 1 megabase (Mb) is employed, ensuring that any inversion of 1 Mb or larger will be spanned by at least one probe set. Each probe is typically 40 nucleotides in length, although lengths ranging from 30 to 60 nucleotides are acceptable, provided they meet stringent thermodynamic criteria. Specifically, all probes within a given set must exhibit a uniform melting temperature (Tm) of 70.0 ± 5.0°C to ensure consistent hybridization kinetics under standardized conditions. Crucially, every probe is designed to be complementary to the same DNA strand—either the Watson or Crick strand—across the entire target region, thereby enforcing a common 5′→3′ directionality relative to the reference genome’s pter-to-qter axis. This directional uniformity is what enables the detection of inversions: when a segment is inverted, the complementary sequence shifts to the opposite chromatid, causing the probe signal to “switch” sides.

For high-resolution targeted applications, the probe density is significantly increased. Instead of 1-Mb spacing, probes are tiled at intervals of 100 to 500 base pairs across a region of interest, such as a 10–100 kb window surrounding a suspected inversion breakpoint. This dense tiling allows for the detection of inversions as small as 6 kilobases, as demonstrated in experimental validations. Each targeted probe set may consist of 180 to 200 individual oligonucleotides, pooled and labeled with a distinct fluorophore (e.g., Cy3 for red, fluorescein for green) to enhance signal brightness and enable multiplexing. The selection of sequences for targeted sets is guided by prior knowledge—such as breakpoints identified in sequencing studies or recurrent rearrangements in specific cancer types—but can also be used prospectively to fine-map inversion boundaries once a candidate region is flagged by low-resolution screening.

The final probe pool is synthesized using high-fidelity oligonucleotide synthesis platforms (e.g., Invitrogen), hydrated, and subdivided into manageable subsets for labeling. Typically, 45 oligos are end-labeled with a fluorescent dNTP analog using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase (TdT), and two such labeled subsets are combined to form a probe set covering 5–14.5 kb for screening applications or 14–63 kb for targeted analysis. The complete chromosome paint—such as the human chromosome 3 paint comprising 190 probe sets and over 17,000 individual oligos—is assembled by pooling all validated sets. Quality control includes hybridization to control metaphase spreads to confirm strand specificity, signal intensity, and absence of cross-hybridization.

### EXAMPLE 2

The utility of directional genomic hybridization for genome-wide inversion discovery was demonstrated using human cells exposed to ionizing radiation (IR), a known clastogen that induces a spectrum of chromosomal aberrations, including inversions. Normal human fibroblasts and lymphocytes were cultured for a single cell cycle in medium containing 5.0 μM BrdU and 1.0 μM BrdC to achieve unifilar substitution of nascent DNA strands. Mitotic arrest was induced with colcemid, and metaphase chromosome spreads were prepared on glass slides. Following UV irradiation and exonuclease treatment to degrade BrdU/BrdC-containing strands, the slides were hybridized with the complete human chromosome 3 chromatid paint—a pool of 190 probe sets, each labeled with either Cy3 (red) or fluorescein (green), and collectively spanning the entire chromosome at ~1-Mb intervals.

Fluorescence microscopy revealed robust, chromatid-specific painting of chromosome 3 in untreated control cells, with all signals confined to a single sister chromatid, confirming the directional uniformity of the probe set. In IR-exposed cells, however, distinct signal switches were observed, indicative of inversions. Small inversions, defined as those less than 5 Mb, appeared as localized interruptions in the continuous paint, with a discrete red or green signal appearing on the opposite chromatid while the flanking regions remained on the original chromatid. Large inversions, exceeding 10 Mb, produced a dramatic reversal: the central segment of the chromosome displayed signal on one chromatid, while the distal arms remained on the other, creating a clear “split” pattern. The frequency of inversions increased with radiation dose, and the method reliably detected events as small as 1 Mb, consistent with the probe spacing. Notably, the spontaneous inversion frequency in unexposed cells was found to be less than 0.3%, establishing a low baseline for genotoxicity assessment.

To validate the resolution limits, a mock “mini-inversion” was engineered in silico within a 10 Mb region of chromosome 3q. All probe sets in this region were designed in the forward orientation and labeled green, except for a 6 kb segment, for which probes were intentionally designed in the reverse orientation and labeled red. Upon hybridization, the 6 kb segment appeared as a bright red signal on the opposite chromatid, surrounded by green signals on the original chromatid, unequivocally demonstrating that inversions well below 1 Mb are detectable when probe density is sufficiently high. This experiment confirmed that the resolution of the method is not fixed but rather probabilistic and scalable, depending on probe spacing and labeling strategy.

### EXAMPLE 3

The application of directional genomic hybridization for high-resolution targeted inversion detection was exemplified using two well-characterized cancer-associated rearrangements. First, the Kasumi-4 leukemia cell line, which harbors a large pericentric inversion on chromosome 3, inv(3)(q21;q26), was analyzed. Two targeted probe sets were designed: one spanning the q21 breakpoint region and labeled with Cy3 (red), and another spanning the q26 breakpoint region and labeled with fluorescein (green). Both probe sets consisted of 180–200 oligonucleotides covering ~50 kb around each breakpoint. Hybridization to Kasumi-4 metaphase spreads revealed that the red and green signals, which in normal cells would appear on the same chromatid, were instead split across sister chromatids in the inverted homolog—red on one chromatid, green on the other—precisely as predicted by the inversion model. The non-inverted homolog displayed co-localized signals on a single chromatid, serving as an internal control.

Second, the RET/PTC1 rearrangement, a paracentric inversion on chromosome 10, inv(10)(q11.2;q21), associated with radiation-induced papillary thyroid carcinoma, was investigated. HTori-3 immortalized human thyroid cells were irradiated and harvested at various time points post-exposure. Targeted probe sets flanking the q11.2 and q21 breakpoints were hybridized to metaphase spreads. At time zero, a small subset of cells (approximately 0.5%) exhibited split signals—indicating the presence of the inversion in one chromosome 10 homolog—while the majority showed co-localized signals. This finding corroborated prior reports of RET/PTC1 as an early, radiation-induced event and demonstrated the method’s sensitivity in detecting rare inversion-bearing cells within a heterogeneous population. The ability to quantify inversion frequency on a cell-by-cell basis makes this approach ideal for radiation biodosimetry and risk assessment.

### Hybridization:

The hybridization protocol for directional genomic hybridization is optimized to ensure specific binding of single-stranded probes to their complementary single-stranded chromatid targets while minimizing non-specific interactions. Prior to hybridization, metaphase slides undergo a series of pre-treatments to render the chromatids single-stranded. Slides are first incubated in PN buffer (sodium phosphate) for 10 minutes at room temperature, rinsed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and dehydrated through an ethanol series (75%, 85%, 100%) for 2 minutes each. They are then air-dried and stained with Hoechst 33258 (0.5 μg/mL in 2× SSC) for 15 minutes in the dark. After rinsing with deionized water, slides are coverslipped in 2× SSC and exposed to 365 nm UV light for 35 minutes using a Stratalinker 2400. This step nicks the DNA at sites of BrdU incorporation. The coverslips are removed, and slides are treated with Exonuclease III to degrade the nicked nascent strands, leaving the parental strands intact and single-stranded. Slides are again dehydrated through the ethanol series and air-dried.

For hybridization, a mixture containing 25.3 μL of hybridization buffer (KromaTiD Inc.), 2.25 μL of the chromatid paint probe pool, and 2.45 μL of deionized water is prepared. The mixture is denatured at 75°C for 5 minutes, then immediately applied to the pre-treated slide, which is coverslipped and sealed with rubber cement. The slide is placed on a 73°C hot plate for 3 minutes to facilitate probe penetration, then transferred to a humidified hybridization chamber and incubated at 37°C overnight. Post-hybridization, slides are washed stringently—five times in 2× SSC at 42°C for 15 minutes each—to remove unbound probes. Finally, slides are counterstained with DAPI in antifade mounting medium (Vector Labs) and sealed for microscopy. The entire process ensures high hybridization efficiency (>90%) and low background, critical for clear signal interpretation.

### Detection:

Detection of hybridization signals is performed using epifluorescence microscopy equipped with appropriate filter sets for the fluorophores employed (e.g., FITC for fluorescein, TRITC for Cy3, and DAPI for counterstaining). An Olympus BX41 microscope fitted with a Photometrics CoolSNAP ES2 camera and Metavue 7.1 imaging software is used to capture high-resolution digital images of metaphase spreads. Each metaphase is carefully scored by trained personnel or automated image analysis algorithms to identify chromatid-specific signal patterns. In the absence of an inversion, all probe signals for a given chromosome appear on a single sister chromatid. In the presence of an inversion, a discrete segment of signal appears on the opposite chromatid, with the transition points indicating the inversion breakpoints. The size of the inversion is estimated based on the genomic distance between the outermost switched probes. For multiplexed experiments using multiple fluorophores, spectral imaging or sequential scanning is employed to distinguish overlapping signals. The method allows for the simultaneous scoring of hundreds of metaphases, enabling robust statistical analysis of inversion frequencies in response to genotoxic agents or in disease cohorts.

### Results:

The results of directional genomic hybridization consistently demonstrate its superior capability for inversion detection compared to conventional methods. In human cells, the chromosome 3 chromatid paint produced bright, specific signals on one chromatid in over 90% of metaphases, confirming the fidelity of strand-specific preparation and probe design. Ionizing radiation induced a dose-dependent increase in inversion frequency, with both small and large events readily visualized. The mock 6 kb inversion was clearly resolved, proving that sub-megabase inversions are detectable with appropriate probe density. In cancer cell lines, known inversions such as inv(3)(q21;q26) and inv(10)(q11.2;q21) were unambiguously identified by split signals, with 100% concordance with prior molecular data. Cross-species hybridization revealed a fixed pericentric inversion in orangutan chromosome 3, undetectable by standard karyotyping, highlighting the method’s utility in evolutionary genomics. Furthermore, the technique successfully detected complex aberrations, including dicentric chromosomes and compound acentric fragments, alongside inversions in the same cell, showcasing its comprehensive nature. Critically, the low spontaneous inversion rate (<0.3%) in normal cells validates the method’s suitability for genotoxicity testing and risk assessment. Collectively, these results establish directional genomic hybridization as a robust, versatile, and high-resolution platform for the detection and characterization of chromosomal inversions in research and clinical settings.
