# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE DISCLOSURE

Transcatheter interventions have become the cornerstone of modern structural heart disease management, offering minimally invasive alternatives to open-heart surgery for a broad spectrum of conditions including atrial septal defect closure, patent foramen ovale occlusion, valvular repair or replacement, left atrial appendage exclusion, and implantation of hemodynamic monitoring devices. These procedures rely heavily on real-time imaging guidance to navigate catheters and deploy therapeutic devices within the complex three-dimensional anatomy of the human heart. Historically, X-ray fluoroscopy has served as the primary intraoperative imaging modality due to its capacity for continuous real-time visualization and its ability to clearly depict radiopaque markers affixed to transcatheter instruments. However, fluoroscopy presents significant limitations that impede optimal procedural execution and safety. Specifically, while fluoroscopy effectively outlines the silhouette of the cardiac shadow and visualizes metallic components of interventional devices, it fails to delineate intracardiac soft tissue structures, which remain radiolucent under standard imaging parameters designed to minimize radiation exposure to both patients and clinical staff. Consequently, clinicians must resort to the intermittent administration of iodinated contrast agents to transiently opacify blood pools and thereby infer the spatial relationship between catheters and surrounding anatomical landmarks—a technique that introduces additional procedural complexity, potential nephrotoxicity, and only provides momentary visualization without persistent structural context.

Moreover, fluoroscopic imaging inherently produces two-dimensional projections of a three-dimensional reality, fundamentally lacking depth perception and thereby requiring operators to mentally reconstruct spatial relationships through sequential imaging from multiple angulations. This cognitive burden is compounded by the absence of direct visualization of critical anatomical substrates such as myocardial walls, valve leaflets, or septal membranes, forcing interventionalists to rely on indirect cues and extensive experience to estimate device positioning relative to invisible targets. To partially address these shortcomings, echocardiography—particularly transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)—has emerged as a complementary imaging modality capable of directly visualizing cardiac structures and blood flow dynamics in real time. While TEE provides superior soft tissue contrast compared to fluoroscopy, its clinical adoption remains constrained by several practical limitations: the requirement for specialized operator expertise, the need for esophageal probe placement that may be contraindicated in certain patient populations, and limited availability of high-quality TEE equipment in many interventional laboratories, particularly outside major academic centers. The combined use of fluoroscopy and echocardiography thus represents a multimodal but fragmented approach that demands constant mental integration of disparate two-dimensional data streams without providing a unified spatial framework for device navigation.

In parallel with intraoperative imaging advancements, preoperative cross-sectional imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging have revolutionized procedural planning by delivering exquisitely detailed three-dimensional anatomical reconstructions of the cardiovascular system. These volumetric datasets enable precise measurement of anatomical dimensions, identification of pathological variants, and simulation of device deployment prior to actual intervention. Efforts to integrate this rich preoperative information into the intraoperative environment have led to the development of image fusion techniques that overlay segmented CT or MR structures onto live fluoroscopic images. However, conventional display paradigms present these hybrid renderings on standard two-dimensional monitors positioned adjacent to or superimposed upon the primary fluoroscopic display, creating visual clutter that often obscures critical real-time information rather than enhancing situational awareness. Furthermore, even when successfully fused, these representations remain confined to flat-screen displays that fail to convey true three-dimensional spatial relationships, thereby perpetuating the fundamental limitation of depth perception that plagues traditional fluoroscopy.

The technical challenge of aligning disparate imaging modalities—known as image registration—has been extensively studied in medical imaging research, yielding various algorithmic approaches categorized broadly as intensity-based, gradient-based, and feature-based methods. Intensity-based registration typically relies on statistical measures such as mutual information to align images by maximizing similarity in pixel intensity distributions; however, this approach proves problematic when applied across different imaging modalities due to inherent differences in how tissues are represented (e.g., bone appears hyperdense in CT but nearly invisible in fluoroscopy). Gradient-based methods attempt to circumvent this limitation by focusing on edges and boundaries that tend to be more consistent across modalities, yet they remain sensitive to noise and partial volume effects common in clinical imaging. Feature-based registration seeks to identify and match distinctive anatomical landmarks or patterns using descriptors such as Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) or Speeded-Up Robust Features (SURF); while theoretically promising, these methods struggle with the low-contrast, noisy nature of fluoroscopic images and the difficulty of reliably extracting consistent features from both preoperative volumetric scans and intraoperative planar projections.

Augmented reality (AR) technology has recently emerged as a potential solution to these longstanding visualization challenges by enabling the projection of three-dimensional digital content directly into the user's physical field of view through head-mounted displays. Unlike virtual reality systems that completely immerse users in synthetic environments, AR preserves the real-world context while overlaying relevant digital information, making it particularly well-suited for surgical guidance applications where maintaining awareness of the actual operative field is paramount. Early medical AR implementations have demonstrated utility in orthopedic and neurosurgical procedures, where relatively static anatomical targets and well-defined bony landmarks facilitate accurate registration and stable holographic overlays. However, these existing systems lack the real-time tracking capabilities, dynamic updating mechanisms, and quantitative feedback essential for guiding flexible catheters through the constantly moving environment of the beating heart. The absence of validated fiducial markers that remain consistently visible across both preoperative CT and intraoperative fluoroscopy further complicates cardiac AR implementation, as traditional external markers are impractical in sterile fields and internal soft tissue structures exhibit poor visibility in fluoroscopic imaging.

Consequently, there exists a critical unmet need for an integrated image-guidance system that overcomes the limitations of current visualization paradigms by providing real-time, three-dimensional spatial awareness during transcatheter cardiac interventions. Such a system must address the fundamental challenges of cross-modality image registration, dynamic catheter tracking, and intuitive three-dimensional visualization while operating within the practical constraints of clinical workflow, radiation safety, and computational efficiency. The present invention directly addresses these needs through a novel augmented reality platform that leverages the spine as a universal fiducial marker for robust registration between preoperative CT anatomy and intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging, enabling precise three-dimensional reconstruction and holographic visualization of catheter position relative to patient-specific cardiac structures.

## SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention provides a comprehensive augmented reality-assisted image guidance system for transcatheter cardiac interventions that overcomes the limitations of conventional two-dimensional imaging modalities by delivering real-time, three-dimensional spatial awareness through holographic visualization. At its core, the system integrates preoperative computed tomography (CT) imaging with intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging through a novel registration methodology that utilizes the spinal column as a universal fiducial marker, thereby establishing a common coordinate system for accurate spatial alignment between patient-specific anatomical models and real-time device tracking. The invention comprises several key technological innovations that collectively enable unprecedented precision in catheter localization and intuitive three-dimensional visualization during complex structural heart procedures.

First, the system employs a sophisticated image segmentation pipeline to extract high-fidelity three-dimensional models of the patient's heart and spine from diagnostic CT scans. This process involves threshold-based binarization followed by noise reduction through selection of the largest connected component, hole filling, and manual refinement using three-dimensional masking techniques. Subsequent processing removes extraneous anatomical structures such as ribs and peripheral vasculature to optimize the model for both three-dimensional printing and augmented reality visualization. For specific procedural applications such as transseptal puncture, the system further incorporates pre-planned navigation paths and target markers directly into the anatomical model to provide explicit visual guidance during intervention.

Second, the invention implements a robust spine detection algorithm that operates effectively across both CT-derived projections and fluoroscopic images. In CT images, spine detection is achieved through generation of two-dimensional projection views that match the acquisition geometry of the fluoroscopic C-arm, while in fluoroscopic images, the algorithm employs Gaussian smoothing followed by adaptive thresholding using Otsu's method to isolate the vertebral column from surrounding tissues. Morphological operations are then applied to refine the segmentation and eliminate residual noise artifacts. Critically, the system selects five thoracic vertebrae (T4-T8) as the fiducial reference region due to their proximity to cardiac structures of interest and consistent visibility in fluoroscopic imaging with minimal radiation exposure.

Third, the invention introduces a Fourier transform-based image registration method that achieves superior accuracy and reliability compared to conventional feature-based approaches. By leveraging the mathematical properties of the Fourier transform, the system decouples rotational, scaling, and translational components of the transformation matrix through conversion to polar-logarithmic coordinates in the frequency domain. This Fourier-Mellin approach converts geometric transformations into simple translations in the transformed space, enabling precise determination of alignment parameters through phase correlation analysis. Experimental validation demonstrates that this method achieves sub-millimeter registration accuracy (0.425 mm mean error) with 100% success rate across multiple imaging angles, significantly outperforming SIFT and SURF-based registration techniques in both precision and computational efficiency.

Fourth, the system incorporates a dual-view catheter detection and three-dimensional reconstruction algorithm that processes fluoroscopic images captured from two distinct C-arm angles to compute the complete spatial coordinates of the catheter within the patient's anatomy. The detection algorithm employs intensity-based edge detection perpendicular to the catheter axis, iteratively tracking the centerline from the insertion point upward through successive regions of interest. Three-dimensional position calculation leverages the known angular relationship between C-arm positions to solve for depth coordinates through trigonometric relationships, achieving remarkable precision with mean standard errors below 0.3 mm in experimental validation.

Finally, the invention delivers an immersive augmented reality visualization experience through integration with head-mounted display technology such as Microsoft HoloLens. The system renders patient-specific heart and spine models as interactive holograms that can be manipulated through natural hand gestures and voice commands, providing intuitive three-dimensional spatial awareness without obstructing the clinician's view of the actual procedural field. Additional visualization features include virtual camera perspectives from the catheter tip, cross-sectional views accessible through voice activation, and pre-configured standard imaging angles that mirror conventional fluoroscopic projections. The entire system operates through wireless communication between the AR headset and a dedicated processing server, enabling seamless real-time updates during active intervention.

Collectively, these innovations represent a paradigm shift in image-guided cardiac interventions, transforming the traditional two-dimensional, mentally reconstructed procedural environment into an intuitive three-dimensional workspace where anatomical structures and interventional devices coexist in accurate spatial relationship. The system not only enhances procedural precision and safety but also serves as a powerful educational tool for training new interventionalists and simulating complex cases prior to actual performance. By addressing the fundamental limitations of current imaging paradigms through robust cross-modality registration, precise device tracking, and immersive visualization, the present invention establishes a new standard for image guidance in structural heart disease interventions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive augmented reality-assisted image guidance system for transcatheter cardiac interventions that integrates preoperative computed tomography (CT) imaging with intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging through a novel registration methodology utilizing the spinal column as a universal fiducial marker. The system establishes a common coordinate system for accurate spatial alignment between patient-specific anatomical models and real-time device tracking, enabling precise three-dimensional reconstruction and holographic visualization of catheter position relative to cardiac structures. The detailed implementation of this system encompasses several interconnected components that work in concert to deliver unprecedented spatial awareness during complex structural heart procedures.

The foundation of the system begins with the acquisition and processing of preoperative CT imaging data, which serves as the source for generating patient-specific three-dimensional anatomical models. Diagnostic CT scans are imported into specialized medical image processing software in DICOM format, where each axial slice undergoes threshold-based binarization to isolate structures of interest based on their characteristic Hounsfield unit values. For cardiac structures, appropriate threshold ranges are selected to differentiate myocardial tissue from surrounding mediastinal fat and pulmonary parenchyma, while for the spine, higher thresholds capture the dense cortical bone of vertebral bodies. This initial thresholding typically produces rough segmentations containing numerous isolated objects due to partial volume effects and image noise inherent in clinical CT acquisitions. To address this, the segmentation algorithm selects only the largest connected component corresponding to the target anatomy, fills any internal voids or holes that may represent trabecular bone spaces or myocardial trabeculations, and applies three-dimensional morphological operations to smooth surface irregularities. 

Subsequent refinement involves manual editing using three-dimensional masking tools that allow operators to add or remove specific regions on individual slices while maintaining topological consistency across the entire volume. This step is particularly important for complex anatomical interfaces such as the atrioventricular junction or great vessel origins, where automated segmentation may produce inaccuracies. The final segmented volumes for both heart and spine are exported as stereolithography (STL) files and imported into mesh processing software for further optimization. Extraneous structures including ribs, sternum, and small peripheral blood vessels are carefully trimmed away to reduce computational complexity during subsequent rendering operations while preserving all clinically relevant cardiac anatomy. For specific procedural applications such as transseptal puncture, additional geometric elements are incorporated into the model, including pre-planned navigation trajectories represented as colored pathways and target zones depicted as concentric rings at intended puncture sites. These enhancements provide explicit visual guidance during intervention while maintaining the anatomical fidelity of the underlying model.

The registration process between preoperative CT anatomy and intraoperative fluoroscopic imaging represents a critical innovation of the present system, addressing the longstanding challenge of cross-modality alignment through utilization of the spinal column as a universal fiducial marker. The spine was selected for this role based on several key characteristics: its consistent visibility in both CT and fluoroscopic imaging modalities, its relative immobility compared to cardiac structures, and its stable anatomical position between preoperative imaging and subsequent intervention when patients are maintained in supine positioning for both procedures. Specifically, five thoracic vertebrae (T4-T8) are designated as the fiducial reference region due to their proximity to the cardiac chambers involved in common transcatheter procedures while remaining sufficiently distant from the heart to avoid interference from respiratory or cardiac motion artifacts. The vertebral bodies are preferred over posterior elements because they provide the strongest radiographic contrast in fluoroscopic imaging, minimizing required radiation dose while maximizing detection reliability.

Spine detection in CT-derived images is accomplished through generation of two-dimensional projection views that precisely match the acquisition geometry of the fluoroscopic C-arm. Using metadata recorded during fluoroscopic imaging that specifies the C-arm angulation (e.g., right anterior oblique 30 degrees), the three-dimensional segmented spine model is rendered from the identical perspective to produce a synthetic projection image. This CT-derived projection serves as the fixed reference image in the subsequent registration process. In parallel, spine detection in actual fluoroscopic images employs a multi-step image processing pipeline designed to handle the low-contrast, noisy nature of clinical fluoroscopy. Initial preprocessing involves application of Gaussian smoothing to reduce high-frequency noise while preserving edge information, followed by adaptive thresholding using Otsu's method to automatically determine optimal intensity cutoffs for binarization. A region of interest is then defined based on the bounding box of the largest connected component in the binarized image, within which refined binarization and morphological closing operations eliminate residual noise particles and ensure continuous vertebral segmentation. The resulting segmented spine from fluoroscopy serves as the moving image in the registration algorithm.

The core registration methodology employs a Fourier transform-based approach that leverages mathematical properties of frequency domain representations to achieve robust alignment between the CT-derived projection and fluoroscopic images. This technique, known as the Fourier-Mellin transform, exploits the fact that geometric transformations in the spatial domain correspond to specific modifications in the frequency domain that can be systematically analyzed and inverted. Starting with the two-dimensional Fourier transforms of both the fixed reference image (Fc) and moving image (Ff), the algorithm first examines the spectral magnitude components, which are invariant to translational shifts. The relationship between these magnitudes reveals that rotation in the spatial domain corresponds to identical rotation in the spectral amplitude, while scaling produces inverse scaling in the frequency domain. By converting the Cartesian frequency coordinates to polar representation (θ, ρ), rotation becomes a simple shift along the angular axis, and scaling affects only the radial coordinate. Further transformation of the radial coordinate to logarithmic scale (γ = log(ρ)) converts scaling into another translational shift, resulting in a polar-logarithmic representation where both rotation and scaling manifest as pure translations.

Application of a second Fourier transform to this polar-logarithmic representation yields a phase correlation function where the location of maximum correlation directly indicates the rotational and scaling parameters required for alignment. Specifically, the horizontal offset corresponds to the rotation angle (α), while the vertical offset represents the logarithm of the scaling factor (κ = log(k)). Once these parameters are determined, the original images are resampled to correct for rotation and scaling differences, after which a final phase correlation analysis in the original Cartesian domain determines the translational offsets (tx, ty) needed for complete alignment. The complete transformation matrix thus derived enables precise mapping between coordinate systems, establishing the foundation for accurate three-dimensional reconstruction of catheter position relative to patient anatomy.

Catheter detection and three-dimensional localization constitute another critical component of the system, employing dual-view fluoroscopic imaging to overcome the inherent depth ambiguity of single-plane projections. The detection algorithm begins by identifying the catheter entry point at the inferior aspect of the fluoroscopic image, where transcatheter devices typically enter the venous system via the femoral approach. An initial region of interest spanning ten rows of pixels at the image bottom undergoes adaptive thresholding to isolate the catheter from surrounding tissues, leveraging the fact that metallic catheter components appear darker than soft tissues due to higher X-ray absorption. Within this region, the algorithm identifies the catheter's lateral edges by computing intensity derivatives perpendicular to the assumed axial direction (initially aligned with the image Y-axis), then calculates the centerline as the midpoint between these edges. This process iteratively propagates upward through the image by defining successive regions of interest centered on the previously detected centerline segment, continuously updating the local orientation estimate to accommodate catheter curvature. The final output comprises a series of discrete points representing the catheter's three-dimensional trajectory, with sufficient density to capture complex bending patterns while maintaining computational efficiency.

Three-dimensional position calculation requires fluoroscopic images captured from at least two distinct C-arm angles, preferably separated by a substantial angular difference to maximize depth resolution. In typical clinical practice, the C-arm rotates around the patient's longitudinal axis, producing views such as anterior-posterior (AP), left anterior oblique (LAO), and right anterior oblique (RAO) projections. The known angular relationship between these views (e.g., 30 degrees between AP and RAO30) enables trigonometric solution for the missing depth coordinate through intersection of projection rays from each view. Mathematically, if P1 = [x1, y1, z1] and P2 = [x2, y2, z2] represent the same physical point in two different coordinate systems related by a rotation matrix R(θ) around the Y-axis, then the depth coordinates z1 and z2 can be solved from the system of equations defined by the rotation relationship. This computation is performed for each detected point along the catheter trajectory, resulting in a complete three-dimensional representation of the device within the patient's anatomy.

The final component of the system involves augmented reality visualization through integration with head-mounted display technology, specifically implemented using Microsoft HoloLens hardware in the described embodiment. The patient-specific heart and spine models, along with the reconstructed catheter trajectory, are rendered as interactive holograms that appear spatially registered within the user's physical environment. The HoloLens platform was selected for three primary reasons: its video see-through optical design preserves the clinician's direct view of the actual procedural field while overlaying digital content; its integrated depth sensors and spatial mapping capabilities enable stable hologram anchoring relative to physical surfaces; and its wireless connectivity facilitates seamless data exchange with the processing server running the image analysis algorithms. 

The visualization interface incorporates several advanced features designed to enhance procedural guidance and user interaction. Natural hand gestures allow operators to grab, rotate, and reposition holographic models using intuitive pinch-and-drag motions, while voice commands provide hands-free control over visualization parameters. Speaking "open" triggers automatic generation of cross-sectional views through the heart model, revealing internal anatomical relationships that would otherwise require mental reconstruction from external surfaces. Pre-configured standard viewing angles corresponding to common fluoroscopic projections (AP, LAO30, RAO30) can be instantly recalled through voice activation, enabling rapid comparison between holographic and actual fluoroscopic perspectives. Additionally, a virtual camera positioned at the catheter tip provides an endoluminal view aligned with the device's orientation, offering unique perspective on target anatomy during critical maneuvers such as transseptal puncture.

System integration follows a client-server architecture where the computationally intensive image processing tasks—including spine detection, image registration, catheter tracking, and three-dimensional reconstruction—are performed on a dedicated server workstation, while the AR headset handles only the final rendering and user interaction components. Processed data is transmitted wirelessly over a secure hospital network using optimized communication protocols that minimize latency and ensure real-time responsiveness during active intervention. The entire workflow, from initial CT segmentation through final AR visualization, has been validated using three-dimensional printed phantom models created from actual patient scans, demonstrating sub-millimeter registration accuracy (0.425 mm mean error) and exceptional catheter localization precision (0.298 mm mean standard error). These performance metrics significantly exceed the spatial requirements of clinical procedures such as transseptal puncture, where a 5 mm safety margin around the target site is typically considered acceptable.

While the current implementation focuses on static anatomical models derived from single-phase CT acquisitions, the system architecture readily accommodates future enhancements to address cardiac and respiratory motion. Potential extensions include integration of electrocardiogram-gated multi-phase CT datasets to generate four-dimensional heart models that capture cyclic contraction patterns, incorporation of real-time ultrasound imaging for direct soft tissue visualization, and development of motion compensation algorithms that dynamically adjust holographic overlays based on detected physiological motion. Additionally, machine learning approaches could be employed to improve spine segmentation in challenging clinical scenarios involving obese patients or those with degenerative spinal changes, further expanding the system's applicability across diverse patient populations.

The comprehensive nature of this augmented reality guidance system represents a transformative advance in image-guided cardiac interventions, addressing fundamental limitations of current visualization paradigms through robust cross-modality registration, precise device tracking, and intuitive three-dimensional visualization. By providing clinicians with unprecedented spatial awareness during complex transcatheter procedures, the system enhances procedural precision, reduces complications, shortens learning curves for trainees, and enables simulation-based planning for challenging cases. The modular design ensures compatibility with existing clinical workflows while establishing a foundation for future innovations in real-time physiological monitoring and adaptive guidance during dynamic cardiac interventions.
