# DESCRIPTION

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a method for the treatment of glioblastoma in a human subject, comprising administering to the subject an effective amount of a fusion protein comprising an antibody or antibody fragment that specifically binds to the A1 domain of tenascin-C and interleukin-2 (IL-2), in combination with temozolomide. The fusion protein is preferably F16–IL2, wherein the F16 moiety is an antibody or antibody fragment that specifically recognizes and binds to the A1 domain of human tenascin-C, and the IL-2 moiety is human interleukin-2. This therapeutic regimen demonstrates synergistic anti-tumor activity in both subcutaneous and orthotopic models of human glioblastoma, resulting in complete tumor eradication in subcutaneous models and significant tumor volume reduction and prolonged survival in intracranial models, without evidence of increased systemic toxicity.

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) represents the most aggressive and lethal form of primary brain tumor in adults, characterized by rapid growth, diffuse infiltration into surrounding brain parenchyma, resistance to conventional therapies, and inevitable recurrence. Despite advances in surgical resection, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy—particularly with the alkylating agent temozolomide—the median survival for patients diagnosed with GBM remains approximately 15 months, with fewer than 10% of patients surviving beyond five years. The limitations of current therapeutic approaches underscore the urgent need for novel strategies that can overcome the intrinsic resistance mechanisms of glioblastoma and enhance anti-tumor immune responses within the central nervous system microenvironment.

The invention capitalizes on the unique biology of the tumor extracellular matrix (ECM), which undergoes profound remodeling during malignant progression. Tenascin-C, a large hexameric glycoprotein normally expressed during embryonic development and transiently during wound healing, is aberrantly and persistently upregulated in the stroma of many solid tumors, including high-grade gliomas. Within the tenascin-C molecule, the A1 domain constitutes a splice variant that is selectively expressed in pathological conditions such as cancer and is virtually absent in healthy adult tissues. This tumor-restricted expression pattern renders the A1 domain of tenascin-C an ideal target for antibody-based therapeutics designed to deliver cytotoxic payloads or immune modulators specifically to the tumor site while sparing normal tissues.

The F16 antibody, from which the targeting moiety of the fusion protein is derived, is a fully human monoclonal antibody that exhibits high affinity and specificity for the A1 domain of human tenascin-C. Unlike many murine-derived antibodies, F16 does not cross-react with rodent tenascin-C, which has important implications for preclinical modeling but does not compromise its utility in human therapy. When engineered as a small immunoprotein (SIP) format—a dimeric structure composed of a single-chain Fv fragment fused to the CH3 domain of human IgG1—or when used as part of a larger immunocytokine construct such as F16–IL2, the antibody retains its binding specificity and demonstrates excellent tumor-targeting properties in vivo.

Interleukin-2 (IL-2) is a pleiotropic cytokine that plays a central role in the activation, proliferation, and differentiation of various immune cell populations, particularly natural killer (NK) cells and T lymphocytes. Systemic administration of recombinant IL-2 has been explored in cancer immunotherapy but is limited by severe dose-dependent toxicities, including capillary leak syndrome, hypotension, and multi-organ dysfunction, which arise from widespread activation of immune cells throughout the body. To circumvent these limitations, the present invention employs a targeted delivery strategy wherein IL-2 is genetically fused to the F16 antibody, creating an immunocytokine that concentrates IL-2 activity at the tumor site. This approach enhances local immune stimulation while minimizing systemic exposure and associated adverse effects.

Temozolomide, an oral alkylating agent that methylates DNA at the O6 position of guanine, is the standard first-line chemotherapeutic agent for glioblastoma. It induces DNA damage that triggers cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in rapidly dividing tumor cells. However, resistance to temozolomide commonly develops due to the expression of O6-methylguanine-DNA methyltransferase (MGMT), a DNA repair enzyme that reverses the cytotoxic lesions induced by the drug. Moreover, temozolomide monotherapy rarely achieves durable responses, highlighting the need for combinatorial approaches that can augment its efficacy.

The inventors have discovered that the combination of F16–IL2 and temozolomide produces a synergistic anti-glioblastoma effect that surpasses the activity of either agent alone. In preclinical studies using immunodeficient mice bearing human U87MG glioblastoma xenografts—both subcutaneously and intracranially implanted—the co-administration of F16–IL2 and temozolomide resulted in complete and sustained tumor regression in the subcutaneous model, with all treated animals remaining tumor-free for over 160 days post-initiation of therapy. In the more clinically relevant intracranial model, which recapitulates the anatomical and microenvironmental challenges of human brain tumors, the combination therapy significantly reduced tumor burden and extended animal survival compared to monotherapies or control treatments.

Mechanistically, this synergy arises from complementary modes of action: temozolomide exerts direct cytotoxic effects on tumor cells by inducing DNA damage and apoptosis, while F16–IL2 recruits and activates innate immune effector cells—primarily NK cells and macrophages—to the tumor site. Immunofluorescence analyses of tumor sections revealed a marked increase in the infiltration of CD45+ leukocytes, asialo GM1+ NK cells, and F4/80+ macrophages in tumors treated with the combination regimen, localized predominantly around CD31+ vascular structures where F16–IL2 accumulates. Notably, no significant immune cell infiltration was observed in normal organs such as liver and kidney, confirming the tumor-selective nature of the immune response.

Furthermore, ex vivo detection of the F16–IL2 fusion protein using an anti-human IL-2 antibody confirmed its specific localization within both subcutaneous and intracranial glioblastoma xenografts, co-localizing with tumor vasculature. This targeted delivery ensures that IL-2 activity is concentrated where it is most needed—within the tumor microenvironment—thereby maximizing anti-tumor immunity while avoiding systemic immune activation.

Additional analyses demonstrated that the combination therapy profoundly alters the balance between tumor cell proliferation and death. TUNEL assays revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cells in combination-treated tumors, while Ki67 staining showed near-complete suppression of proliferating tumor cells. These findings indicate that the dual regimen simultaneously enhances tumor cell killing and halts tumor growth, providing a comprehensive anti-tumor effect.

Importantly, the combination therapy did not result in increased toxicity, as evidenced by minimal and comparable weight loss across all treatment groups—never exceeding 9%—and the absence of therapy-related distress or mortality. This favorable safety profile supports the clinical translatability of the approach.

Given that F16–IL2 is a fully human immunocytokine currently under evaluation in Phase Ib clinical trials for other malignancies (in combination with doxorubicin or paclitaxel) and has demonstrated an excellent safety profile in non-human primates, and considering that temozolomide is already an approved standard-of-care agent for glioblastoma, the proposed combination represents a rational and readily implementable therapeutic strategy for patients with this devastating disease.

### VL CDR3 SEQ ID NO: 10

The F16 antibody utilized in the fusion protein of the present invention comprises a variable light chain (VL) complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) having the amino acid sequence set forth in SEQ ID NO: 10. This specific CDR3 sequence is critical for the high-affinity and selective recognition of the A1 domain of human tenascin-C. The precise molecular interactions mediated by this CDR3 loop contribute to the exceptional specificity of F16 for its target antigen, distinguishing it from other tenascin-C isoforms and ensuring minimal off-target binding. The inclusion of this defined VL CDR3 sequence within the antibody component of the immunocytokine is essential for achieving the tumor-localized delivery of IL-2 that underpins the therapeutic efficacy of the invention. Variants or derivatives of the F16 antibody that retain this CDR3 sequence, or conservative substitutions thereof that do not substantially impair antigen binding, are encompassed within the scope of the claimed invention. The structural integrity and functional competence of this CDR3 motif enable the consistent and reproducible targeting performance observed in both preclinical biodistribution studies and therapeutic efficacy experiments, thereby forming a foundational element of the disclosed combination therapy for glioblastoma.

## TERMINOLOGY

### Antibody Molecule

As used herein, the term “antibody molecule” refers to a polypeptide or polypeptide complex that specifically binds to a predetermined antigen through one or more antigen-binding sites. An antibody molecule may be a full-length immunoglobulin, such as an IgG, IgM, IgA, IgD, or IgE, or it may be an antibody fragment that retains the ability to specifically bind antigen. Examples of antibody fragments include, but are not limited to, Fab, Fab′, F(ab′)₂, Fv, single-chain Fv (scFv), diabodies, triabodies, tetrabodies, minibodies, domain antibodies (dAbs), and small immunoproteins (SIPs). The antibody molecule may be of any origin, including murine, chimeric, humanized, or fully human, provided it maintains specific binding to the target antigen. In the context of the present invention, the antibody molecule is preferably a fully human antibody or antibody fragment that specifically recognizes the A1 domain of human tenascin-C, such as the F16 antibody or its functional derivatives. The antibody molecule may be monovalent or multivalent and may exist as a monomer, dimer, or higher-order oligomer, depending on its structural design. For use in the fusion protein of the invention, the antibody molecule serves as the targeting moiety that directs the conjugated cytokine—interleukin-2—to sites of tenascin-C A1 expression, which are predominantly localized within the tumor microenvironment of glioblastoma and other solid malignancies. The antibody molecule must retain sufficient immunoreactivity after conjugation or genetic fusion to ensure effective in vivo targeting, as demonstrated by immunohistochemical validation and quantitative biodistribution studies showing preferential accumulation in tumor tissue relative to normal organs.

### Antigen-Binding Site

As used herein, the term “antigen-binding site” refers to the region of an antibody molecule that is responsible for the specific recognition and binding of an epitope on a target antigen. The antigen-binding site is formed by the three-dimensional arrangement of the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the heavy and light chains of the antibody, which together create a surface complementary in shape, charge, and hydrophobicity to the epitope. In a typical immunoglobulin, there are two identical antigen-binding sites, each composed of three CDRs from the variable heavy chain (VH CDR1, CDR2, CDR3) and three CDRs from the variable light chain (VL CDR1, CDR2, CDR3). The CDR3 regions, particularly VH CDR3, often contribute the majority of the binding energy and specificity due to their greater sequence diversity and structural flexibility. In the context of the present invention, the antigen-binding site of the F16 antibody is specifically configured to recognize and bind with high affinity to the A1 domain of human tenascin-C, a splice variant that is selectively expressed in pathological tissues such as glioblastoma but is absent or minimally expressed in healthy adult tissues. The integrity of this antigen-binding site is essential for the tumor-targeting function of the F16–IL2 fusion protein, as it ensures the selective delivery of interleukin-2 to the tumor microenvironment. Any modification to the antibody component of the fusion protein that preserves the structural and functional characteristics of this antigen-binding site—including conservative amino acid substitutions, framework adjustments, or format conversions (e.g., from IgG to SIP)—is considered to fall within the scope of the invention, provided that specific binding to the tenascin-C A1 domain is maintained at a level sufficient to achieve therapeutic efficacy as demonstrated in the described preclinical models.

## EXPERIMENTS

### Summary

The experimental program detailed herein was designed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of the F16–IL2 immunocytokine, both as a monotherapy and in combination with temozolomide, in preclinical models of human glioblastoma. The studies encompassed multiple complementary approaches, including immunohistochemical characterization of target antigen expression, quantitative biodistribution analysis of the radiolabeled fusion protein, efficacy assessments in both subcutaneous and orthotopic (intracranial) xenograft models, microscopic evaluation of immune cell infiltration and tumor vascular density, ex vivo localization of the administered immunocytokine, and molecular analyses of apoptosis and proliferation within treated tumors. Collectively, these experiments demonstrate that the A1 domain of tenascin-C is robustly expressed in human glioblastoma specimens and in U87MG xenografts, that F16–IL2 selectively accumulates in tumor tissue with high tumor-to-background ratios, and that the combination of F16–IL2 with temozolomide results in synergistic anti-tumor activity characterized by complete tumor eradication in subcutaneous models and significant tumor regression and survival extension in intracranial models. Mechanistic investigations revealed that this synergy is associated with enhanced recruitment of innate immune effector cells—particularly natural killer cells and macrophages—into the tumor microenvironment, localized delivery of IL-2 around tumor vasculature, and a profound shift toward apoptosis and away from proliferation in tumor cells. Critically, these therapeutic benefits were achieved without evidence of increased systemic toxicity, as monitored by daily weight measurements and clinical observation. The experimental data provide a robust scientific foundation for the clinical translation of this combination regimen for the treatment of glioblastoma in human patients.

### Immunohistochemistry on Human Glioblastoma Samples and on Glioblastoma Xenografts

To validate the relevance of the A1 domain of tenascin-C as a therapeutic target in glioblastoma, immunohistochemical analyses were performed on surgically resected human glioblastoma specimens and on U87MG human glioblastoma xenografts grown subcutaneously in immunodeficient mice. Freshly resected human tumor tissues and mouse xenografts were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, snap-frozen, and stored at −80°C until sectioning. Cryosections of 10 μm thickness were prepared and subjected to standardized immunohistochemical staining protocols. Prior to antibody incubation, sections were fixed in ice-cold acetone, rehydrated in Tris-buffered saline (50 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 0.001% aprotinin, pH 7.4), and blocked with 20% fetal calf serum in the same buffer to minimize non-specific binding. Biotinylated small immunoprotein (SIP) formats of the F16 antibody (specific for the A1 domain of tenascin-C) and the L19 antibody (specific for the extradomain B of fibronectin, used as a positive control based on prior literature demonstrating its expression in high-grade gliomas) were applied to the sections at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL. Detection was achieved using a streptavidin–alkaline phosphatase conjugate, with Fast Red serving as the chromogenic substrate, and nuclei were counterstained with Gill’s hematoxylin. Negative controls were included in every experiment by omitting the primary antibody to confirm staining specificity.

Microscopic evaluation using a Zeiss Axiovert S100TV inverted microscope equipped with 5×, 10×, and 20× objectives and Axiovision Release 4 acquisition software revealed strong and specific immunoreactivity for both F16 and L19 in human glioblastoma samples and U87MG xenografts. The staining patterns were comparable between the two antibodies, with prominent labeling observed in perivascular regions and throughout the tumor stroma, consistent with the known deposition of extracellular matrix proteins in high-grade gliomas. Importantly, F16 staining confirmed the abundant and selective expression of the tenascin-C A1 domain in both human and xenografted glioblastoma tissues, thereby establishing the molecular rationale for using F16-based therapeutics in this disease context. The reproducibility of these results was ensured by preparing all antibody aliquots from a single manufacturing batch, storing them at 4°C, and using each aliquot only once, thereby eliminating inter-experimental variability due to reagent degradation or batch differences. These findings collectively demonstrate that the A1 domain of tenascin-C is a viable and highly expressed target in glioblastoma, suitable for antibody-directed therapy.

### Cell Lines and Animals

All in vivo experiments were conducted using the human glioblastoma cell line U87MG (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC HTB-14), which was maintained in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 100 U/mL ampicillin, and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For the establishment of subcutaneous xenografts, 5 × 10⁶ U87MG cells were injected into the right flank of 8-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Charles River Laboratories, Sulzfeld, Germany). Orthotopic (intracranial) xenografts were generated by stereotactic implantation of 5 × 10⁴ U87MG cells into the brains of 6-week-old male BALB/c nude mice, following established protocols that ensure consistent tumor engraftment and growth. Animal housing and experimental procedures for subcutaneous models were performed under a project license granted by the Veterinäramt des Kantons Zürich (license number 198/2005), while orthotopic studies adhered to the institutional animal care guidelines of the University of Milan. Mice were monitored daily for signs of distress, neurological impairment (in intracranial models), or therapy-related toxicity, and humane endpoints were strictly enforced, including euthanasia when subcutaneous tumors reached a volume of 3000 mm³, exhibited necrosis or hemorrhage, or when intracranial tumor-bearing mice displayed overt neurological symptoms. No animals required premature euthanasia due to treatment-related adverse effects, underscoring the tolerability of the therapeutic regimens tested.

### Antibodies and Therapeutic Agents

The therapeutic agents employed in this study included the F16–IL2 fusion protein and temozolomide. The F16 antibody, which specifically targets the A1 domain of human tenascin-C, and the L19 antibody, specific for the extradomain B of fibronectin, have been previously described in the scientific literature and were used here in biotinylated small immunoprotein (SIP) format for immunohistochemistry or as the full-length immunocytokine F16–IL2 for therapy. The F16–IL2 fusion protein consists of the F16 antibody genetically fused to human interleukin-2, enabling targeted delivery of the cytokine to tenascin-C A1-expressing tumor sites. Temozolomide, an alkylating chemotherapeutic agent, was obtained from ABCR GmbH & Co. KG (Karlsruhe, Germany) and dissolved in a vehicle consisting of 0.9% saline containing 10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for intraperitoneal administration. The dosing regimen for temozolomide was carefully calibrated to correspond to a human equivalent dose of 75 mg/m² per injection, based on body surface area conversion factors, aligning with the standard clinical dosing used in glioblastoma patients during concurrent radiotherapy. This dose was selected to be therapeutically relevant yet well below the LD₁₀ (lethal dose for 10% of animals) in nude mice, ensuring that observed effects were attributable to anti-tumor activity rather than excessive toxicity. All therapeutic agents were prepared fresh before each administration, and injection volumes were standardized to 100 μL for intravenous delivery of F16–IL2 and 150 μL for intraperitoneal delivery of temozolomide or vehicle control.

### Biodistribution Experiments

To assess the in vivo tumor-targeting capability of the F16–IL2 immunocytokine, quantitative biodistribution studies were performed in female BALB/c nude mice bearing established subcutaneous U87MG xenografts. When tumors reached a palpable volume of approximately 200 mm³, mice (n=5 per group) were administered 10 μg (3.6 μCi) of radioiodinated F16–IL2 via intravenous injection into the lateral tail vein. Prior to in vivo use, the immunoreactivity of the radiolabeled preparation was confirmed by affinity chromatography on TNC-A1-Sepharose resin; the immunoreactivity, calculated as the percentage of radioactivity bound and eluted relative to total applied radioactivity, was determined to be 84%, indicating that the majority of the labeled protein retained functional antigen-binding capacity. Twenty-four hours post-injection, mice were euthanized, and major organs—including tumor, blood, liver, spleen, kidneys, lungs, heart, and muscle—were harvested, weighed, and analyzed for radioactivity using a Packard Cobra gamma counter. The results were expressed as the percentage of the injected dose per gram of tissue (%ID/g). The data revealed preferential accumulation of F16–IL2 in tumor tissue (2.3 %ID/g), yielding a tumor-to-blood ratio of 11.5 and favorable tumor-to-organ ratios across all assessed normal tissues. These findings confirm that F16–IL2 effectively localizes to glioblastoma xenografts in vivo, providing the pharmacokinetic basis for its use as a targeted delivery vehicle for interleukin-2 in the tumor microenvironment.

### Subcutaneous Glioma Mouse Models

Therapeutic efficacy was first evaluated in a subcutaneous U87MG glioblastoma xenograft model in female BALB/c nude mice. Twelve days after tumor cell implantation, when average tumor volumes reached 300 mm³, mice were stratified into four treatment groups (n=5 per group) to ensure baseline uniformity: (1) F16–IL2 monotherapy (20 μg administered intravenously in 100 μL PBS on days 12, 15, 18, 21, and 24); (2) temozolomide monotherapy (0.525 mg administered intraperitoneally in 150 μL saline/10% DMSO on the same schedule); (3) combination therapy (both agents administered as above); and (4) vehicle control (150 μL saline/10% DMSO intraperitoneally). Tumor dimensions were measured three times weekly using digital calipers, and volumes were calculated using the formula: volume = length × width² / 2. Animals were monitored daily for weight changes and signs of toxicity. The results demonstrated that while temozolomide alone induced significant initial tumor regression, three out of five mice experienced tumor relapse by day 45. F16–IL2 monotherapy produced only modest growth delay compared to control. In stark contrast, all mice receiving the combination therapy achieved complete tumor eradication by day 40 and remained tumor-free for the duration of the observation period (>160 days). Weight loss across all treatment groups remained below 9% at all time points, confirming the absence of significant systemic toxicity. These findings establish the superior and durable anti-tumor efficacy of the F16–IL2 and temozolomide combination in a peripheral tumor model.

### Immunofluorescence Assessment of Tumour-Infiltrating Cells and of Microvascular Density in Subcutaneous Glioma Xenografts

To investigate the immunological mechanisms underlying the observed therapeutic effects, immunofluorescence microscopy was performed on tumor sections from mice treated with F16–IL2, temozolomide, their combination, or vehicle control. Subcutaneous and intracranial U87MG tumor-bearing mice (n=3 per group) received three doses of therapy (on days 12, 15, and 18 post-implantation) and were euthanized 24 hours after the final injection. Tumors were excised, snap-frozen in OCT medium, and sectioned at 10 μm thickness. Sections were fixed in acetone, blocked with a mixture of donkey and goat serum, and incubated with primary antibodies against immune cell markers: rat anti-mouse F4/80 (macrophages), rabbit anti-asialo GM1 (NK cells), and rat anti-mouse CD45 (pan-leukocyte marker). Vascular structures were identified using rat or rabbit anti-mouse CD31 antibodies. Secondary detection was achieved with Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated antibodies, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss Axioskop 2 mot plus fluorescence microscope equipped with an HXP 120 lamp and Axiovision software. Quantitative analysis of three representative fields per section was conducted using ImageJ software, with results expressed as the percentage of positively stained area relative to total tissue area. The combination therapy group exhibited the highest levels of immune cell infiltration, with significant increases in F4/80⁺ macrophages and asialo GM1⁺ NK cells localized around CD31⁺ vascular networks. In contrast, minimal immune cell presence was detected in vehicle- or monotherapy-treated tumors. Parallel analysis of normal liver and kidney tissues from the same animals showed no CD45⁺ cell infiltration, confirming the tumor-restricted nature of the immune response. These data indicate that F16–IL2 promotes focal recruitment of innate immune effectors into the tumor, an effect potentiated by temozolomide co-administration.

### Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using appropriate parametric or non-parametric tests as dictated by the data distribution and experimental design. Comparisons of continuous variables—such as tumor volume, immune cell infiltration percentages, apoptosis indices, and proliferation rates—between two groups were conducted using a two-tailed Student’s t-test, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. For survival studies, Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to estimate survival probabilities over time, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. All p-values reported are two-sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation unless otherwise specified. The use of these rigorous statistical methods ensures the reliability and reproducibility of the conclusions drawn from the experimental data, supporting the robustness of the observed synergistic interaction between F16–IL2 and temozolomide in glioblastoma therapy.
