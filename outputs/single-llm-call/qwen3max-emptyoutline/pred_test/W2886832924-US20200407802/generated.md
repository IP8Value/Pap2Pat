## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of epigenetics and genomic analysis, specifically to methods and systems for identifying, characterizing, and utilizing partially methylated domains (PMDs) and highly methylated domains (HMDs) in mammalian genomes. More particularly, the invention provides a novel approach for defining and analyzing PMD/HMD structure based on a specific subset of CpG dinucleotides—namely, solo-WCGW CpGs—that exhibit heightened susceptibility to replication-dependent DNA methylation loss. The invention further encompasses methods for determining cellular mitotic history, assessing biological age, evaluating cancer progression, and predicting retrotransposon activity through the quantitative analysis of methylation patterns at these sequence-context-defined loci. The disclosed technology enables robust detection of PMD architecture even in low-coverage or single-cell whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) data, thereby expanding the utility of epigenetic profiling in clinical diagnostics, developmental biology, aging research, and oncology.

## BACKGROUND

DNA methylation is a fundamental epigenetic modification that plays a critical role in regulating gene expression, maintaining genomic stability, and ensuring proper cellular differentiation. In mammals, DNA methylation occurs predominantly at cytosine residues within CpG dinucleotides, forming 5-methylcytosine (5mC). Aberrant DNA methylation patterns are hallmarks of numerous diseases, including cancer, where global hypomethylation coexists with locus-specific hypermethylation. A major feature of the cancer methylome is the presence of large genomic regions—spanning hundreds of kilobases to megabases—known as partially methylated domains (PMDs), which exhibit reduced average methylation levels compared to the rest of the genome, referred to as highly methylated domains (HMDs). While PMDs were initially described in cultured cell lines and later in solid tumors, their biological significance, mechanisms of formation, and relationship to normal physiology have remained incompletely understood.

Early studies identified PMDs primarily through whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) or high-density methylation microarrays, using computational segmentation algorithms such as hidden Markov models (HMMs) to delineate methylated and hypomethylated regions. However, these approaches often suffered from limited resolution in low-coverage datasets, inconsistency across sample types, and difficulty in distinguishing true biological variation from technical noise. Moreover, the underlying determinants of why certain genomic regions become PMDs while others remain HMDs have been elusive. It has been hypothesized that PMD formation is linked to nuclear architecture, replication timing, and transcriptional activity, but a unifying mechanistic framework has been lacking.

Concurrently, it has long been recognized that local DNA sequence context influences the efficiency of DNA methylation maintenance. For instance, CpG density—the number of neighboring CpG sites within a short genomic window—has been shown to affect the processivity of DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1), the primary enzyme responsible for copying methylation patterns during DNA replication. Additionally, the nucleotides immediately flanking a CpG dinucleotide (i.e., the tetranucleotide context, such as WCGW, where W denotes A or T) have been implicated in modulating methylation dynamics, though the in vivo relevance and consistency of these effects across cell types and species have not been systematically established.

Compounding these challenges is the observation that DNA methylation levels in PMDs vary widely between samples, even among histologically normal tissues, raising questions about whether PMDs are pathological artifacts of cell culture or cancer, or whether they represent a physiological feature of normal development and aging. Some studies have reported PMDs in post-natal tissues, while others have failed to detect them, leading to conflicting interpretations. Furthermore, the relationship between PMD hypomethylation and cumulative cell divisions—often termed the “mitotic clock”—has been suggested but not rigorously demonstrated across diverse cell lineages and developmental stages.

A critical limitation in prior art has been the inability to reliably detect PMD structure in low-input or single-cell epigenomic assays, which are increasingly important for studying heterogeneous tissues such as tumors or developing embryos. Conventional methods require high sequencing depth (>10×) to achieve sufficient statistical power for domain calling, rendering them impractical for many emerging applications. There exists a pressing need for a more sensitive, robust, and biologically grounded approach to define PMDs that leverages intrinsic sequence features predictive of methylation loss, thereby enhancing signal-to-noise ratio and enabling analysis across a wide range of experimental conditions.

The present invention addresses these gaps by identifying a specific class of CpG dinucleotides—solo-WCGWs—that are exquisitely sensitive to replication-dependent methylation loss and serve as optimal markers for PMD architecture. By focusing exclusively on these loci, the invention provides a unified framework for understanding PMD formation across development, aging, and disease, and enables unprecedented sensitivity in epigenetic profiling.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method for identifying, characterizing, and utilizing partially methylated domains (PMDs) and highly methylated domains (HMDs) in mammalian genomes through the selective analysis of a defined subset of CpG dinucleotides exhibiting maximal susceptibility to replication-dependent DNA methylation loss. Specifically, the invention identifies that CpG sites characterized by two key sequence features—(1) the absence of any neighboring CpG dinucleotides within a ±35 base pair window (termed “solo” CpGs), and (2) a tetranucleotide context wherein the CpG is flanked by adenine or thymine (A/T) on both sides, i.e., the motif WCGW, where W = A or T—are significantly more prone to hypomethylation than other CpG contexts. This combined sequence signature, herein designated as “solo-WCGW,” represents approximately 13% of all CpGs in the human genome and serves as a highly sensitive and specific biomarker for PMD structure.

The invention demonstrates that solo-WCGW CpGs exhibit consistent hypomethylation patterns across diverse cell types, developmental stages, and species (including human and mouse), and that their methylation status robustly reflects the cumulative number of cell divisions a cell lineage has undergone. Critically, the spatial organization of hypomethylation at solo-WCGW sites reveals a largely conserved genomic architecture of PMDs that is shared across normal post-natal tissues, cancer cells, and even certain embryonic lineages, with the notable exception of germ cells and early embryos undergoing global epigenetic reprogramming.

The invention further discloses that the standard deviation (SD) of methylation levels at solo-WCGW CpGs across multiple samples, when calculated in fixed genomic bins (e.g., 100-kilobase windows), exhibits a bimodal distribution that can be used to objectively classify genomic regions as either PMDs or HMDs. This SD-based classification method is superior to conventional mean-based approaches because it captures the inherent variability of methylation loss in PMDs—a property directly linked to stochastic, replication-dependent erosion of methylation marks—while remaining stable across biological replicates and experimental platforms.

Moreover, the invention establishes that PMD hypomethylation, as measured by solo-WCGW methylation, is strongly correlated with chronological age in multiple somatic tissues, including blood, liver, and skin, and that this association reflects the total mitotic history of the cell, including contributions from developmental proliferation, tissue homeostasis, and environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation. In cancer, the degree of PMD hypomethylation correlates with tumor mutational burden, somatic copy number alterations, and LINE-1 retrotransposon activity, providing a functional link between epigenetic instability and genomic rearrangements.

The invention also elucidates the molecular determinants of methylation maintenance at solo-WCGW sites, revealing that two independent factors—replication timing and the histone mark H3K36me3—modulate methylation efficiency. Late-replicating genomic regions show pronounced hypomethylation at solo-WCGW CpGs, consistent with a limited time window for post-replicative remethylation before mitosis. Conversely, the presence of H3K36me3, a mark of active transcription, recruits the de novo methyltransferase DNMT3B via its PWWP domain, thereby protecting associated CpGs from methylation loss regardless of replication timing. This dual-regulation model explains the long-standing paradox of why transcribed gene bodies are highly methylated despite often residing in late-replicating regions.

Importantly, the invention enables accurate reconstruction of PMD structure from extremely low-coverage WGBS data (as low as 0.05× average genome coverage) and from single-cell WGBS experiments, overcoming a major limitation of prior methodologies. This capability opens new avenues for epigenetic analysis in rare cell populations, archival specimens, and clinical samples with limited DNA availability.

In summary, the invention provides: (1) a sequence-defined set of CpG markers (solo-WCGW) for sensitive PMD detection; (2) a robust, SD-based algorithm for genome-wide PMD/HMD segmentation; (3) a quantitative metric for cellular mitotic age and biological aging; (4) a predictive tool for cancer aggressiveness and retrotransposon activity; and (5) a mechanistic framework linking DNA replication, histone modifications, and methylation maintenance. These advances collectively transform the interpretation of DNA methylation landscapes in health and disease.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Terms (Definitions)

As used herein, the term “solo-WCGW CpG” refers to a cytosine-guanine dinucleotide (CpG) in which the cytosine residue is not preceded or followed by another CpG dinucleotide within a genomic window of ±35 base pairs (i.e., “solo”), and wherein the immediate 5' and 3' flanking nucleotides are each independently adenine (A) or thymine (T), thereby forming the tetranucleotide sequence motif WCGW, where W denotes A or T. The term “solo-WCGW” thus defines a specific subclass of CpG sites distinguished by both low local CpG density and an A/T-rich flanking sequence context.

The term “partially methylated domain” or “PMD” refers to a contiguous genomic region, typically spanning at least 100 kilobases, that exhibits a statistically significant reduction in average DNA methylation levels compared to the genomic background, and that displays elevated inter-sample variability in methylation levels when assessed across a cohort of biologically related samples. PMDs are operationally defined herein by a standard deviation of methylation beta values at solo-WCGW CpGs, calculated in fixed genomic bins (e.g., 100-kb windows), that exceeds a predetermined threshold (e.g., SD > 0.125 in human tumor samples).

The term “highly methylated domain” or “HMD” refers to a genomic region that is not classified as a PMD, characterized by consistently high methylation levels and low inter-sample variability at solo-WCGW CpGs. HMDs typically correspond to early-replicating, transcriptionally active, and euchromatic regions of the genome.

The term “methylation beta value” refers to a quantitative measure of DNA methylation at a given CpG site, calculated as the ratio of methylated cytosine signal to the sum of methylated and unmethylated cytosine signals, ranging from 0 (completely unmethylated) to 1 (fully methylated). Beta values may be derived from whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS), reduced representation bisulfite sequencing (RRBS), or methylation microarray platforms such as the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation450 (HM450) or EPIC arrays.

The term “standard deviation (SD)-based classification” refers to a computational method for segmenting the genome into PMDs and HMDs by calculating the standard deviation of methylation beta values at solo-WCGW CpGs across a panel of samples within fixed genomic bins, and applying a threshold derived from a bimodal distribution of SD values to assign each bin to either the PMD or HMD class.

The term “mitotic history” or “mitotic age” refers to the cumulative number of cell divisions a cell or cell lineage has undergone since its origin, which is reflected in the progressive, stochastic loss of DNA methylation at susceptible loci such as solo-WCGW CpGs in PMDs. Mitotic history is distinct from chronological age, although the two are often correlated.

The term “replication timing” refers to the temporal order in which genomic regions are duplicated during the S phase of the cell cycle, typically measured as a continuous score derived from Repli-Seq or similar assays. Early-replicating regions are duplicated in the first half of S phase, while late-replicating regions are duplicated in the latter half.

The term “H3K36me3” refers to the trimethylation of lysine 36 on histone H3, a post-translational modification associated with actively transcribed gene bodies and serving as a binding platform for the recruitment of DNA methyltransferase DNMT3B.

The term “low-coverage WGBS” refers to whole-genome bisulfite sequencing data with an average genomic coverage of less than 1×, including single-cell WGBS experiments, which traditionally provide sparse methylation information at individual CpG sites but can be aggregated across defined genomic features for meaningful analysis.

### Example 1

The identification of solo-WCGW CpGs as optimal markers for PMD structure was achieved through systematic analysis of whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) data from a core set of 40 tumor samples and 9 adjacent normal tissue samples, representing eight major cancer types. Initial segmentation of PMDs using the MethPipe hidden Markov model (HMM) algorithm revealed that methylation levels within PMDs were strongly influenced by local sequence context. Specifically, CpG density within a ±35 bp window was found to be inversely correlated with the degree of hypomethylation, with the lowest methylation observed at sites lacking any neighboring CpGs (“solo” CpGs). Concurrently, tetranucleotide sequence analysis demonstrated that CpGs flanked by A/T nucleotides on both sides (WCGW motif) exhibited significantly lower methylation than those flanked by G/C nucleotides (SCGS or SCGW motifs, where S = G or C). The combination of these two features—solo status and WCGW context—defined a subset of CpGs that were maximally prone to hypomethylation. In colon tumor and adjacent normal samples, only solo-WCGW CpGs showed significant hypomethylation in normal tissue, indicating that this sequence signature enhances the detection of subtle PMD structures that are otherwise obscured when analyzing all CpGs collectively. This pattern was validated across all core samples using both WGBS and matched HM450 microarray data, and further confirmed in an expanded dataset of 390 human and 206 mouse WGBS samples, with the exception of germ cell samples that undergo global epigenetic reprogramming. The focus on solo-WCGW CpGs, constituting 13% of the human genome, thus provided a high-fidelity signal for PMD architecture, enabling clearer visualization of domain boundaries and improved quantification of hypomethylation depth, even in weakly hypomethylated tumors such as COAD-3518, which could not be attributed to low tumor purity. This example establishes the foundational discovery that sequence context dictates susceptibility to methylation loss, and that selective analysis of the most vulnerable CpGs yields superior epigenetic insights.

### Example 2

The discovery that most PMDs are shared across cancer and normal tissues emerged from comparative analysis of solo-WCGW methylation profiles in the core tumor and normal sample sets. Genomic plots of mean methylation at solo-WCGW CpGs revealed striking concordance in PMD locations between tumors and their adjacent normal counterparts, despite substantial differences in the absolute degree of hypomethylation. Multi-scale analysis confirmed that PMDs ranging from 100 kb to 5 Mb overlapped extensively between the two groups, with normals exhibiting shallower hypomethylation. To quantify this structural conservation, the standard deviation (SD) of methylation beta values was calculated in 100-kb bins across all core tumors and separately across all core normals. Both groups exhibited a bimodal distribution of SD values, with high-SD bins corresponding to PMDs and low-SD bins to HMDs. Using Gaussian mixture modeling, thresholds of SD > 0.125 for tumors and SD > 0.07 for normals were established to dichotomize the genome. Remarkably, this simple SD-based classification yielded 83% concordance between tumor and normal PMD assignments, demonstrating that the underlying genomic architecture of PMDs is largely invariant across malignant and non-malignant states. Furthermore, these SD-defined PMDs covered 95% of previously reported colorectal cancer PMDs and 93% of PMDs in IMR90 fibroblasts, validating the method’s accuracy. This example illustrates that PMDs are not cancer-specific aberrations but reflect a fundamental organizational principle of the mammalian genome that is modulated in intensity rather than location during tumorigenesis.

### Example 3

The extension of PMD conservation across developmental lineages was demonstrated by integrating the core TCGA dataset with 343 published human and 206 mouse WGBS samples, categorized into six human and four mouse groups based on tissue origin and developmental stage. Analysis of solo-WCGW methylation averages revealed that five of the six human groups—including post-natal non-blood tissues (PN), peripheral blood cells (PB), immortalized cell lines (CL), solid tumors (TM), and hematopoietic malignancies—exhibited largely overlapping PMD structures, which coincided with lamina-associated domains (LADs) and late-replicating regions. The sole exception was the germline and embryo (GE) group, where PMD patterns were variable or absent due to global demethylation events. Notably, disease-free post-natal tissues, including those from the Schultz et al. study that had previously failed to identify PMDs, clearly displayed shared PMD architecture when analyzed using solo-WCGW CpGs. Brain tissues were an outlier within the PN group, showing minimal PMD structure, possibly due to post-mitotic de novo methylation. In blood lineages, both myeloid and lymphoid cells exhibited shared PMDs, with activated lymphocyte subtypes showing deeper hypomethylation than naïve cells, consistent with clonal expansion during immune responses. Hematopoietic malignancies mirrored their developmental origins, with lymphoid-derived tumors more hypomethylated than myeloid-derived ones, except for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), which resembled normal lymphoid cells, potentially reflecting the younger age of patients. SD-based PMD definitions in each group showed high overlap with the core tumor PMDs (e.g., 82% for PN, 84% for PB), reinforcing the concept of a cell-type-invariant PMD landscape. This example establishes that PMDs are a pervasive feature of somatic cell types across development and differentiation, shaped by common nuclear and replicative constraints.

### Example 4

The emergence of PMD hypomethylation during embryonic development was traced by analyzing solo-WCGW methylation in gametes and early embryonic stages. Human sperm showed high global methylation with minimal PMD structure, whereas human germinal vesicle oocytes exhibited deep PMD hypomethylation, albeit with boundaries distinct from somatic tissues. Inner cell mass (ICM) and blastocyst samples, though globally demethylated via active mechanisms, retained weak PMDs resembling those of oocytes. Primordial germ cells (PGCs) displayed near-complete methylation erasure, precluding PMD detection. Embryonic somatic tissues (<8 weeks) were rapidly remethylated, obscuring PMD structure, but mature fetal samples clearly exhibited PMDs. Longitudinal analysis of fetal tissues from 9 to 22 weeks post-gestation revealed a progressive, nearly linear accumulation of PMD hypomethylation, indicating that replication-dependent methylation loss begins early in development and continues postnatally. The similarity of PMD structure across ICMs, embryonic stem cells (ESCs), fetal, and post-natal samples suggests that the genomic blueprint for PMDs is established during embryogenesis and maintained thereafter. This example demonstrates that PMD formation is not a postnatal or pathological phenomenon but a developmental process initiated in utero and amplified over time through cell division.

### Example 5

The association between PMD hypomethylation and chronological age was investigated by comparing solo-WCGW methylation in samples from donors of different ages. CD4+ T cells from a 103-year-old individual showed PMD hypomethylation levels approaching those of T-cell leukemia, far exceeding those of newborn T cells. In larger HM450 datasets, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from elderly donors exhibited significantly greater PMD hypomethylation than newborns, and adult liver samples were more hypomethylated than fetal liver. Fetal tissues from four lineages showed a linear increase in hypomethylation with gestational age, statistically significant in three of four. In skin, both dermal and epidermal cells displayed age-associated PMD hypomethylation without sun exposure, but UV radiation dramatically accelerated this process specifically in epidermal cells, indicating that environmental insults amplify mitotic history. Hematopoietic lineages showed differential rates of age-related hypomethylation, with lymphoid cells losing methylation faster than myeloid cells, contradicting prior reports that monocytes lack PMD hypomethylation. This example establishes PMD hypomethylation as a mitotic clock that integrates chronological age, developmental proliferation, and environmental stressors, providing a more direct measure of cellular replicative history than chronological age alone.

### Example 6

The link between PMD hypomethylation and mitotic cell division in cancer was substantiated through analysis of 9,072 TCGA tumors across 33 cancer types. PMD hypomethylation, measured by solo-WCGW probes in common PMDs, was nearly universal but highly variable. The degree of hypomethylation in tumors correlated with that of their tissue of origin, supporting a developmental continuity model. Crucially, higher somatic mutation densities—known to accumulate in a mitotic clock-like manner—were significantly associated with deeper PMD hypomethylation, suggesting a shared dependence on cell division history. Similarly, somatic copy number aberration density and LINE-1 retrotransposon insertions were enriched in PMD regions, with tumors exhibiting deeper hypomethylation harboring more LINE-1 insertions in eight of nine cancer types. Gene expression analysis revealed that the 60 genes most correlated with PMD hypomethylation were enriched for proliferation and mitotic functions, and strongly overlapped with known cell-cycle-dependent genes from Cyclebase. Even in highly proliferative tumors expressing high levels of DNMT1, DNMT3A/B, and UHRF1, PMD hypomethylation persisted, indicating that maintenance machinery is overwhelmed by replication frequency. TET gene expression was not elevated in hypomethylated tumors, ruling out active demethylation as a driver. This example provides compelling evidence that cumulative cell divisions are the primary engine of PMD hypomethylation in cancer, linking epigenetic and genetic instability.

### Example 7

The roles of replication timing and H3K36me3 in modulating methylation at solo-WCGW CpGs were dissected using comprehensive epigenomic data from IMR90 fibroblasts. Genome-wide correlation analysis showed that solo-WCGW methylation was most strongly associated with replication timing, followed by H3K36me3. Stratified analysis revealed that the 14% of solo-WCGWs overlapping H3K36me3 peaks were highly methylated regardless of replication timing or gene annotation, while the remaining 86% showed strong replication-timing dependence, with late-replicating regions being markedly hypomethylated. In IMR90, the protective effect of early replication outweighed that of H3K36me3, but in H1 embryonic stem cells—which exhibit high DNMT3A/B activity—the opposite was true, highlighting cell-type-specific regulation. The independence of these two mechanisms explains why transcribed gene bodies remain methylated despite late replication: H3K36me3 recruits DNMT3B to maintain methylation. This model resolves the paradox of positive gene body methylation and accounts for methylation loss in cancers with NSD1 mutations (which reduce H3K36me3). The findings support a “re-methylation window” hypothesis, wherein late replication provides insufficient time for DNMT1-mediated maintenance before mitosis, unless compensated by H3K36me3-guided DNMT3B activity. This example provides a mechanistic foundation for PMD formation, integrating nuclear architecture, histone modifications, and enzymatic kinetics.

### Example 8

The utility of solo-WCGW analysis in low-coverage and single-cell epigenomics was demonstrated by down-sampling bulk WGBS data to average coverages as low as 0.05× and applying the method to existing single-cell WGBS datasets. Despite extreme sparsity—where most individual CpGs have zero or one read coverage—the aggregation of methylation signals across solo-WCGW CpGs within predefined PMD regions enabled accurate reconstruction of PMD structure. This sensitivity arises because solo-WCGWs are concentrated in PMDs and exhibit coherent hypomethylation, amplifying the signal relative to noise. In contrast, conventional methods using all CpGs fail at such low coverages due to dilution by resistant CpGs in HMDs. This capability was validated in both human and mouse single-cell data, where PMD patterns matched those from bulk analyses. The invention thus enables epigenetic profiling in scenarios previously considered intractable, such as circulating tumor cells, early embryonic blastomeres, or forensic samples with degraded DNA. This example underscores the transformative potential of the solo-WCGW approach for advancing precision medicine and basic research through enhanced epigenetic resolution in resource-limited settings.
