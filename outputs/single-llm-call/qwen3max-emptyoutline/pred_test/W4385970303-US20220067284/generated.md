## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention generally relates to the field of natural language processing and artificial intelligence, and more specifically to systems and methods for controllable abstractive text summarization using neural sequence-to-sequence models. The invention provides a framework that enables users to dynamically control the content, focus, length, and purpose of automatically generated summaries through the use of keywords and optional prompting mechanisms, without requiring retraining of the underlying model for each distinct control dimension. The technical domain encompasses machine learning architectures, particularly transformer-based pretrained language models such as BART, and their adaptation to personalized information extraction and condensation tasks across diverse textual domains including news articles, scientific literature, and patent documents.

## BACKGROUND

Automatic text summarization has long been a central challenge in computational linguistics and artificial intelligence, aiming to distill lengthy source documents into concise, coherent representations that preserve essential information. Traditional approaches have bifurcated into extractive and abstractive paradigms. Extractive summarization identifies and concatenates salient sentences or phrases directly from the source document, leveraging techniques such as sentence ranking, graph-based algorithms, or statistical measures of importance. While preserving factual accuracy, extractive methods often produce summaries that lack fluency, coherence, and the ability to synthesize information across disparate parts of the source. Abstractive summarization, by contrast, generates novel sentences that paraphrase and condense the source content, mimicking human summarization behavior. Recent advances in deep learning, particularly neural sequence-to-sequence models with attention mechanisms, have significantly improved the quality of abstractive summaries, enabling more fluent and contextually appropriate generation.

However, conventional abstractive summarization systems operate in a generic, user-agnostic manner. Given a source document, they produce a single summary that reflects the model’s internal notion of salience, typically learned from training data where reference summaries represent editorial or authorial judgments of importance. This one-size-fits-all approach fails to accommodate the diverse informational needs of different readers. For instance, a sports fan may wish to read only about their favorite team’s performance in a multi-game report, while a researcher might seek only the methodological contributions of a scientific paper, ignoring background context. A patent examiner could require a concise statement of an invention’s purpose rather than its intricate technical implementation. Such personalization demands go beyond mere content selection; they require dynamic, user-directed control over the summarization process itself.

Prior attempts at controllable summarization have largely addressed specific control dimensions in isolation, necessitating specialized model architectures and training procedures for each task. For entity-centric control, systems might incorporate named entity recognition modules and condition generation on identified entities, requiring annotated training data that pairs documents with entity-focused summaries. Length control has been approached through explicit length tokens, reinforcement learning objectives, or auxiliary length prediction networks, again demanding tailored training regimes. Similarly, topic-focused or query-guided summarization often relies on datasets with explicit queries or topic labels, limiting generalizability. These approaches suffer from a fundamental inflexibility: a model trained for entity control cannot easily adapt to length control without retraining, and introducing a new control dimension requires collecting new annotations and modifying the model architecture or training pipeline. This fragmentation impedes the development of versatile, user-adaptive summarization systems.

Keyword-guided summarization has emerged as a promising alternative, where keywords serve as soft constraints or guidance signals during summary generation. Existing keyword-based methods, however, have primarily focused on improving standard summarization quality or reducing hallucination by ensuring key terms from the source appear in the summary, rather than enabling broad user control. Some works explore length control via keyword counts but do not generalize to other control aspects. Crucially, these methods often embed the keyword signal deeply into the model architecture—through cross-attention mechanisms, gating networks, or specialized encoder modifications—making it difficult to decouple the control mechanism from the core summarization model. This tight coupling limits the flexibility to experiment with different control strategies at inference time without retraining.

Moreover, the rise of large pretrained language models (PLMs) like BART, T5, and GPT has demonstrated remarkable few-shot and zero-shot capabilities through prompting—crafting input templates that elicit desired behaviors without task-specific fine-tuning. However, prompt-based control alone often proves insufficient for precise content manipulation in summarization, as prompts primarily influence the decoder’s initial state and may not effectively guide the encoder’s representation of the source document. There exists a need for a unified framework that leverages the strengths of both keyword conditioning and prompting, while maintaining a clean separation between the core summarization model and the control logic, thereby enabling generic, adaptable, and efficient controllable summarization.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Controllable Summarization Overview

The present invention introduces a novel framework for controllable abstractive summarization, termed CTRLSUM, which fundamentally rethinks the relationship between user intent, model training, and inference-time control. At its core, CTRLSUM decouples the learning of the summarization capability from the specification of the control aspect. Instead of hardcoding a particular control dimension (e.g., entity, length, topic) into the model architecture or training objective, CTRLSUM trains a single, general-purpose model to generate summaries conditioned on an arbitrary set of keywords. These keywords act as a flexible interface between the user and the model, representing the user’s preference or focus for the desired summary.

Formally, let \(x\) denote a source document, \(y\) a target summary, and \(z\) a set of keywords. The CTRLSUM model is trained to learn the conditional probability distribution \(p(y|x, z)\). During training, the keywords \(z_{\text{train}}\) are automatically extracted from the pair \((x, y)\) using a deterministic algorithm that identifies content words present in both the source and the reference summary. This ensures that the model learns a robust correlation between the presence of specific keywords in the input and their manifestation or contextual relevance in the output summary, without requiring any external control annotations.

The critical innovation lies in the inference phase. Here, a user provides a control signal \(c\), which can take various forms depending on the desired control task—for example, an entity name, a desired length bucket, a question, or a high-level intent like "summarize the contributions." A control function \(g_{\text{control}}(x, c)\) then maps this user signal \(c\), along with the source document \(x\), to a specific set of keywords \(z_{\text{test}}\). This mapping is entirely external to the trained CTRLSUM model. By engineering different \(g_{\text{control}}\) functions, the same underlying model can be adapted to perform a wide array of control tasks without any modification to its parameters or retraining. This design provides a clean separation of concerns: the model learns the general skill of keyword-conditioned summarization, while the control logic is encapsulated in a modular, pluggable function.

For instance, in an entity control task, \(c\) is the name of the entity of interest (e.g., "LeBron James"), and \(g_{\text{control}}\) simply returns this entity as the keyword \(z_{\text{test}} = \{"LeBron James"\}\). In a length control task, \(c\) is a discrete length code (e.g., 0 for very short, 4 for very long), and \(g_{\text{control}}\) uses an auxiliary keyword extractor to select a number of top-scoring keywords from \(x\) that corresponds to the desired length. For more complex intents, such as summarizing the purpose of a patent, \(c\) is the task identifier, and \(g_{\text{control}}\) outputs a fixed, human-written guiding phrase like "the purpose of the present invention is" as the keyword. This framework is remarkably generic, as the control function can be arbitrarily complex, potentially incorporating external knowledge bases, heuristics, or even another learned model, all while leaving the core summarization engine untouched.

To prevent the model from becoming overly reliant on the highly informative training keywords and neglecting the source document, a keyword dropout technique is employed during training. A random subset of the extracted keywords is masked out, forcing the model to learn to use the keywords as a guide while still attending to and extracting necessary information from the full source text. This regularization ensures that the model remains robust and grounded in the source document, even when the test-time keywords are sparse or imperfect.

### Computer Environment

The CTRLSUM system is implemented within a modern computing environment suitable for large-scale machine learning. The core components include a sequence-to-sequence neural network model, a keyword extraction module, and a control function manager. The primary summarization model is based on the BART (Bidirectional and Auto-Regressive Transformers) architecture, a denoising autoencoder for pretraining sequence-to-sequence models. BART is chosen for its strong performance on a variety of text generation tasks, including summarization. The model is implemented using a deep learning framework such as PyTorch or TensorFlow and is trained on a distributed computing cluster equipped with multiple high-performance Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), for example, NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPUs with 16GB of memory each.

The training process involves feeding the model with sequences constructed by concatenating the keyword sequence \(z\) and the source document \(x\), separated by a special delimiter token (e.g., "=>"). The model is then optimized to predict the target summary \(y\) using a standard cross-entropy loss. The training is performed with a batch size of 64, using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 3e-5, following the fine-tuning recommendations for BART. The number of training steps is dataset-dependent, for example, 20,000 steps for the CNN/DailyMail dataset, 50,000 for arXiv, and 300,000 for BIGPATENT, to account for the varying sizes and complexities of the corpora.

A separate BERT-based sequence tagger is trained to serve as the automatic keyword extractor at inference time. This tagger takes a document as input and outputs a selection probability for each token, indicating its likelihood of being a keyword. It is trained on the same datasets as the summarization model, using the training keywords \(z_{\text{train}}\) as labels. The tagger is also implemented in PyTorch/TensorFlow and trained on the same GPU cluster with a learning rate of 5e-5 and a batch size of 128. To handle documents longer than the standard 512-token limit of BERT, a sliding window approach is used, where the document is processed in overlapping segments, and the final token probabilities are aggregated.

At inference time, the system operates as follows: a user provides a source document \(x\) and a control signal \(c\). The control function manager, which contains a library of predefined \(g_{\text{control}}\) functions, selects the appropriate function based on \(c\). This function may call the BERT tagger to extract keywords from \(x\), or it may directly return a static keyword sequence. The resulting keywords \(z_{\text{test}}\) are then prepended to \(x\), and the combined sequence is fed into the trained CTRLSUM model, which autoregressively generates the controlled summary \(y\). The entire pipeline is designed to be modular, allowing for easy swapping of the underlying sequence-to-sequence model (e.g., to T5 or PEGASUS) or the keyword extractor, making the system highly adaptable and future-proof.

### Controllable Summarization Work Flows

The CTRLSUM framework supports a variety of controllable summarization workflows, each defined by a specific control function \(g_{\text{control}}\). The following describes five primary workflows that demonstrate the versatility of the system.

**1. Entity-Centric Summarization Workflow.** In this workflow, the user wishes to generate a summary that focuses on a specific entity mentioned in the document. The control signal \(c\) is the name of the entity (e.g., "Dwyane Wade"). The control function \(g_{\text{control}}(x, c=e)\) is trivial: it directly returns the entity name as the keyword set, \(z_{\text{test}} = \{e\}\). The CTRLSUM model, having learned during training that the presence of a keyword strongly influences the summary's content, will generate a summary that prominently features and discusses the specified entity, often ensuring the entity appears in the output. This workflow is useful for personalized news reading, where a user is only interested in updates about a particular person, company, or location.

**2. Length-Controllable Summarization Workflow.** This workflow allows the user to specify the desired length of the summary. The control signal \(c\) is a discrete length code \(l \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}\), representing five buckets of increasing length. During a preprocessing step, the training data is partitioned into these five buckets based on the length of the reference summaries. For each bucket, the average number of keywords \(K_l\) is computed. At inference, given a document \(x\) and a length code \(l\), the control function first uses the BERT tagger to compute a selection probability for every token in \(x\). It then selects the top \(K_l\) tokens with the highest probabilities as the keyword set \(z_{\text{test}}\). The intuition is that a longer summary requires more source content to be covered, which is signaled by providing more keywords. The CTRLSUM model responds to this signal by generating a summary whose length closely matches the user’s request, with a high correlation between the input length code and the actual output length.

**3. Contribution Summarization Workflow (for Scientific Papers).** Standard scientific paper summaries (abstracts) often include extensive background and motivation, which can obscure the paper’s specific technical contributions. In this workflow, the user’s intent is to obtain a summary that explicitly lists the paper’s novel contributions. The control signal \(c\) is the task identifier "contribution". The control function \(g_{\text{control}}(x, c=\text{``contribution''})\) returns a fixed, human-written guiding phrase as the keyword, for example, "the main contributions of this paper are: (1)". This phrase serves a dual purpose: as a keyword, it guides the encoder to focus on parts of the document that discuss contributions (often found in the introduction or conclusion); as a prompt, it conditions the decoder to start the summary with this phrase, structuring the output as a list of contributions. This workflow is invaluable for researchers who need to quickly assess the novelty of a large number of papers.

**4. Invention Purpose Summarization Workflow (for Patents).** Patent documents are notoriously dense and technical, making it difficult for non-experts to grasp the fundamental purpose of the invention. This workflow aims to generate a one-sentence summary that states the invention’s goal. The control signal \(c\) is "purpose". The control function \(g_{\text{control}}(x, c=\text{``purpose''})\) returns a guiding phrase like "the purpose of the present invention is". Similar to the contribution workflow, this phrase acts as both a keyword and a prompt. The model is guided to locate and condense the core objective of the patent, often stated in the background or summary sections, and to output it in a clear, concise, and non-technical manner. This is particularly useful for patent examiners, investors, or legal professionals who need a quick understanding of a patent’s essence.

**5. Question-Guided Summarization Workflow (Zero-Shot Reading Comprehension).** This workflow explores the connection between summarization and reading comprehension. The user poses a question \(q\) about the document, and the system should generate a summary that answers this question. The control signal \(c\) is the question itself, formatted as "Q: [question text]? A:". The control function \(g_{\text{control}}(x, c=\text{``Q: ...? A:''})\) directly uses this formatted question as the keyword. The CTRLSUM model, trained on general summarization, is able to leverage this strong prompting signal to locate relevant information in the document and generate a direct answer, effectively performing zero-shot reading comprehension without having been trained on any question-answer pairs. This demonstrates the potential of summarization as a powerful transfer task for other NLP problems.

In all these workflows, the same CTRLSUM model is used. The only difference is the control function that translates the user’s high-level intent into a concrete set of keywords. This modularity is the key strength of the framework, enabling rapid prototyping and deployment of new control dimensions without the costly and time-consuming process of data collection and model retraining.

### Example Performance

The efficacy of the CTRLSUM framework has been rigorously evaluated across the five control workflows on three distinct datasets: CNN/DailyMail (news), arXiv (scientific papers), and BIGPATENT (patent documents). The underlying model is a fine-tuned BART-LARGE, and performance is measured using standard metrics like ROUGE and BERTScore, as well as human evaluations for subjective qualities.

In **entity-centric summarization** on the CNN/DailyMail dataset, CTRLSUM demonstrates a high success rate of approximately 95% in including the requested entity in the output summary, significantly outperforming prior work. When provided with "oracle" entities from the reference summary, it achieves a higher ROUGE-2 score than the baseline, confirming its ability to utilize the control signal. Human evaluation shows that CTRLSUM summaries are more relevant to the control intent (e.g., focusing on the specified entity) compared to a standard BART model, which often produces generic summaries that ignore the control signal, especially for "unimportant" entities.

For **length-controllable summarization**, CTRLSUM achieves a high Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) between the input length bucket code and the actual output length bucket, indicating precise control. It also reduces the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) of the output length from the target, showing more consistent length adherence than an unconstrained baseline. This is a notable improvement over methods that use simple length codes, which are often ignored by strong pretrained models.

In the **contribution and purpose summarization** tasks, where reference summaries are not available in standard datasets, CTRLSUM is compared against a BART baseline that uses only the prompt (without the keyword). On a newly collected dataset of paper contributions, CTRLSUM (using both keyword and prompt) achieves superior ROUGE and BERTScore metrics. The BERTScore precision is notably higher, indicating that CTRLSUM avoids the over-generation problem of the prompt-only baseline, which tends to produce full, generic abstracts instead of concise contribution lists. A similar pattern is observed for patent purpose summarization, where CTRLSUM generates more focused and accurate one-sentence statements of purpose.

The **question-guided summarization** workflow is tested in a zero-shot setting on the NewsQA and SQuAD reading comprehension benchmarks. Remarkably, a BART model fine-tuned only on summarization (without any QA data) already outperforms a pretrained GPT-2 model on these tasks, suggesting that summarization is a strong pretraining task for comprehension. CTRLSUM, by combining the question as both a keyword and a prompt, further boosts the F1 score by 15-18 points, approaching the performance of supervised models like MatchLSTM on NewsQA. This validates the hypothesis that summarization can subsume reading comprehension when properly guided.

Finally, in the standard **unconstrained summarization** setting (where the control signal is empty, and the BERT tagger provides automatic keywords), CTRLSUM performs comparably or superior to strong baselines like BART and PEGASUS on the CNN/DailyMail and arXiv datasets, demonstrating that the keyword conditioning does not degrade general summarization performance. On BIGPATENT, it is comparable in BERTScore, though slightly lower in ROUGE-2, which may be attributed to the inherent differences between the BART and PEGASUS architectures.

Human evaluations for controlled summarization tasks confirm that CTRLSUM summaries are more relevant to the user’s control intent. Annotators rated CTRLSUM higher on "Control Relevance," penalizing summaries that contained redundant, off-topic information. This highlights the practical utility of the system in delivering truly personalized and focused information to the user.
