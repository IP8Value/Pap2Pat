# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of antiviral therapeutics, specifically to methods and compositions for inhibiting dengue virus replication in a host. More particularly, the invention provides novel therapeutic strategies based on modulating host cellular pathways that are upregulated during dengue virus infection, including the ubiquitin-proteasome system and interferon-stimulated gene responses. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising proteasome inhibitors such as MG-132 or ALLN, or functional equivalents thereof, for use in treating dengue virus infection. Additionally, the invention includes diagnostic and prognostic applications based on the detection of elevated levels of specific chemokines—namely IP-10 (CXCL10) and I-TAC (CXCL11)—in biological samples from individuals infected with dengue virus. The invention also discloses the antiviral role of viperin (virus inhibitory protein, endoplasmic reticulum-associated, interferon-inducible), an interferon-stimulated gene product, in suppressing dengue virus replication, thereby establishing viperin as both a biomarker of effective host defense and a potential therapeutic agent or target. Collectively, the invention leverages insights from genome-wide expression profiling of dengue-infected human cells and validation in clinical specimens to identify druggable host factors and measurable immune correlates that can be exploited for the development of diagnostics, prophylactics, and therapeutics against dengue fever and its severe manifestations, including dengue hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome.

## BACKGROUND

Dengue virus, a member of the Flaviviridae family, is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus transmitted primarily by Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes. It exists as four distinct serotypes (DENV-1 through DENV-4), and infection with one serotype confers lifelong homotypic immunity but only transient and partial heterotypic protection. This immunological phenomenon underlies the increased risk of severe disease—dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS)—during secondary heterologous infections, largely attributed to antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE). Despite an estimated 50 to 100 million annual cases of dengue fever globally, and 250,000 to 500,000 cases progressing to life-threatening DHF/DSS, no specific antiviral therapy is currently approved for clinical use. Supportive care remains the standard of treatment, highlighting an urgent unmet medical need.

The pathogenesis of severe dengue is incompletely understood, partly due to the absence of a robust animal model that recapitulates human disease and the complex interplay between viral and host factors. Early targets of dengue infection include dendritic cells, Langerhans cells, monocytes, macrophages, and endothelial cells, with evidence of viral replication in hepatocytes, splenocytes, and renal tissues. Clinical manifestations range from self-limiting febrile illness (dengue fever) to plasma leakage, thrombocytopenia, and circulatory collapse in DHF/DSS. Host immune responses, particularly dysregulated cytokine production and impaired interferon (IFN) signaling, are believed to contribute significantly to disease severity. Dengue virus has evolved mechanisms to antagonize type I IFN (IFN-α/β) signaling, notably by inhibiting JAK-STAT pathway activation, thereby evading innate antiviral defenses and facilitating higher viremia.

In recent years, high-throughput genomic technologies have enabled systematic interrogation of host transcriptional responses to viral infection. Microarray studies in dengue-infected cell lines—including human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), ECV304 cells, and primary macrophages—have identified alterations in genes involved in immune activation, apoptosis, cytoskeletal dynamics, and interferon responses. However, these studies were limited in scope, often lacked functional validation, and did not consistently bridge findings from in vitro models to human disease. Moreover, while some reports noted upregulation of classical interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) such as OAS, MxA, and TNF-α, the functional relevance of these changes to viral control remained speculative.

Proteasome inhibitors, such as MG-132 (carbobenzoxy-leucinyl-leucinyl-leucinal) and ALLN (N-acetyl-leucinyl-leucinyl-norleucinal), are well-characterized compounds that block the chymotrypsin-like activity of the 26S proteasome, thereby preventing degradation of polyubiquitinated proteins. These inhibitors have been used extensively in research to study protein turnover, NF-κB activation, and viral life cycles. In certain viral infections—including hepatitis C virus (HCV) and human cytomegalovirus (HCMV)—the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) has been shown to either support or restrict viral replication, depending on the virus and cellular context. However, prior to the present invention, the role of the UPS in dengue virus replication was unknown, and no therapeutic strategy targeting this pathway had been proposed for dengue.

Similarly, viperin (also known as RSAD2, radical S-adenosyl methionine domain-containing protein 2) is an interferon-inducible protein localized to the endoplasmic reticulum that exhibits broad-spectrum antiviral activity against HCMV, HCV, influenza, and West Nile virus. Its mechanism involves disruption of lipid rafts, inhibition of viral budding, and modulation of cellular metabolism. Although viperin is transcriptionally induced by type I IFNs, its specific role in dengue infection had not been investigated. Likewise, while numerous chemokines have been reported in dengue patients—including IL-8, RANTES, and MCP-1—the significance of IP-10 (CXCL10) and I-TAC (CXCL11), potent ligands for the CXCR3 receptor expressed on activated T cells and NK cells, had not been established in the context of dengue pathogenesis or diagnosis.

Existing diagnostic methods for dengue rely on RT-PCR for viral RNA detection during the acute phase or serological assays (IgM/IgG) in later stages. However, these lack utility for early prognosis of disease severity. There remains a critical need for biomarkers that predict progression to DHF/DSS and for host-directed antivirals that circumvent the limitations of direct-acting antivirals, such as rapid viral resistance. The present invention addresses these gaps by identifying, through integrated genomic and functional analyses, key host pathways—specifically the UPS and ISG responses—that are not only dysregulated during dengue infection but are also amenable to pharmacological intervention. By demonstrating that proteasome inhibition suppresses dengue replication without significant cytotoxicity at therapeutic doses, and that viperin overexpression confers resistance to infection, the invention establishes novel therapeutic paradigms. Furthermore, by validating the consistent upregulation of IP-10 and I-TAC across cell lines and patient sera, the invention provides the foundation for new diagnostic and prognostic tools.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and compositions for the treatment, diagnosis, and prevention of dengue virus infection based on the discovery that host cellular pathways—particularly the ubiquitin-proteasome system and interferon-stimulated gene responses—are critically involved in dengue virus replication and host defense. In one aspect, the invention features a method of inhibiting dengue virus replication in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of a proteasome inhibitor, wherein the proteasome inhibitor is selected from the group consisting of MG-132, ALLN, and functional equivalents thereof. The invention further demonstrates that such inhibitors reduce viral titers by more than 50% in human hepatocytic (HepG2) and lung epithelial (A549) cell lines without inducing significant cytotoxicity at effective concentrations, thereby establishing their potential as antiviral agents.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method of enhancing antiviral defense against dengue virus by upregulating or administering viperin, an interferon-inducible protein. Specifically, the invention shows that stable overexpression of viperin in A549 cells significantly reduces dengue virus production, both in the presence and absence of exogenous interferon-β, thereby confirming viperin as a functional mediator of the host’s intrinsic antiviral response to dengue. This finding supports the development of viperin-based therapeutics, including recombinant viperin proteins, gene therapy vectors encoding viperin, or small molecules that induce endogenous viperin expression.

In yet another aspect, the invention relates to diagnostic and prognostic methods based on the detection of elevated levels of the chemokines IP-10 (CXCL10) and I-TAC (CXCL11) in biological samples from individuals infected with dengue virus. The invention establishes that IP-10 and I-TAC are among the most highly upregulated genes across multiple experimental systems—including HepG2 cells, A549 cells, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from dengue fever patients—and that their protein levels are significantly elevated in patient serum during the acute phase of infection compared to convalescent or non-dengue febrile controls. These chemokines, which are induced primarily via NF-κB activation rather than interferon signaling in the context of dengue, serve as robust biomarkers for early diagnosis and may correlate with disease severity, particularly thrombocytopenia.

The invention also encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising a proteasome inhibitor (e.g., MG-132 or ALLN) or a viperin-inducing agent, formulated in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, for use in treating dengue infection. These compositions may be administered orally, intravenously, or via other routes, and may be combined with supportive therapies or other antivirals. Additionally, the invention includes kits for detecting IP-10 and/or I-TAC in serum or plasma, useful for point-of-care diagnosis of dengue fever in endemic regions.

Collectively, the invention represents a paradigm shift from virus-targeted to host-targeted antiviral strategies, leveraging systems biology to identify conserved host responses that are both mechanistically linked to viral control and clinically relevant. By validating findings across in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo-relevant human samples, the invention ensures translational applicability. The disclosed methods and compositions offer significant advantages over existing approaches, including reduced risk of viral resistance (since host targets evolve slowly), potential for broad-spectrum activity against multiple dengue serotypes, and utility in both therapeutic and diagnostic settings.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Dengue

Dengue virus is a mosquito-borne flavivirus that poses a major public health burden in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide. The virus comprises four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4), each capable of causing a spectrum of disease ranging from asymptomatic infection to severe, potentially fatal dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) and dengue shock syndrome (DSS). Primary infection with any serotype typically results in mild dengue fever, characterized by high fever, headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash, and leukopenia. However, upon secondary infection with a heterologous serotype, the risk of developing DHF/DSS increases dramatically due to antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), a phenomenon wherein subneutralizing levels of cross-reactive antibodies facilitate enhanced viral entry into Fc receptor-bearing cells such as monocytes and macrophages. This leads to increased viral replication, exaggerated immune activation, and cytokine storm, culminating in vascular leakage, coagulopathy, and hypovolemic shock.

The lack of a suitable small animal model that fully recapitulates human dengue pathogenesis has hindered mechanistic studies and therapeutic development. Non-human primates develop viremia but rarely exhibit clinical signs of severe disease. Mouse models, including AG129 (deficient in IFN-α/β and -γ receptors) and humanized mice, have provided insights but suffer from limitations in immune system fidelity. Consequently, much of the understanding of dengue-host interactions derives from in vitro studies using human cell lines and ex vivo analysis of patient samples. Key target cells for dengue infection include dendritic cells, monocytes, macrophages, hepatocytes, and endothelial cells. Viral entry is mediated by interactions between the envelope (E) glycoprotein and host receptors such as DC-SIGN, HSP70/90, and integrins. Following endocytosis, the viral RNA is released into the cytoplasm, where it serves as mRNA for translation of a single polyprotein that is cleaved by viral and host proteases into three structural (C, prM, E) and seven non-structural (NS1–NS5) proteins. Replication occurs in association with virus-induced membranous structures derived from the endoplasmic reticulum.

Dengue virus actively subverts host innate immune responses to establish infection. Notably, NS proteins (e.g., NS2A, NS4B, NS5) interfere with type I interferon (IFN-α/β) production and signaling by blocking IRF3 activation, degrading STAT2, and inhibiting JAK-STAT phosphorylation. This suppression of antiviral defenses permits unchecked viral replication and contributes to high viremia, a key determinant of disease severity. Despite these evasion strategies, the host mounts a robust transcriptional response involving hundreds of genes, many of which are part of conserved defense programs such as the interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) network, NF-κB-mediated inflammation, and protein homeostasis pathways. The present invention builds upon this knowledge by systematically mapping the host transcriptome during dengue infection and functionally validating key nodes within these networks as therapeutic targets or diagnostic markers.

### Assays for Dengue

Accurate and timely diagnosis of dengue infection is critical for clinical management and epidemiological surveillance. Current diagnostic modalities include molecular, serological, and antigen-based assays. During the first five days of illness (febrile phase), reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is the gold standard for detecting viral RNA in serum, plasma, or whole blood. Multiplex RT-PCR assays can simultaneously detect and differentiate all four dengue serotypes with high sensitivity and specificity. Viral isolation in cell culture (e.g., C6/36 mosquito cells) is possible but is time-consuming and not routinely used in clinical settings.

After day five, when viremia declines, serological tests become more useful. IgM capture ELISA detects anti-dengue IgM antibodies, which typically appear by day 4–5 and persist for 2–3 months, indicating recent infection. IgG ELISA can distinguish primary from secondary infections based on antibody avidity and titer kinetics. However, cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses (e.g., Zika, West Nile) can complicate interpretation. The NS1 antigen test, which detects a secreted viral glycoprotein, offers high sensitivity during the acute phase and can be performed as a rapid lateral flow assay, making it suitable for point-of-care use in resource-limited settings.

Beyond these standard methods, the present invention introduces novel diagnostic approaches based on host response biomarkers. Specifically, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) for IP-10 and I-TAC in serum or plasma provide a means to diagnose dengue infection early in the course of illness, even before seroconversion. As demonstrated herein, IP-10 and I-TAC levels are significantly elevated in dengue patients compared to those with non-dengue febrile illnesses and return to baseline during convalescence. These chemokines can be measured using commercially available ELISA kits or multiplex immunoassays, enabling integration into existing laboratory workflows. Furthermore, quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) panels targeting a core set of 50 dengue-responsive genes—including IP-10, I-TAC, viperin, OAS1, and HERC5—can be applied to whole blood RNA stabilized in PAXgene tubes, offering a molecular signature of active infection. Such assays may also stratify patients by risk of progression to severe disease, particularly when combined with clinical parameters like platelet count and hematocrit.

For research and drug screening purposes, plaque assays remain the benchmark for quantifying infectious dengue virus. In this method, serial dilutions of virus-containing supernatants are added to confluent monolayers of permissive cells (e.g., BHK-21), overlaid with methylcellulose to restrict viral spread, and incubated for 5–7 days. Plaques are visualized by crystal violet staining and counted to calculate plaque-forming units per milliliter (pfu/ml). Flow cytometry using fluorescently labeled anti-E protein antibodies (e.g., clone 4G2) allows quantification of the percentage of infected cells. Real-time RT-PCR targeting conserved regions of the viral genome (e.g., capsid or NS5) provides a rapid, sensitive measure of viral RNA load, though it does not distinguish infectious from non-infectious particles. Together, these assays form a comprehensive toolkit for evaluating antiviral efficacy in vitro, as exemplified by the demonstration that proteasome inhibitors reduce pfu/ml by >50% without cytotoxicity.

### Ubiquitin-Proteasome Pathway

The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) is a highly conserved eukaryotic pathway responsible for the selective degradation of intracellular proteins. It plays pivotal roles in cell cycle regulation, DNA repair, signal transduction, antigen presentation, and stress responses. The process begins with the covalent attachment of ubiquitin—a 76-amino acid protein—to lysine residues of target substrates via an enzymatic cascade involving E1 (ubiquitin-activating), E2 (ubiquitin-conjugating), and E3 (ubiquitin ligase) enzymes. Polyubiquitination, typically through Lys48-linked chains, marks proteins for recognition and degradation by the 26S proteasome, a large multi-subunit protease complex. Alternatively, monoubiquitination or atypical chain linkages (e.g., Lys63) can regulate non-proteolytic functions such as endocytosis, kinase activation, and DNA repair.

Viruses have evolved intricate relationships with the UPS. Some hijack E3 ligases to degrade host restriction factors (e.g., HIV Vpu targets CD4 and BST-2), while others encode deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs) to stabilize viral proteins or dampen immune signaling. Conversely, the host may exploit the UPS to eliminate viral components—a process termed “xenophagy.” In the context of dengue virus, prior art provided no clear evidence of UPS involvement in the viral life cycle. However, the present invention reveals, through transcriptomic profiling, significant upregulation of UPS-related genes—including HERC5, HERC6, UBE2L6, USP15, and USP18—in dengue-infected HepG2 and A549 cells and in patient PBMCs. These genes encode E3 ligases, E2 conjugating enzymes, and deubiquitinases implicated in interferon responses and protein quality control.

Functional interrogation using pharmacological inhibitors demonstrated that the proteasome is required for efficient dengue virus replication. Treatment of HepG2 cells with MG-132 or ALLN at non-cytotoxic concentrations (e.g., 0.5–1 µM) reduced viral titers by more than 50%, as measured by plaque assay. Higher concentrations (>2 µM) achieved >90% inhibition but were associated with reduced cell viability, as assessed by fluorescein diacetate (FDA) staining. These findings suggest that dengue virus depends on proteasomal activity at one or more steps in its life cycle—potentially viral polyprotein processing, assembly, budding, or release. Notably, the effect appears specific, as proteasome inhibitors do not universally suppress all viruses; for instance, they have minimal impact on influenza A virus replication. The precise mechanism by which proteasome inhibition blocks dengue remains to be elucidated but may involve accumulation of misfolded viral proteins, disruption of ER-associated degradation (ERAD), or altered trafficking of viral components to the plasma membrane.

This discovery has profound therapeutic implications. Proteasome inhibitors like bortezomib are already FDA-approved for cancer (multiple myeloma), suggesting potential for repurposing. However, chronic systemic inhibition carries toxicity risks, necessitating careful dosing or localized delivery. Alternatively, targeting specific E3 ligases or DUBs upregulated during infection (e.g., HERC5, which mediates ISG15 conjugation) could offer greater selectivity. The invention thus opens a new avenue for host-directed antiviral therapy against dengue, grounded in rigorous genomic and functional validation.

### Screening Assays

The identification of host factors essential for dengue virus replication enables the development of high-throughput screening (HTS) platforms for antiviral discovery. The present invention provides robust cell-based assays suitable for HTS of compound libraries, siRNA collections, or CRISPR guide RNAs. In a preferred embodiment, HepG2 or A549 cells are seeded in 96- or 384-well plates, infected with dengue virus (e.g., DENV-2 strain TSV01) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1, and treated with test agents. After 48–72 hours, viral replication is quantified by plaque assay, RT-qPCR for viral RNA, or immunofluorescence for E protein expression. Cytotoxicity is concurrently assessed using FDA, resazurin, or ATP-based viability assays to calculate a selectivity index (SI = CC50/EC50).

Reporter virus systems further enhance throughput. For example, dengue replicons encoding luciferase or GFP under the control of the viral 5′ UTR allow real-time monitoring of RNA replication without producing infectious virions, improving biosafety. Similarly, pseudotyped viruses bearing dengue E protein on a lentiviral or vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) core can assess entry inhibitors. The invention also contemplates phenotypic screens based on host gene expression. TaqMan Low-Density Arrays (TLDAs) or NanoString nCounter panels targeting the 50-gene dengue response signature can classify compounds as “antiviral” if they reverse the infection-induced transcriptional profile.

For target-based screening, recombinant UPS components (e.g., purified 20S proteasome, HERC5 E3 ligase) can be used in biochemical assays to identify inhibitors. Fluorescent ubiquitin-AMC substrates enable continuous measurement of proteasome activity, while TR-FRET or AlphaScreen technologies detect protein-protein interactions (e.g., between NS3 and host E2 enzymes). Hits from primary screens undergo secondary validation in dose-response curves, time-of-addition experiments (to define mechanism of action), and cross-serotype testing (against DENV-1–4).

Importantly, the invention emphasizes the use of physiologically relevant models. While immortalized cell lines offer reproducibility, primary human cells—such as monocyte-derived dendritic cells (moDCs) or hepatocytes—better reflect in vivo biology. Patient-derived PBMCs from the EDEN cohort can also be used ex vivo to confirm that candidate antivirals modulate the same pathways (e.g., IP-10 secretion, viperin induction) observed in natural infection. Such multi-tiered screening cascades increase the likelihood of identifying clinically translatable therapeutics.

### Variants and Functional Equivalents of ALLN and MG-132

While MG-132 (Z-Leu-Leu-Leu-al) and ALLN (N-acetyl-Leu-Leu-Norleu-al) are prototypical peptide aldehyde proteasome inhibitors, their clinical utility is limited by poor pharmacokinetics, off-target effects (e.g., calpain inhibition by ALLN), and toxicity. The present invention therefore encompasses not only these compounds but also structurally related analogs, derivatives, and functionally equivalent agents that achieve comparable inhibition of dengue virus replication with improved safety profiles.

MG-132 is a reversible inhibitor that preferentially targets the chymotrypsin-like (β5) activity of the proteasome. Variants may include modifications to the peptide backbone (e.g., D-amino acids to resist proteolysis), replacement of the aldehyde warhead with boronic acid (as in bortezomib) or epoxyketone (as in carfilzomib) groups for irreversible binding, or conjugation to targeting moieties (e.g., galactose for hepatocyte-specific delivery). Similarly, ALLN, which inhibits both proteasome and calpain, can be optimized by substituting norleucine with other hydrophobic residues or incorporating non-peptidic scaffolds to enhance selectivity.

Functional equivalents are defined as any compound that reduces dengue virus titers by ≥50% in HepG2 cells at non-cytotoxic concentrations (CC50 > 10 µM) and acts primarily through proteasome inhibition, as confirmed by rescue experiments (e.g., co-treatment with excess ubiquitin-aldehyde reverses antiviral effect). This category includes:
- Clinically approved proteasome inhibitors: bortezomib, carfilzomib, ixazomib.
- Natural products: epoxomicin, lactacystin, salinosporamide A.
- Novel synthetic inhibitors: LU-102, ONX 0914 (immunoproteasome-selective).
- Allosteric modulators that disrupt proteasome assembly or regulator binding (e.g., PA28, PA700).

The invention also covers prodrugs of these inhibitors designed to improve oral bioavailability or tissue penetration. For example, esterification of carboxylic acid groups can enhance intestinal absorption, followed by intracellular hydrolysis to the active form. Nanoparticle encapsulation may further prolong half-life and reduce systemic exposure.

Crucially, the antiviral efficacy of these variants must be validated in the context of dengue infection, as proteasome dependence varies by virus. Compounds that show pan-antiviral activity (e.g., against HCV, HIV) are particularly valuable for treating co-infections in endemic areas. Structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies guided by crystallography of inhibitor-proteasome complexes can accelerate lead optimization. Ultimately, the goal is to identify candidates with high SI values (>100) suitable for preclinical development.

### Pharmaceutical Compositions

The invention provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising a proteasome inhibitor (e.g., MG-132, ALLN, or a functional equivalent) or a viperin-inducing agent, formulated with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient. These compositions are intended for the treatment or prevention of dengue virus infection in humans or animals.

For proteasome inhibitors, dosage forms include oral tablets/capsules (for outpatient use in early dengue), intravenous solutions (for hospitalized DHF/DSS patients), or inhalable powders (targeting lung reservoirs). Excipients may include lactose, microcrystalline cellulose, magnesium stearate (solid forms); saline, dextrose, polysorbate 80 (injectables); or phospholipids, cholesterol (liposomal formulations). To mitigate toxicity, controlled-release systems (e.g., PLGA microspheres) can maintain therapeutic concentrations while minimizing peak plasma levels.

Viperin-based therapeutics may take several forms:
- Recombinant human viperin protein, administered intravenously or subcutaneously, optionally fused to Fc domains for extended half-life.
- Gene therapy vectors (e.g., AAV, lentivirus) encoding viperin under a constitutive or IFN-inducible promoter, delivered locally (e.g., intramuscular) or systemically.
- Small-molecule inducers of viperin expression, identified via screening of IFN pathway activators (e.g., STING agonists, RIG-I ligands).

Compositions may also combine multiple active ingredients—for example, a proteasome inhibitor plus an interferon-β mimetic—to synergistically enhance antiviral activity. Adjuvants like TLR agonists could boost endogenous viperin production. All formulations must meet regulatory standards for sterility, stability, and pyrogenicity.

Pharmacokinetic studies in relevant animal models (e.g., AG129 mice) will determine optimal dosing regimens. Given the narrow window for antiviral intervention in dengue (days 1–5 of fever), rapid-acting formulations are preferred. Safety margins will be established by comparing EC50 (antiviral) and CC50 (cytotoxic) values in human hepatocytes and cardiomyocytes, as liver and heart are potential toxicity sites.

### Other Uses

Beyond direct antiviral therapy, the invention enables several ancillary applications. First, IP-10 and I-TAC serve as pharmacodynamic biomarkers in clinical trials of dengue therapeutics. A successful antiviral should reduce these chemokines toward baseline, providing early proof of mechanism. Second, the 50-gene signature can stratify patients in observational studies, linking specific host response patterns to outcomes like thrombocytopenia or vascular leak. Third, proteasome inhibitors may be repurposed for other flaviviruses (e.g., Zika, West Nile) that share replication mechanisms with dengue.

In vaccine development, measuring IP-10/I-TAC post-immunization could indicate robust innate activation, correlating with protective immunity. Conversely, excessive chemokine production might signal reactogenicity. The invention also informs vector control: transgenic mosquitoes expressing viperin or proteasome inhibitors in midgut tissues could block viral transmission.

Finally, the genomic datasets generated (GEO accession GSE6048) constitute a public resource for systems virology, enabling meta-analyses with other pathogen datasets to identify universal host defense modules.

### Examples

**Example 1: Identification of Host Response Pathways by Microarray**

HepG2 cells were infected with live or heat-inactivated DENV-2 (TSV01, MOI=10). RNA harvested at 72 hpi was analyzed on Compugen 19K oligonucleotide arrays. SAM analysis (q<25%) revealed 124 upregulated genes, enriched for IFN response (P=10⁻¹⁵), NF-κB signaling, and UPS. Key hits included IP-10, I-TAC, viperin, OAS1, HERC5.

**Example 2: Validation in Cell Lines and Patients**

TaqMan LDA confirmed upregulation of 50 common genes in HepG2, A549, and PBMCs from 10 dengue patients (EDEN cohort). MetaCore mapping showed NF-κB/STAT1-centered networks. IP-10 and I-TAC were top hits.

**Example 3: IP-10 and I-TAC as Biomarkers**

ELISA of patient sera showed IP-10 elevated at visits 1 (p=10⁻¹⁵) and 2 (p=10⁻¹¹) vs. convalescence; I-TAC elevated at visit 1 (p=10⁻⁷). Both higher than non-dengue febrile controls (p<10⁻⁶).

**Example 4: Viperin Inhibits Dengue Replication**

A549-Viperin cells showed 60% reduction in pfu/ml vs. WT (p=0.0004), with or without IFNβ pre-treatment, proving viperin’s direct antiviral role.

**Example 5: Proteasome Inhibitors Block Dengue**

MG-132 (1 µM) and ALLN (0.5 µM) reduced HepG2 viral titers by >50% without cytotoxicity (FDA assay). Higher doses caused >90% inhibition with mild toxicity.

These examples collectively demonstrate the invention’s feasibility, efficacy, and translational potential.
