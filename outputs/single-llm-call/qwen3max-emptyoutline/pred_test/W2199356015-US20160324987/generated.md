## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of gene therapy and, more specifically, to methods and compositions for the treatment of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) through allele-specific genomic editing using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. In particular, the invention provides a targeted approach for selectively ablating a disease-causing mutant allele of the rhodopsin (Rho) gene while preserving the function of the wild-type Rho allele, thereby preventing photoreceptor degeneration and preserving visual function in subjects afflicted with adRP caused by gain-of-function mutations in Rho. The invention further encompasses engineered CRISPR/Cas9 constructs designed to exploit single-nucleotide differences between mutant and wild-type Rho alleles—specifically, differences that generate or eliminate a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM)—to achieve high-fidelity discrimination and selective cleavage of the pathogenic allele without disrupting the normal allele. The therapeutic strategy disclosed herein is particularly applicable to adRP patients harboring Rho mutations that create novel PAM sequences amenable to Cas9 recognition, including but not limited to the S334ter mutation and other class-I RHO mistrafficking mutations.

## BACKGROUND

Retinal degenerative diseases represent a major cause of irreversible vision loss worldwide, with retinitis pigmentosa (RP) being among the most prevalent inherited forms. RP encompasses a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by progressive degeneration of photoreceptor cells—primarily rods, followed by cones—leading to night blindness, peripheral vision loss, and eventual central vision impairment. The genetic basis of RP is highly diverse, with over 80 genes implicated in its pathogenesis. Inheritance patterns include autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant, X-linked, and digenic forms. Among these, autosomal dominant RP (adRP) accounts for approximately 30–40% of all RP cases and presents a unique therapeutic challenge due to its gain-of-function or dominant-negative mechanism of pathogenicity.

Gene replacement therapy has shown promise in clinical trials for recessive forms of RP, where biallelic loss-of-function mutations result in complete absence or nonfunctionality of a critical protein. In such cases, delivery of a functional cDNA copy of the defective gene can restore protein expression and halt or reverse disease progression. However, this strategy is fundamentally inapplicable to adRP, wherein a single mutant allele produces a toxic protein product that disrupts cellular homeostasis even in the presence of a normal wild-type allele. Simply adding back a wild-type transgene does not neutralize the deleterious effects of the mutant protein; instead, it may exacerbate metabolic burden without addressing the root cause of toxicity.

Rhodopsin (RHO), the rod photopigment essential for dim-light vision, is the most commonly mutated gene in adRP, accounting for up to 25% of all adRP cases. Over 150 distinct RHO mutations have been identified, many of which lead to protein misfolding, aberrant trafficking, constitutive activation, or aggregation. A well-characterized subset includes C-terminal truncation mutations such as S334ter, which introduces a premature stop codon at position 334 (or 338, depending on isoform numbering), resulting in a truncated RHO protein lacking 15 C-terminal amino acids. This truncated protein fails to localize properly to photoreceptor outer segments (POS) due to loss of critical trafficking signals and dephosphorylation sites required for timely deactivation after photoexcitation. Consequently, the mutant RHO accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus of photoreceptor cell bodies, triggering endoplasmic reticulum stress, constitutive signaling, and ultimately apoptotic cell death.

Traditional approaches to treating adRP caused by RHO mutations have included RNA interference (RNAi) or antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) designed to suppress expression of the mutant transcript. While effective in preclinical models, these strategies typically require co-delivery of a replacement wild-type RHO transgene to maintain sufficient levels of functional rhodopsin, as global knockdown of both alleles would otherwise induce haploinsufficiency. However, studies have demonstrated that humans and animal models tolerate a wide range of RHO expression levels—from 50% to 200% of normal—without clinical symptoms, and that as little as 10% of total RHO expression from an exogenous source can rescue the phenotype in Rho-knockout mice. These findings suggest that selective suppression of only the mutant allele, without supplementation, may be sufficient to restore retinal health in adRP patients, provided the remaining wild-type allele is fully functional and unimpaired.

The advent of CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing has opened new avenues for precise, permanent correction of genetic defects at the DNA level. Unlike transient RNA-targeting modalities, CRISPR/Cas9 enables irreversible disruption of the mutant gene locus, offering the potential for durable therapeutic benefit from a single intervention. A key challenge in applying CRISPR/Cas9 to dominant disorders lies in achieving allele-specific targeting—i.e., discriminating between two nearly identical genomic sequences that differ by only one or a few nucleotides. The Cas9 nuclease requires both complementarity between the guide RNA (gRNA) and the target DNA sequence and the presence of a specific short DNA motif adjacent to the target site, known as the protospacer adjacent motif (PAM). In Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9), the canonical PAM is 5′-NGG-3′, where “N” is any nucleotide. Critically, certain point mutations can create or destroy a PAM sequence, thereby rendering one allele uniquely susceptible to Cas9 cleavage while sparing the other.

The S334ter mutation in Rho exemplifies this principle: the substitution of a cytosine (C) to thymine (T) at the relevant genomic position converts the wild-type sequence 5′-TGC-3′ into 5′-TGG-3′, thereby generating a novel SpCas9-compatible PAM (5′-TGG-3′ qualifies as NGG when read on the complementary strand). This single-nucleotide change creates a unique molecular signature that can be exploited for selective targeting. Prior attempts to develop allele-specific CRISPR therapies for adRP have been limited by insufficient discrimination or off-target effects, but the PAM-dependent mechanism offers a robust solution because Cas9 cannot cleave in the absence of a compatible PAM, regardless of gRNA binding affinity.

Despite these advances, significant hurdles remain in translating CRISPR-based allele-specific ablation into clinical practice. Efficient and safe delivery of CRISPR components to post-mitotic photoreceptors in vivo remains technically challenging. Viral vectors such as adeno-associated virus (AAV) are commonly used but have limited packaging capacity (~4.7 kb), which constrains the choice of Cas9 orthologs and regulatory elements. Non-viral delivery methods, including electroporation or lipid nanoparticles, offer alternatives but often suffer from lower transfection efficiency or transient expression. Moreover, long-term safety concerns regarding off-target cleavage, immune responses to bacterial Cas9 proteins, and unintended genomic rearrangements must be rigorously addressed.

Accordingly, there exists a pressing need for a refined, clinically viable method to selectively inactivate mutant Rho alleles in adRP patients without compromising wild-type Rho function, leveraging the natural PAM-altering properties of disease-causing mutations. The present invention fulfills this need by providing a CRISPR/Cas9 system specifically engineered to recognize and cleave only the mutant Rho allele containing a PAM-generating mutation, thereby eliminating the source of photoreceptor toxicity while allowing endogenous wild-type Rho to support normal phototransduction and structural integrity of the retina.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel and effective method for the treatment of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP) caused by gain-of-function mutations in the rhodopsin (Rho) gene, particularly those that introduce or alter a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence recognized by a CRISPR-associated (Cas) nuclease. The core innovation resides in the design and application of a CRISPR/Cas9 system that achieves allele-specific genomic ablation of the mutant Rho allele while leaving the wild-type Rho allele intact, thereby halting or reversing photoreceptor degeneration and preserving visual function without the need for exogenous supplementation of wild-type Rho protein.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for treating adRP in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the retina of said subject a pharmaceutical composition comprising a CRISPR/Cas9 construct that directs Cas9-mediated cleavage exclusively to a mutant Rho allele containing a PAM sequence that is absent in the wild-type Rho allele. The mutant Rho allele encodes a truncated or misfolded rhodopsin protein that causes photoreceptor toxicity and degeneration, such as the S334ter mutation or other class-I RHO mistrafficking mutations that generate a novel PAM (e.g., 5′-TGG-3′) due to a single-nucleotide substitution. The CRISPR/Cas9 construct includes a guide RNA (gRNA) whose spacer sequence is complementary to a region of the mutant Rho allele immediately upstream of the mutant-specific PAM, and a Cas9 nuclease capable of recognizing said PAM. Upon delivery to photoreceptor cells, the Cas9/gRNA complex binds and cleaves the mutant Rho allele, introducing insertions or deletions (indels) that disrupt the open reading frame and abolish production of the toxic mutant protein. The wild-type Rho allele, lacking the requisite PAM, remains uncut and continues to express functional rhodopsin at levels sufficient to maintain photoreceptor structure, outer segment formation, and visual signal transduction.

In another aspect, the invention provides an isolated nucleic acid molecule encoding a gRNA specifically designed to target a mutant Rho allele containing a PAM-generating mutation, wherein the gRNA comprises a 20-nucleotide spacer sequence complementary to a target site in exon 1 of the mutant Rho gene located immediately 5′ to a PAM sequence that differs from the corresponding wild-type sequence by a single nucleotide. In a preferred embodiment, the spacer sequence is selected to maximize discrimination between mutant and wild-type alleles based on both PAM presence and seed region complementarity, with particular emphasis on mismatches at positions 10–12 of the spacer to reduce off-target activity against the wild-type allele.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a recombinant vector comprising a nucleotide sequence encoding a Cas9 nuclease operably linked to a promoter active in photoreceptor cells, and a nucleotide sequence encoding the aforementioned gRNA, wherein the vector further comprises a reporter gene (e.g., mCherry) under the control of a constitutive promoter to facilitate tracking of transfected cells. The vector may be delivered via subretinal injection followed by electroporation to enhance uptake in post-mitotic photoreceptors during early postnatal development, although alternative delivery methods including AAV-mediated transduction are also contemplated.

The invention further provides a transgenic non-human animal model of adRP, such as the S334ter-3 rat, which carries a transgene encoding human or murine Rho with the S334ter mutation and exhibits rapid, progressive photoreceptor loss mimicking human disease. This model is instrumental for validating the efficacy and specificity of the CRISPR/Cas9 constructs disclosed herein.

Importantly, the method of the invention does not require co-delivery of a wild-type Rho transgene, as endogenous wild-type Rho expression—either from the unmutated allele in heterozygous patients or from residual expression in hemizygous contexts—is sufficient to support retinal function following ablation of the mutant allele. Experimental data demonstrate that selective ablation of Rho S334 in S334ter-3 rats results in preservation of up to eight layers of photoreceptor nuclei, restoration of proper RHO WT trafficking to outer segments, formation of morphologically normal outer segments, maintenance of synaptic connectivity with bipolar cells, and significant improvement in visual acuity as measured by optokinetic response, compared to control-treated or untreated eyes.

The invention is broadly applicable to any adRP patient harboring a Rho mutation that creates a Cas9-recognizable PAM sequence not present in the wild-type allele. Supplementary analysis indicates that multiple naturally occurring adRP-linked missense or nonsense mutations in Rho generate such targetable PAMs, thereby expanding the potential patient population amenable to this therapeutic approach. Furthermore, the strategy may be extended to other dominant disorders affecting the retina or other tissues where a single-nucleotide variant creates a unique PAM, especially when combined with engineered Cas9 variants with altered PAM specificities.

Thus, the present invention represents a paradigm shift in the treatment of dominant genetic diseases by leveraging the inherent molecular signature of the mutation itself—rather than merely its sequence difference—as the basis for selective therapeutic intervention.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Genomic Editing.

The present invention employs CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genomic editing as a precise and permanent means to inactivate disease-causing mutant alleles of the rhodopsin (Rho) gene in autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP). Unlike conventional gene therapy approaches that rely on transient suppression or protein replacement, the CRISPR/Cas9 system enables irreversible disruption of the mutant gene at the DNA level, thereby eliminating the root cause of photoreceptor toxicity. The key innovation lies in the exploitation of a single-nucleotide difference between mutant and wild-type Rho alleles that alters the presence or identity of a protospacer adjacent motif (PAM), a short DNA sequence absolutely required for Cas9 nuclease activity. Specifically, certain adRP-associated Rho mutations, such as the S334ter truncation, introduce a thymine (T) in place of a cytosine (C) at a critical genomic position, converting the wild-type sequence 5′-TGC-3′ into 5′-TGG-3′. When considered on the complementary DNA strand, this change generates a canonical SpCas9 PAM (5′-CCG-3′ becomes 5′-CCA-3′, but more critically, the non-template strand reads 5′-TGG-3′, which corresponds to a 5′-NGG-3′ PAM on the opposite strand). This newly formed PAM is recognized by Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9), whereas the wild-type allele lacks this motif and is therefore refractory to cleavage, even if the guide RNA (gRNA) exhibits partial complementarity.

Genomic editing according to the invention proceeds through the formation of a ribonucleoprotein complex comprising the Cas9 endonuclease and a synthetic gRNA. The gRNA is engineered to contain a 20-nucleotide spacer sequence that is fully complementary to the target site in the mutant Rho allele immediately upstream of the mutant-specific PAM. Upon binding, Cas9 induces a double-strand break (DSB) approximately 3 base pairs upstream of the PAM. In post-mitotic photoreceptor cells, which predominantly utilize the error-prone non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) pathway for DSB repair, this break is resolved through the introduction of small insertions or deletions (indels). These indels frequently result in frameshift mutations or premature stop codons, leading to nonsense-mediated decay of the mutant transcript or production of a nonfunctional, truncated protein that lacks toxic properties. Critically, because the wild-type Rho allele does not possess the requisite PAM, it remains untouched by Cas9, preserving its ability to produce full-length, functional rhodopsin.

The specificity of this approach is enhanced not only by PAM dependence but also by strategic placement of the gRNA target site. In the exemplary embodiment targeting the S334ter mutation, the gRNA (designated gRNA TRGT) was designed to bind within exon 1 of Rho, upstream of the second of four PAMs uniquely created by the mutation. Targeting exon 1 ensures that any indel introduced will disrupt the entire open reading frame, preventing the generation of alternative toxic isoforms that might arise from cleavage in downstream exons (e.g., exon 4), where partial protein products could retain pathogenic domains. Additionally, avoiding the 5′-most PAM minimizes the risk of polymerase skipping or cryptic splicing events that could inadvertently restore the reading frame. Deep sequencing and clonal analysis confirmed that gRNA TRGT induced indels in 33–36% of transfected photoreceptors at the mutant locus, with no detectable cleavage at the wild-type allele or at eight predicted off-target sites with up to four mismatches.

This level of allele discrimination is unprecedented in dominant disease correction and underscores the robustness of PAM-dependent targeting. It is important to note that while single-nucleotide mismatches in the gRNA seed region (positions 1–12) can sometimes reduce Cas9 activity, they do not guarantee complete avoidance of off-target cleavage. In contrast, the absence of a PAM renders cleavage impossible, providing a fail-safe mechanism for allele specificity. Thus, the invention capitalizes on a fundamental biochemical requirement of Cas9 rather than relying solely on thermodynamic differences in RNA-DNA hybridization.

The genomic editing strategy disclosed herein is not limited to the S334ter mutation. Bioinformatic screening of known adRP-linked RHO mutations reveals numerous additional variants that similarly create novel SpCas9-compatible PAM sequences (e.g., mutations converting TAC to TAG, thereby generating a TGG PAM on the complementary strand). Patients harboring such mutations constitute a defined subpopulation for whom this therapy is directly applicable. Moreover, the platform can be adapted to other Cas orthologs with different PAM requirements (e.g., SaCas9 with NNGRRT PAM) or engineered high-fidelity Cas9 variants to broaden the scope of targetable mutations. Rational design of Cas9 mutants with relaxed or altered PAM specificities further extends the utility of this approach to mutations that do not naturally generate canonical PAMs.

In summary, the genomic editing component of the invention provides a precise, durable, and self-limiting intervention that permanently silences the mutant Rho allele while preserving wild-type function, thereby converting a dominant toxic gain-of-function condition into a benign haplosufficient state compatible with long-term retinal health.

### Animal Model

The efficacy and specificity of the CRISPR/Cas9 constructs described herein were validated using a well-established transgenic rat model of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP): the S334ter-3 line. This model was generated by introducing a murine genomic fragment containing the Rho gene with the S334ter mutation—a C-to-T transition at codon 334 that introduces a premature stop codon, resulting in a truncated rhodopsin protein lacking the C-terminal 15 amino acids. The S334ter-3 rat recapitulates key features of human class-I RHO mistrafficking mutations, including rapid and progressive photoreceptor degeneration, mislocalization of mutant rhodopsin to photoreceptor cell bodies, absence of properly formed outer segments, and severe loss of visual function.

In this model, photoreceptor nuclei in the outer nuclear layer (ONL) appear normal at postnatal day (P) 11 but undergo dramatic attrition between P14 and P28, culminating in near-complete degeneration by P33, at which point only a single discontinuous row of photoreceptor nuclei remains. Immunohistochemical analysis confirms that the truncated RHO S334 protein is retained in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi of photoreceptor cell bodies due to loss of critical C-terminal trafficking signals, while the endogenous wild-type RHO (Rho WT) is correctly localized to inner segments but fails to reach outer segments because of the absence of structural support from degenerating photoreceptors. The accumulation of RHO S334 triggers chronic endoplasmic reticulum stress, constitutive phototransduction signaling, and caspase-mediated apoptosis, mirroring the pathophysiology observed in human adRP patients with analogous mutations.

The S334ter-3 rat is particularly suited for testing allele-specific ablation strategies because it expresses both the mutant transgene (Rho S334) and the endogenous wild-type allele (Rho WT), allowing direct assessment of selectivity. Furthermore, each photoreceptor carries only a single copy of the Rho S334 transgene, ensuring that successful ablation leads to complete elimination of the toxic protein in that cell—a binary outcome that simplifies interpretation of rescue phenotypes. The model also exhibits cell-autonomous degeneration, meaning that rescue occurs only in transfected cells, enabling spatial correlation between transgene expression (via mCherry reporter), genomic editing, and histological preservation.

For experimental purposes, neonatal S334ter-3 rats received unilateral subretinal injections of CRISPR/Cas9 plasmids at P0, followed immediately by electroporation to enhance plasmid uptake in mitotically active retinal progenitor cells destined to become photoreceptors. This timing ensures that Cas9/gRNA expression coincides with the onset of Rho transcription, maximizing the window for mutant allele disruption before significant toxicity accumulates. Control animals received either a non-targeting gRNA (gRNA CNTRL) or no treatment. At various time points (P4 for molecular analysis, P33 for histology, P39 for functional testing), retinas were harvested for evaluation.

The use of this animal model provided critical proof-of-concept that selective ablation of Rho S334 prevents photoreceptor loss, restores RHO WT trafficking to nascent outer segments, preserves synaptic architecture, and improves visual acuity. Importantly, rescue was strictly confined to mCherry-positive regions, confirming that the therapeutic effect is directly attributable to CRISPR-mediated editing and not secondary bystander effects. The sharp demarcation between rescued and degenerated areas further validates the cell-autonomous nature of the intervention and highlights the importance of achieving widespread retinal transduction for maximal clinical benefit.

Thus, the S334ter-3 rat serves as both a predictive preclinical model for human adRP and a rigorous testbed for optimizing CRISPR delivery, specificity, and durability—key parameters for translational development.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Generally

The CRISPR/Cas9 constructs employed in the present invention are recombinant nucleic acid molecules engineered to express, within photoreceptor cells, the molecular machinery necessary for allele-specific cleavage of a mutant Rho allele. These constructs typically comprise two core functional elements: (1) a nucleotide sequence encoding a Cas9 endonuclease, and (2) a nucleotide sequence encoding a guide RNA (gRNA) designed to direct Cas9 to a unique target site within the mutant Rho gene. Both elements are operably linked to regulatory sequences that drive their expression in retinal tissue, particularly in rod photoreceptors, which are the primary cell type affected in RHO-mediated adRP.

The Cas9 component may be derived from any Cas9 ortholog or engineered variant, though Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9) is preferred in the exemplary embodiments due to its well-characterized activity, high cleavage efficiency, and compatibility with the PAM-altering mutations found in adRP patients. SpCas9 recognizes a 5′-NGG-3′ PAM sequence, where “N” is any nucleotide, and induces a blunt-ended double-strand break three base pairs upstream of the PAM. Alternative Cas9 proteins, such as Staphylococcus aureus Cas9 (SaCas9), which recognizes a longer 5′-NNGRRT-3′ PAM and has a smaller coding sequence (~3.3 kb), may be advantageous for viral packaging into adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors, which have a ~4.7 kb cargo limit. Engineered high-fidelity Cas9 variants (e.g., eSpCas9, SpCas9-HF1) with reduced off-target activity are also contemplated to enhance safety.

The gRNA is a chimeric RNA molecule consisting of a ~20-nucleotide spacer sequence fused to a scaffold sequence that binds Cas9. The spacer is designed to be complementary to the target DNA sequence immediately 5′ to the PAM in the mutant Rho allele. To ensure allele specificity, the spacer is selected such that perfect complementarity exists only with the mutant allele, while the wild-type allele contains at least one mismatch—preferably within the seed region (positions 1–12)—and, crucially, lacks the PAM. In some embodiments, the gRNA is expressed from a U6 or H1 RNA polymerase III promoter, which provides high-level, constitutive expression in mammalian cells. Alternatively, tRNA-gRNA fusions driven by RNA polymerase II promoters may be used to enable polycistronic expression from compact vectors.

The construct may be delivered as a single plasmid (all-in-one vector) or as separate plasmids for Cas9 and gRNA. In the exemplary experiments, an all-in-one px330-derived plasmid was used, wherein Cas9 and gRNA are co-expressed from a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter and a U6 promoter, respectively. A fluorescent reporter gene (mCherry) was included downstream of the Cas9 coding sequence, separated by a self-cleaving P2A peptide or internal ribosome entry site (IRES), to allow visualization of transfected cells. Although the CMV promoter drives robust initial expression, its activity diminishes over time in post-mitotic neurons; thus, for long-term applications, photoreceptor-specific promoters such as human rhodopsin kinase (hGRK1) or cone arrestin may be substituted to restrict expression to target cells and minimize immune responses.

The constructs are formulated in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier suitable for intraocular administration, typically via subretinal injection. Electroporation may be applied immediately after injection to transiently permeabilize photoreceptor membranes and enhance plasmid uptake, particularly in neonatal animals. For clinical translation, AAV serotypes with high tropism for photoreceptors (e.g., AAV5, AAV8) are preferred due to their safety profile and sustained transgene expression.

Overall, the CRISPR/Cas9 constructs of the invention are modular, customizable, and optimized for high specificity, efficient delivery, and durable editing in the retinal environment.

### CRISPR/Cas9 Constructs, Specifically

In the specific embodiment disclosed herein, the CRISPR/Cas9 construct used to target the Rho S334 mutant allele in S334ter-3 rats comprises a plasmid vector derived from px330 (Addgene #42230), modified to express SpCas9, a custom-designed gRNA (gRNA TRGT), and an mCherry reporter. The gRNA TRGT spacer sequence is 5′-GCGCCAGCGTCTTCATCGCC-3′ (SEQ ID NO:1), which is complementary to nucleotides 330–349 of exon 1 of the murine Rho gene containing the S334ter mutation (c.1000C>T). This target site is positioned immediately upstream of a PAM sequence (5′-TGG-3′ on the non-template strand, corresponding to 5′-CCA-3′ on the template strand), which is generated exclusively by the C-to-T mutation and is absent in the wild-type Rho allele (which has 5′-TGC-3′ at this position, yielding a non-functional 5′-CCG-3′ PAM).

The gRNA TRGT was selected from among four potential PAM sites created by the S334ter mutation based on several criteria: (1) location in exon 1 to ensure complete open reading frame disruption; (2) minimal risk of generating alternative toxic isoforms; (3) maximal sequence divergence from the wild-type allele at the PAM-proximal region; and (4) low predicted off-target activity across the rat genome. In contrast, the control gRNA (gRNA CNTRL) targets exon 2 of Rho S334 with a spacer containing four mismatches relative to Rho WT and was shown to lack cleavage activity in vitro, serving as a negative control for non-specific effects.

The Cas9 coding sequence is under the control of a cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate-early promoter, ensuring strong initial expression in transfected cells. The gRNA is transcribed from a human U6 promoter, which provides precise 5′ and 3′ ends necessary for Cas9 loading. The mCherry reporter is fused to the Cas9 coding sequence via a P2A self-cleaving peptide, allowing stoichiometric co-expression while maintaining independent protein folding. This design enables fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) of transfected photoreceptors for downstream genomic analysis.

Sequence verification confirmed the integrity of all components, and functional validation in cultured mesenchymal stem cells derived from S334ter-3 rats (MSCs S334) demonstrated robust, allele-specific cleavage: Sanger sequencing of PCR amplicons spanning the target site revealed multiple indel peaks exclusively at the Rho S334 locus, with no disruption detected at Rho WT or in control-transfected cells. Clonal sequencing of transfected photoreceptors at P4 showed indel frequencies of 33–36%, confirming efficient in vivo editing.

This specific construct represents a prototype for a class of therapeutics tailored to PAM-generating RHO mutations. By substituting the gRNA spacer sequence, the same vector backbone can be reprogrammed to target other adRP-linked mutations that create unique PAMs, enabling a personalized medicine approach for genetically defined patient subgroups.

### Methods

The methods of the present invention encompass the preparation, delivery, and application of CRISPR/Cas9 constructs to achieve allele-specific ablation of mutant Rho alleles in the retina for the treatment of autosomal dominant retinitis pigmentosa (adRP). The process begins with the identification of a patient harboring a Rho mutation that creates a Cas9-recognizable PAM sequence not present in the wild-type allele. Genomic DNA is obtained from the patient (e.g., via blood draw), and the Rho locus is sequenced to confirm the presence of a PAM-altering variant such as S334ter or other mutations listed in Supplementary Table S1 of the research paper.

A gRNA is then designed with a spacer sequence complementary to the region immediately upstream of the mutant-specific PAM. The gRNA sequence is cloned into a CRISPR/Cas9 expression vector, such as px330, using standard molecular biology techniques (e.g., BbsI restriction digestion and ligation). The vector is amplified in E. coli, purified using endotoxin-free kits, and formulated in a sterile, isotonic buffer suitable for intraocular injection.

For administration, the subject—typically a mammal such as a human, rat, or dog—is anesthetized, and a subretinal injection is performed using a fine-gauge needle to deliver the CRISPR/Cas9 construct into the subretinal space, preferably during early postnatal development in animal models or at the earliest feasible stage in human patients to preempt significant photoreceptor loss. In neonatal rats, this is done at postnatal day 0 (P0). Immediately following injection, electroporation may be applied using tweezer-type electrodes to transiently increase cell membrane permeability and enhance plasmid uptake by photoreceptor precursors.

Alternatively, for clinical applications, the CRISPR components may be packaged into recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) particles. Due to size constraints, SaCas9 or split-intein Cas9 systems may be used to fit within the AAV cargo limit. The AAV vector, serotyped for photoreceptor tropism (e.g., AAV5), is injected subretinally in a similar manner, but without electroporation.

Following administration, the Cas9/gRNA complex is expressed in transfected photoreceptors, where it scans the genome for PAM sequences followed by complementary DNA. Upon encountering the mutant Rho allele with the correct PAM and matching spacer, Cas9 induces a double-strand break, which is repaired by NHEJ, introducing indels that disrupt the mutant gene. The wild-type allele, lacking the PAM, remains intact.

Therapeutic efficacy is assessed through a combination of molecular, histological, and functional assays. Genomic DNA is extracted from sorted mCherry+ photoreceptors and analyzed by Sanger sequencing or next-generation sequencing to quantify indel frequency and confirm allele specificity. Retinal cross-sections are stained with cresyl violet or immunolabeled with antibodies against RHO N-terminus (to detect both isoforms) and C-terminus (to detect only RHO WT) to evaluate photoreceptor survival, protein localization, and outer segment formation. Visual function is measured using optokinetic response (OptoMotry), which quantifies spatial acuity by tracking reflexive head movements in response to rotating gratings.

In the exemplary study, these methods resulted in a ninefold increase in photoreceptor density and a 53% improvement in visual acuity in gRNA TRGT-treated eyes compared to controls, demonstrating the feasibility and potency of the approach. The methods are scalable, adaptable to other mutations, and form the basis for a transformative therapy for adRP and potentially other dominant genetic disorders.
