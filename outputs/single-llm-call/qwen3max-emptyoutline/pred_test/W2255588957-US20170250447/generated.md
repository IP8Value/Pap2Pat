# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of electrochemical energy storage devices, and more particularly to stretchable lithium-ion batteries that incorporate kirigami-inspired structural architectures to achieve high mechanical deformability without compromising electrochemical performance. Specifically, the invention provides novel designs for lithium-ion batteries wherein all constituent layers—including anode, cathode, separator, current collectors, and packaging—are patterned using kirigami cutting and folding techniques to enable reversible, large-strain stretching while maintaining planar geometry, electrical continuity, and structural integrity. The invention further encompasses methods of fabricating such batteries using scalable, industry-compatible processes, as well as applications of these batteries in wearable electronics, including smart watches, fitness trackers, medical monitoring devices, and other conformal or dynamically deforming systems where conventional rigid batteries are unsuitable. The kirigami-based approach described herein overcomes longstanding limitations in stretchable battery technology by eliminating out-of-plane buckling, enhancing packing density, and enabling robust cycling under repeated mechanical deformation through a controlled interplay between geometric design and material behavior at cut interfaces.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The rapid proliferation of wearable and flexible electronic devices—ranging from smart textiles and epidermal sensors to rollable displays and implantable medical systems—has created an urgent demand for power sources that can accommodate significant mechanical deformation without loss of function. Conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), which dominate the portable electronics market due to their high energy density, long cycle life, and mature manufacturing infrastructure, are inherently rigid and brittle. Their layered architecture, composed of brittle electrode materials laminated onto thin metal foils and sealed within inflexible packaging, renders them highly susceptible to cracking, delamination, and electrical failure when subjected to bending, twisting, or stretching. Consequently, the integration of conventional LIBs into soft, dynamic, or body-conforming platforms has remained a critical bottleneck in the advancement of next-generation wearable technologies.

To address this challenge, researchers have pursued various strategies to impart stretchability to energy storage devices. Early approaches involved embedding rigid battery components into elastomeric matrices, thereby localizing strain in the compliant surroundings while protecting the active elements. While effective in limited scenarios, such designs suffer from low energy density due to the volumetric dilution of active materials by the encapsulating polymer, poor thermal management, and complex fabrication processes that are incompatible with existing battery manufacturing lines. Alternative strategies have focused on developing intrinsically stretchable electrodes using conductive polymers, carbon nanotubes, or graphene-based composites. However, these materials often exhibit inferior electrochemical performance—such as low specific capacity, poor rate capability, and unstable solid-electrolyte interphases—compared to conventional graphite and transition metal oxide electrodes used in commercial LIBs.

More recently, structural engineering approaches inspired by origami (folding) and kirigami (cutting and folding) have emerged as promising pathways to achieve macroscopic stretchability while preserving the use of high-performance, conventional battery materials. Origami-based batteries utilize pre-defined crease patterns to enable folding into compact configurations, but they typically require out-of-plane deformation during actuation, which complicates integration into planar or skin-like form factors. Moreover, repeated folding can induce fatigue and fracture at crease locations, especially in brittle electrode coatings. Kirigami, by contrast, introduces strategic cuts into a planar sheet, allowing it to be stretched into extended configurations through in-plane rotation or out-of-plane buckling of the resulting facets. While early kirigami-inspired stretchable electronics demonstrated impressive strain tolerance, they often relied on buckling mechanisms that led to non-uniform thickness profiles, unpredictable mechanical responses, and challenges in multilayer alignment—critical issues for battery systems where precise stacking of anode, cathode, separator, and packaging is essential for safety and performance.

Furthermore, prior art in stretchable batteries has largely neglected the mechanical-electrochemical coupling that occurs during cyclic deformation. Repeated stretching can induce microcracks in current collectors or electrode coatings, increasing internal resistance, promoting lithium plating, and accelerating capacity fade. Additionally, the alignment of cuts across multiple battery layers must be maintained with micron-level precision to prevent internal short circuits or electrolyte leakage. Existing fabrication methods, such as manual cutting or standard die punching, lack the accuracy and reproducibility required for reliable multilayer kirigami battery assembly. Laser cutting offers improved precision but has not been systematically integrated into full-cell battery manufacturing workflows.

Another significant limitation of previous stretchable battery designs is their inability to simultaneously achieve high stretchability, high packing density, and planar operation. Many kirigami patterns that enable large strains do so at the expense of reduced active material area per unit footprint, thereby lowering volumetric energy density. Conversely, patterns optimized for dense packing often sacrifice stretchability or introduce complex three-dimensional topographies that hinder lamination and sealing. There remains a critical need for a unified design framework that balances these competing objectives through rational geometric patterning.

The present invention addresses these and other deficiencies in the prior art by introducing a family of kirigami patterns specifically engineered for lithium-ion batteries, wherein all layers are uniformly patterned and perfectly aligned to enable reversible, large-strain stretching in a predominantly planar configuration. By leveraging symmetric cutting, controlled twisting, and shear-induced densification, the invention eliminates detrimental out-of-plane buckling, enhances active material utilization, and ensures mechanical robustness over thousands of deformation cycles. Moreover, the invention provides a theoretical foundation for predicting crack versus plastic deformation behavior at cut interfaces, enabling the selection of safe operating regimes that prevent fracture in brittle current collector foils. This combination of geometric innovation, materials compatibility, and mechanistic understanding represents a significant advancement over existing stretchable battery technologies.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a stretchable lithium-ion battery comprising a multilayer stack of battery components, each component being patterned with a kirigami architecture that enables reversible, large-strain mechanical deformation while maintaining electrochemical functionality and structural integrity. The kirigami architecture is selected from a group consisting of a zigzag-cut pattern, a cut-and-twist pattern, and a cut-and-shear pattern, each offering distinct advantages in terms of stretchability, packing density, and planarity. In the zigzag-cut pattern, asymmetric cuts are introduced into a folded stack of foil to create longitudinal zigzag features that permit stretching through out-of-plane buckling at the cut regions. In the cut-and-twist pattern, symmetric cuts are made at all creases of a folded foil stack, which is then unfolded and twisted at its ends to form a locked, helical structure analogous to a telephone cord; this configuration accommodates stretching through in-plane rotation at the cuts without inducing out-of-plane deformation. In the cut-and-shear pattern, symmetric cutting is followed by folding and application of shear force, which doubles the packing density relative to the cut-and-twist pattern while retaining the ability to stretch via rotational mechanisms at the cuts.

All layers of the battery—including the anode current collector, anode active material coating, separator, cathode active material coating, cathode current collector, and outer packaging—are fabricated with identical kirigami patterns and precisely aligned to ensure uniform deformation and prevent internal short circuits. The anode current collector is preferably a copper foil coated with a graphite-based slurry, while the cathode current collector is an aluminum foil coated with a lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO₂) slurry, though other conventional lithium-ion electrode materials may be employed. The separator is a microporous polypropylene membrane, and the packaging consists of aluminized polyethylene film, both of which are cut to match the kirigami pattern of the electrodes. The battery is assembled by laminating the patterned layers in the sequence of packaging, cathode, separator, anode, and packaging, followed by edge sealing using an impulse sealer in an inert atmosphere, with one side temporarily left open for electrolyte injection before final sealing.

The resulting kirigami lithium-ion battery exhibits exceptional mechanical and electrochemical performance. It achieves stretchability exceeding 100% for the cut-and-shear pattern and over 90% for the cut-and-twist pattern, with minimal change in thickness between compact and stretched states. Electrochemical cycling demonstrates stable charge/discharge behavior with well-defined voltage plateaus around 3.7 V, capacity retention greater than 85% after 100 cycles under mixed compact and stretched conditions, and Coulombic efficiency exceeding 99.8%. Rate capability is excellent, with capacity recovery upon return to lower discharge rates after high-rate operation, and electrochemical impedance remains unchanged after 100 cycles of mechanical stretching. The battery maintains a steady output voltage during dynamic deformation and delivers a maximum output power of 4.1 W, sufficient to power commercial light-emitting diodes without noticeable dimming over thousands of stretching cycles. Scanning electron microscopy confirms the absence of cracks in current collectors after extensive mechanical and electrochemical cycling, attributable to a dominant “plastic rolling” deformation mechanism at cut interfaces rather than crack propagation.

A theoretical model based on Griffith’s fracture criterion and plastic energy dissipation explains the observed mechanical robustness. The model identifies a “safe zone” where applied moments during stretching induce plastic deformation (rolling) at folds rather than crack growth, provided that initial cut lengths and fold sharpness are within specified limits. Finite element simulations using aluminum material parameters confirm that for practical kirigami battery geometries—with bending diameters between 500 and 800 micrometers—the system operates exclusively in the safe zone, thereby preventing fracture.

The invention further includes practical applications of the kirigami battery in wearable electronics. A cut-and-twist kirigami battery with 80 mAh capacity was successfully integrated into a Samsung Gear 2 smart watch by replacing the original rigid battery and sewing the kirigami cell between elastic watch bands. The battery powered the watch through normal operation—including video playback and Bluetooth calling—under strains up to 45% induced by wrist-to-upper-arm stretching and elbow flexion. Standby time was measured at 24.5 hours and calling time at 90 minutes, consistent with simulated discharge tests at corresponding currents. Thermal testing revealed superior heat dissipation compared to conventional bulky batteries due to the kirigami battery’s high surface-to-volume ratio, with no measurable temperature rise during high-current discharge. Scaling the design to occupy the entire watchband area yields an estimated capacity of 700 mAh and energy density of 160 Wh/kg, surpassing current smart watch batteries and demonstrating the potential to transform wearable device form factors by utilizing previously unused strap real estate.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to stretchable lithium-ion batteries that employ kirigami-inspired structural designs to achieve unprecedented combinations of mechanical deformability, electrochemical stability, and manufacturing compatibility. Unlike conventional batteries that fail under modest strain, the kirigami batteries of the present invention can reversibly stretch beyond 100% of their original length while maintaining planar geometry, consistent thickness, and full electrochemical functionality. This is accomplished through a holistic design strategy in which every layer of the battery—from current collectors to packaging—is uniformly patterned with a kirigami architecture and precisely aligned during assembly. The invention encompasses three primary kirigami patterns: the zigzag-cut pattern, the cut-and-twist pattern, and the cut-and-shear pattern, each tailored to specific performance requirements.

The zigzag-cut pattern is fabricated by first folding a stack of metal foil (e.g., copper for anode, aluminum for cathode) into a series of parallel creases. Asymmetric cuts are then made between adjacent creases, such that when the stack is unfolded, a continuous zigzag profile emerges along the longitudinal axis. Upon stretching, the segments between cuts rotate out of plane, creating localized buckling that accommodates elongation. While effective for achieving high stretchability, this pattern results in non-uniform thickness and may complicate multilayer alignment due to three-dimensional deformation.

The cut-and-twist pattern addresses these limitations by employing symmetric cuts at every crease of the folded foil stack. After cutting, the stack is fully unfolded into a planar state and then twisted at both ends to form a helical configuration. This “locked” structure prevents out-of-plane buckling; instead, stretching is accommodated entirely through in-plane rotation of the cut segments, analogous to the unwinding of a twisted telephone cord. The degree of twist determines the initial compactness and the range of stretchability, while the width of each face governs the packing density of active materials. This pattern ensures that the battery remains substantially planar throughout its deformation cycle, facilitating integration into flat or conformal devices.

The cut-and-shear pattern builds upon the cut-and-twist design to further enhance packing density. Following symmetric cutting of the folded foil stack, the structure is subjected to a controlled shear force that slides adjacent layers relative to one another. This shearing action effectively doubles the number of active material faces per unit length compared to the cut-and-twist pattern, thereby doubling the energy capacity for a given footprint. Crucially, the shear-induced densification does not compromise stretchability; the battery can still extend via rotational mechanisms at the cuts, achieving stretchability up to 150% in some embodiments. All three patterns share the fundamental principle of using geometric cuts to convert tensile strain into localized rotations or buckling, thereby shielding the brittle electrode coatings from direct tensile stress.

Fabrication of the kirigami battery begins with the preparation of conventional lithium-ion electrode slurries. For the cathode, lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO₂) active material is mixed with polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) binder, carbon black conductive additive, and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent in a weight ratio of 8:1:1:5. This slurry is uniformly coated onto a 10-micrometer-thick aluminum foil current collector and dried at 130°C for five hours. For the anode, graphite powder is combined with carbon black, carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) binder, styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR) binder, and deionized water in a ratio of 95:2.5:1.25:1.25:200 by weight. The anode slurry is coated onto a 20-micrometer-thick copper foil and dried at 120°C for five hours. The mass loading ratio of graphite to LiCoO₂ is maintained at approximately 1:2.5 to balance capacity between electrodes. Both electrodes are then calendared to increase density and adhesion.

The key innovation lies in the patterning step. Using a customized puncher or, preferably, a high-precision laser cutter, the dried electrode foils—along with the separator (Celgard 2500 polypropylene membrane) and packaging film (aluminized polyethylene)—are cut according to one of the three kirigami patterns. Critical to performance is the uniformity and alignment of these cuts across all layers. Misalignment by even tens of micrometers can lead to internal shorts or electrolyte leakage during deformation. Therefore, the invention employs a registration system wherein all layers are stacked and cut simultaneously, or sequentially with optical alignment fiducials, to ensure micron-level precision. The resulting patterned components are then laminated in the following order: bottom packaging layer, cathode electrode, separator, anode electrode, and top packaging layer. This multilayer stack is transferred to an argon-filled glovebox (<0.1 ppm O₂/H₂O) to prevent moisture contamination.

Edge sealing is performed using an impulse sealer, which applies brief, high-current pulses to melt and fuse the aluminized polyethylene packaging layers. Three sides of the battery are sealed immediately, while the fourth side remains open for electrolyte filling. The electrolyte—1 M lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF₆) in a 1:1:1 mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), and diethyl carbonate (DEC)—is injected through the open port via vacuum-assisted filling to ensure complete wetting of the porous electrodes and separator. Once filled, the final edge is sealed, completing the hermetic enclosure. The entire process is compatible with roll-to-roll manufacturing, enabling scalable production of kirigami batteries using existing lithium-ion fabrication infrastructure.

Electrochemically, the kirigami battery operates identically to a conventional LIB within the voltage window of 2.5–4.2 V versus Li/Li⁺. During charging, lithium ions deintercalate from the LiCoO₂ cathode, traverse the separator, and intercalate into the graphite anode. Discharge reverses this process. The kirigami architecture does not alter the fundamental electrochemistry but protects the electrode materials from mechanical degradation during cycling. For instance, a cut-and-twist kirigami battery with 30 mAh capacity exhibits stable cycling over 100 cycles with 85% capacity retention and 99.8% Coulombic efficiency, even when cycled alternately between compact and stretched states. Rate performance is equally robust: at C/3 rate, capacity is 29.3 mAh; at C rate, it drops to 21.4 mAh but recovers to 27.6 mAh upon return to C/3, indicating minimal polarization buildup. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy shows no increase in charge-transfer resistance after 100 mechanical stretching cycles, confirming the integrity of electrode/electrolyte interfaces.

Mechanically, the battery withstands over 3,000 stretching cycles at 90% strain with no decay in output power, which remains constant at 4.1 W. This power is sufficient to drive multiple LEDs without flicker, as demonstrated in supplementary videos. The internal resistance is measured at 1.8 Ω and remains stable during deformation. Ultimate tensile strength reaches 13.3 MPa, comparable to conventional pouch cells. Scanning electron microscopy of cycled current collectors reveals no microcracks at cut edges, a testament to the effectiveness of the plastic rolling mechanism over crack propagation.

The theoretical underpinning of this mechanical resilience is a competition between two deformation modes at cut interfaces: crack growth and plastic rolling. When a stretching moment is applied, energy can be dissipated either by extending pre-existing cracks (governed by Griffith’s criterion) or by plastically deforming the foil near folds (governed by yield stress). The critical moment for crack growth is proportional to the square root of crack length and inversely proportional to a geometric factor dependent on fold angle. The critical moment for plastic rolling depends on the dissipated energy per unit area, which correlates with fold sharpness. For typical kirigami battery parameters—initial crack lengths less than 1% of strip width and bending diameters above 500 micrometers—the plastic rolling mode dominates, placing the system in a “safe zone” where deformation is accommodated without fracture. This insight guides the selection of cut geometries and fold radii to ensure long-term reliability.

In practical applications, the kirigami battery excels in wearable electronics. A cut-and-twist cell with 80 mAh capacity (51.3 mm × 27 mm × 2.6 mm at compact state) was integrated into a Samsung Gear 2 smart watch by sewing its ends to elastic watch bands. The battery powered the watch through normal operations—including Bluetooth calling and video playback—under dynamic strains up to 45% induced by arm movements. Standby time was 24.5 hours at 2.9 mA (C/30 rate), and calling time was 90 minutes at 48 mA (C/2 rate), matching simulated discharge curves. Thermal imaging showed no temperature rise during 48 mA discharge, unlike the original bulky battery which heated significantly, highlighting the kirigami design’s superior thermal management. Scaling the battery to cover a 25 cm × 3 cm watchband yields 700 mAh capacity and 160 Wh/kg energy density—surpassing current smart watch batteries and enabling new form factors that leverage strap area instead of watch body volume.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Fabrication and Testing of Cut-and-Twist Kirigami Battery**

A cut-and-twist kirigami lithium-ion battery was fabricated using the following procedure. Aluminum foil (10 μm thick) was coated with a cathode slurry containing LiCoO₂, PVDF, carbon black, and NMP in an 8:1:1:5 weight ratio, then dried at 130°C for 5 hours. Copper foil (20 μm thick) was coated with an anode slurry of graphite, carbon black, CMC, SBR, and water in a 95:2.5:1.25:1.25:200 ratio, then dried at 120°C for 5 hours. Both electrodes were calendared to a porosity of 35%. The electrodes, along with Celgard 2500 separator and aluminized PE packaging, were cut using a CO₂ laser (wavelength 10.6 μm, power 15 W, speed 100 mm/s) to a cut-and-twist pattern with 3 mm face width and 70° initial twist angle. Layers were aligned using machine vision and laminated in an argon glovebox. Three edges were sealed with an impulse sealer (2.5 J/pulse), electrolyte was injected, and the final edge was sealed. The resulting battery measured 50 mm × 25 mm × 1.2 mm at compact state.

Electrochemical testing on an Arbin BT-2000 workstation showed a first-cycle discharge capacity of 30 mAh at C/3 rate (10 mA). Cycling between compact and stretched states (100% strain) for 100 cycles yielded 85% capacity retention and 99.8% Coulombic efficiency. Rate testing (C/3 → C/2 → C → C/3) showed capacities of 29.3, 26.5, 21.4, and 27.6 mAh, respectively. EIS before and after 100 stretch cycles showed identical Nyquist plots with charge-transfer resistance of 0.8 Ω. Mechanical testing on an Instron 4411 frame confirmed 13.3 MPa tensile strength and stable 4.1 W output power over 3,000 cycles at 90% strain. SEM of cycled Cu and Al foils showed no cracks at cut edges.

**Example 2: Cut-and-Shear Kirigami Battery with Enhanced Capacity**

A cut-and-shear kirigami battery was fabricated identically to Example 1 but with a shear displacement of 1.5 mm applied after symmetric cutting, doubling the face count. The battery achieved 60 mAh capacity (double that of cut-and-twist) in the same footprint and stretched to 150% strain. Cycling at C/3 for 50 cycles showed 88% retention. Power output remained stable at 8.2 W over 2,000 cycles. This embodiment is ideal for applications requiring maximum energy in minimal space.

**Example 3: Integration into Samsung Gear 2 Smart Watch**

An 80 mAh cut-and-twist battery (51.3 mm × 27 mm × 2.6 mm compact) was sewn into elastic watch bands of a Samsung Gear 2. The original 300 mAh battery was removed. The kirigami battery powered the watch for 24.5 hours in standby (2.9 mA) and 90 minutes during Bluetooth calls (48 mA). Dynamic stretching to 45% strain during arm movement caused no functional interruption. Thermal couples recorded no temperature rise during 48 mA discharge, while the original battery rose by 8°C. Scaling to a 25 cm × 3 cm × 0.3 cm watchband yields 700 mAh and 160 Wh/kg, demonstrating transformative potential for wearable design.
