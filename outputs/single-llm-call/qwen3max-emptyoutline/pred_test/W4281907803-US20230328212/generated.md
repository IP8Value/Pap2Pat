## BACKGROUND

The field of virtual production has undergone a transformative evolution with the advent of LED-based volumetric stages, commonly referred to as LED volumes. These stages utilize high-resolution LED panels arranged to surround performers and physical set elements, thereby enabling the display of dynamic, immersive virtual environments directly within the filming space. This approach represents a significant departure from traditional chroma key (greenscreen) workflows, offering numerous advantages including real-time in-camera visual effects, accurate interactive lighting, and enhanced performer immersion through natural eyelines and contextual environmental cues. The core principle underlying this methodology involves the projection of high dynamic range image-based lighting (HDR IBL) environments onto the LED surfaces, which simultaneously serves as both the background imagery captured by the camera and the primary illumination source for the foreground subjects.

Despite these compelling benefits, the practical implementation of RGB LED volumes for lighting reproduction has revealed persistent and significant challenges related to color fidelity, particularly when attempting to simulate broad-spectrum real-world illuminants such as daylight, incandescent, or warm white LED lighting. The fundamental issue arises from the inherent spectral characteristics of conventional RGB LED emitters. Unlike natural light sources that exhibit relatively continuous and broad emission spectra across the visible wavelength range, RGB LEDs produce light through discrete, narrowband emission peaks corresponding to red, green, and blue channels. When combined to produce white or other composite colors, the resulting spectral power distribution (SPD) contains pronounced gaps between these peaks, leading to metameric failure—where colors appear matched under one illuminant but mismatched under another due to differences in their underlying spectral reflectance properties.

This spectral deficiency manifests in predictable and visually objectionable color rendition errors. Notably, human skin tones are particularly susceptible: lighter complexions tend to shift unnaturally toward pink hues, while darker skin tones exhibit an undesirable red cast. Similarly, saturated materials demonstrate systematic deviations; orange fabrics shift toward red, cyan objects lean excessively blue, and yellow materials often appear darker and less vibrant than they would under the target illuminant. These inaccuracies undermine the photorealism of the final imagery and can necessitate extensive and costly post-production color correction, thereby partially negating one of the primary economic and creative advantages of in-camera virtual production.

Existing calibration methodologies for LED volumes have predominantly focused on optimizing the system as a display device rather than as a lighting instrument. Standard industry practices, as implemented in platforms such as Unreal Engine and described in workflows from leading visual effects studios, involve a primary-based color calibration. This process entails capturing images of pure red, green, and blue patches displayed on the LED panels using the principal photography camera. From these measurements, a 3×3 color transformation matrix is computed to ensure that the camera records the displayed primaries as matching the intended color values in the source content. While this technique effectively guarantees accurate reproduction of the in-camera background imagery—since all displayed colors are linear combinations of the calibrated primaries—it fails to address the critical issue of how the same LED illumination interacts with physical materials in the scene.

The limitation of this conventional approach becomes evident when considering the dual role of the LED volume: it must simultaneously function as a high-fidelity display for background elements and as a spectrally accurate light source for foreground subjects. A calibration optimized solely for display accuracy results in a metameric match for neutral colors (such as the white patch of a color chart) but produces significant color errors for non-neutral, spectrally selective materials. This occurs because the narrowband SPD of the RGB LEDs cannot adequately stimulate the full range of human color vision or accurately render the complex reflectance spectra of real-world objects, regardless of how well the displayed colors are matched to the camera’s color response.

Alternative approaches to improving spectral fidelity have been explored in specialized lighting contexts. For instance, research by Wenger et al. and LeGendre et al. has demonstrated that adding supplementary spectral channels—such as amber, lime, or broad-spectrum white LEDs—to a lighting array can fill the spectral gaps inherent in RGB systems, thereby enhancing color rendering capabilities. Similarly, professional cinema lighting manufacturers have begun incorporating broad-spectrum emitters into their RGB fixtures to achieve higher color rendering indices (CRI) and more accurate skin tone reproduction. However, these solutions are not readily applicable to existing LED panel infrastructure used in virtual production stages. Panel manufacturers prioritize wide color gamut and high brightness for display applications, where the addition of redundant white or other broad-spectrum channels offers no perceptual benefit since white can already be synthesized through RGB mixing. Consequently, the vast majority of commercially available LED panels remain constrained to the standard RGB architecture, leaving virtual production practitioners without practical hardware-based solutions to the color rendition problem.

In light of these constraints, there exists a critical need for a software-based calibration methodology that can mitigate the color rendition deficiencies of RGB LED volumes without requiring modifications to the underlying panel hardware. Such a solution must reconcile the competing demands of accurate background display and faithful foreground illumination, operating within the practical limitations of existing virtual production pipelines. The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing a novel multi-matrix color calibration framework that decouples the color correction requirements for in-frustum (background) and out-of-frustum (lighting) content, thereby enabling simultaneous optimization of both display accuracy and material color fidelity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive and practical method for calibrating RGB LED-based virtual production stages to achieve improved color rendition for both in-camera background imagery and foreground subjects illuminated by the LED panels. The core innovation lies in the strategic application of multiple 3×3 color correction matrices—specifically, a pre-correction matrix for out-of-frustum content, a distinct pre-correction matrix for in-frustum content, and a post-correction matrix applied to the final recorded footage—thereby enabling independent optimization of display accuracy and lighting fidelity without requiring spectral measurements or hardware modifications.

In accordance with the invention, the calibration process begins with the acquisition of four key calibration images using the principal photography camera. The first image captures pure red, green, and blue patches displayed on the in-frustum LED panels, which is used to compute a primary-based pre-correction matrix M. This matrix ensures that the LED panels, when displaying arbitrary content, produce colors that match the intended source values as observed by the camera—a standard requirement for accurate in-camera background reproduction.

The remaining three calibration images record the appearance of a standardized color chart illuminated individually by each of the red, green, and blue spectral channels of the LED volume. These measurements capture the system’s spectral response characteristics and enable the prediction of how any given HDR IBL environment will render the color chart when displayed on the LED panels. Using this data in conjunction with a reference photograph of the same color chart under the target real-world illuminant, a post-correction matrix Q is computed. This matrix is designed to minimize the color difference between the physically lit chart in the virtual production stage and its real-world counterpart, effectively compensating for the spectral deficiencies of the RGB LED illumination by desaturating problematic hues such as overly pink skin tones or reddish oranges.

A critical insight of the invention is that applying the post-correction matrix Q globally to the final image would inadvertently distort the accurately calibrated in-camera background. To resolve this conflict, the invention introduces a second pre-correction matrix N, specifically applied to the in-frustum content displayed on the background LED panels. This matrix is derived as the product of the original matrix M and the inverse of the post-correction matrix Q (i.e., N = MQ⁻¹). By pre-compensating the background content with N, the subsequent application of Q to the entire image restores the background to its intended appearance while simultaneously correcting the foreground illumination. This elegant decoupling eliminates the need for complex foreground/background segmentation or rotoscoping during post-production.

Furthermore, the invention addresses a secondary but significant source of color error: the non-zero albedo of LED panels, which causes them to reflect incident illumination from out-of-frustum panels onto the in-frustum background. This reflected light introduces an unwanted color cast that degrades background fidelity. The invention mitigates this effect through a per-lighting-environment black level subtraction procedure. Specifically, with the out-of-frustum content active and the in-frustum background disabled, the camera records the average RGB value contributed by panel reflections. This value is then scaled relative to the system’s white point and subtracted from the rendered in-frustum content prior to display, effectively neutralizing the contamination from bounced light.

The resulting calibration framework requires no spectral instrumentation, operates entirely within the RGB color space familiar to cinematographers and digital imaging technicians, and integrates seamlessly into existing virtual production rendering pipelines through simple shader modifications. By leveraging only four calibration images and basic linear algebra operations, the invention delivers substantial improvements in color fidelity for both actors and set pieces while preserving the integrity of the in-camera background—thereby fulfilling a long-standing need in the virtual production industry for a practical, effective solution to the color rendition challenges of RGB LED volumes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

### EXAMPLE EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a method and system for calibrating RGB LED-based virtual production stages to simultaneously optimize color fidelity for both in-camera background display and foreground subject illumination. The following detailed description sets forth exemplary embodiments of the invention, including specific calibration procedures, mathematical formulations, and implementation considerations. It should be understood that these examples are provided for illustrative purposes and do not limit the scope of the invention, which encompasses all equivalent implementations and variations falling within the spirit and scope of the appended claims.

The calibration methodology according to the invention operates under several foundational assumptions regarding the linearity and alignment of the virtual production system components. First, it is assumed that the LED volume has been pre-calibrated to function as a linear display device. This means that the luminance output of each LED channel is directly proportional to its input pixel value, without gamma encoding or other non-linear transformations. Linearity can be verified through standard techniques such as capturing images of grayscale ramps or series of increasing pixel values, and corrected if necessary through per-channel lookup tables or similar methods. Similarly, the principal photography camera is assumed to have a linear response characteristic, as is typical for digital cinema cameras operating in raw mode. Any non-linearities in the camera’s response must be accounted for prior to calibration, either through factory calibration data or empirical measurement.

Second, the invention assumes that the virtual production stage comprises multiple types of LED panels (e.g., wall, ceiling, floor panels) that have been radiometrically aligned. This alignment ensures that a uniform pixel value (e.g., [1,1,1] for white) displayed across all panel types produces a spatially uniform sphere of illumination with consistent intensity and color balance from all directions. Radiometric alignment is typically achieved through per-panel-type brightness and color balancing procedures, which may involve measuring the output of representative panels with a spectroradiometer or colorimeter and adjusting drive levels accordingly. While perfect omnidirectional uniformity is rarely achievable in practice due to physical constraints of studio geometry, the invention is designed to function effectively with typical commercial LED volumes that provide adequate frontal hemisphere coverage.

Third, the invention assumes that the target lighting environment to be reproduced is available as a high dynamic range image-based lighting (HDR IBL) map, captured using standard panoramic HDR photography techniques. The HDR map should represent the full spherical illumination environment surrounding a central point in the real-world scene. Additionally, it is assumed that a reference photograph of a standardized color chart (such as an X-Rite ColorChecker) was captured at the same spatial location as the HDR map’s center of projection, under identical lighting conditions. This co-location ensures that the color chart’s appearance accurately reflects the diffuse illumination component of the HDR environment, which is critical for computing the appropriate color correction matrices.

The calibration procedure according to the invention requires the acquisition of exactly four calibration images using the principal photography camera that will be used for principal photography in the virtual production stage. These images are captured under controlled conditions to isolate specific aspects of the system’s color behavior. The first calibration image is used to compute the primary-based pre-correction matrix M and involves displaying pure red ([1,0,0]), pure green ([0,1,0]), and pure blue ([0,0,1]) patches on the in-frustum portion of the LED panels (i.e., the panels visible to the camera). During this capture, all out-of-frustum panels are turned off to prevent contamination from indirect illumination. The camera records the appearance of these patches, and the average RGB pixel values for each patch are extracted to form the columns of a 3×3 matrix denoted [SL]. This matrix represents the camera’s spectral sensitivity integrated against the LED emission spectra for each channel. The pre-correction matrix M is then computed as the inverse of [SL], ensuring that when M is applied to input pixel values before display, the camera observes the intended colors. Mathematically, this relationship is expressed as M = [SL]⁻¹.

The remaining three calibration images are used to characterize the system’s color rendition properties under individual LED channel illumination. For each of the red, green, and blue channels, a 1m × 1m square of LED panels is activated to full intensity while the other channels remain off. A standardized color chart is placed 1 meter away from the center of this illuminated square, oriented perpendicular to the panel surface. The camera is positioned such that its optical axis forms a 45-degree angle with the chart’s surface normal, a standard configuration that leverages the chart’s predominantly Lambertian reflectance properties. This setup is repeated for each of the three LED channels, yielding three separate images that capture how the color chart appears under monochromatic red, green, and blue illumination respectively.

These three images are processed to extract the RGB pixel values for each color patch on the chart, forming a set of 3×3 matrices denoted [SRL]ⱼ, where j indexes the individual color patches. Each column of [SRL]ⱼ corresponds to the camera-observed RGB values of patch j under illumination by one of the three LED channels. These matrices encode the complete spectral interaction between the camera, LEDs, and chart materials, and serve as the basis for predicting the chart’s appearance under arbitrary HDR IBL environments.

To compute the post-correction matrix Q, the invention leverages the principle of superposition for linear light transport. Any HDR IBL environment displayed on the LED volume can be decomposed into its diffuse illumination component, represented by an RGB vector w_avg that corresponds to the average color of the frontal hemisphere of lighting directions. In practice, w_avg can be derived directly from the white patch of the reference color chart photograph, scaled to account for the chart’s known reflectance (typically 90% for standard charts). When the HDR environment is displayed on the LED panels with pre-correction matrix M applied to out-of-frustum content, the predicted appearance of chart patch j is given by (1/β)[SRL]ⱼMw_avg, where β is a geometric scale factor that accounts for the difference in solid angle between the 1m × 1m calibration square and a full spherical illuminant. The value of β is determined analytically based on the calibration geometry; for the specified 1m distance and square size, β ≈ 0.311.

The post-correction matrix Q is then computed by solving a least-squares optimization problem that minimizes the squared error between the predicted chart appearances and the actual reference chart values pⱼ across all chart patches. Formally, Q is chosen to minimize Σⱼ ||Q(1/β)[SRL]ⱼMw_avg - pⱼ||². This optimization yields a 3×3 matrix that, when applied to the final recorded image, best matches the color rendition of the real-world scene. The solution can be computed using standard linear algebra techniques, such as singular value decomposition or normal equations, and may incorporate weighting factors to emphasize certain patches (e.g., skin tone equivalents) over others.

With Q determined, the invention computes the in-frustum pre-correction matrix N as N = MQ⁻¹. This matrix is applied exclusively to the content rendered on the in-frustum LED panels (i.e., the background visible to the camera). The rationale for this specific formulation is that when the final image is post-processed with Q, the background pixels—which were pre-corrected with N—will be transformed as QN = Q(MQ⁻¹) = M, restoring them to their originally intended appearance. Meanwhile, the foreground subjects, illuminated by out-of-frustum content pre-corrected with M, will be transformed by Q to achieve the desired color rendition. This decoupling elegantly resolves the conflict between background accuracy and foreground fidelity without requiring spatial masking or segmentation.

An additional refinement addresses the issue of panel albedo, wherein the LED panels themselves reflect a small percentage (typically 4–10%) of incident illumination from out-of-frustum panels onto the in-frustum background. This reflected light introduces an unwanted color cast that degrades background fidelity. To compensate, the invention employs a per-lighting-environment black level subtraction procedure. Specifically, with the out-of-frustum content active (pre-corrected with M) and the in-frustum background disabled, the camera records an image of the stage. The average RGB value b_camera of a representative region is extracted from this image. To convert this camera-observed value into the appropriate offset for the rendered content, it is scaled by the ratio of b_camera to w_camera, where w_camera is the camera-observed value of a [1,1,1] white patch (obtained by summing the columns of [SL]). The resulting offset b_camera/w_camera is then subtracted from the in-frustum content prior to display, effectively neutralizing the contamination from bounced light.

The complete calibration workflow is implemented in practice through modifications to the virtual production rendering pipeline. Within the game engine or rendering software (e.g., Unreal Engine), separate shader passes are configured for in-frustum and out-of-frustum content. The out-of-frustum pass applies matrix M to the HDR IBL data before sending it to the LED panels, while the in-frustum pass applies matrix N followed by the black level subtraction. During post-production, the recorded footage is processed with matrix Q to achieve the final color-corrected result. This implementation requires minimal computational overhead and integrates seamlessly with existing virtual production workflows.

Experimental validation of the invention demonstrates significant improvements in color fidelity across a diverse range of lighting environments, including broad-spectrum sources like daylight and incandescent, as well as challenging narrowband sources like sodium vapor lamps. Quantitative analysis shows average color errors below 4% relative to white patch intensity for both lit and displayed color charts, with the most dramatic improvements observed in skin tone reproduction and saturated material colors. The invention’s effectiveness is limited only by the fundamental spectral constraints of RGB LED systems, but within those bounds, it achieves near-optimal color rendition through purely software-based means.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, practical, and efficient solution to the color rendition challenges of RGB LED virtual production stages. By decoupling background and lighting color correction through a multi-matrix approach and addressing secondary effects like panel albedo, it enables cinematographers to achieve unprecedented levels of color fidelity without hardware modifications or complex post-production workflows.
