# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The development of solid-state lighting based on group III-nitride semiconductor materials has revolutionized the illumination industry, offering significant advantages in energy efficiency, longevity, and environmental sustainability compared to traditional incandescent and fluorescent light sources. Among these materials, gallium nitride (GaN) and its alloys, particularly indium gallium nitride (InGaN), have emerged as the cornerstone of modern light-emitting diodes (LEDs) operating in the visible spectrum, especially in the blue and green regions. Since the commercialization of high-brightness blue GaN LEDs in the 1990s, extensive research and engineering efforts have focused on extending device performance into the green spectral range—a domain historically plagued by the so-called “green gap,” where internal quantum efficiency drops significantly compared to blue counterparts. This efficiency deficit stems primarily from fundamental challenges in the epitaxial growth of high-indium-content InGaN quantum wells (QWs), which are essential for achieving longer emission wavelengths but are inherently prone to phase separation, compositional inhomogeneity, and interfacial degradation during standard metalorganic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) processes.

A critical factor influencing the cost and scalability of GaN-based LEDs is the choice of substrate material. Historically, sapphire (Al₂O₃) and silicon carbide (SiC) have been the dominant substrates for GaN epitaxy due to their reasonable lattice match and thermal stability. However, both suffer from significant drawbacks: sapphire is electrically insulating, necessitating complex lateral device architectures, while SiC is prohibitively expensive despite its superior thermal conductivity. These limitations have motivated the exploration of alternative substrates, with silicon (Si) emerging as a highly attractive candidate. Silicon offers compelling advantages, including low cost, large wafer diameters (up to 300 mm), excellent mechanical robustness, high thermal conductivity relative to sapphire, and compatibility with established silicon microfabrication infrastructure. Nevertheless, the heteroepitaxial growth of GaN on silicon is fraught with challenges arising from substantial mismatches in both lattice constant (~17%) and thermal expansion coefficient (~56%) between GaN and Si. These mismatches induce high tensile stress during cooling from growth temperatures, often leading to wafer bowing, cracking, and degraded crystal quality in the resulting GaN epilayers.

To mitigate these issues, sophisticated buffer layer architectures—typically involving graded AlGaN or AlN/AlGaN superlattices—have been developed to gradually accommodate the lattice and thermal strain, enabling the growth of crack-free GaN films on silicon substrates. While such advances have made Si-based GaN LEDs commercially viable, further optimization is required to enhance optical performance, particularly for green-emitting devices. The core of this challenge lies in the multi-quantum well (MQW) active region, where alternating layers of InGaN (wells) and GaN (barriers) confine charge carriers to generate light upon recombination. For green emission, the indium content in the InGaN wells must be increased to approximately 25–30%, which exacerbates several growth-related problems. High indium incorporation requires lower growth temperatures (typically 700–800°C) to prevent indium desorption, but subsequent barrier and p-type cladding layers are grown at significantly higher temperatures (often >1000°C) to ensure good crystal quality and dopant activation. This thermal ramp-up subjects the freshly deposited InGaN wells to conditions that promote indium out-diffusion, surface decomposition, and roughening, thereby degrading the well/barrier interface abruptness and introducing non-radiative recombination centers.

Conventional MOCVD growth sequences for InGaN/GaN MQWs typically involve depositing the InGaN well at a low temperature, followed immediately by a temperature increase to the barrier growth temperature, during which a thin GaN layer may be deposited either isothermally or during the ramp. However, without adequate protection, the InGaN surface is exposed to high ammonia partial pressures and elevated temperatures before the barrier fully encapsulates it, leading to irreversible damage. Prior art has attempted to address this through various strategies, such as pulsed growth, in-situ annealing, or the use of surfactants, but these often introduce complexity, reduce throughput, or fail to provide sufficient protection for high-indium-content wells. Moreover, the precise role of an intermediate “cap” layer—deposited at the well growth temperature immediately after the InGaN well and before the temperature ramp—has not been systematically investigated or optimized in the context of Si-based green LEDs. While some studies have incidentally included thin GaN caps, their thickness has been treated as a fixed parameter rather than a tunable design variable, and the correlation between cap thickness and the resulting structural, optical, and interfacial properties of the MQW has remained unclear.

This gap in understanding is particularly acute for devices grown on silicon, where the additional stress from the substrate may influence the thermal stability of the InGaN wells during subsequent processing. Furthermore, existing literature lacks a comprehensive analysis linking cap layer thickness to measurable outcomes such as X-ray diffraction satellite peak clarity, fluorescence microscopy uniformity, and photoluminescence spectral characteristics—all of which are direct indicators of MQW quality. Without such a systematic study, manufacturers are left without clear design rules for optimizing the cap layer in production environments, leading to suboptimal device performance and yield. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for a method and structure that deliberately engineer the GaN cap layer thickness as a critical parameter to preserve the integrity of high-indium InGaN quantum wells during the high-temperature steps of LED fabrication on silicon substrates, thereby enabling high-efficiency, cost-effective green LEDs suitable for general lighting applications.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel and non-obvious method for fabricating high-efficiency green light-emitting diodes (LEDs) based on indium gallium nitride/gallium nitride (InGaN/GaN) multi-quantum well (MQW) active regions grown on silicon (111) substrates, wherein the key innovation resides in the deliberate engineering of a GaN cap layer thickness deposited immediately after each InGaN quantum well and prior to the temperature ramp required for subsequent barrier and p-type layer growth. Specifically, the invention discloses that by increasing the thickness of this same-temperature GaN cap layer from conventional sub-10 Å dimensions to at least 15 Å—and preferably to 30 Å or greater—the thermal decomposition and indium segregation of the underlying InGaN quantum well during the high-temperature barrier growth phase are substantially mitigated. This results in a quantum well with significantly improved compositional homogeneity, sharper well/barrier interfaces, enhanced photoluminescence intensity, reduced spectral linewidth, and a red-shifted emission wavelength consistent with higher and more uniform indium incorporation.

The invention overcomes a longstanding technical problem in the field: the inherent instability of high-indium-content InGaN layers when exposed to the elevated temperatures and ammonia-rich environment necessary for growing high-quality GaN barriers and p-GaN cladding layers. Traditional growth protocols either omit this cap layer entirely or employ a nominal thickness insufficient to provide a protective barrier against indium desorption and surface roughening. The present invention recognizes that the cap layer functions not merely as a transitional layer but as a critical thermal and chemical shield, and that its efficacy is directly proportional to its thickness within a specific operational range. Through systematic experimentation, the inventors have determined that a cap thickness of less than 10 Å (e.g., 8 Å) fails to prevent well degradation, manifesting as indistinct X-ray diffraction satellite peaks, non-uniform fluorescence microscopy images with bright defect spots, and broad, low-intensity photoluminescence spectra. In contrast, cap thicknesses of 15 Å and 30 Å progressively improve all measured quality metrics, with 30 Å yielding the most homogeneous indium distribution, the sharpest interfacial definition, and the highest optical output.

The method according to the invention comprises the following steps: providing a silicon (111) substrate; cleaning the substrate to remove organic contaminants and native oxide; performing in-situ high-temperature hydrogen bake to further purify the surface; depositing a thin aluminum film at approximately 800°C to prevent silicon melt-back and nitridation; growing a stress-relief buffer stack consisting of AlN and compositionally graded AlGaN layers; depositing a thick n-type GaN layer; forming an InGaN strain pre-relief layer; and then sequentially growing the MQW active region. Each period of the MQW includes an InGaN quantum well grown at a first temperature T₁ (typically 700–800°C), followed immediately by a GaN cap layer also grown at T₁, wherein the cap layer thickness is controlled to be at least 15 Å, and more preferably 30 Å. Only after the cap layer is fully deposited is the reactor temperature increased to a second temperature T₂ (typically T₁ + 100–150°C, e.g., 900–950°C) for the growth of the GaN barrier layer. This sequence is repeated for the desired number of MQW periods, after which a p-type GaN cladding layer is deposited.

The resulting LED structure exhibits superior optical and structural characteristics compared to prior art devices. X-ray diffraction rocking curves display clear, high-order satellite peaks indicative of a well-defined periodic MQW structure with abrupt interfaces. Fluorescence microscopy reveals spatially uniform emission without localized bright spots, confirming homogeneous indium distribution. Photoluminescence measurements show a significant increase in peak intensity, a narrowing of the full width at half maximum (FWHM), and a red shift in emission wavelength—collectively demonstrating enhanced radiative recombination efficiency and consistent high-indium incorporation. These improvements directly translate to higher external quantum efficiency in fabricated LED devices, making the invention particularly valuable for closing the green gap in solid-state lighting.

Thus, the invention provides a simple yet highly effective process modification that leverages a controllable growth parameter—the GaN cap thickness—to solve a fundamental materials challenge in nitride semiconductor optoelectronics. By establishing a quantitative relationship between cap thickness and MQW quality, the invention enables predictable, high-yield manufacturing of green LEDs on low-cost silicon substrates, thereby advancing the commercial viability of energy-efficient general lighting solutions.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Overview

The present invention is directed to a method for fabricating high-performance green light-emitting diodes (LEDs) utilizing an InGaN/GaN multi-quantum well (MQW) active region grown on a silicon (111) substrate, wherein the critical innovation lies in the strategic control of a GaN cap layer thickness deposited immediately after each InGaN quantum well and before the temperature ramp to the GaN barrier growth temperature. This cap layer, grown at the same temperature as the InGaN well, serves as a protective barrier that prevents the thermal decomposition and indium segregation of the quantum well during subsequent high-temperature processing steps. The invention establishes that a minimum cap thickness of 15 Å is necessary to achieve significant improvements in MQW quality, with optimal results obtained at 30 Å or greater, as evidenced by structural, optical, and spectroscopic characterization techniques.

The method begins with the preparation of a silicon (111) substrate, which is subjected to a rigorous cleaning protocol to remove organic residues and the native silicon dioxide layer. This typically involves immersion in a sulfuric acid/hydrogen peroxide mixture (commonly known as piranha solution) followed by a dilute hydrofluoric acid etch. The cleaned substrate is then loaded into a metalorganic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) reactor, where it undergoes an in-situ high-temperature anneal in a hydrogen atmosphere to further desorb surface contaminants and reconstruct the silicon surface. To prevent the catastrophic reaction between molten gallium and silicon—which can cause substrate pitting and alloying—and to inhibit the formation of a silicon nitride layer due to ammonia exposure, a thin aluminum film is deposited at an intermediate temperature of approximately 800°C using trimethylaluminum (TMAl) as the precursor. This aluminum interlayer acts as a diffusion barrier and nucleation template for the subsequent nitride layers.

Following the aluminum interlayer deposition, a stress-relief buffer structure is grown to accommodate the large lattice and thermal expansion coefficient mismatches between silicon and GaN. This buffer typically consists of a thin AlN layer followed by a compositionally graded AlₓGa₁₋ₓN layer, where the aluminum fraction x is gradually reduced from near 1 to 0 over a thickness of several hundred nanometers. This graded layer effectively transitions the crystal lattice from that of silicon to that of GaN while minimizing the accumulation of dislocations and preventing crack formation in the overlying GaN film. On top of this buffer, a thick n-type GaN layer (approximately 3 micrometers) is deposited using silane (SiH₄) as the n-type dopant source. This n-GaN layer serves as the foundation for the active region and provides the primary conduction path for electrons in the final device.

Prior to the MQW growth, an InGaN strain pre-relief layer is deposited. This layer, typically 200 nanometers thick, incorporates a moderate amount of indium to partially compensate for the compressive strain that would otherwise build up in the MQW due to the underlying GaN template. This pre-relief layer helps to maintain the structural integrity of the MQW during growth and subsequent cooling. The MQW active region itself comprises multiple periods (typically 4 to 8, though 6 is used in the exemplary embodiment) of alternating InGaN quantum wells and GaN barriers. Each period is grown in a specific sequence designed to maximize well integrity: first, the InGaN well is deposited at a low temperature T₁ (ranging from 700°C to 800°C, depending on the target indium content and emission wavelength); immediately thereafter, without changing the reactor temperature, a GaN cap layer is grown to a precisely controlled thickness of at least 15 Å, and preferably 30 Å; only after the cap layer is complete is the reactor temperature ramped to a higher temperature T₂ (typically T₁ + 100°C to 150°C, e.g., 900°C to 950°C) for the deposition of the GaN barrier layer. This sequence ensures that the sensitive InGaN surface is never exposed to high temperatures or high ammonia flows without a protective GaN overcoat.

The cap layer thickness is a critical parameter that directly governs the degree of protection afforded to the quantum well. Experimental data demonstrate that a cap thickness of 8 Å is insufficient to prevent indium desorption and surface roughening during the temperature ramp, leading to heterogeneous indium distribution and poor interfacial quality. In contrast, cap thicknesses of 15 Å and 30 Å provide progressively better shielding, with 30 Å yielding the most uniform and abrupt MQW structure. After the MQW stack is complete, a p-type GaN cladding layer (approximately 130 nanometers thick) is deposited using bis-cyclopentadienyl magnesium (Cp₂Mg) as the magnesium dopant source, completing the basic LED epitaxial structure.

The resulting epitaxial wafers are characterized using a suite of analytical techniques to validate the efficacy of the cap layer strategy. Double-crystal X-ray diffraction (DCXRD) rocking curve measurements reveal the presence of clear satellite peaks for samples with 15 Å and 30 Å cap layers, indicating a well-defined periodic MQW structure with sharp interfaces. The absence of such peaks in the 8 Å sample confirms the degradation of the quantum well periodicity. Fluorescence microscopy under appropriate excitation and filtering conditions shows a dramatic improvement in emission uniformity with increasing cap thickness, with the 30 Å sample exhibiting no localized bright spots that would indicate indium-rich clusters or defects. Photoluminescence spectroscopy demonstrates a concurrent increase in emission intensity, a red shift in peak wavelength (from 500 nm to 530 nm), and a narrowing of the spectral linewidth (reduced FWHM), all of which are hallmarks of improved quantum well quality and higher internal quantum efficiency.

This invention thus provides a straightforward, manufacturable solution to a persistent problem in green LED technology. By simply adjusting the thickness of a layer that is already commonly included in MQW growth sequences—but previously overlooked as a critical parameter—the invention achieves substantial gains in device performance without requiring exotic materials, complex process modifications, or additional fabrication steps. The method is fully compatible with standard MOCVD tools and processes, making it readily adoptable by LED manufacturers seeking to improve yields and efficiencies in their green LED product lines, particularly those targeting cost-sensitive general lighting markets with silicon-based substrates.

## EXAMPLE

An exemplary embodiment of the present invention is now described in detail to illustrate the method and resulting structure, though it is understood that the invention is not limited to this specific implementation. A silicon (111) substrate, nominally 2 inches in diameter, was first cleaned by immersion in a heated mixture of sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in a 3:1 volume ratio for 15 minutes to remove organic contaminants. The substrate was then rinsed thoroughly with deionized water and etched in a 5% aqueous hydrofluoric acid (HF) solution for 30 seconds to strip the native silicon dioxide layer. After a final rinse and nitrogen blow-dry, the substrate was loaded into a Thomas Swan 6 × 2″ close-coupled showerhead metalorganic chemical vapor deposition (MOCVD) reactor.

Inside the reactor, the substrate was subjected to an in-situ anneal at 1100°C for 30 minutes under a flowing hydrogen (H₂) atmosphere to further desorb surface impurities and reconstruct the silicon surface. Subsequently, trimethylaluminum (TMAl) was introduced into the reactor at a temperature of 800°C for a duration of 2 minutes to form a thin aluminum film on the silicon surface. This aluminum interlayer served to prevent the reaction between molten gallium and silicon during subsequent GaN growth and to inhibit the formation of silicon nitride from the reaction of silicon with ammonia (NH₃).

Following the aluminum interlayer deposition, a stress-relief buffer structure was grown. First, a 0.3-micrometer-thick aluminum nitride (AlN) layer was deposited at 1100°C using TMAl and NH₃ as precursors. Then, a compositionally graded AlₓGa₁₋ₓN layer was grown by gradually reducing the TMAl flow rate while maintaining a constant trimethylgallium (TMGa) flow, resulting in a 3-micrometer-thick layer where the aluminum mole fraction x decreased from 1.0 to 0 over the growth period. This graded buffer effectively accommodated the lattice and thermal expansion mismatches between silicon and GaN, enabling the growth of a crack-free GaN film.

On top of the buffer, a 3-micrometer-thick n-type GaN layer was deposited at 1050°C using TMGa and NH₃ as the group III and V precursors, respectively, with silane (SiH₄) introduced as the n-type dopant. The carrier concentration of this layer was approximately 1×10¹⁸ cm⁻³. Next, a 200-nanometer-thick InGaN strain pre-relief layer was grown at 800°C using TMGa, trimethylindium (TMIn), and NH₃, with an indium composition of approximately 5% to partially compensate for the compressive strain in the subsequent MQW.

The multi-quantum well (MQW) active region was then grown, consisting of six identical periods. Each period was fabricated according to the following sequence: (1) an InGaN quantum well was deposited at 750°C for 60 seconds using TMIn, TMGa, and NH₃, resulting in a well thickness of approximately 30 Å and an indium composition sufficient to target green emission; (2) immediately after the well deposition, without changing the reactor temperature, a GaN cap layer was grown at 750°C using TMGa and NH₃, with the growth time adjusted to achieve a precise thickness of either 8 Å, 15 Å, or 30 Å—these three variations defining Sample A, Sample B, and Sample C, respectively; (3) after the cap layer was complete, the reactor temperature was ramped to 870°C over a period of 5 minutes; (4) at 870°C, a 120-Å-thick GaN barrier layer was deposited using TMGa and NH₃. This sequence was repeated six times to form the complete MQW stack.

Finally, a 130-nanometer-thick p-type GaN cladding layer was grown at 950°C using TMGa, NH₃, and bis-cyclopentadienyl magnesium (Cp₂Mg) as the magnesium dopant source. The resulting epitaxial structure was removed from the reactor and subjected to comprehensive characterization.

Double-crystal X-ray diffraction (DCXRD) omega/2theta rocking scans were performed with a scan range of ±5000 arcseconds centered on the GaN (0002) reflection. For Sample A (8 Å cap), no discernible satellite peaks were observed on the low-angle side of the main GaN peak, indicating a lack of periodic structure and severe degradation of the quantum wells. In contrast, Samples B (15 Å) and C (30 Å) exhibited clear first- through fourth-order satellite peaks, confirming the formation of a well-defined MQW with abrupt interfaces. Notably, the third- and fourth-order peaks for Sample B were broader than those for Sample C, suggesting slightly inferior interface quality in Sample B.

Fluorescence microscopy was conducted using an excitation wavelength of 405 nm, a dichroic mirror with a cutoff at 430 nm, and a barrier filter transmitting above 450 nm. Sample A displayed a highly non-uniform image with numerous extra-bright spots, indicative of indium segregation and localized defect formation. Sample B showed improved uniformity with only minor local variations, while Sample C exhibited a remarkably homogeneous emission profile with no detectable bright spots, confirming the effectiveness of the thicker cap layer in preserving indium homogeneity.

Photoluminescence (PL) measurements were carried out at room temperature using a helium-cadmium (He-Cd) laser emitting at 325 nm as the excitation source. All samples emitted in the green spectrum, but with distinct differences: Sample A peaked at 500 nm with low intensity and a broad full width at half maximum (FWHM) of approximately 65 nm; Sample B peaked at 515 nm with moderate intensity and an FWHM of 55 nm; Sample C peaked at 530 nm with high intensity and a narrow FWHM of 45 nm. The progressive red shift and linewidth narrowing with increasing cap thickness directly correlate with higher and more uniform indium incorporation in the quantum wells, while the intensity increase reflects reduced non-radiative recombination due to fewer defects.

These results conclusively demonstrate that the GaN cap layer thickness is a decisive factor in determining the quality of InGaN/GaN MQWs grown on silicon substrates for green LEDs. By implementing a cap thickness of at least 15 Å—and optimally 30 Å—the present invention enables the fabrication of high-efficiency green LEDs that overcome the limitations of prior art methods, thereby providing a practical pathway to cost-effective, high-performance solid-state lighting.
