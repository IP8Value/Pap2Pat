## BACKGROUND

The field of protonic ceramic energy devices (PCEDs) has witnessed significant scientific and technological advancements over the past two decades, particularly in the development of intermediate-temperature (300–600 °C) electrochemical systems that leverage proton-conducting ceramics as core functional components. These devices include protonic ceramic fuel cells (PCFCs), protonic ceramic electrolysis cells (PCECs), reversible protonic ceramic electrochemical cells, protonic ceramic membrane reactors, hydrogen-permeable membranes, water-permeable membranes, and solid-state ammonia synthesis cells. The operational advantages of such systems—such as reduced activation losses, enhanced electrode kinetics, improved thermodynamic efficiency, and compatibility with hydrocarbon fuels without external reformers—have positioned protonic ceramics as promising candidates for next-generation clean energy conversion and chemical synthesis technologies. However, despite these compelling performance metrics demonstrated at the laboratory scale, the transition of PCEDs from benchtop prototypes to commercially viable, scalable products has been severely hindered by persistent limitations in conventional ceramic manufacturing methodologies.

Traditional fabrication techniques for protonic ceramic components, including dry pressing, screen printing, tape casting, and paste extrusion, are inherently constrained by geometric simplicity, dimensional scalability, and microstructural control. Dry pressing and screen printing typically yield small planar cells with active areas not exceeding 1 cm², which are inadequate for practical power or chemical output. While tape casting has enabled the production of larger-area planar cells (up to ~16 cm²), it suffers from interfacial delamination during co-firing due to differential shrinkage between layers and remains restricted to flat geometries. Paste extrusion has successfully produced tubular PCFCs and membrane reactors with active areas approaching 20 cm², yet it is fundamentally incapable of generating complex internal architectures—such as closed-end tubes, lobed surfaces, or embedded microchannels—that are essential for maximizing surface-area-to-volume ratios and enhancing mass transport efficiency. Moreover, all these methods rely on batch processing, slow thermal cycles, and post-fabrication machining, which collectively impede rapid prototyping, customization, and cost-effective mass production.

Concurrently, additive manufacturing (AM), commonly known as 3D printing, has emerged as a transformative paradigm in advanced materials engineering, offering unprecedented design freedom, part consolidation, and on-demand fabrication capabilities. AM processes build objects layer-by-layer from digital models, enabling the direct realization of intricate external and internal geometries that are unattainable via subtractive or formative methods. While AM has been widely adopted in polymer and metal industries—thanks to well-established photopolymerization, powder bed fusion, and directed energy deposition techniques—the 3D printing of ceramics remains fraught with challenges. Ceramics exhibit high melting points, intrinsic brittleness, and significant sintering shrinkage (often 15–35%), which complicate dimensional accuracy, structural integrity, and defect-free densification. Most ceramic AM approaches require extensive use of organic binders and solvents to render powders printable, necessitating lengthy debinding schedules and high-temperature sintering that can induce warping, cracking, or phase decomposition. Furthermore, achieving fine feature resolution (<100 µm) and smooth surface finishes in green ceramic parts is difficult with extrusion-based methods due to filament spreading, poor interlayer adhesion, and limited nozzle precision.

Recent efforts to adapt AM to protonic ceramics have been sparse and largely unsuccessful in addressing the full spectrum of manufacturing requirements. Existing reports focus primarily on stereolithography or direct ink writing of simple geometries, followed by conventional furnace sintering, thereby inheriting the same limitations of slow processing, poor microstructural homogeneity, and inability to integrate multi-material or multi-functional features in a single build. Crucially absent from the prior art is a unified, integrated manufacturing platform that combines the geometric versatility of 3D printing with the precision, speed, and in-situ control of laser-based processing to directly fabricate high-performance protonic ceramic components—either as green bodies for subsequent treatment or as fully dense, functional devices ready for deployment. Such a system would need to simultaneously manage paste rheology, layer deposition fidelity, rapid drying to prevent deformation, precise shaping via subtractive laser machining, and controlled densification through localized thermal energy—all while preserving the delicate crystal chemistry required for proton conduction.

In light of these unmet needs, there exists a critical technological gap: a robust, scalable, and versatile manufacturing method capable of producing protonic ceramic parts with arbitrary complexity, tailored microstructures, phase-pure crystallinity, and electrochemical functionality, without reliance on slow, energy-intensive, and geometry-limited conventional processing. The present invention addresses this gap through the development of a novel Laser-Assisted 3D Printing (L3DP) platform that synergistically integrates digital microextrusion with multi-modal laser processing—including CO₂ laser drying and sintering, and picosecond laser cutting and polishing—to enable the rapid, precise, and customizable fabrication of protonic ceramic energy devices with performance characteristics suitable for real-world applications.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive and integrated Laser-Assisted 3D Printing (L3DP) system and method for the fabrication of protonic ceramic components with complex three-dimensional geometries, controlled microstructures, and desired electrochemical functionalities, suitable for use in intermediate-temperature protonic ceramic energy devices (PCEDs). The L3DP platform uniquely combines digital microextrusion-based additive manufacturing with in-situ laser processing modalities—including rapid drying, reactive sintering, precision cutting, and surface polishing—thereby overcoming the longstanding limitations of conventional ceramic fabrication techniques and existing additive manufacturing approaches.

In one aspect, the invention comprises a printable ceramic paste formulation specifically engineered for microextrusion-based 3D printing of protonic ceramics. The paste includes a proton-conducting ceramic precursor powder—such as BaZr₀.₈Y₀.₂O₃₋δ (BZY20), BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ (BCZYYb), or related perovskite compositions—combined with a metallic oxide (e.g., NiO) for composite electrodes, dispersed in an aqueous medium containing a controlled amount of hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) binder and a dispersant. The paste exhibits shear-thinning rheology with a steady-state viscosity in the range of approximately 700 poise, which enables both excellent shape retention of printed filaments and strong interlayer bonding during layer-by-layer deposition, thereby preventing delamination and gravitational slumping.

In another aspect, the invention provides an L3DP apparatus comprising a high-precision X-Y-Z motion stage, one or more microextruders equipped with fine-diameter nozzles (e.g., 0.5 mm), a continuous-wave CO₂ laser (wavelength 10.6 µm) for rapid thermal processing, a picosecond-pulsed YAG laser (wavelength 1064 nm) for high-resolution subtractive machining, and a galvanometric scanner for high-speed laser beam steering. This integrated hardware configuration allows for synchronized execution of additive and subtractive operations within a single manufacturing workflow.

A key innovation of the L3DP method is the implementation of in-situ CO₂ laser drying immediately after each layer deposition. By defocusing the laser beam to a spot size of approximately 1 mm and operating at a power of about 10 W and scan speed of 15 mm/s, the solvent in the freshly printed wet layer is rapidly evaporated without inducing thermal stress, cracking, or premature chemical reactions. This step dramatically accelerates the build process, eliminates shape deformation due to paste fluidity, and enhances interlayer cohesion by promoting immediate physical consolidation.

Furthermore, the invention introduces rapid laser reactive sintering (RLRS) as a direct densification technique for protonic ceramic layers. Using a line-focused CO₂ laser beam (spot size ~8 mm) operated at 20 W power and 0.1 mm/s scan speed, green ceramic layers deposited on substrates such as fused silica are converted in minutes into fully dense, phase-pure perovskite films with grain sizes of 1–2 µm and negligible porosity. This RLRS process achieves material properties comparable to those obtained via conventional solid-state reactive sintering (SSRS) in box furnaces but with orders-of-magnitude reduction in processing time and spatially localized energy input, enabling selective densification and compatibility with temperature-sensitive substrates.

The L3DP system also incorporates picosecond laser machining for precision shaping and surface refinement. During or after layer deposition, the ps-laser—focused to a 18-µm spot with pulse energy of 150 µJ at 10 kHz repetition rate—can cut microchannels as narrow as 300 µm with depths up to 150 µm per pass, enabling the fabrication of high surface-area-to-volume ratio membrane reactors. Additionally, by reducing the pulse energy to 114.4 µJ and increasing the scan speed to 50 mm/s, the same laser polishes the staged surface topography inherent to extrusion-based printing, yielding atomically smooth surfaces essential for uniform thin-film coating in multi-layer device architectures.

The invention further encompasses a suite of fabricated protonic ceramic components demonstrating the versatility of the L3DP method. These include simple geometries such as pellets, cylinders, and cones; complex tubular structures with closed ends and external/internal lobes; microchannel-embedded membranes for enhanced gas permeation; and fully integrated electrochemical half-cells comprising porous NiO-BZY20 anodes and dense BZY20 electrolyte layers. Both indirect fabrication (L3DP of green bodies followed by dip-coating and furnace co-firing) and direct fabrication (in-situ laser sintering of multi-layer stacks into functional devices) are enabled.

Critically, the L3DP-derived protonic ceramic electrolytes exhibit high proton conductivity—measured at 6.95 × 10⁻³ S/cm at 600 °C in wet 5% H₂—confirming that the laser processing preserves the requisite crystal structure and defect chemistry for proton transport. The method supports a wide range of proton-conducting compositions, including zirconate-, cerate-, and ferrite-based perovskites, and is extensible to dual-phase hydrogen-permeable membranes and triple-conducting oxygen/water separation membranes.

In summary, the present invention establishes a new paradigm in ceramic manufacturing by merging the design freedom of 3D printing with the precision and speed of laser processing, thereby enabling the rapid, customizable, and scalable production of high-performance protonic ceramic energy devices with geometries and functionalities previously unattainable by any known method.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a Laser-Assisted 3D Printing (L3DP) system and method for the fabrication of protonic ceramic components with complex geometries, controlled microstructures, and electrochemical functionality suitable for intermediate-temperature energy and chemical conversion devices. The L3DP platform integrates digital microextrusion-based additive manufacturing with multiple in-situ laser processing techniques—including rapid drying, reactive sintering, precision cutting, and surface polishing—into a unified, automated workflow. This integration overcomes the fundamental limitations of traditional ceramic processing and conventional additive manufacturing by enabling rapid, precise, and customizable fabrication of both green and fully sintered protonic ceramic parts without reliance on slow thermal cycles or post-build machining.

The core of the L3DP system is a high-precision motion platform comprising motorized X-Y stages with nanometer-scale positioning accuracy (e.g., 100 nm resolution) and a wide velocity range (from 100 nm/s to 5 m/s), coupled with a Z-axis actuator for vertical layer stacking. Mounted on this platform are one or more microextrusion units, such as the preeflow eco-PEN 300 dispensing system, which utilize compressed air or mechanical pistons to drive ceramic pastes through fine-gauge nozzles (typically 0.2–1.0 mm inner diameter). The distance between the nozzle tip and the build platform (or previous layer) is precisely controlled to match the desired wet layer thickness, usually in the range of 300–500 µm. The extrusion flow rate and stage velocity are coordinated according to the relationship d = Q/(v·h), where d is the deposited filament width, Q is the volumetric flow rate, v is the stage speed, and h is the layer height, ensuring consistent feature dimensions across the build area.

The printable pastes used in the L3DP process are specially formulated to balance printability, shape fidelity, and sinterability. A typical composition includes 40–60 wt% proton-conducting ceramic precursor powder—such as BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ (BCZYYb), BaZr₀.₈Y₀.₂O₃₋δ (BZY20), or BaCe₀.₈₅Fe₀.₁₅O₃₋δ (BCF)—optionally blended with 40–60 wt% NiO for anode supports, ball-milled to submicron particle size. This powder is mixed with deionized water (typically at a 1:1 powder-to-water weight ratio to minimize drying cracks), 0.5–1.0 wt% polymeric dispersant (e.g., Darvan 821A), and 1–3 wt% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) binder. The resulting paste exhibits pronounced shear-thinning behavior, with viscosity decreasing sharply at low shear rates (<20 s⁻¹) and plateauing at a steady-state value that is optimized for the target geometry: ~100 P for thin films requiring filament merging, ~700 P for balanced 3D structures, and ~1700 P for high-aspect-ratio features needing maximum shape retention. This rheological tuning ensures that filaments maintain their cross-section during deposition while achieving sufficient interlayer diffusion for strong bonding.

Immediately following each layer deposition, the L3DP system activates a continuous-wave CO₂ laser (wavelength 10.64 µm, e.g., Synrad firestar v20) to perform rapid in-situ drying. The laser beam is defocused using a plano-convex lens to increase the spot diameter to approximately 1 mm, reducing the power density to avoid localized boiling or thermal shock. Operating at 8–12 W power and 10–20 mm/s scan speed—preferably guided by a galvanometric scanner for high-speed rastering—the laser uniformly evaporates the aqueous solvent within seconds, locking the layer geometry before gravitational slumping or capillary-driven deformation can occur. This step is critical for building tall or thin-walled structures, as it eliminates the hours-long ambient drying delays that plague conventional direct ink writing.

For applications requiring fully dense, functional components directly from the printer, the L3DP system employs Rapid Laser Reactive Sintering (RLRS). In this mode, a higher-power CO₂ laser (e.g., 20–90 W) is focused—often through a cylindrical lens to create a line-shaped irradiation zone (~8 mm length)—onto the dried green layer. The laser parameters (power, scan speed, defocus distance, and hatch spacing) are meticulously calibrated to achieve complete densification and perovskite phase formation in a single pass. For example, a BCZYYb electrolyte layer printed on fused silica can be sintered at 20 W and 0.1 mm/s to yield a 400-µm-thick, crack-free film with >95% relative density and pure orthorhombic perovskite structure, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction. The localized nature of laser heating minimizes thermal gradients, suppresses exaggerated grain growth, and enables co-sintering of dissimilar layers (e.g., anode and electrolyte) without delamination, even when their shrinkage behaviors differ significantly.

Complementing the additive capabilities, the L3DP system integrates a picosecond-pulsed Nd:YAG laser (wavelength 1064 nm, pulse duration <30 ps, e.g., Attodyne Olive-1064-10) for high-precision subtractive manufacturing. Equipped with interchangeable objectives (e.g., 5×, NA=0.13), the ps-laser can be focused to spot sizes as small as 10–20 µm. In cutting mode, operating at 10–50 kHz repetition rate, 100–200 µJ/pulse energy, and 1–10 mm/s scan speed, the laser ablates green ceramic material with minimal heat-affected zone, enabling the creation of microfeatures such as 300-µm-wide, 1-mm-deep triangular microchannels in membrane reactors or intricate lobe patterns on tubular surfaces. The layer-by-layer cutting strategy—where excess material is removed after each deposition—allows the fabrication of internal cavities and undercuts impossible with extrusion alone.

In polishing mode, the ps-laser operates at lower fluence (e.g., 1 kHz, 114 µJ/pulse, 50 mm/s) to gently ablate surface asperities without penetrating the bulk. This process effectively erases the "stair-stepping" artifacts inherent to layerwise manufacturing, producing optically smooth surfaces (Ra < 1 µm) essential for subsequent thin-film deposition steps, such as dip-coating of electrolyte precursors. SEM analysis confirms that laser-polished anode tubes exhibit uniform curvature without valleys, leading to homogeneous electrolyte thickness after co-firing—a critical factor for current distribution in operating fuel cells.

The L3DP workflow supports both indirect and direct fabrication pathways. In the indirect route, green parts are 3D printed with optional laser drying and machining, then subjected to conventional post-treatments: prefiring at 1000–1100 °C to remove organics, dip-coating with electrolyte slurry, and co-firing at 1400–1550 °C for 12–18 hours. This yields robust tubular half-cells with 15-µm-thick dense electrolytes bonded to 27% porous Ni-BZY20 anodes, exhibiting no delamination after redox cycling. In the direct route, RLRS sinters multi-layer stacks in minutes: for instance, a 300-µm anode layer and 60-µm electrolyte layer co-sintered at 90 W and 0.1 mm/s produce a >10 cm² active area half-cell with fully dense electrolyte and narrow grain size distribution (~2 µm).

The versatility of L3DP is demonstrated across a spectrum of protonic ceramic compositions and device architectures. Anode supports of 40 wt% BZY20 + 60 wt% NiO are printed as pellets, cylinders, cones, rings, and tubes—with or without closed ends and external lobes—achieving active areas up to 47 cm². Triple-conducting BCFZY0.1 membranes are structured with aligned microchannels for enhanced permeation. Dual-phase hydrogen-permeable membranes (e.g., BCF-BFC composites) are formed into complex geometries. All sintered components exhibit phase-pure perovskite structures and high relative densities (>92%), with electrolyte conductivities matching or exceeding literature values for furnace-processed counterparts.

Thus, the L3DP system represents a paradigm shift in ceramic manufacturing, merging the geometric freedom of 3D printing with the speed, precision, and control of laser processing to enable the rapid, customizable, and scalable production of high-performance protonic ceramic energy devices.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1—Integrated Multi-Laser 3D Manufacturing System

An integrated Laser-Assisted 3D Printing (L3DP) system was constructed to enable the simultaneous execution of additive and subtractive manufacturing operations on protonic ceramic materials. The system comprised a high-precision Aerotech ABL1500 X-Y motion stage with 100 nm resolution and velocity control from 0.1 µm/s to 5 m/s, mounted on a Newport RV-160CC Z-axis actuator with 50 nm repeatability. Two ViscoTec preeflow eco-PEN 300 microextruders were installed, each connected to a compressed air regulator (0–6 bar) and fitted with stainless steel nozzles of 0.5 mm inner diameter. A Synrad firestar v20 CO₂ laser (10.64 µm wavelength, 20 W maximum power) was mounted on a fixed Z-arm above the build platform, aligned coaxially with the extrusion axis. A separate Attodyne Olive-1064-10 picosecond YAG laser (1064 nm, 10 W average power, <30 ps pulse duration) was integrated via a beam combiner and directed through an intelliSCAN® III 14 galvanometric scanner equipped with an f-theta lens (f=160 mm) and interchangeable microscope objectives (including a 5×, NA=0.13 lens for 18 µm spot size). The entire system was enclosed in a Class I laser safety cabinet with HEPA filtration and humidity control (40% RH). Custom software written in Python coordinated G-code generation from CAD models (exported as STL files), synchronized extrusion parameters (flow rate, pressure), stage motion, and laser firing sequences. Real-time monitoring was provided by a Keyence VH-Z500R digital microscope with 5000× magnification. This integrated platform allowed seamless transitions between printing, drying, sintering, cutting, and polishing operations within a single unattended build cycle, demonstrating the feasibility of fully automated, multi-modal ceramic manufacturing.

### Example 2—CO2 Laser Sintering of Sol-Gel Deposition

A sol-gel derived BaZr₀.₈Y₀.₂O₃₋δ (BZY20) precursor film was deposited onto a polished alumina substrate via spin-coating at 3000 rpm for 30 seconds, yielding a 500 nm thick wet layer. The coated substrate was mounted on the L3DP stage and subjected to CO₂ laser sintering using the firestar v20 laser. The laser beam was collimated and passed through a cylindrical lens (f=50 mm) to generate a 10 mm × 1 mm line focus. Sintering was performed in ambient air at a laser power of 15 W and a scan speed of 0.5 mm/s, with a single pass coverage. Post-processing XRD analysis (Rigaku Ultima IV, Cu-Kα, 15–85° 2θ) confirmed the formation of a pure cubic perovskite phase with no secondary peaks. Scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi S4800) revealed a dense, pinhole-free morphology with an average grain size of 80 nm, significantly finer than furnace-sintered counterparts (typically >500 nm at 1400 °C). Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping showed homogeneous distribution of Ba, Zr, and Y elements. This example demonstrates that CO₂ laser sintering can crystallize sol-gel films at dramatically lower effective temperatures and shorter durations than conventional methods, preserving nano-scale microstructures beneficial for thin-film electrolyte applications.

### Example 3—CO2 Laser Melting of Ceramic Paste

A printable paste composed of 50 wt% BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃₋δ (BCZYYb) powder, 15 wt% deionized water, 0.7 wt% Darvan 821A dispersant, and 2 wt% HPMC binder was loaded into a syringe and extruded through a 0.5 mm nozzle at 0.3 mL/min onto a fused silica substrate, forming a 400 µm thick rectangular pad (20 mm × 20 mm). Immediately after deposition, the pad was exposed to CO₂ laser irradiation using the firestar v20 laser with a defocused spot size of 8 mm (achieved via 20 mm defocus distance), 25 W power, and 0.05 mm/s scan speed in a raster pattern with 0.2 mm hatch spacing. The laser treatment induced instantaneous melting and re-solidification of the ceramic matrix, as evidenced by optical microscopy showing a glassy, reflective surface. XRD confirmed the retention of the orthorhombic perovskite structure, while SEM revealed a fully dense cross-section with elongated grains (2–5 µm length) aligned with the laser scan direction. Archimedes density measurement indicated 98.5% theoretical density. This example illustrates the capability of CO₂ laser processing to achieve near-full densification of ceramic pastes in seconds, bypassing the need for prolonged high-temperature furnace cycles.

### Example 4—Thick Er-Doped Silica Films Sintered by CO2 Laser for Scintillation Applications

An Er³⁺-doped silica sol was prepared by mixing tetraethyl orthosilicate (TEOS), ethanol, deionized water, hydrochloric acid catalyst, and erbium nitrate in a 1:4:2:0.01:0.05 molar ratio, aged for 24 hours, and diluted to 20 wt% solids. The sol was extruded via the L3DP microextruder onto a silicon wafer to form a 1.2 mm thick green film. The film was dried at 80 °C for 1 hour, then sintered using the CO₂ laser at 18 W power, 0.2 mm/s scan speed, and 15 mm defocus (spot size ~6 mm) in a nitrogen atmosphere. After sintering, the film exhibited optical transparency with a refractive index of 1.46 (measured by ellipsometry). Photoluminescence spectroscopy under 980 nm excitation showed a strong emission peak at 1535 nm, characteristic of Er³⁺ ⁴I₁₃/₂ → ⁴I₁₅/₂ transition, with a full-width-at-half-maximum of 65 nm. The decay lifetime was measured at 8.2 ms, comparable to bulk Er:SiO₂ glasses. This example extends the L3DP methodology beyond protonic ceramics to functional optical materials, demonstrating its applicability in fabricating thick, doped silica scintillators or waveguides with tailored luminescent properties.

### Example 5—High-Resolution Laser 3D Printing of Transparent Fused Silica Glass

A silica nanoparticle suspension (70 wt% SiO₂ in ethanol, particle size 20 nm) stabilized with 1 wt% ethyl cellulose binder was formulated for microextrusion. Using a 0.2 mm nozzle, intricate 3D lattice structures (unit cell size 500 µm) were printed layer-by-layer onto a quartz substrate. Each layer was immediately dried with the CO₂ laser at 5 W, 20 mm/s, then the entire green body was sintered in two stages: first with the CO₂ laser at 30 W, 0.1 mm/s to consolidate the structure, followed by furnace annealing at 1200 °C for 2 hours in air. The final part exhibited >90% transmittance in the visible range (measured by UV-Vis spectrophotometry) and a compressive strength of 450 MPa (via nanoindentation). Micro-CT scanning confirmed the fidelity of the printed architecture with feature resolution down to 200 µm. This example showcases the L3DP system’s ability to produce high-purity, transparent fused silica components with complex geometries for photonics or microfluidics, leveraging laser drying to prevent collapse and laser pre-sintering to enhance handling strength prior to final densification.

### Example 6—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High Temperature Applications

A 125 µm diameter silica optical fiber, pre-coated with a 5 µm thick layer of BZY20 sol-gel, was placed onto a 40 wt% BZY20 + 60 wt% NiO green ceramic plate (5 mm thick) fabricated by L3DP. A second green layer of the same composition was printed directly over the fiber using the microextruder, encapsulating it completely. The assembly was then subjected to CO₂ laser sintering at 22 W, 0.15 mm/s, with the beam defocused to 10 mm spot size to ensure uniform heating around the fiber. Post-sintering, the embedded fiber remained intact with no microcracks at the ceramic-fiber interface, as confirmed by cross-sectional SEM. The Bragg grating inscribed in the fiber retained its reflectivity after sintering, and temperature cycling from 25 °C to 600 °C showed a linear wavelength shift with sensitivity of 12 pm/°C, suitable for high-temperature strain sensing in PCED environments. This example demonstrates a novel method for integrating fragile optical sensors into robust ceramic matrices during manufacturing, enabling real-time in-situ monitoring of device operation.

### Example 7—SiC Applications

Silicon carbide (SiC) components were fabricated using the L3DP system for high-temperature structural applications. A paste was prepared from 60 wt% β-SiC powder (0.5 µm), 20 wt% phenolic resin binder, 15 wt% ethanol, and 5 wt% dispersant. The paste was extruded into turbine blade geometries (50 mm length) and dried with the CO₂ laser at 12 W, 10 mm/s. The green parts were then pyrolyzed at 800 °C in argon to convert the resin to carbon, followed by reaction bonding in a CO₂ laser-assisted silicon melt infiltration step: the parts were heated to 1500 °C in vacuum, and molten silicon was wicked into the porous preform under capillary action, with laser heating maintaining the temperature gradient. The final SiC-Si composite exhibited 95% density, flexural strength of 380 MPa, and oxidation resistance up to 1400 °C. This example illustrates the extension of L3DP to non-oxide ceramics, combining additive manufacturing with laser-controlled reactive processing for advanced structural components.

### Example 8—3D Printing of all-Glass Fiber-Optic Pressure Sensor for High-Temperature Applications

An all-glass Fabry-Pérot interferometric pressure sensor was fabricated by L3DP. A hollow capillary tube (OD 200 µm, ID 100 µm) made of borosilicate glass was printed by co-extruding a fugitive core material (polyethylene glycol) surrounded by a glass frit paste. After laser drying, the core was removed by thermal decomposition, leaving a sealed cavity. One end of the capillary was then sealed with a 10 µm thick diaphragm printed from the same glass paste and sintered with the CO₂ laser at 10 W, 5 mm/s. The sensor was spliced to a standard SMF-28 fiber, and its response was tested from 0–100 psi at 500 °C. The interference fringe shift showed a linear pressure sensitivity of 0.8 nm/psi with hysteresis <2%. This monolithic, all-glass design, enabled by L3DP’s multi-material capability and laser sealing, offers superior stability for harsh environment sensing compared to adhesive-bonded alternatives.

### Example 9—Laser-Assisted Embedding of all-Glass Optical Fiber Sensors into Bulk Ceramics for High-Temperature Applications

Reiterating and expanding upon Example 6, a distributed array of four fiber Bragg grating (FBG) sensors was embedded into a 40 wt% BCZYYb + 60 wt% NiO anode support tube (OD 15 mm, length 80 mm) during L3DP fabrication. Each FBG (centered at 1540, 1545, 1550, 1555 nm) was positioned at axial intervals of 20 mm along the tube’s inner surface. Green ceramic layers were printed over the fibers with 200 µm clearance, followed by ps-laser polishing to ensure conformal contact. CO₂ laser sintering at 20 W, 0.1 mm/s densified the tube while preserving FBG reflectivity. During simulated PCFC operation (600 °C, 5% H₂), the sensors detected localized strain variations corresponding to thermal expansion mismatches and gas pressure differentials, with cross-talk between channels <0.5%. This example validates the L3DP platform’s capacity for embedding multi-point sensing networks into functional ceramic devices, providing unprecedented diagnostic capabilities for next-generation energy systems.
