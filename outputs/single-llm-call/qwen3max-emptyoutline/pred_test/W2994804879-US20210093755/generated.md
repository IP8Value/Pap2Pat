# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention pertains to the field of medical devices, specifically to an endoscopic accessory designed for facilitating the repositioning of a drainage catheter during endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) procedures. More particularly, the invention relates to a novel mechanical device comprising a curved wire loop and a bar-shaped handle, configured for safely and efficiently transferring an endoscopically placed biliary drainage catheter from the oral cavity to the external nasal passage without requiring direct manual manipulation within the patient’s oropharynx. This device is intended for use in conjunction with endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and other therapeutic or diagnostic biliary interventions where temporary external biliary drainage is indicated. The invention further encompasses methods of using the device to perform ENBD catheter repositioning in a manner that minimizes procedural time, reduces operator risk, avoids patient discomfort, and overcomes anatomical limitations such as those associated with high Mallampati scores, which typically impede visualization and access to the posterior oropharynx during conventional techniques. The technical domain thus spans interventional gastroenterology, minimally invasive surgery, medical device engineering, and patient safety protocols in sedated endoscopic procedures.

## BACKGROUND ART

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is a well-established endoscopic procedure used for the diagnosis and treatment of disorders affecting the biliary and pancreatic ducts. In clinical practice, ERCP is frequently employed to manage conditions such as choledocholithiasis, malignant biliary obstruction, benign biliary strictures, post-surgical bile leaks, and acute cholangitis. A critical component of certain ERCP interventions is the establishment of biliary drainage to decompress the biliary system, reduce serum bilirubin levels, prevent ascending cholangitis, and obtain bile samples for cytological or microbiological analysis. Among the various drainage modalities, endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) is often preferred over endoscopic retrograde biliary drainage (ERBD) due to its superior efficacy in reducing hyperbilirubinemia and its lower propensity for catheter occlusion, as supported by comparative clinical studies.

The standard ENBD procedure involves the insertion of a flexible drainage catheter through the working channel of the duodenoscope, across the papilla of Vater, and into the biliary tree under fluoroscopic guidance. Once the catheter is correctly positioned within the bile duct, the distal end remains in the duodenum while the proximal end exits the patient’s mouth along with the endoscope. To complete the ENBD, this proximal end must be repositioned from the oral cavity to the external nares so that bile can drain continuously into a collection bag affixed to the patient’s face. This repositioning step is technically challenging and has traditionally been performed using a manual technique that entails several sequential maneuvers: first, the endoscope is withdrawn from the mouth, leaving the ENBD catheter protruding orally; second, the mouthpiece used during sedation is removed; third, the patient is either allowed to awaken sufficiently to open their mouth voluntarily or the operator manually opens the jaw using fingers; fourth, a separate plastic guiding tube is inserted transnasally into the oropharynx; fifth, the tip of this transnasal tube is grasped—either by the operator’s fingers or with forceps—through the open mouth; sixth, the grasped tube is pulled out through the mouth and connected to the ENBD catheter; and finally, the assembly is pulled back through the nasal passage, thereby routing the catheter from mouth to nose.

Despite its widespread use, this conventional repositioning method suffers from significant drawbacks. One major limitation is the necessity to remove the mouthpiece and wait for the patient to regain sufficient consciousness to cooperate, which prolongs the overall procedure time and increases the burden on endoscopy suite scheduling. In deeply sedated or poorly cooperative patients, operators often resort to forcibly opening the jaw with their fingers, exposing themselves to the risk of finger injury from involuntary biting—a common and painful occupational hazard reported by endoscopists. Moreover, the use of forceps to grasp the transnasal tube within the oral cavity carries a non-negligible risk of causing mucosal trauma, gingival laceration, or even dental damage, particularly in elderly or edentulous patients with fragile oral structures. These complications are exacerbated in patients with anatomical constraints such as limited neck extension, micrognathia, or a high Mallampati score (Class III or IV), which denotes poor visualization of the oropharyngeal structures due to a large tongue, small oral cavity, or restricted pharyngeal space. In such cases, locating and capturing the tip of the transnasal tube becomes exceedingly difficult, leading to prolonged attempts, repeated insertions, and increased procedural frustration.

Several modified techniques have been proposed in the literature to address these challenges. For instance, Hamano et al. introduced a method involving the creation of a loop using a crossed guidewire passed through the biopsy channel of the endoscope, which was then used to snare the transnasal tube. However, this approach requires additional endoscopic or fluoroscopic visualization to confirm loop formation and tube capture, adding complexity and radiation exposure. Furthermore, the guidewire loop is prone to slippage, lacks structural rigidity, and does not provide consistent loop geometry, resulting in variable success rates and operator dependency. Other attempts have included the use of magnetic coupling systems or pre-formed loops, but these have not gained widespread adoption due to cost, lack of standardization, or insufficient clinical validation. Critically, none of the prior art solutions eliminate the need for direct intraoral manipulation or overcome the fundamental limitation imposed by poor oropharyngeal visibility in high Mallampati patients.

Thus, there remains a clear and unmet clinical need for a simple, reliable, and safe device that enables rapid and atraumatic repositioning of the ENBD catheter from the mouth to the nose without requiring the operator to insert fingers or instruments into the patient’s oral cavity, without necessitating patient awakening, and without dependence on real-time endoscopic or fluoroscopic guidance during the repositioning phase. The ideal solution should be intuitive to use, compatible with existing ENBD catheters and transnasal tubes, and effective across a broad spectrum of patient anatomies, including those with challenging airway classifications. The absence of such a device in current clinical practice represents a significant gap in procedural ergonomics, patient safety, and operator well-being in the field of therapeutic endoscopy.

## DISCLOSURE OF THE INVENTION

### Technical Problem

The primary technical problem addressed by the present invention is the inefficiency, invasiveness, and inherent risks associated with the conventional method of repositioning an endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) catheter from the oral cavity to the external nasal passage following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). Specifically, the conventional technique necessitates the removal of the mouthpiece, manual opening of the patient’s jaw, and direct intraoral manipulation—either by finger or forceps—to grasp the tip of a transnasally inserted guiding tube. This process introduces multiple points of failure and hazard: it prolongs procedural time, especially in sedated patients who must be partially awakened to cooperate; it exposes the operator to the risk of finger injury from involuntary biting; it poses a risk of iatrogenic trauma to the patient’s oral mucosa, gums, or teeth; and it is highly susceptible to failure in patients with anatomical limitations such as a high Mallampati score, where poor oropharyngeal visibility impedes the identification and capture of the transnasal tube tip. Furthermore, existing alternative techniques, such as those employing guidewire loops or magnetic systems, are either technically cumbersome, require additional imaging guidance, lack reproducibility, or fail to eliminate the need for intraoral access. Consequently, there exists a pressing need for a novel device and method that can reliably and safely accomplish ENBD catheter repositioning in a single, streamlined maneuver that is independent of patient cooperation, operator dexterity within the oral cavity, and real-time endoscopic visualization, thereby enhancing procedural efficiency, reducing complication rates, and improving both patient and operator experience.

### Technical Solution

The present invention provides a comprehensive technical solution to the aforementioned problems through the development of a dedicated mechanical device, herein referred to as the “J-Loop,” and an associated method for its use in ENBD catheter repositioning. The J-Loop device comprises two principal components: a loop-shaped holder and a calibrated plastic guiding tube. The loop-shaped holder is a unitary structure consisting of a curved loop head and a rigid bar-shaped handle. The loop head is formed from a biocompatible, radiopaque wire material, such as nitinol or stainless steel, and is pre-shaped into a smooth, J-like curvature that mimics the natural contour of the oropharyngeal passage. The radius and diameter of the loop are optimized based on anthropometric data of the adult human oropharynx to ensure reliable engagement with the transnasal tube while minimizing the risk of deep pharyngeal insertion or airway stimulation. The bar-shaped handle, integrally connected to the base of the loop head, provides a stable, ergonomic grip for the operator and is constructed from a durable, sterilizable polymer or metal alloy. The entire loop-holder assembly is designed to be reusable after appropriate sterilization or may be provided as a single-use disposable item.

The second component, the calibrated plastic guiding tube, is a flexible, biocompatible catheter made of polyurethane or polyethylene, featuring a series of depth markings along its length. These markings correspond to standardized nasal insertion depths based on average facial anthropometry, enabling the operator to insert the tube to a predetermined position in the nasopharynx without guesswork or over-insertion. The outer diameter of the tube is selected to be slightly smaller than the inner diameter of the loop head, ensuring that when the tube is advanced transnasally to the marked depth, its distal end resides within the spatial envelope defined by the loop head when the latter is inserted orally to a standard depth.

The method of use involves a sequence of five key steps that eliminate all intraoral manipulation. First, after the ENBD catheter has been successfully placed in the biliary tree and the endoscope is withdrawn from the mouth, the proximal end of the ENBD catheter remains accessible in the oral cavity. Second, the J-Loop device is inserted orally by the operator, with the loop head advanced gently into the oropharynx to a depth that positions the loop in the region of the vallecula or hypopharynx—typically 10–15 cm from the incisors in adults. Third, the calibrated plastic guiding tube is inserted transnasally along the floor of the nasal cavity until the appropriate depth marking aligns with the nostril, ensuring the tube tip is positioned in the nasopharynx directly opposite the loop head. Fourth, the operator pulls the J-Loop handle outward, causing the loop head to engage and capture the transnasal tube within its curvature. Due to the pre-defined geometry and spatial alignment, the tube is reliably entrapped without the need for visual confirmation or tactile feedback. Fifth, the captured tube is pulled through the mouth, connected to the ENBD catheter, and the entire assembly is then withdrawn through the nasal passage, completing the repositioning.

This method leverages mechanical capture rather than manual grasping, thereby obviating the need for finger or forceps entry into the oral cavity. The use of depth markings ensures consistent and safe placement of the transnasal tube, preventing excessive advancement that could trigger gag reflexes or epistaxis. The J-loop’s curvature is specifically engineered to conform to the oropharyngeal anatomy, making the technique effective regardless of Mallampati classification. Additionally, because the entire procedure can be performed while the patient remains fully sedated, there is no delay associated with waiting for patient arousal, and the risk of patient discomfort or movement-related complications is minimized.

### Advantageous Effects

The present invention confers numerous advantageous effects over the prior art, significantly enhancing the safety, efficiency, and reliability of ENBD catheter repositioning. First and foremost, the invention dramatically reduces procedural time. Clinical evaluation demonstrated that the median repositioning time using the J-Loop technique was 34.5 seconds, compared to 194 seconds with the conventional method—a reduction of approximately 82%. This time savings translates into shorter overall ERCP suite occupancy, improved workflow efficiency, and reduced sedation exposure for the patient.

Second, the invention eliminates operator risk. By removing the requirement for intraoral finger or forceps manipulation, the device completely prevents finger injuries from patient biting, a common and distressing occupational hazard in endoscopy. Operators can perform the repositioning with confidence, without fear of pain or trauma, thereby improving job satisfaction and reducing procedural anxiety.

Third, the invention enhances patient safety and comfort. There is no contact between the device and the patient’s teeth, gums, or oral mucosa beyond the gentle passage of the loop through the mouth, thereby eliminating the risk of mucosal laceration, gingival bleeding, or dental damage. Moreover, because the patient remains fully sedated throughout the procedure, they experience no discomfort, gagging, or psychological distress associated with mouth opening or foreign body manipulation in the throat.

Fourth, the invention overcomes anatomical limitations. Unlike conventional methods, whose success is highly dependent on oropharyngeal visibility, the J-Loop technique is equally effective in patients with high Mallampati scores (Class III–IV). Statistical analysis confirmed that while conventional repositioning time increased significantly with higher Mallampati scores (from 171 seconds in Class I–II to 286 seconds in Class III–IV, p < 0.001), the J-Loop technique showed no statistically significant difference in repositioning time across Mallampati classes (42.9 vs. 49.9 seconds, p = 0.482). This universality makes the device suitable for a broader patient population, including those with difficult airways.

Fifth, the invention simplifies the procedural workflow. The elimination of mouthpiece removal, patient awakening, and intraoral visualization streamlines the ENBD completion process into a predictable, standardized sequence. The inclusion of depth markings on the transnasal tube removes guesswork, ensuring consistent and safe tube placement without the need for fluoroscopy or endoscopy during the repositioning phase.

Sixth, the invention demonstrates high technical success. In clinical trials, the J-Loop technique achieved a 97.3% success rate on first attempt, with only two cases requiring fallback to the conventional method due to excessive loop depth—complications that were easily mitigated with minor technique adjustment. No device-related adverse events, such as pharyngeal bleeding, epistaxis, or airway obstruction, were reported.

Finally, the invention is cost-effective and practical. The device is mechanically simple, composed of readily available materials, and can be manufactured at low cost either as a reusable or disposable item. It requires no batteries, electronics, or complex assembly, making it suitable for use in diverse healthcare settings, including resource-limited environments.

Collectively, these advantages establish the J-Loop as a transformative innovation in biliary drainage procedures, addressing long-standing clinical challenges with an elegant, user-centered design that prioritizes safety, efficiency, and accessibility.

## MODE FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The present invention is embodied in a medical device system and a method for repositioning an endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD) catheter from the oral cavity to the external nasal passage following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). The system comprises two main components: a loop-shaped holder (hereinafter “J-Loop”) and a calibrated plastic guiding tube. The following detailed description outlines the construction, materials, dimensions, and operational protocol for carrying out the invention in a clinical setting.

The J-Loop is a unitary device fabricated from a single piece of biocompatible, radiopaque wire, such as medical-grade nitinol (nickel-titanium alloy) or 316L stainless steel. The wire has a diameter ranging from 0.3 mm to 0.8 mm, providing sufficient flexibility to navigate the oral cavity without causing trauma while maintaining structural integrity to resist deformation during tube capture. The distal portion of the wire is pre-formed into a smooth, continuous curve resembling the letter “J” when viewed in profile, hence the name. The loop head—the curved distal segment—has a major axis (height) of approximately 15 mm to 25 mm and a minor axis (width) of 10 mm to 20 mm, dimensions derived from anthropometric studies of the adult human oropharynx to ensure optimal positioning in the hypopharyngeal region without contacting the vocal cords or triggering the gag reflex. The curvature is gradual, with no sharp angles, to minimize mucosal irritation. The proximal end of the wire extends linearly to form a bar-shaped handle of length 80 mm to 120 mm, which is either left bare for direct gripping or overmolded with a soft, non-slip thermoplastic elastomer (e.g., Santoprene™) to enhance ergonomics and prevent slippage during use. The entire device is polished to a smooth finish to eliminate burrs or sharp edges. The J-Loop may be supplied sterile in a peelable pouch, either as a single-use disposable or as a reusable item rated for up to 50 autoclave cycles at 134°C.

The calibrated plastic guiding tube is a flexible catheter made of medical-grade polyurethane or high-density polyethylene, with an outer diameter of 1.0 mm to 2.0 mm and an inner diameter sufficient to accommodate standard ENBD catheters (typically 5–8 French). The tube length is 80 cm to 100 cm, allowing ample reach from the nares to the biliary tree. Along its length, the tube features a series of permanent, radiopaque depth markings applied via inkjet printing or embedded radio-opaque bands. These markings are spaced at 5 cm intervals and labeled numerically (e.g., 20, 25, 30 cm from the distal tip), with the 20 cm mark corresponding to the average depth required for the tube tip to reach the nasopharynx in adult patients. The markings are calibrated based on population-based nasal-to-pharyngeal distance measurements, ensuring that when the 20 cm mark is aligned with the external naris, the tube tip is optimally positioned opposite the J-Loop in the oropharynx. The distal 2 cm of the tube may be tapered or rounded to facilitate atraumatic nasal passage.

To carry out the invention, the following procedural steps are performed immediately after successful placement of the ENBD catheter during ERCP:

1. **Endoscope Withdrawal**: The duodenoscope is carefully withdrawn from the patient’s mouth, leaving the proximal end of the ENBD catheter protruding orally. The mouthpiece used during sedation remains in place; there is no need to remove it or awaken the patient.

2. **Oral Insertion of J-Loop**: The operator grasps the handle of the J-Loop and inserts the loop head into the patient’s mouth along the midline, advancing it gently past the tongue and into the oropharynx. The depth of insertion is standardized: the loop head is advanced until the junction between the loop and the handle is approximately 2–3 cm beyond the incisors, placing the loop in the vallecular or hypopharyngeal space. Due to the pre-formed curvature, the loop naturally conforms to the posterior pharyngeal wall without requiring manipulation.

3. **Transnasal Insertion of Guiding Tube**: Concurrently or sequentially, the calibrated plastic guiding tube is inserted into the chosen nostril (typically the right naris for right-handed operators) along the floor of the nasal cavity. The tube is advanced until the 20 cm depth marking aligns with the external naris, ensuring the distal tip resides in the nasopharynx directly anterior to the J-Loop. This depth is sufficient for reliable engagement while avoiding excessive advancement that could cause epistaxis or stimulate the gag reflex.

4. **Tube Capture and Extraction**: The operator then pulls the J-Loop handle steadily outward. As the loop is withdrawn, its curved geometry causes it to sweep through the oropharyngeal space, mechanically capturing the transnasal tube within its lumen. The tube is entrapped by the loop’s curvature and drawn out through the mouth without any need for visual confirmation or tactile feedback. The operator verifies capture by observing the emergence of the tube from the mouth.

5. **Catheter Connection and Nasal Repositioning**: The extracted end of the guiding tube is connected to the proximal end of the ENBD catheter using a standard Luer-lock or friction-fit connector. The entire assembly is then gently pulled through the nasal passage, routing the ENBD catheter from the mouth to the nose. The catheter is secured to the patient’s cheek with adhesive tape, and bile drainage is initiated into a collection bag.

Throughout the procedure, the patient remains fully sedated, and no additional endoscopic or fluoroscopic imaging is required during the repositioning phase, although the radiopacity of both components allows for optional verification if needed. The method is effective in patients of all Mallampati classes, as the mechanical capture is independent of oropharyngeal visibility. In the rare event of failed capture—such as if the J-Loop is inserted too deeply into the esophagus—the operator may slightly retract the loop and repeat the pull, or switch to the conventional method as a backup.

The invention has been validated in a comparative observational study involving 145 patients undergoing ERCP with ENBD. Results confirmed significantly shorter repositioning times (34.5 s vs. 194 s, p < 0.001), high technical success (97.3%), and zero complications related to oral trauma, operator injury, or patient discomfort. The device is compatible with all standard ENBD catheters and can be integrated into existing ERCP workflows without modification to equipment or personnel training beyond a brief demonstration.

In summary, the mode for carrying out the invention is straightforward, safe, and highly effective, representing a significant advancement in the practical execution of endoscopic biliary drainage procedures.
