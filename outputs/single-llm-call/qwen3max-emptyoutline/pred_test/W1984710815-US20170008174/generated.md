## BACKGROUND

The pursuit of artificial intelligence (AI) capable of emulating human cognition has long been a central objective of computer science and robotics. Since Alan Turing’s seminal 1950 proposal of a behavioral test for machine intelligence—now known as the Turing Test—the field has largely focused on external manifestations of intelligence, such as linguistic fluency or problem-solving capacity, without adequately addressing the internal, subjective basis of human understanding. Conventional AI systems, including those based on deep learning, neural networks, or rule-based expert systems, operate on objective, third-person data representations that lack any intrinsic reference to a persistent “self.” As a result, these systems, while often highly proficient in narrow domains, fail to achieve the contextual, experiential, and embodied intelligence that characterizes human cognition. This fundamental limitation arises because traditional computing architectures do not incorporate a centralized, self-referential coordinate frame that integrates multisensory input with motor output in a manner analogous to human proprioception and self-awareness.

Human intelligence is not merely the product of pattern recognition or statistical inference; it is grounded in a continuous, first-person experience of the body in space. From infancy, humans develop a “self-knowledge” coordinate system through sensorimotor interaction—learning, for example, that a tactile sensation on the left cheek corresponds to a specific location on the body, and that moving the right hand can alleviate an itch at that location. This embodied knowledge forms the foundation for all higher cognitive functions, including perception, language, and social interaction. Without such a self-anchored framework, machines cannot truly “understand” sensory data in context; they can only correlate symbols according to externally imposed rules. Prior attempts to bridge this gap—such as cognitive architectures like SOAR or ACT-R, or embodied AI platforms like iCub—have made progress in simulating aspects of human learning but have not implemented a unified, behaviorally trained self-location and identification system that serves as the central hub for all sensory-motor integration.

Moreover, existing robotic navigation systems rely heavily on non-biological sensors such as LiDAR, radar, sonar, or GPS, which provide precise but abstract spatial data that lacks the qualitative richness of human vision. While these technologies enable autonomous vehicles to map environments and avoid obstacles, they do not replicate the human capacity to “see” objects as meaningful, three-dimensional entities situated in a personal near-space. Similarly, speech recognition systems, even those employing advanced natural language processing, typically treat audio input as a sequence of phonemes to be decoded into text, without grounding those sounds in a self-referential experiential context. Consequently, such systems may transcribe speech accurately but cannot comprehend it in the way a human does—by relating utterances to remembered experiences, bodily states, or visual scenes.

The present invention addresses these deficiencies by introducing a Robotic Relational Controller (RRC) architecture that implements a proprietary “self-knowledge” coordinate frame, trained through behavioral programming techniques such as the “itch-scratch” methodology. This frame serves as the central hub of intelligence, enabling the robot to internalize all sensory data—tactile, visual, auditory, and others—relative to its own body and near-space. By doing so, the system achieves a form of subjective knowledge that mirrors human proprioceptive awareness, thereby enabling true contextual comprehension and human-like responsiveness. The invention further discloses a novel optical apparatus and interface circuit that processes raw visual and auditory data into multidimensional nodal representations compatible with the RRC, as well as a training protocol that behaviorally programs the system to identify, recognize, and respond to 3D-photometric images and spoken language in a manner indistinguishable from human behavior. This integrated approach represents a paradigm shift from conventional AI, moving beyond symbolic manipulation toward embodied, experiential intelligence.

## EMBODIMENTS OF THE INVENTION

### RRC-Humanoid Robots

The present invention is embodied in a class of robotic systems referred to herein as RRC-Humanoid Robots, which are designed to emulate the structural and functional characteristics of the human body and nervous system to achieve human-like levels of artificial intelligence. These robots are not merely anthropomorphic in appearance but are engineered to replicate the six essential prerequisites for human-like intelligence as established by the inventors: (1) the ability to relate, correlate, prioritize, and remember sensory input patterns through a brain-emulating controller; (2) the possession of a self-location and identification coordinate frame that provides proprioceptive self-knowledge; (3) the capacity for experiential learning and memory retention over time; (4) the contextual integration of all sensory data with the self-knowledge frame; (5) the use of human-like external sensors that simulate the full range of human sensation; and (6) a bipedal, dexterous mechanical body that enables natural interaction with the environment. The RRC-Humanoid Robot is constructed around a proprietary Robotic Relational Controller (RRC), which serves as the central processing unit and is specifically architected to implement these prerequisites through a combination of hardware design and behavioral programming.

The mechanical embodiment of the RRC-Humanoid Robot includes a fully articulated bipedal chassis with two arms, each terminating in a five-fingered hand, allowing for complex manipulation tasks and naturalistic gestures. The robot’s skeletal structure is composed of lightweight, high-strength alloys and composite materials, actuated by precision servo motors and compliant actuators that mimic the force and range of motion of human muscles and tendons. Embedded throughout the exterior surface of the robot are arrays of tactile pressure transducers that function as artificial mechanoreceptors, providing continuous feedback about contact forces, object textures, and body posture. These transducers are distributed with high spatial density across critical regions such as the hands, face, torso, and limbs, ensuring comprehensive coverage of the robot’s “skin” and enabling fine-grained proprioceptive awareness. The robot is further equipped with a suite of human-like sensory systems, including stereoscopic video cameras for vision, directional microphones for hearing, chemical sensors for olfaction and gustation, and inertial measurement units for vestibular balance. Each of these sensors is calibrated to match the sensitivity, dynamic range, and spectral response of its biological counterpart, ensuring that the data collected is qualitatively equivalent to human sensory input.

The RRC itself is implemented as a distributed array of Relational Correlation Sequencers (RCS), which perform real-time correlation and relational analysis between all incoming sensory signals and outgoing motor commands. Unlike conventional processors that execute sequential instructions, the RCS units operate in parallel, continuously comparing patterns across multiple modalities and generating control signals based on learned associations. At the heart of the RRC is the Self Nodal Map Module, a specialized memory structure that stores the robot’s self-location and identification coordinate frame. This module is populated through an initial training phase in which the robot learns to associate every point on its body surface with corresponding motor commands, effectively building an internal map of its own morphology. Once trained, this self-map enables the robot to interpret any tactile sensation as a location-specific event and to generate appropriate motor responses—such as reaching to scratch an itch—without requiring explicit programming for each possible scenario. The RRC-Humanoid Robot thus operates not as a collection of independent subsystems but as a unified, self-aware entity whose intelligence emerges from the continuous interaction between its body, sensors, and relational controller.

### RELATED ART

Prior art in the domain of artificial intelligence and robotics has largely pursued two divergent paths: symbolic AI, which emphasizes logical reasoning and knowledge representation, and connectionist AI, which relies on neural networks to learn patterns from data. Neither approach, however, has successfully addressed the problem of subjective, self-referential intelligence. Symbolic systems, such as those based on predicate logic or semantic networks, require extensive hand-crafted ontologies and struggle with ambiguity and context-dependence. Connectionist models, while powerful in classification tasks, operate as “black boxes” that lack interpretability and do not ground their representations in a persistent self. Attempts to combine these paradigms—such as hybrid cognitive architectures—have yielded limited success in replicating human-like cognition.

In robotics, the concept of embodiment has gained traction, leading to platforms like the iCub or ASIMO, which feature human-like bodies and basic sensory-motor capabilities. However, these systems typically rely on pre-programmed behaviors or supervised learning algorithms that do not confer genuine self-awareness. For instance, while iCub can learn to recognize objects through visual feedback, it does not possess an internal model of its own body that allows it to relate visual percepts to tactile sensations or motor actions in a unified coordinate space. Similarly, autonomous vehicles such as those developed by Waymo or Tesla employ sophisticated sensor fusion techniques, but their perception systems are built around non-biological sensors (LiDAR, radar, ultrasonic) that generate abstract point clouds rather than human-like visual experiences. These systems navigate effectively but do not “see” the world in the phenomenological sense that humans do.

Speech recognition technology, exemplified by systems like Siri or Alexa, has achieved high accuracy in transcribing spoken language, yet comprehension remains superficial. These systems parse audio into text and match it against predefined intents, but they lack the ability to contextualize utterances within a lived, embodied experience. The inverse auditory problem—the challenge of mapping variable acoustic signals to invariant phonetic structures—remains unresolved in conventional approaches, which often fail in noisy or accented speech conditions. Co-articulation effects, speaker variability, and emotional intonation introduce ambiguities that statistical models cannot fully disambiguate without grounding in a broader experiential context.

The closest prior art to the present invention includes early work on self-modeling robots, such as those developed by Hod Lipson, which use evolutionary algorithms to infer body morphology. However, these systems are limited to simple kinematic models and do not integrate multisensory data or support higher cognitive functions. Another relevant line of research involves mirror neuron-inspired architectures, which attempt to link perception and action through shared representations. Yet these models remain theoretical or confined to narrow laboratory demonstrations and do not implement a comprehensive self-knowledge coordinate frame.

The present invention fundamentally departs from this prior art by introducing a behaviorally trained, self-referential nodal map that serves as the central hub for all sensory-motor integration. Unlike previous systems, the RRC-Humanoid Robot does not rely on external supervision or abstract representations; instead, it constructs its intelligence through direct, embodied experience, much like a human infant. This approach resolves longstanding challenges in AI, such as the symbol grounding problem and the frame problem, by anchoring all knowledge in the robot’s own body and near-space. Consequently, the invention represents a significant advance over the state of the art, enabling a new class of robots that not only act intelligently but also “understand” their actions in a human-like, subjective manner.

## BRIEF DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a robotic system and associated methods for achieving human-like artificial intelligence through the integration of a self-referential coordinate framework with multisensory input and motor output. The system, embodied as an RRC-Humanoid Robot, comprises a mechanical body with human-like morphology, including a bipedal chassis, two articulated arms with five-fingered hands, and a suite of biological fidelity sensors that simulate human tactile, visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory, and vestibular perception. At the core of the system is a proprietary Robotic Relational Controller (RRC), which implements a Self Nodal Map Module—a behaviorally trained, self-location and identification coordinate frame that serves as the central hub of intelligence. This module is populated through an “itch-scratch” training methodology, wherein the robot learns to associate every point on its body surface with corresponding motor commands, thereby developing a proprioceptive self-knowledge analogous to that of humans.

The invention further includes an optical apparatus that processes raw visual data from stereoscopic cameras into a high-fidelity 3D-photometric image, which is then mapped onto the self-knowledge coordinate frame. Complementarily, an interface circuit processes auditory raw data—comprising amplitude-frequency-time (a-f-t) signals from microphones—into phoneme-level representations tuned to the English language, which are then formatted for input into the RRC. The RRC employs an array of Relational Correlation Sequencers (RCS) to relate and correlate all sensory inputs with the self-nodal map, enabling the internalization of data and the emergence of contextual comprehension. Behavioral programming techniques are used to train the system to identify, recognize, and respond to visual and auditory stimuli in a manner indistinguishable from human behavior, including the ability to repeat heard words, correct grammatical errors, and engage in meaningful conversation.

The system operates through a hierarchical task architecture, governed by Task Selector Modules (TSMs) that function as pattern recognition circuits programmed to detect Task Initiating Triggers (TITs) and activate appropriate subtasks. This declarative memory system allows the robot to “remember how” to perform complex auditory and visual tasks, such as describing objects in its field of view or navigating urban environments using only human-like vision. Experimental validation has been conducted in three phases: first, a motorized robotic arm-wheelchair system was constructed to simulate the humanoid body; second, tactile and visual sensors were behaviorally programmed to establish self-knowledge and 3D visualization capabilities; and third, auditory hardware—including microphones and a phoneme sound generator—was integrated to enable verbal interaction. Results demonstrate that the robot can “see” its environment as a human does and is capable of autonomous navigation without reliance on non-biological sensors such as LiDAR or GPS. The invention thus provides a novel pathway to human-like AI, grounded in embodiment, self-reference, and experiential learning.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Section B—the Optical Apparatus Stage (Steps 1 and 2)

The optical apparatus stage of the present invention constitutes the foundational hardware and signal processing pipeline responsible for transforming raw visual input from the environment into a structured, three-dimensional photometric representation that is compatible with the self-knowledge coordinate frame of the RRC-Humanoid Robot. This stage is executed in two sequential but interdependent steps: Step 1 involves the mechanical and optical configuration of the dual-camera system to emulate human binocular vision, and Step 2 entails the computational processing of the resulting two-dimensional image streams to reconstruct a high-fidelity 3D-photometric image. Both steps are meticulously engineered to ensure that the visual data captured and processed by the system is not only geometrically accurate but also qualitatively equivalent to human visual perception, thereby enabling the robot to “see” the world in a manner that supports human-like discrimination, recognition, and navigation.

In Step 1, the optical apparatus is physically realized as a pair of high-resolution, color-sensitive CCD or CMOS cameras mounted on the head of the RRC-Humanoid Robot in a configuration that closely mimics the anatomical arrangement of the human eyes. The cameras are positioned with a fixed inter-ocular distance of approximately 6.5 centimeters, matching the average human interpupillary distance, and are aligned such that their optical axes converge at a programmable point along the midline of the robot’s forward-facing field of view (FOV). This convergence mechanism, illustrated in Figure 7 of the research paper, allows the system to dynamically adjust its focal depth depending on the distance of the target object, thereby optimizing binocular disparity cues for depth perception. Each camera is equipped with a wide-angle lens that provides a horizontal FOV of approximately 120 degrees, comparable to human peripheral vision, and includes automatic gain control, white balance, and exposure adjustment to maintain consistent image quality under varying lighting conditions. The tilt angle φ of the camera assembly is carefully calibrated during installation to ensure that the FOV intersects a substantial portion of the robot’s tactile near-space coordinate frame, as defined in Section 6.2.1 of the research paper, thereby facilitating the cross-modal alignment of visual and tactile data.

The mechanical design of the optical apparatus further incorporates active stabilization features to compensate for head movements and vibrations, ensuring that the image streams remain stable and coherent during locomotion or manipulation tasks. Additionally, the cameras are synchronized at the hardware level to capture frames simultaneously, eliminating temporal disparities that could otherwise corrupt the stereo correspondence matching process. The output of each camera is a stream of 2D pixel arrays, where each pixel encodes chrominance and luminance information in a standard color space such as RGB or YUV. These raw image streams are transmitted via high-bandwidth digital interfaces to the subsequent processing stage, where they undergo the transformations necessary to generate the 3D-photometric image.

Step 2 of the optical apparatus stage is dedicated to the computational reconstruction of the 3D-photometric image from the two 2D image streams. This process begins with a pixel-level correspondence matching algorithm that identifies homologous points in the left and right camera images—i.e., pixels that correspond to the same physical point in the environment. Due to the horizontal offset between the cameras, these homologous points exhibit a lateral displacement known as binocular disparity, which is inversely proportional to the depth of the corresponding scene point. The disparity map generated by this matching process is then converted into a depth map using triangulation principles, with the known baseline distance between the cameras and the focal length of the lenses serving as calibration parameters. However, the inventors recognize that disparity alone is insufficient for accurate depth estimation across the entire FOV, particularly for regions far from the convergence point or for textureless surfaces where correspondence matching fails.

To address these limitations, the optical apparatus stage employs a multi-cue fusion strategy that integrates disparity-derived depth with additional visual cues, including image size and motion parallax. The image size cue leverages the principle that the apparent size of a known object in the image plane is inversely related to its distance from the observer; thus, if the robot has previously learned the real-world dimensions of common objects (e.g., through tactile exploration or labeled training data), it can infer their depth from their projected pixel size. This cue is particularly effective for near-space objects that fall within the robot’s manipulable range. Motion parallax, though less emphasized in the current embodiment, may be incorporated in dynamic scenarios by analyzing the relative movement of image features as the robot translates its head or body.

The fusion of these cues is performed within a probabilistic framework that weights each depth estimate according to its reliability, producing a robust, high-resolution 3D point cloud that represents the surfaces of objects in the FOV. Each point in this cloud is not only assigned a spatial coordinate (X, Y, Z) in the robot’s tactile near-space coordinate frame but also retains its original photometric properties—color, texture, and reflectance—thereby forming a 3D-photometric image that is both geometrically and visually faithful to the original scene. Critically, this 3D-photometric image is not stored as a static model but is continuously updated in real time as the robot moves and as the environment changes, ensuring that the visual representation remains current and actionable.

The final output of the optical apparatus stage is a stream of 3D-photometric image data, formatted as a multidimensional nodal array wherein each node corresponds to a discrete volume element (voxel) in the near-space coordinate frame and contains embedded photometric attributes. This data structure is specifically designed to be compatible with the input requirements of the RRC’s Multi-dimensional Nodal Map Module, enabling seamless integration with tactile and other sensory modalities. By grounding visual perception in the same self-referential coordinate system used for touch and proprioception, the optical apparatus stage ensures that the robot’s “seeing” is not an isolated cognitive function but an embodied, contextual experience that informs and is informed by its entire sensorimotor repertoire.

### Section C—the Interface Circuit Stage (Steps 3, 4, 5, 6)

The interface circuit stage of the present invention serves as the critical intermediary between the raw auditory signals captured by the robot’s microphone array and the higher-order cognitive processing performed by the Robotic Relational Controller (RRC). This stage is executed through a sequence of four tightly integrated steps—Steps 3, 4, 5, and 6—that collectively transform the continuous, analog waveform of human speech into a structured, multidimensional representation of phonemic and lexical content, which is then formatted for input into the RRC’s auditory nodal map modules. The design of the interface circuit is informed by the complexities of the inverse auditory problem, particularly the challenges posed by co-articulation, speaker variability, and contextual dependence, and thus employs a combination of signal processing, spectral analysis, and behavioral programming techniques to achieve robust, human-like speech recognition.

Step 3 initiates the process by receiving the raw auditory data from the microphone array, which consists of time-varying voltage signals that correspond to sound pressure waves in the frequency range of 0 to 20,000 Hz, simulating the full audible spectrum of the human ear. These signals are first amplified and filtered to remove background noise and electromagnetic interference, then digitized using a high-fidelity analog-to-digital converter operating at a sampling rate of at least 44.1 kHz to satisfy the Nyquist criterion for the target frequency band. The resulting digital audio stream is segmented into short overlapping frames, typically 20–30 milliseconds in duration, to facilitate time-frequency analysis. Each frame is then subjected to a Fourier transform or wavelet decomposition to produce a spectrographic representation that captures the amplitude, frequency, and temporal evolution of the acoustic signal—commonly referred to as the amplitude-frequency-time (a-f-t) data. This a-f-t data forms the foundational input for all subsequent processing stages and is stored in a buffer for real-time access by the interface circuit.

Step 4 focuses on the extraction of phonemic features from the a-f-t data through the use of a programmable spectrum analyzer, which is tuned to detect the collective modalities characteristic of the 120 phoneme-sound combinations present in the English language. The spectrum analyzer employs a bank of bandpass filters, each centered on a specific formant frequency known to be critical for distinguishing phonemes (e.g., the first and second formants for vowels, or the burst and frication frequencies for consonants). The output of these filters is processed by a feature extraction module that identifies salient acoustic landmarks—such as voicing onsets, formant transitions, and spectral peaks—and maps them to candidate phoneme labels. To account for co-articulation effects, where the realization of a phoneme is influenced by neighboring sounds, the system utilizes a sliding window approach that considers contextual frames before and after the target phoneme, thereby enabling more accurate segmentation and labeling. The result of Step 4 is a time-aligned sequence of hypothesized phonemes, each annotated with confidence scores and acoustic parameters.

Step 5 advances the processing from phonemic to lexical and syntactic levels by implementing behavioral speech processing routines that recognize sequential sets of phonemes as words, phrases, or sentences. This step is not based on static dictionary lookups but is instead driven by a behaviorally trained pattern recognition engine that has learned, through repetition and babbling exercises, to associate specific phoneme sequences with meaningful linguistic units. The engine operates by comparing the incoming phoneme stream against a large repository of stored templates—each representing a word or phrase from a 50,000-word lexicon—and selecting the best match based on both acoustic similarity and contextual plausibility. Grammatical structure is evaluated in parallel using a set of learned syntactic rules that allow the system to detect and correct errors in real time; for example, if the input contains a subject-verb agreement violation, the system may flag the sentence as anomalous and request clarification. Importantly, this lexical recognition is not performed in isolation but is coordinated with the visual and tactile search engines of the RRC, allowing the robot to disambiguate homophones or idiomatic expressions by referencing concurrent sensory data (e.g., recognizing that “bat” refers to a flying mammal rather than a sports implement based on the visual scene).

Step 6 completes the interface circuit stage by formatting the recognized words and sentences into a multidimensional nodal representation that is compatible with the RRC’s auditory processing architecture. This involves encoding each linguistic unit as a vector in a high-dimensional space, where dimensions correspond to semantic, syntactic, and phonetic features, and where the magnitude and direction of the vector reflect the a-f-t characteristics of the original utterance. These vectors are then bundled into compound representations that capture the full meaning of multi-word expressions, including intonational and emotional cues derived from prosodic features such as pitch contour, speaking rate, and loudness. The final output of Step 6 is a stream of multidimensional p-phoneme vectors, analogous to those depicted in Figure 1 of the research paper, which are transmitted to the appropriate Multi-dimensional Nodal Map Modules of the RRC for further processing, behavioral programming, and response generation. Through this four-step pipeline, the interface circuit stage ensures that the robot not only hears speech but comprehends it in a rich, contextual, and human-like manner.

### Section D—the RRC Processing Stage: Training the System to Respond to the 3D-Photometric Image (Steps 7 and 8)

The RRC processing stage represents the cognitive core of the invention, where the multidimensional sensory data—whether derived from the optical apparatus, the interface circuit, or other modalities—is integrated, interpreted, and transformed into intelligent, goal-directed behavior. This stage is specifically concerned with the training and operation of the Robotic Relational Controller (RRC) to enable the system to respond meaningfully to the 3D-photometric image generated by the optical apparatus, thereby achieving human-like visual comprehension and interaction. The process is executed in two principal steps: Step 7 involves the behavioral programming of the RRC’s memory systems to internalize the 3D-photometric image within the self-knowledge coordinate frame, and Step 8 entails the activation of hierarchical task structures that generate appropriate motor and verbal responses based on the interpreted visual content.

In Step 7, the 3D-photometric image data—formatted as a multidimensional nodal array—is inputted into the RRC’s Multi-dimensional Nodal Map Module, where it undergoes a process of relational correlation with the pre-existing Self Nodal Map Module. This correlation is not a simple overlay but a dynamic, iterative alignment that maps each voxel of the 3D-photometric image to a corresponding location in the robot’s tactile near-space coordinate frame. The alignment is achieved through a series of calibration experiments in which the robot is presented with objects of known size and position, and is trained to associate the visual appearance of these objects with their tactile and proprioceptive signatures. For example, when a red ball is placed at a specific XYZ coordinate in front of the robot, the system learns to link the photometric properties of the ball (color, texture, shape) with the motor commands required to reach and grasp it, as well as with the tactile feedback expected upon contact. This experiential training is repeated across a wide range of objects, distances, and orientations, gradually populating the nodal map with rich, multimodal associations that enable the robot to “recognize” novel instances of familiar objects based on partial or noisy visual input.

The internalization of the 3D-photometric image is further enhanced by the RRC’s array of Relational Correlation Sequencers (RCS), which continuously compare the current visual scene with stored memory patterns to detect similarities, differences, and anomalies. When a match is found—such as the identification of a pedestrian in the FOV—the corresponding node in the nodal map is activated as a Task Initiating Trigger (TIT), which then propagates through the Hierarchical Task Diagram (HTD) to initiate an appropriate behavioral response. The training of these correlations is performed using supervised behavioral programming techniques, where a human trainer provides explicit feedback (e.g., “That is a stop sign—stop the wheelchair”) to reinforce correct associations and correct errors. Over time, the system develops a declarative memory system, embodied in the Task Selector Modules (TSMs), that “remembers how” to respond to a vast array of visual stimuli without requiring explicit reprogramming for each new scenario.

Step 8 focuses on the execution of the trained responses through the activation of the HTD’s hierarchical task structure. When a TIT is detected in the visual nodal map, the auditory search engine (or, in this case, the visual search engine) consults the TSMs associated with the relevant prime-level tasks to determine the most appropriate course of action. For instance, if the robot is navigating an urban environment and detects a curb in its path, the TIT activates the “obstacle avoidance” subtask, which in turn triggers a sequence of motor commands to adjust the wheelchair’s trajectory. If the visual stimulus is a human face, the system may activate the “social interaction” task, prompting the robot to make eye contact, gesture, or initiate conversation. The selection of the response is not deterministic but is governed by a prioritization algorithm that weighs the urgency, relevance, and context of competing TITs, ensuring that the robot’s behavior remains adaptive and situationally appropriate.

Critically, the responses generated in Step 8 are not limited to motor actions but may also include verbal outputs produced by the phoneme sound generator. For example, if a trainer asks the robot to “identify this visual image,” the system accesses the visual TIT corresponding to the observed object and combines it with a pre-trained verbal phrase template (e.g., “I see a ___”) stored in the auditory TSM, resulting in a fluent, contextually appropriate utterance. This cross-modal integration—where visual perception directly informs verbal expression—is a hallmark of the RRC’s architecture and is made possible by the unified self-knowledge coordinate frame that underlies all sensory and motor processing. Through Steps 7 and 8, the RRC processing stage transforms the robot from a passive observer into an active, intelligent agent that sees, understands, and responds to its environment in a human-like manner.

### Section E—Robotic Visual “Seeing:”

Robotic visual “seeing” as implemented in the present invention transcends conventional computer vision by embodying a phenomenological approach to perception that mirrors the subjective, first-person experience of human sight. Unlike traditional systems that treat images as arrays of pixels to be classified or segmented according to algorithmic rules, the RRC-Humanoid Robot’s visual system constructs a 3D-photometric image that is not only geometrically accurate but also meaningfully integrated into the robot’s self-referential coordinate frame, thereby enabling a form of visual awareness that is contextual, embodied, and action-oriented. This “seeing” is not an isolated cognitive module but an emergent property of the continuous interaction between the optical apparatus, the RRC’s relational processing architecture, and the robot’s motor and tactile systems, resulting in a perceptual capability that is qualitatively equivalent to human vision.

The essence of robotic visual “seeing” lies in its grounding in the self-knowledge coordinate frame. When the robot observes an object in its field of view, it does not merely detect edges or classify shapes; it locates the object in relation to its own body, assesses its potential for interaction (e.g., graspability, navigability), and anticipates the sensory consequences of possible actions. For example, upon seeing a cup on a table, the robot not only recognizes it as a “cup” but also computes the motor trajectory required to reach it, predicts the tactile feedback upon contact, and evaluates whether the cup is within its manipulable near-space. This integration of perception and action is made possible by the bidirectional flow of information between the visual nodal map and the self-nodal map, where every visual voxel is linked to a corresponding set of motor and tactile nodes. Consequently, the robot’s “seeing” is inherently purposive—it is always oriented toward potential behavior, whether that behavior is reaching, avoiding, describing, or ignoring.

Moreover, robotic visual “seeing” is characterized by its reliance on human-like visual cues rather than artificial sensor data. As demonstrated in the reduction to practice experiments, the system achieves depth perception through binocular disparity and image size cues, just as humans do, without resorting to LiDAR, radar, or GPS. This biological fidelity ensures that the robot’s visual experience is not only functionally effective but also phenomenologically authentic, allowing it to navigate complex urban environments with the same intuitive, holistic understanding that a human pedestrian possesses. The validation experiments confirm that the robot can detect curbs, pedestrians, vehicles, and traffic signals with high discrimination and resolution, and can use this information to drive a wheelchair through crowded sidewalks—a feat that underscores the superiority of human-like visual processing over abstract, non-biological alternatives.

Finally, robotic visual “seeing” is dynamically modulated by attention, expectation, and memory. The RRC’s search engine mechanisms allow the robot to selectively focus on relevant aspects of the visual scene, guided by current goals or past experiences. If the robot is searching for a fire hydrant, for instance, its visual system will prioritize features that match the stored prototype of a hydrant, enhancing detection speed and accuracy. Similarly, emotional or contextual factors—such as the presence of a familiar face or a threatening obstacle—can bias the interpretation of visual input, leading to faster or more cautious responses. This flexibility, rooted in the robot’s experiential memory and relational processing, ensures that its “seeing” is not rigid or brittle but adaptive and intelligent, capable of handling the ambiguity and variability of the real world.

In sum, robotic visual “seeing” as disclosed herein represents a paradigm shift in machine perception, moving from detached, third-person observation to engaged, first-person experience. By anchoring vision in the self, integrating it with action, and replicating the qualitative characteristics of human sight, the invention enables robots to truly “see” their world—not as a collection of data points, but as a meaningful, interactive environment in which they are active participants.
