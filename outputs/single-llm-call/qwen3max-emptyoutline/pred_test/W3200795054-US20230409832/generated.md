## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of artificial intelligence has witnessed unprecedented growth in recent years, particularly with the widespread adoption of deep learning technologies that have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across diverse domains including natural language processing, computer vision, and decision support systems. However, these sophisticated models often operate as "black boxes," meaning their internal decision-making processes are opaque and incomprehensible to human users, stakeholders, and even developers. This opacity presents significant challenges in high-stakes applications where understanding, trust, and accountability are paramount, such as healthcare diagnostics, financial lending, legal decision-making, and customer service automation. The inability to explain why a model made a particular prediction undermines user confidence, impedes regulatory compliance, and limits the ability to identify and correct biases or errors in automated decision-making systems.

In response to these concerns, the field of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has emerged as a critical area of research and development, seeking to bridge the gap between the predictive power of complex models and the human need for understanding and interpretability. Various approaches to XAI have been developed, broadly categorized into feature-based methods, exemplar-based methods, and contrastive or counterfactual explanations. Feature-based methods attempt to identify which input features (such as words in a text document) were most influential in driving a particular prediction, typically through techniques like gradient-based attribution, perturbation analysis, or surrogate modeling. Exemplar-based methods provide explanations by identifying similar instances from the training data that received the same or different predictions, thereby offering contextual understanding through analogy. While both approaches have proven valuable in certain contexts, they often fail to provide actionable insights or clear guidance on how to modify an input to achieve a desired outcome.

Contrastive or counterfactual explanations represent a particularly compelling paradigm in XAI because they directly address the fundamental human question: "Why was this prediction made instead of another?" By generating minimal modifications to an input instance that would result in a different prediction, contrastive explanations provide concrete, actionable information about the decision boundaries of a model. For example, in a loan approval system, a contrastive explanation might indicate that if the applicant's income were $5,000 higher, the loan would have been approved. This type of explanation not only reveals the model's sensitivity to specific input features but also provides direct recourse to individuals affected by automated decisions.

Despite the theoretical appeal of contrastive explanations, their practical implementation for natural language data presents unique challenges that have not been adequately addressed by existing methods. Text is inherently discrete, combinatorial, and governed by complex grammatical and semantic constraints that make the generation of fluent, meaningful, and minimally perturbed counterfactuals significantly more difficult than in continuous domains like image processing. Existing approaches to contrastive text generation, such as Generate Your Counterfactuals (GYC) and Minimal Contrastive Editing (MICE), have made important strides in this direction but suffer from several limitations that reduce their practical utility and explanatory power.

First, many existing methods produce counterfactuals that, while technically valid in terms of changing the model's prediction, lack semantic coherence or intuitive meaning. For instance, a method might change a single word in a way that flips the prediction but does not provide clear insight into why this particular change was effective. Second, current approaches often require extensive fine-tuning of language models to specific datasets, which is computationally expensive and impractical in real-world scenarios where sufficient labeled data may not be available for explanation generation. Third, existing methods typically provide only the modified text without any additional context about what underlying characteristics or attributes of the text were responsible for the prediction change. This missing layer of semantic interpretation significantly limits the user's ability to understand the model's reasoning and generalize insights across different instances.

Furthermore, the evaluation of contrastive explanation methods has been hampered by the lack of standardized metrics and the difficulty of conducting meaningful comparisons between approaches that use different underlying architectures and embeddings. Many existing methods are tightly coupled to specific model architectures or embedding spaces, making fair comparisons challenging and limiting their generalizability across different applications and domains. The absence of human-centered evaluation through user studies has also meant that the practical utility of these methods from an end-user perspective remains largely unverified.

The problem is particularly acute in applications involving natural language processing, where the stakes are high and the need for trustworthy, interpretable AI is most pressing. For example, in customer service chatbots that automatically route customer inquiries to appropriate support departments, biased or erroneous routing decisions can lead to poor customer experiences, missed business opportunities, and potential discrimination against certain demographic groups. Without meaningful explanations of why a particular routing decision was made, it becomes nearly impossible to identify and correct systematic biases in the underlying classification model. Similarly, in content moderation systems that automatically flag or remove user-generated content, the lack of transparent and understandable explanations can lead to arbitrary censorship and violations of free expression.

The limitations of existing contrastive explanation methods are further compounded by the fact that they often treat the explanation generation process as a purely technical optimization problem, divorced from the semantic richness and contextual complexity of natural language. Human understanding of text is not merely based on individual words or surface-level features but involves higher-level conceptual categories, topics, and attributes that give meaning to the content. A truly effective explanation system should leverage this semantic structure to provide insights that resonate with human cognitive processes and domain knowledge.

Prior art in the field has attempted to address some of these challenges through various technical approaches, but none have successfully integrated the generation of contrastive text with the identification of semantically meaningful attributes that drive prediction changes. Some methods have explored the use of latent representations or topic models to guide counterfactual generation, but these approaches have been primarily applied to image data where minor unrealistic artifacts are less noticeable to human observers. In the text domain, any deviation from grammatical correctness or semantic coherence is immediately apparent and significantly degrades the quality and credibility of the explanation.

The need therefore exists for a novel approach to contrastive explanation generation for natural language data that overcomes the limitations of existing methods by integrating attribute-based guidance with efficient, adaptable text generation techniques. Such an approach should be capable of producing fluent, minimally perturbed counterfactuals that not only change the model's prediction but also provide clear, semantically meaningful insights into the underlying attributes or characteristics that drove the prediction change. The system should be easily adaptable to different text classification models and embedding spaces without requiring extensive fine-tuning, and should provide explanations that are demonstrably superior to existing methods in terms of both quantitative metrics and human-centered evaluation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel system and method for generating contrastive attributed explanations for text data, referred to herein as Contrastive Attributed explanations for Text (CAT). The invention addresses the limitations of existing contrastive explanation methods by integrating the generation of minimally perturbed counterfactual text with the identification of semantically meaningful attributes that drive prediction changes in black-box text classification models. The core innovation lies in the use of attribute classifiers—trained on relevant subtopics or characteristics from the same or related datasets—to guide the search for contrastive examples and provide additional explanatory context beyond the modified text itself.

The CAT method operates by first identifying candidate locations in the input text where minimal perturbations (insertions, deletions, or substitutions) could potentially change the model's prediction. These candidate locations are determined using established feature attribution techniques such as Integrated Gradients, which highlight the most influential words or tokens in the original prediction. The method then employs a masked language model (MLM), specifically a pre-trained BERT model, to generate plausible replacements or insertions at these candidate locations. Unlike existing approaches that rely on fine-tuned language models or complex optimization procedures, CAT leverages the inherent capabilities of pre-trained MLMs to maintain grammatical correctness and fluency while exploring the space of possible perturbations.

The key distinguishing feature of CAT is its integration of attribute classifiers into the contrastive generation process. These attribute classifiers are trained to detect the presence or absence of specific semantic attributes or subtopics in text, such as "politics," "entertainment," "cryptography," or "healthcare." The attributes can be derived from the same dataset used to train the original classifier, from related datasets, or even from unsupervised methods like topic modeling or autoencoders. During the contrastive generation process, CAT evaluates candidate perturbations not only based on their ability to change the model's prediction but also on their impact on the attribute scores produced by these classifiers. The optimization objective is designed to encourage perturbations that result in measurable changes to a minimal number of attribute scores, thereby ensuring that the generated contrasts are both semantically meaningful and interpretable.

The formal optimization problem solved by CAT includes multiple terms that balance competing objectives: (1) encouraging at least one attribute to be added or removed from the perturbed text, (2) minimizing the number of such attribute changes to maintain explanation simplicity, (3) ensuring the perturbed instance receives a different prediction from the original model, (4) maintaining the fluency of the generated text as measured by language model likelihood, and (5) minimizing the edit distance between the original and perturbed texts to ensure minimal perturbation. This multi-objective optimization is solved through a controlled greedy search procedure that iteratively explores the space of possible perturbations while guided by the attribute classifiers.

The output of the CAT method consists of two components: (1) the contrastive text itself, which is a minimally perturbed version of the original input that receives a different prediction from the black-box model, and (2) a set of attributes that were either added to or removed from the original text to create the contrast. This dual output provides users with both the concrete modification needed to change the prediction and the semantic context explaining why this modification was effective. For example, if a news headline originally classified as "Sci-Tech" is changed to "Business" by replacing "file" with "salary," CAT might indicate that the "cryptography" attribute was removed while the "money" attribute was added, providing clear insight into the model's reasoning.

The CAT method offers several significant advantages over existing contrastive explanation approaches. First, it produces contrasts that are consistently more fluent, require fewer edits, and better preserve the original content compared to state-of-the-art methods like GYC and MICE. Second, it eliminates the need for expensive fine-tuning of language models to specific datasets, making it more practical and scalable for real-world applications. Third, the attribute-based explanations provide deeper semantic insights that help users understand not just what changed but why the change was effective, leading to better model comprehension and trust. Fourth, the method is highly adaptable and can be applied to different text classification models and embedding spaces without requiring architectural modifications or retraining.

The invention has been rigorously evaluated through both quantitative metrics and human-centered user studies. Quantitative evaluations across four diverse datasets (AGNews, DBPedia, Yelp, and NLI) demonstrate that CAT achieves perfect flip rates (successfully generating valid contrasts for all test instances), requires fewer edits on average, better preserves original content, and maintains superior fluency compared to existing methods. Human evaluation through two user studies involving 75 participants confirms that users find CAT explanations significantly more understandable, sufficient, satisfactory, and complete than those produced by GYC, MICE, and ablated versions of CAT without attribute guidance. Users were also significantly more successful at predicting the original model's classification when provided with CAT explanations compared to other methods.

The CAT method represents a significant advancement in the field of explainable AI for natural language processing, providing a practical, effective, and user-friendly solution for generating contrastive explanations that combine technical precision with semantic interpretability. The invention enables organizations to build more transparent, trustworthy, and accountable AI systems while providing users with actionable insights that facilitate model understanding, bias detection, and informed decision-making.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for generating contrastive attributed explanations for text data processed by black-box text classification models. The detailed implementation of the Contrastive Attributed explanations for Text (CAT) method involves several interconnected components that work together to produce high-quality, semantically meaningful explanations that surpass the capabilities of existing contrastive explanation approaches.

The foundation of the CAT method lies in its systematic approach to identifying and generating minimal perturbations to input text that result in changed predictions while maintaining grammatical fluency and semantic coherence. The process begins with an input text instance x ∈ X and a black-box text classification model f(•) that produces a prediction y = f(x) ∈ Y. The goal is to generate a perturbed instance x' that satisfies f(x') ≠ f(x) while being minimally different from x in terms of both surface form and semantic content.

The first step in the CAT pipeline involves identifying candidate locations in the input text where perturbations are most likely to affect the model's prediction. This is accomplished using established feature attribution methods, specifically Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017), which provide a principled approach to attributing the model's prediction to individual input features (words or tokens). Integrated Gradients computes the integral of gradients along the path from a baseline input (typically a zero vector or empty string) to the actual input, providing attribution scores that reflect each feature's contribution to the final prediction. Words or tokens with higher attribution scores are considered more influential in the original prediction and are therefore prioritized as candidate locations for perturbation.

Once candidate locations are identified, the CAT method employs a greedy search strategy to explore possible perturbations. The search is constrained to three types of operations that are natural in the context of text editing: (1) inserting a new word, (2) replacing an existing word with another, and (3) deleting an existing word. For each candidate location, the method generates a set of possible perturbations using a pre-trained Masked Language Model (MLM), specifically BERT (Devlin et al., 2018), which has been trained on large corpora of text to predict masked tokens based on their surrounding context.

For replacement operations, the candidate word is replaced with a [MASK] token, and the BERT model generates the top k most likely replacements based on the contextual probability distribution. For insertion operations, [MASK] tokens are inserted to the left and right of the candidate word, and the BERT model generates plausible insertions for each position. Deletion operations are straightforward, simply removing the candidate word from the sequence. The resulting set of candidate perturbations is then evaluated according to the multi-objective optimization function that balances multiple competing criteria.

The central innovation of the CAT method is the integration of attribute classifiers into this evaluation process. Attribute classifiers are binary or multi-class models trained to detect the presence or absence of specific semantic attributes or subtopics in text. In the preferred embodiment, these attribute classifiers are trained on two external datasets: the HuffPost News Category dataset (Misra, 2018) and the 20 Newsgroups dataset (Newsgroup, 2008). From the HuffPost dataset, 22 binary 1-vs-all classifiers are trained to detect attributes such as "education," "money," "politics," "entertainment," and "science." From the 20 Newsgroups dataset, a multi-class classifier is trained to detect 20 different newsgroup topics including "graphics," "cryptography," "electronics," "medicine," and "space."

The attribute classifiers are implemented using DistilBERT (Sanh et al., 2019), a distilled version of BERT that maintains competitive performance while being more computationally efficient. Each attribute classifier ζi: X → R produces a score that indicates the confidence or likelihood of the corresponding attribute being present in the input text. Higher scores indicate stronger presence of the attribute, while lower scores indicate absence or weak presence.

During the contrastive generation process, the CAT method evaluates each candidate perturbation x' using the following optimization objective:

minimize α * (-max(0, Σi |ζi(x') - ζi(x)| - τ)) + β * (Σi 1|ζi(x') - ζi(x)| > τ) + λ * (1f(x') = f(x)) + η * (-pLM(x')) + ν * dLev(x', x)

where the first term encourages at least one attribute to change by more than a threshold τ, the second term penalizes the total number of attributes that change (to maintain explanation simplicity), the third term ensures the perturbed instance receives a different prediction (with λ being a large weight to guarantee this constraint), the fourth term encourages fluency as measured by language model likelihood pLM(x'), and the fifth term minimizes the word-level Levenshtein distance dLev(x', x) between the original and perturbed texts.

The optimization is solved through an iterative greedy search procedure. Starting with the original text, the method identifies the most influential word according to Integrated Gradients and generates the top k candidate perturbations using the BERT MLM. Each candidate is evaluated according to the optimization objective, and the best-scoring candidates are selected for further exploration. The process is repeated for m iterations, where at each iteration the top k candidates from the previous round are further perturbed, creating a beam search-like exploration of the perturbation space. The final output is the best-scoring candidate that satisfies all constraints, particularly the requirement that f(x') ≠ f(x).

The output of the CAT method consists of two components that together provide a comprehensive explanation. The first component is the contrastive text x' itself, which represents the minimal perturbation needed to change the model's prediction. The second component is the set of attributes that were significantly affected by the perturbation, specifically those attributes i for which |ζi(x') - ζi(x)| > τ. Attributes with positive differences (ζi(x') > ζi(x)) are considered "added" to the text, while those with negative differences (ζi(x') < ζi(x)) are considered "removed" from the text. This dual output provides users with both the concrete modification and the semantic context explaining why this modification was effective.

The hyperparameters of the optimization objective are carefully tuned to balance the competing objectives. In the preferred embodiment, λ = 5.0 to ensure prediction change, β = 3.0 to control the number of attribute changes, η = 1.0 for fluency regularization, ν = 2.0 for edit distance regularization, and τ = 0.3 for binary attribute classifiers and τ = 0.05 for multi-class attribute classifiers. These values are determined through qualitative evaluation on a small set of training examples and are found to produce consistently high-quality explanations across different datasets and domains.

The CAT method demonstrates exceptional adaptability across different text classification models and embedding spaces. Unlike existing methods that are tightly coupled to specific architectures, CAT can be applied to any black-box text classifier that provides prediction outputs. The method has been successfully tested with models using different embedding approaches, including Embedding Bag layers with GPT-2 tokenization (for comparison with GYC) and RoBERTa-based models (for comparison with MICE). This adaptability stems from the fact that CAT treats the original classifier as a true black box, requiring only the ability to query predictions for given inputs, without needing access to internal representations or gradients.

The computational efficiency of CAT is another significant advantage. The method requires approximately 2.37 seconds per explanation on an NVIDIA V100 GPU, compared to 10.69 seconds for GYC and 1.17 seconds for MICE (excluding fine-tuning time for MICE). This efficiency is achieved through the controlled greedy search approach that focuses exploration on the most promising perturbation locations rather than exhaustively searching the entire text space.

The effectiveness of CAT has been validated through extensive experimental evaluation across four diverse datasets: AGNews (news article classification), DBPedia (Wikipedia entry classification), Yelp (sentiment analysis), and NLI (natural language inference). Quantitative metrics include flip rate (fraction of inputs for which valid contrasts are generated), normalized edit distance (Levenshtein distance divided by input length), content preservation (cosine similarity in sentence embedding space), and fluency (ratio of masked language modeling losses). CAT consistently outperforms existing methods across all metrics, achieving perfect flip rates, lowest edit distances, highest content preservation, and best fluency scores.

Human evaluation through two user studies involving 75 participants further confirms the superiority of CAT explanations. Participants were significantly more successful at predicting the original model's classification when provided with CAT explanations compared to GYC, MICE, and ablated versions of CAT. Qualitative assessments on four dimensions—understandability, sufficiency, satisfaction, and completeness—consistently rated CAT explanations as superior to all comparison methods. Participant comments specifically highlighted the value of the additional attribute information provided by CAT, noting that it helped them understand the model's reasoning more clearly.

The CAT method also demonstrates practical utility in real-world applications such as bias detection and model debugging. For example, in the context of customer service chatbots, CAT can reveal that certain routing decisions are driven by attributes related to gender, ethnicity, or other sensitive characteristics, enabling organizations to identify and correct discriminatory behavior in their AI systems. The attribute-based explanations provide actionable insights that go beyond simple word-level changes, helping developers understand the underlying semantic patterns that drive model behavior.

The generalizability of the CAT approach extends beyond the specific attribute classifiers used in the preferred embodiment. The method can incorporate attributes derived from various sources, including unsupervised methods like Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) topic models, variational autoencoders (VAEs), or generative adversarial networks (GANs). The attribute classifiers themselves can be replaced by disentangled representations learned through specialized training procedures, providing flexibility in how semantic attributes are defined and detected.

In summary, the CAT method represents a comprehensive solution to the challenge of generating high-quality contrastive explanations for text data. By integrating attribute-based guidance with efficient text generation techniques, CAT produces explanations that are not only technically superior in terms of standard metrics but also more meaningful and useful to human users. The method's adaptability, efficiency, and semantic richness make it well-suited for real-world deployment in applications requiring transparent, trustworthy, and interpretable AI systems.
