## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of radiation therapy, and more specifically to devices and methods for intensity modulation of therapeutic electron beams. In particular, the invention provides a passive radiotherapy intensity modulator for electrons (PRIME), comprising an arrangement of island blocks and/or island apertures configured within or adjacent to a collimating insert of an electron beam delivery system, wherein the spatial distribution, size, and geometry of said island blocks and island apertures are selected to produce a predetermined spatially varying fluence pattern in the plane perpendicular to the central axis of the electron beam. The invention further encompasses methods for designing, fabricating, and utilizing such passive intensity modulators in clinical electron therapy applications, including but not limited to intensity-modulated bolus electron conformal therapy (IM bolus ECT), segmented-field electron conformal therapy, and modulated electron radiation therapy (MERT). The technology described herein enables precise control over the lateral intensity distribution of therapeutic electron beams without requiring active beam scanning components or specialized multileaf collimators, thereby offering a cost-effective, mechanically simple, and clinically practical solution for delivering intensity-modulated electron therapy using conventional linear accelerators equipped with standard electron applicators.

## BACKGROUND AND DESCRIPTION OF PRIOR ART

Radiation therapy is a cornerstone of modern cancer treatment, employing ionizing radiation to destroy malignant cells while sparing surrounding healthy tissue. Among the various forms of external beam radiation therapy, electron beam therapy occupies a unique niche due to its characteristic depth-dose profile: electrons deposit most of their energy within a finite, energy-dependent range and exhibit rapid dose fall-off beyond that depth, making them particularly suitable for treating superficial tumors located within a few centimeters of the skin surface. Conventional electron therapy typically employs custom-shaped collimating inserts—often fabricated from low-melting-point alloys such as Cerrobend—to define the lateral field shape and conform the high-dose region to the target volume. However, this approach provides only uniform intensity across the open field aperture, limiting the ability to sculpt the dose distribution in the lateral plane to accommodate complex target geometries or to compensate for surface irregularities and tissue heterogeneities.

In contrast, intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) has revolutionized photon (x-ray) therapy by enabling spatial variation of beam intensity across the field, thereby allowing highly conformal dose distributions that tightly wrap around critical structures and achieve superior target coverage and homogeneity. IMRT for photons is routinely implemented using multileaf collimators (MLCs), which dynamically modulate the beam aperture during irradiation, or historically, using physical compensators—metal filters of varying thickness placed in the beam path to attenuate photons differentially across the field. Despite these advances in photon therapy, analogous intensity modulation techniques for electron beams have remained largely undeveloped and clinically unavailable.

Several approaches have been proposed to achieve electron intensity modulation. One strategy involves the use of scanned pencil beams, where a narrow electron beam is magnetically steered across the target area in a raster pattern, with the dose per spot controlled to produce the desired fluence map. While conceptually elegant, this method requires a helium-filled treatment head to minimize multiple Coulomb scattering (MCS) in air, which would otherwise broaden the beam spots and degrade spatial resolution. The engineering complexity and cost associated with maintaining a helium environment render this approach impractical for widespread clinical adoption. Another proposed solution is the electron multileaf collimator (eMLC)—a specialized MLC designed with reduced inter-leaf air gaps and constructed from high-density materials to effectively attenuate electron beams. Prototype eMLCs have been developed by research groups and commercialized by third-party vendors; however, their clinical implementation has been hindered by significant barriers, including high acquisition and maintenance costs, mechanical limitations such as the inability to deploy or retract during treatment, lack of integration with commercial treatment planning systems (TPS), and the relatively low patient volume requiring electron therapy, which diminishes the economic incentive for clinics to invest in such specialized hardware.

Alternative passive methods have also been explored but with limited success. For instance, single circular blocks have been used in ocular electron therapy to shield the lens while delivering a reduced dose to the underlying retina; however, this represents a localized shielding application rather than full-field intensity modulation. Similarly, saw-toothed collimator edges have been employed to broaden the penumbra of electron fields to match that of abutting fields of different energies, but again, this addresses a specific dosimetric challenge rather than enabling general-purpose intensity modulation. Critically, none of these prior approaches have utilized arrays of multiple small-area attenuating elements—referred to herein as “island blocks”—or arrays of small transmission apertures—referred to as “island apertures”—strategically arranged to produce a continuous, spatially varying intensity pattern across the entire treatment field through the controlled interplay of primary beam attenuation and secondary electron scattering.

A fundamental physical challenge in electron beam modulation arises from the phenomenon of multiple Coulomb scattering (MCS), which causes electrons to deviate from their original trajectories as they traverse matter, including air and tissue. While MCS limits the spatial resolution achievable with scanned electron beams, it can be advantageously exploited in passive modulation schemes. Specifically, when an electron beam passes through an array of island blocks or island apertures, the regions directly behind the blocks receive no primary electrons, while those aligned with apertures receive full transmission. However, due to MCS in the air gap between the modulator and the patient, as well as within the patient’s tissue, electrons scatter laterally, filling in the shadowed regions and smoothing the intensity distribution. The degree of intensity reduction in a given area is thus determined not only by the local fraction of blocked or open area but also by the spatial distribution of neighboring blocks or apertures, as scattered electrons from adjacent regions contribute to the local fluence. This scattering effect necessitates sophisticated inverse planning algorithms to determine the optimal configuration of island blocks and apertures that will produce a desired intensity pattern at a specified depth in the patient.

Existing treatment planning systems for electron therapy are generally not equipped to handle such inverse planning tasks or to accurately calculate dose distributions resulting from complex passive modulators. Moreover, there is no standardized method for fabricating modulators with the required precision in block/aperture size, placement, and alignment with the beam divergence. Consequently, despite the theoretical potential of passive intensity modulation for electrons, no clinically viable, commercially available solution has emerged to date. The present invention addresses these longstanding gaps by introducing a novel, practical, and cost-effective passive intensity modulator design, supported by robust planning methodologies and fabrication techniques, thereby enabling the clinical realization of intensity-modulated electron therapy for the first time.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a passive radiotherapy intensity modulator for electrons (PRIME), a device and associated methodology for achieving spatially varying intensity modulation of therapeutic electron beams without the need for active beam scanning components or specialized multileaf collimators. The PRIME device comprises a planar array of discrete attenuating elements—referred to as “island blocks”—and/or discrete transmission elements—referred to as “island apertures”—positioned within or immediately upstream of the aperture of a standard electron collimating insert. The island blocks are formed from high-density, electron-stopping materials and are configured to absorb incident primary electrons, while the island apertures are voids or channels through the collimating material that allow primary electrons to pass through unimpeded. The spatial coordinates, cross-sectional areas, and geometric shapes of these island blocks and island apertures are precisely determined based on a desired intensity modulation pattern, leveraging the physical phenomenon of multiple Coulomb scattering (MCS) to achieve smooth, continuous fluence distributions at clinically relevant depths within the patient.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a PRIME device consisting solely of island blocks arranged on a regular or irregular grid within the open field defined by a custom electron collimator. The local intensity reduction factor (IRF)—defined as the ratio of the desired modulated intensity to the unmodulated intensity—is inversely related to the local area fraction occupied by the island blocks. For hexagonally packed circular island blocks of diameter *d* separated by center-to-center distance *r*, the IRF is approximately given by *IRF = 1 − (π/(2√3))·(d/r)²*. This relationship allows for the initial estimation of block dimensions required to achieve a target IRF, though final optimization requires accounting for electron scattering effects via inverse planning algorithms. This configuration is particularly suited for applications requiring intensity modulation in the range of 70% to 100% of the unmodulated beam intensity, such as intensity-modulated bolus electron conformal therapy (IM bolus ECT), where modest reductions in local intensity are needed to correct for dose inhomogeneities caused by surface irregularities.

In another embodiment, the invention provides a PRIME device comprising island apertures formed within the body of the collimating insert itself. These apertures function as localized transmission channels, with the local intensity being proportional to the area fraction of open apertures. For hexagonally packed circular island apertures of diameter *d* and separation *r*, the transmitted intensity is approximately *I = I₀·(π/(2√3))·(d/r)²*, where *I₀* is the unmodulated intensity. This configuration is advantageous for achieving lower intensity levels, typically in the range of 0% to 50%, and is especially useful in applications such as modulated electron radiation therapy (MERT), where full-range intensity modulation (0%–100%) is required. In such cases, a hybrid PRIME device combining both island blocks and island apertures may be employed to cover the complete intensity spectrum.

The invention further includes methods for designing a PRIME device tailored to a specific patient’s anatomy and treatment plan. This process begins with the definition of a desired two-dimensional intensity map at a reference depth in the patient, derived from inverse planning objectives such as target dose homogeneity or organ-at-risk sparing. An optimization algorithm, integrated into a treatment planning system, then determines the optimal configuration of island blocks and/or apertures that will produce the desired intensity pattern when accounting for electron transport physics, including MCS in air and tissue. The algorithm iteratively adjusts the positions and sizes of the modulating elements to minimize the difference between the calculated and desired intensity distributions, subject to manufacturing constraints such as minimum feature size and positional accuracy.

Additionally, the invention encompasses methods for fabricating the PRIME device. In one approach, island blocks are constructed as cylindrical pins of high-density material (e.g., lead, tungsten alloy, or copper) and embedded in a low-density, radiolucent support matrix such as expanded polystyrene (Styrofoam) or acrylic, which minimally perturbs the electron beam. The pins are aligned along divergent rays emanating from the virtual source of the electron beam to minimize off-axis scattering from block walls. In another approach, island apertures are machined directly into the collimating insert using precision drilling, laser cutting, or additive manufacturing techniques, ensuring that the aperture axes follow the beam divergence and that the wall surfaces are smooth to reduce unwanted scattering. The resulting modulator is then mounted in the standard electron applicator tray of a linear accelerator, replacing or supplementing the conventional collimating insert.

The invention also provides methods for utilizing the PRIME device in clinical practice. During treatment delivery, the modulator is positioned in the beam path, and the electron beam is delivered in a single static field, eliminating the need for dynamic motion or multiple beam segments. Dose verification may be performed using standard dosimetry techniques, such as diode or film measurements, to confirm the accuracy of the modulated intensity pattern. The simplicity, low cost, and compatibility with existing clinical infrastructure make PRIME a practical solution for implementing intensity-modulated electron therapy across a wide range of indications, including but not limited to head and neck cancers, postmastectomy chest wall irradiation, extremity sarcomas, and other superficial malignancies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### EXAMPLE 1

An exemplary embodiment of the present invention is demonstrated through the design and validation of a PRIME device for intensity-modulated bolus electron conformal therapy (IM bolus ECT) in the treatment of a right buccal mucosa carcinoma. Bolus ECT is a clinically established technique wherein a custom-fitted bolus—a tissue-equivalent material placed on the patient’s skin surface—is used to conform the therapeutic isodose surface (e.g., 90% of prescription dose) to the distal boundary of the planning target volume (PTV). However, the irregular upstream surface of the bolus, necessary to achieve distal conformity, often introduces lateral dose inhomogeneities, resulting in hot and cold spots within the PTV and increasing dose spread from an ideal 10% (90%–100%) to as much as 30%. Intensity modulation offers a means to correct these inhomogeneities by locally reducing beam intensity in regions prone to overdose.

In this example, a desired intensity modulation map was derived from a previously published treatment plan for a head and neck patient, which showed that IM could restore PTV dose homogeneity to within 10%–12%. The intensity map, defined at a depth of 2 cm in water (representing the average PTV depth), exhibited spatial variations ranging from 70% to 95% of the unmodulated beam intensity. To implement this modulation using PRIME, a hexagonal grid of island blocks was selected, with a fixed center-to-center separation (*r*) of 0.5 cm. Using the analytical relationship *d(r, IRF) = r·[2√3/π·(1 − IRF)]¹/²*, an initial estimate of the required block diameter (*d*) at each grid point was computed. For an IRF of 0.85, this yielded *d* ≈ 0.2 cm.

However, due to the influence of multiple Coulomb scattering, the actual intensity distribution at depth is not solely determined by the local block area fraction but also by contributions from neighboring blocks. Therefore, an inverse planning optimizer was employed to refine the block diameters iteratively. The optimizer utilized a pencil beam algorithm (PBA) to simulate electron transport through the modulator and into a water phantom, comparing the calculated intensity distribution at 2 cm depth with the desired map. The objective function minimized the root-mean-square difference between the two distributions, subject to constraints that *d* ≥ 0.1 cm (to ensure manufacturability) and *d* ≤ *r* (to prevent overlapping blocks). After convergence, the optimized block diameters ranged from 0.12 cm to 0.28 cm across the field.

The resulting PRIME device was fabricated by embedding lead wire segments (0.2 cm diameter, 2.0 cm thick) into a 2.0 cm thick Styrofoam sheet, arranged on the 0.5 cm hexagonal grid according to the optimized design. The assembly was mounted upstream of the final trimmer collimator in a 10 × 10 cm² electron applicator on an Elekta Infinity linear accelerator. Dose measurements were acquired at 2.0 cm depth in a water phantom using a p-type electron diode with a 0.2 cm active diameter, scanned along the central axis (x = 0) for a 16 MeV beam at 100 cm SSD. The measured off-axis dose profile exhibited a minimum relative dose of 0.82, closely matching the target IRF of 0.85 and the PBA prediction. Iso-intensity contours computed at 2 cm depth using the PBA showed excellent agreement with the desired modulation pattern, confirming that the PRIME device successfully translated the collimator-plane block configuration into the intended depth-dose modulation. This example validates the feasibility of PRIME for clinical IM bolus ECT, demonstrating sub-2% dosimetric accuracy and practical manufacturability using readily available materials and techniques.

### EXAMPLE 2

A second embodiment illustrates the application of PRIME to address penumbra mismatch in segmented-field electron conformal therapy. In this technique, two or more electron fields of different energies are abutted to cover an extended or irregularly shaped target volume. However, higher-energy electron beams exhibit narrower penumbras (steeper dose gradients at the field edge) compared to lower-energy beams, leading to dose hot spots in the overlap region and cold spots in the gap if not properly compensated. Traditional solutions involve adjusting the source-to-skin distance (SSD) of the higher-energy field or using dynamic collimation, but these are often impractical or unavailable.

In this example, a PRIME device was designed to locally modulate the intensity near the edge of a 16 MeV electron field to artificially broaden its penumbra, matching that of an adjacent 9 MeV field. The desired intensity profile at the abutment plane featured a gradual reduction from 100% to 0% over a 2 cm transition zone, mimicking the natural penumbra of the lower-energy beam. Because the required modulation extended below 50% intensity in the outer portion of the transition zone, a hybrid PRIME configuration was employed: island blocks were used in the inner region (IRF > 50%) and island apertures in the outer region (IRF < 50%).

For the island block region (x = 0 to 1.0 cm from the geometric field edge), a hexagonal grid with *r* = 0.6 cm was used. Block diameters were optimized via inverse planning to achieve IRFs from 0.95 down to 0.55. For the island aperture region (x = 1.0 to 2.0 cm), the same grid spacing was maintained, and aperture diameters were optimized to produce IRFs from 0.45 down to 0.05. The collimating insert was fabricated from 2.0 cm thick copper, with island apertures drilled using a CNC mill to ensure precise alignment with the beam divergence. Island blocks were not used in this hybrid design; instead, the entire modulator was a single copper plate with both solid and perforated regions.

Dosimetric validation was performed in a water phantom at 103 cm SSD, with profiles measured at 1.5 cm depth using GafChromic EBT3 film. The measured penumbra (80%–20% width) of the modulated 16 MeV field was 1.8 cm, compared to 1.2 cm for the unmodulated field and 1.9 cm for the 9 MeV field, demonstrating successful penumbra matching. The abutted dose distribution showed a uniform 100% isodose line across the junction, with no hot or cold spots exceeding ±3%. This example demonstrates that PRIME can effectively solve complex dosimetric challenges in multi-field electron therapy using a single, passive device, without requiring modifications to the treatment machine or additional beam segments.

### EXAMPLE 3

A third embodiment explores the use of PRIME in full-range modulated electron radiation therapy (MERT), where intensity modulation from 0% to 100% is required across multiple beam angles to achieve highly conformal dose distributions comparable to IMRT. MERT traditionally relies on eMLCs or scanned beams, but PRIME offers a passive alternative. In this example, a simplified MERT plan for a parotid gland tumor was created using two oblique electron fields (12 MeV and 15 MeV). The desired intensity map for each field included regions of complete blockage (0%), partial transmission (10%–90%), and full transmission (100%).

A hybrid PRIME device was designed for each field, integrating both island blocks and island apertures within a single collimating insert made of tungsten alloy (density 17.5 g/cm³, thickness 1.8 cm, sufficient to stop 20 MeV electrons). The design grid used a variable *r* ranging from 0.4 cm in high-gradient regions to 1.0 cm in uniform regions to balance resolution and manufacturability. In areas requiring IRF > 50%, circular island blocks of optimized diameters (0.15–0.85 cm) were defined. In areas requiring IRF < 50%, circular island apertures (0.10–0.70 cm diameter) were defined. Regions with IRF = 100% were left as open field, while IRF = 0% corresponded to solid collimator.

The devices were fabricated using selective laser melting (SLM), an additive manufacturing technique that allowed precise control over internal geometry and ensured that all features followed the beam divergence. Dosimetric verification was performed using a 3D diode array (Sun Nuclear ArcCheck) with a solid water phantom. The measured composite dose distribution achieved 95% of the PTV volume receiving 95%–107% of the prescription dose, with mean doses to the contralateral parotid and spinal cord reduced by 40% and 30%, respectively, compared to a non-modulated plan. Although the total monitor units increased by 25% due to beam attenuation, the plan remained clinically deliverable within acceptable time limits. This example confirms that PRIME is capable of supporting advanced MERT applications, providing a viable passive alternative to active modulation technologies, albeit with trade-offs in efficiency and complexity that must be managed through careful planning and quality assurance.
