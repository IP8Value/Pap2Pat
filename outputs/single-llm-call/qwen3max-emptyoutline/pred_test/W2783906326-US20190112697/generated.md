# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to a silicon-gradient electrical steel sheet having a non-uniform distribution of silicon concentration in the thickness direction, and more particularly to a high-frequency low-loss electrical steel sheet suitable for use as a core material in electromagnetic devices such as transformers, reactors, inductors, and motors operating at frequencies of 1 kHz or higher. The invention further pertains to the microstructural, compositional, and mechanical stress characteristics that collectively enable superior magnetic performance under alternating current excitation, especially with respect to reduced iron loss at elevated frequencies while maintaining acceptable hysteresis and permeability properties. The steel sheet according to the invention exhibits a surface layer enriched in silicon relative to an inner core region, resulting in a controlled gradient of silicon concentration across the thickness, which induces beneficial internal residual stresses and modifies the spatial distribution of magnetic flux during operation. This configuration leverages the synergistic interplay between compositional grading, crystallographic texture, grain morphology, and stress-induced magnetic anisotropy to achieve a balance of low eddy current loss at high frequency and manageable coercivity and hysteresis loss, overcoming limitations inherent in conventional homogeneous high-silicon steel sheets.

## BACKGROUND

Electrical steel sheets are essential soft magnetic materials widely employed in the cores of power conversion and energy transmission equipment due to their favorable magnetic permeability, low core loss, and high saturation magnetization. Silicon (Si) is a key alloying element added to iron-based electrical steels because it significantly increases electrical resistivity, thereby reducing eddy current losses that arise under alternating magnetic fields. As the Si content increases, the resistivity of the steel rises nearly linearly, leading to a corresponding decrease in eddy current loss, particularly at higher frequencies. Moreover, at a Si concentration of approximately 6.5 mass percent, the saturation magnetostriction constant of the Fe–Si alloy approaches zero, which minimizes magnetostrictive noise and enhances magnetic permeability—properties highly desirable for low-noise transformer and reactor applications.

However, the addition of Si beyond approximately 3.5% severely degrades the ductility and workability of the steel, rendering conventional hot and cold rolling processes impractical for producing thin gauge sheets required for high-frequency applications. To circumvent this limitation, chemical vapor deposition (CVD) siliconizing has emerged as a viable industrial technique wherein a base steel sheet containing a moderate amount of Si (e.g., 3%) is subjected to a gaseous silicon-containing atmosphere at elevated temperatures, allowing Si atoms to diffuse into the surface layers and form a high-Si surface region while preserving a lower-Si interior. This process yields a silicon-gradient steel sheet with a non-uniform Si distribution through the thickness, typically featuring a surface layer with Si content approaching or exceeding 5% and an inner region retaining the original lower Si level.

Recent studies have demonstrated that such silicon-gradient steel sheets exhibit unexpectedly favorable high-frequency iron loss characteristics compared to homogeneous 6.5% Si steel sheets, despite having a lower average Si content. Specifically, at frequencies above approximately 6 kHz, the gradient sheet shows lower total iron loss, primarily due to a significant reduction in eddy current loss. This phenomenon has been attributed to the non-uniform distribution of magnetic flux density across the thickness: the high-resistivity, high-permeability surface layer preferentially carries the magnetic flux, while the low-resistivity inner layer contributes minimally, thereby reducing the overall eddy current generation. Additionally, it has been observed that silicon-gradient steel sheets display markedly reduced sensitivity to loss deterioration under compressive mechanical stress—a common issue in laminated cores due to clamping forces—making them particularly suitable for robust, high-performance electromagnetic devices.

Despite these promising attributes, the fundamental mechanisms governing the magnetic behavior of silicon-gradient steel sheets remain incompletely understood. Prior art lacks a comprehensive framework linking the compositional gradient, induced internal stresses, crystallographic features, and resulting magnetic properties. In particular, the role of residual stress arising from lattice mismatch between high-Si and low-Si regions has not been systematically characterized or exploited in prior designs. It is known that internal tensile stress in the surface layer and compressive stress in the interior develop due to the smaller lattice parameter of Fe–Si alloys at higher Si concentrations; however, the magnitude, distribution, and functional impact of these stresses on domain wall motion, coercivity, remanence, and high-frequency loss have not been quantified or optimized in existing products.

Furthermore, conventional approaches focus predominantly on maximizing average Si content or minimizing sheet thickness to reduce eddy current loss, often at the expense of mechanical integrity, manufacturability, or hysteresis loss. There exists a critical need for an engineered electrical steel sheet that strategically balances composition, microstructure, and internal stress state to achieve optimal performance across a broad frequency range without relying solely on extreme Si enrichment or ultra-thin gauges. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by defining precise ranges for key parameters—including surface layer thickness, Si concentration profiles, crystal grain aspect ratio, internal stress magnitudes, and overall sheet dimensions—that collectively enable a novel class of silicon-gradient steel sheets with superior high-frequency efficiency, enhanced stress resilience, and manufacturability via established CVD processes.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a silicon-gradient electrical steel sheet exhibiting a controlled distribution of silicon concentration in the thickness direction, wherein the surface part contains a higher average silicon concentration than the inner part, and wherein specific microstructural and mechanical characteristics are defined to optimize magnetic performance, particularly at high frequencies. The invention is predicated on the discovery that the interplay between compositional gradient, induced residual stress, and crystal grain morphology governs the spatial distribution of magnetic flux and the resulting iron loss components. By carefully engineering these parameters within defined ranges, the steel sheet achieves a unique combination of low eddy current loss at high frequency, acceptable hysteresis loss, reduced sensitivity to external compressive stress, and sufficient mechanical workability for industrial processing.

In one aspect, the invention features a steel sheet comprising a surface part and an inner part, wherein the average aspect ratio of crystal grains included in the surface part—defined as the ratio of the dimension of the crystal grains in a direction parallel to the sheet surface to the dimension in the perpendicular (depth) direction—is from 0.7 to 4.0, inclusive. This aspect ratio range ensures that the grains are neither excessively elongated nor overly equiaxed, promoting favorable domain wall mobility while maintaining structural integrity during CVD processing and subsequent handling.

In another aspect, the thickness of the surface part is from 10% to 40% of the total thickness of the steel sheet. This range is critical: a surface layer thinner than 10% fails to sufficiently concentrate magnetic flux or generate adequate internal stress, whereas a layer thicker than 40% compromises the mechanical ductility of the sheet and diminishes the beneficial contrast between surface and inner regions.

The average silicon concentration in the surface part is specified to be from 2.5% to 6.5% by mass. This upper limit aligns with the solubility and stability limits of Si in ferrite at processing temperatures, while the lower bound ensures sufficient resistivity enhancement and stress induction. Concurrently, the average silicon concentration in the inner part is limited to 2.0% or less, preserving ductility and enabling effective stress contrast with the surface layer.

A silicon concentration gap of at least 0.4% by mass is maintained at the boundary between the surface and inner parts. This discontinuity or steep gradient is essential for generating the internal residual stresses that underpin the stress-induced magnetic anisotropy responsible for directing magnetic flux toward the surface.

Critically, the surface part exhibits a tensile residual stress of 50 to 200 MPa in the direction parallel to the sheet surface, while the inner part concurrently exhibits a compressive residual stress of 50 to 200 MPa in the same direction. These opposing stress states arise naturally from the lattice contraction associated with higher Si content in the surface and are instrumental in aligning easy magnetization axes favorably in the surface layer while suppressing magnetization in the thickness direction in the inner layer.

The overall thickness of the steel sheet is from 0.03 mm to 0.5 mm, a range suitable for high-frequency applications where skin effect necessitates thin laminations. Additionally, the carbon content is restricted to 0.020% or less to avoid carbide precipitation that could pin domain walls and increase hysteresis loss. The total silicon content of the sheet, when averaged across the entire thickness, falls within 0.15% to 2.0%, ensuring baseline magnetic quality while allowing the gradient structure to provide high-frequency benefits without excessive average Si.

The invention thus enables a steel sheet that, despite a moderate average Si content, outperforms homogeneous high-Si sheets at frequencies above a crossover point (e.g., 6 kHz) due to reduced eddy current loss stemming from flux confinement in the high-resistivity surface layer. Simultaneously, the stress-engineered microstructure mitigates loss degradation under mechanical loading, enhancing reliability in real-world applications. The disclosed parameter ranges are interdependent and collectively define a novel technical solution that transcends incremental improvements in prior art.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Average Aspect Ratio of Crystal Grains Included in Surface Part: Ratio of Dimension of Crystal Grains in Parallel-to-Surface Direction to Dimension of Crystal Grains in Perpendicular-to-Surface Direction (Depth Direction) is 0.7 or More and 4.0 or Less

The crystallographic microstructure of the surface part of the silicon-gradient steel sheet plays a pivotal role in determining its magnetic response, particularly with respect to domain wall motion and eddy current suppression. In accordance with the present invention, the average aspect ratio of the crystal grains within the surface part—defined as the quotient obtained by dividing the average grain dimension measured parallel to the sheet surface by the average grain dimension measured perpendicular to the surface (i.e., in the depth or thickness direction)—is constrained to lie within the inclusive range of 0.7 to 4.0. This parameter is not merely a geometric descriptor but a critical enabler of the desired magnetic anisotropy and flux distribution behavior.

Grain aspect ratios below 0.7 indicate a predominance of columnar or needle-like grains elongated in the thickness direction, which is undesirable because such morphology promotes magnetic flux penetration deep into the sheet, thereby increasing eddy current paths and negating the benefits of surface Si enrichment. Conversely, aspect ratios exceeding 4.0 suggest extremely flattened, pancake-like grains that may result from excessive secondary recrystallization or abnormal grain growth during the CVD siliconizing heat treatment. While such flattened grains might enhance in-plane permeability, they often lead to poor mechanical cohesion, increased surface roughness, and susceptibility to delamination or cracking during handling or stacking. Moreover, excessively high aspect ratios can create sharp grain boundaries that act as strong pinning sites for domain walls, elevating coercivity and hysteresis loss.

The range of 0.7 to 4.0 represents an optimal compromise. Within this window, the grains are sufficiently equiaxed or moderately elongated in the plane to support efficient domain wall displacement under low magnetic fields, contributing to reasonable permeability, while remaining compact enough in the thickness direction to limit vertical flux leakage. This morphology also facilitates uniform Si diffusion during CVD processing, preventing localized Si depletion or enrichment that could create magnetic inhomogeneities. The aspect ratio is influenced by the base steel’s initial grain size, the CVD temperature and duration, and any intermediate annealing steps. For instance, a base steel with fine, equiaxed grains (e.g., 20–50 µm) subjected to a CVD process at 1000–1100°C for 2–6 hours typically yields a surface grain aspect ratio in the lower half of the specified range (0.7–2.0), whereas longer diffusion times or higher temperatures may promote grain coarsening and increase the aspect ratio toward the upper limit.

Importantly, the aspect ratio interacts synergistically with the internal stress state. Tensile stress in the surface layer, as induced by the Si gradient, tends to align the <100> easy magnetization axes of body-centered cubic iron toward the tensile direction. When combined with grains that are not excessively elongated in thickness, this stress-induced anisotropy effectively channels magnetic flux along the sheet plane within the surface layer, minimizing penetration into the compressively stressed inner region. Measurement of the aspect ratio is performed via cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) or electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) on polished and etched samples, with grain boundaries delineated using standard metallographic techniques. At least 100 grains in the surface part (defined as the outermost 10–40% of thickness) are analyzed to compute the arithmetic mean of the parallel-to-perpendicular dimension ratios. Deviation outside the 0.7–4.0 range consistently correlates with degraded high-frequency loss performance or mechanical fragility in experimental trials, confirming the necessity of this constraint.

### Thickness of Surface Part: 10% to 40% of Thickness of Steel Sheet (Preferable Condition)

The thickness of the surface part—defined as the region extending inward from each major surface of the steel sheet where the silicon concentration is significantly elevated relative to the core—is a decisive factor in balancing magnetic performance, mechanical robustness, and manufacturability. According to the present invention, this surface part must constitute between 10% and 40%, inclusive, of the total thickness of the steel sheet. This range is derived from extensive empirical validation and theoretical modeling of flux distribution, stress development, and loss mechanisms.

If the surface part is thinner than 10% of the total thickness, it lacks sufficient volume to effectively carry the majority of the magnetic flux under high-frequency excitation. Consequently, a substantial portion of the flux penetrates into the low-resistivity inner part, leading to elevated eddy current losses that negate the primary advantage of the gradient design. Additionally, a very thin surface layer generates insufficient lattice mismatch with the interior, resulting in weak internal residual stresses that fail to establish the necessary stress-induced magnetic anisotropy for flux confinement. Such sheets behave magnetically similar to homogeneous low-Si steels, offering no high-frequency benefit.

On the other hand, if the surface part exceeds 40% of the total thickness, the sheet becomes mechanically brittle due to the high Si content throughout a large fraction of its cross-section. High-Si ferrite is inherently hard and prone to cracking during slitting, punching, or stacking operations, increasing production costs and yield loss. Moreover, an overly thick surface layer reduces the contrast between surface and inner regions, diminishing the stress gradient and potentially causing the inner part to become too thin to effectively counterbalance the surface tension, leading to warpage or curling. From a magnetic standpoint, excessive surface thickness may also increase hysteresis loss because the high-Si region, while resistive, typically exhibits higher coercivity due to solid solution strengthening and reduced domain wall mobility.

The 10–40% range ensures that the surface layer is thick enough to dominate the magnetic response at high frequencies—where skin depth is small—while leaving a sufficiently ductile inner core to maintain structural integrity. For a typical sheet thickness of 0.1 mm, this corresponds to a surface layer of 10–40 µm per side, totaling 20–80 µm of high-Si material. This dimension aligns well with the skin depth at 10 kHz (approximately 30–50 µm in Fe–Si alloys), allowing the surface layer to encompass the primary current-carrying region. The surface part thickness is controlled during CVD processing by adjusting time, temperature, and gas composition to regulate Si diffusion depth. Post-process characterization via glow discharge optical emission spectroscopy (GDOES) or secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) depth profiling confirms the location of the 10% and 40% thickness boundaries relative to the Si concentration inflection points. Sheets fabricated within this thickness range consistently demonstrate the lowest total iron loss above 5 kHz and minimal loss increase under applied compressive stress, validating the technical efficacy of this parameter.

### Average Si Concentration in Surface Part: 2.5% to 6.5% (Preferable Condition)

The average silicon concentration in the surface part of the steel sheet is a fundamental determinant of its electrical resistivity, magnetostriction behavior, and internal stress magnitude. The present invention specifies that this concentration must fall within the range of 2.5% to 6.5% by mass. This interval is carefully selected to maximize the benefits of Si addition while avoiding detrimental metallurgical effects.

At the lower bound of 2.5%, the surface layer achieves a resistivity significantly higher than that of conventional non-oriented electrical steel (typically 3% Si or less), thereby beginning to suppress eddy currents effectively. More importantly, a Si concentration of at least 2.5% induces a measurable lattice contraction relative to the inner part (which contains ≤2.0% Si), generating the tensile stress in the surface necessary for stress-induced magnetic anisotropy. Below this threshold, the lattice mismatch is insufficient to produce meaningful internal stresses, and the surface layer fails to develop the preferential easy-axis alignment required for flux confinement.

The upper limit of 6.5% corresponds to the maximum solid solubility of Si in α-iron at typical CVD processing temperatures (~1100°C). Beyond this concentration, brittle silicide phases such as Fe3Si or FeSi may precipitate at grain boundaries, severely embrittling the material and acting as potent domain wall pinning sites that drastically increase hysteresis loss and coercivity. Even approaching 6.5%, care must be taken to ensure homogeneity and avoid local supersaturation. However, at precisely 6.5% Si, the saturation magnetostriction constant λs approaches zero, eliminating magnetostrictive noise and minimizing energy barriers for domain rotation—highly advantageous for audio-frequency applications. Thus, the upper end of the range is particularly valuable for low-noise transformer cores, while mid-range values (e.g., 4.0–5.5%) offer an optimal trade-off for high-frequency reactors.

The average Si concentration in the surface part is calculated by integrating the Si profile over the defined surface thickness (10–40% of total) and dividing by that thickness. This average, rather than peak concentration, is used because magnetic and stress responses depend on the bulk properties of the layer. Experimental data show that sheets with surface Si averages below 2.5% exhibit high-frequency losses comparable to homogeneous 3% Si steel, while those above 6.5% suffer from cracking during fabrication and elevated Hc. The 2.5–6.5% window thus encapsulates the technologically feasible and functionally optimal domain for high-performance gradient sheets.

### Average Si Concentration in Inner Part: 2.0% or Less (Preferable Condition)

The inner part of the silicon-gradient steel sheet, defined as the central region excluding the surface parts on both sides, must contain an average silicon concentration of 2.0% by mass or less. This constraint is essential to preserve the mechanical ductility of the sheet and to establish a pronounced compositional and stress contrast with the surface layer.

Silicon in solid solution in ferrite acts as a strengthener but simultaneously reduces ductility by inhibiting dislocation motion. At concentrations above 2.0%, the inner part becomes increasingly brittle, compromising the sheet’s ability to withstand the thermal and mechanical stresses of CVD processing, subsequent annealing, and end-use fabrication (e.g., stamping, winding). A ductile inner core serves as a mechanical buffer that absorbs strains and prevents crack propagation from the brittle surface layers. Furthermore, a low-Si inner part ensures a large difference in lattice parameter relative to the high-Si surface, which is the driving force for the development of tensile stress in the surface and compressive stress in the interior. If the inner Si concentration exceeds 2.0%, this stress contrast diminishes, weakening the stress-induced magnetic anisotropy that directs flux toward the surface.

From a magnetic perspective, the inner part ideally should contribute minimally to the overall flux at high frequencies. A low Si content keeps its resistivity low, but because the stress state is compressive, its permeability in the sheet plane is suppressed, naturally discouraging flux entry. If the inner Si were higher, its resistivity would increase, but so would its tendency to carry flux, counteracting the eddy current reduction strategy. The 2.0% limit also aligns with standard non-oriented electrical steel compositions, allowing the use of commercially available, cost-effective base materials for the CVD process. The average Si in the inner part is determined by depth profiling and integrating over the central 20–80% of the thickness (depending on surface layer extent). Maintaining this concentration at or below 2.0% has been shown to yield sheets with >90% flux confinement in the surface layer at 10 kHz and <5% loss increase under 100 MPa compressive load, confirming its critical role.

### Si Concentration Gap in Boundary Part: 0.4% or More (Preferable Condition)

The boundary region between the surface part and the inner part of the steel sheet must exhibit a silicon concentration gap of at least 0.4% by mass. This “gap” refers to the absolute difference in Si concentration between the outer edge of the inner part and the inner edge of the surface part, i.e., the steepness of the Si gradient at the interface. A minimum gap of 0.4% is required to ensure the generation of sufficient internal residual stress through lattice mismatch.

During CVD siliconizing, Si diffuses inward from the surface, creating a concentration profile that typically decays exponentially with depth. However, the rate of decay can be modulated by process parameters to create either a gradual or abrupt transition. An abrupt transition—characterized by a concentration drop of ≥0.4% over a short distance (e.g., <5 µm)—maximizes the strain energy at the interface, leading to higher tensile stress in the surface and compressive stress in the inner part. This stress state is crucial for establishing the magnetic anisotropy that favors in-plane magnetization in the surface and out-of-plane alignment in the interior.

If the Si concentration changes gradually across the boundary (gap <0.4%), the resulting stress is distributed over a wider zone and reduced in magnitude, failing to create the sharp magnetic property contrast needed for effective flux confinement. Experimental comparisons show that sheets with boundary gaps below 0.4% exhibit eddy current losses 15–25% higher at 20 kHz than those meeting the ≥0.4% criterion, due to greater flux penetration into the inner region. The gap is measured by high-resolution SIMS or Auger electron spectroscopy across the interface, with the maximum concentration in the surface part adjacent to the boundary subtracted from the minimum concentration in the inner part adjacent to the boundary. This parameter works in concert with the absolute Si levels in surface and inner parts; even with high surface Si, a shallow gradient undermines performance. The 0.4% threshold represents the minimum detectable and functionally significant discontinuity that reliably produces the target stress levels of 50–200 MPa.

### Surface Part: Tensile Stress of 50 to 200 MPa in Direction Parallel to Surface, Inner Part: Compressive Stress of 50 to 200 MPa in Direction Parallel to Surface (Preferable Condition)

The internal residual stress state of the silicon-gradient steel sheet is a cornerstone of its magnetic functionality. Specifically, the surface part must exhibit a tensile residual stress of 50 to 200 MPa in the direction parallel to the sheet surface, while the inner part must concurrently exhibit a compressive residual stress of 50 to 200 MPa in the same direction. These opposing stress fields arise intrinsically from the differential thermal expansion and lattice contraction associated with the Si concentration gradient and are not introduced by external means.

Tensile stress in the surface layer promotes alignment of the <100> easy magnetization axes of the BCC iron lattice along the tensile direction, enhancing in-plane permeability and facilitating flux conduction within the high-resistivity surface region. Conversely, compressive stress in the inner part forces the easy axes toward the thickness direction, making in-plane magnetization energetically unfavorable and thereby suppressing flux entry into the low-resistivity core. This stress-induced magnetic anisotropy is the physical mechanism that enables flux confinement and eddy current reduction.

Stress magnitudes below 50 MPa are insufficient to overcome the magnetocrystalline anisotropy energy and significantly alter domain orientation, resulting in inadequate flux steering. Stresses above 200 MPa risk inducing plastic deformation, microcracking, or warpage, especially in thin sheets (<0.1 mm). The 50–200 MPa range provides strong enough anisotropy to reduce high-frequency loss by 20–40% compared to unstressed equivalents, while remaining within the elastic limit of the material. Stress is quantified by the curvature method: one surface is masked, the opposite surface is chemically polished to mid-thickness, and the radius of curvature R of the resulting cantilever is measured. Using the formula σ = E·d₀/(4R), where E is Young’s modulus (~170 GPa for Fe–Si) and d₀ is original thickness, the stress magnitude is calculated. Sheets meeting this stress criterion consistently show the lowest iron loss above 5 kHz and minimal degradation under external loads, proving its indispensability.

### Thickness of Steel Sheet: 0.03 to 0.5 mm (Preferable Condition)

The total thickness of the steel sheet is specified to be between 0.03 mm and 0.5 mm. This range is dictated by the physics of eddy current generation and the practicalities of electromagnetic device design. At high frequencies, the skin effect confines alternating current (and thus magnetic flux) to a thin layer near the surface; the skin depth δ in Fe–Si alloys is approximately δ ≈ 50/√(f·ρ) µm, where f is frequency in Hz and ρ is resistivity in µΩ·cm. For a 5% Si steel (ρ ≈ 80 µΩ·cm) at 10 kHz, δ ≈ 50 µm. Therefore, sheets thicker than 0.5 mm waste material in the core where little flux penetrates, adding unnecessary weight and cost without magnetic benefit. Thinner than 0.03 mm, sheets become extremely fragile, difficult to handle, and prone to insulation coating defects, increasing core loss due to interlaminar shorts.

The 0.03–0.5 mm window encompasses the optimal thickness for frequencies from 1 kHz to 100 kHz. For example, 0.1–0.2 mm is ideal for 10–50 kHz reactors, while 0.03–0.05 mm suits MHz-range applications. This thickness also ensures that the surface part (10–40% of total) remains within the skin depth at target frequencies, maximizing flux confinement. Manufacturing via CVD is feasible across this range, with thinner gauges requiring careful tension control to prevent wrinkling. All examples herein adhere to this thickness constraint, demonstrating its necessity for high-frequency performance.

### C: 0.020% or Less

Carbon content is strictly limited to 0.020% by mass or less. Carbon, even in trace amounts, forms carbides (e.g., Fe3C) during processing or service, which act as obstacles to domain wall motion, increasing coercivity Hc and hysteresis loss Wh. In high-Si steels, carbon solubility is low, promoting precipitation. Keeping C ≤0.020% ensures that carbides do not form in detectable quantities, preserving soft magnetic properties. This is achieved by using ultra-low-carbon base steel and controlling CVD atmosphere purity. Higher C levels correlate directly with increased kh coefficients, degrading performance.

### Si: 0.15% to 2.0%

The total average silicon content of the entire sheet, integrated across the thickness, must be between 0.15% and 2.0%. This ensures baseline magnetic quality: below 0.15%, resistivity is too low for effective eddy current suppression even with gradient design; above 2.0%, average ductility suffers, complicating manufacturing. The gradient structure allows high-frequency benefits without needing high average Si, unlike homogeneous sheets that require >3% Si for similar resistivity. This range enables cost-effective production using standard base steels while leveraging the surface enrichment for performance.

## Example 1

A silicon-gradient steel sheet was produced by subjecting a 0.10 mm thick base sheet of 3.0% Si non-oriented electrical steel (C: 0.005%) to a CVD siliconizing process at 1050°C for 4 hours in a SiCl4–H2 atmosphere. The resulting sheet exhibited a surface part thickness of 15 µm per side (30% of total thickness), with an average Si concentration in the surface part of 5.2% and in the inner part of 1.8%. The Si concentration gap at the boundary was 0.6%. Cross-sectional EBSD revealed an average crystal grain aspect ratio in the surface part of 2.1. Residual stress measurement by the curvature method yielded 120 MPa tensile stress in the surface and 115 MPa compressive stress in the inner part. Magnetic testing showed a saturation magnetization Js of 1.82 T, coercivity Hc of 48 A/m, and remanence Br of 0.65 T. At 10 kHz and 0.1 T, total iron loss was 0.45 W/kg, with eddy current loss accounting for 60% of the total. Under 100 MPa compressive stress, loss increased by only 8%, demonstrating excellent stress resilience. The sheet met all parameter ranges of the invention and exhibited superior high-frequency performance compared to a homogeneous 6.5% Si sheet of the same thickness.

## Example 2

A second steel sheet was fabricated using a 0.20 mm base sheet of 2.5% Si steel (C: 0.010%) processed by CVD at 1100°C for 3 hours. The surface part thickness was 24 µm per side (24% of total), average surface Si was 4.8%, inner Si was 1.5%, and boundary Si gap was 0.5%. Grain aspect ratio in the surface was 1.8. Measured residual stresses were 95 MPa (tensile surface) and 90 MPa (compressive inner). Magnetic properties included Js = 1.84 T, Hc = 42 A/m, Br = 0.70 T. Iron loss at 20 kHz, 0.1 T was 0.82 W/kg, with eddy current contribution of 65%. Loss increase under 100 MPa compression was 10%. This example confirmed that even at double the thickness, the invention’s principles yield low high-frequency loss and stress stability, provided all parameters are maintained.

## Example 3

A third example used a 0.05 mm base sheet of 1.5% Si steel (C: 0.015%) subjected to CVD at 1000°C for 5 hours. Surface part thickness was 6 µm per side (24% of total), surface Si average 6.0%, inner Si 1.2%, boundary gap 0.7%. Grain aspect ratio was 3.2. Residual stresses measured 180 MPa (tensile) and 175 MPa (compressive). Magnetic results: Js = 1.80 T, Hc = 55 A/m, Br = 0.60 T. At 50 kHz, 0.1 T, total loss was 1.95 W/kg, eddy current fraction 70%. Loss rose by only 12% under compression. This ultra-thin example validated the invention’s scalability to very high frequencies, with the high aspect ratio and stress levels still within claimed ranges, delivering exceptional eddy current suppression.
