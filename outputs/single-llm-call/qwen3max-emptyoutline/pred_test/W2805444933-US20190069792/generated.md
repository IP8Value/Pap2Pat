# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to systems, methods, and computer-readable media for the diagnosis, characterization, and treatment guidance of atrial fibrillation (AF) in human patients. More specifically, the invention provides a novel computational framework for analyzing intracardiac electrograms (IEGMs) to identify regional electrophysiological features that correlate with critical sites for AF perpetuation. The invention introduces two interrelated quantitative metrics—Regional Dominant Frequency (RDF) and Wave Break Rate (WBR)—derived from high-density, short-duration electrogram recordings, which together enable the identification of putative AF sources or drivers. These metrics are computed using a signal processing pipeline that avoids the error-prone step of local activation time estimation and instead leverages the spatiotemporal coherence of wavefront propagation across a localized region of the atrial endocardium. The invention is particularly applicable in the context of catheter ablation procedures, where it provides real-time or near-real-time mapping of atrial tissue to guide targeted ablation at sites exhibiting high RDF and low WBR, thereby improving procedural efficacy and long-term outcomes in both paroxysmal and persistent forms of atrial fibrillation.

## BACKGROUND

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent sustained cardiac arrhythmia encountered in clinical practice and represents a major cause of morbidity and mortality, primarily due to its strong association with thromboembolic stroke, heart failure, and reduced quality of life. Despite decades of research and technological advancement, the underlying mechanisms responsible for the initiation and, more critically, the perpetuation of AF remain incompletely understood. This knowledge gap has directly contributed to the suboptimal success rates of current therapeutic interventions, particularly catheter ablation, which remains the cornerstone of rhythm control strategies for symptomatic patients refractory to pharmacological management. Conventional ablation approaches have largely been guided by anatomical landmarks—most notably, pulmonary vein isolation (PVI)—or by empirical substrate modification targeting areas of complex fractionated electrograms (CFE), low voltage, or high dominant frequency (DF). However, these strategies have demonstrated inconsistent results, especially in patients with persistent or long-standing persistent AF, where recurrence rates can exceed 50% within one year post-procedure.

The limitations of existing approaches stem from fundamental challenges in accurately interpreting the chaotic and heterogeneous electrical activity that characterizes AF. Intracardiac electrograms recorded during AF often exhibit rapid, irregular, and fractionated morphologies, reflecting the complex interplay of multiple reentrant wavelets, focal impulses, and wavefront collisions. Traditional signal analysis techniques, such as the calculation of CFE-mean or global dominant frequency, provide only a coarse-grained view of this complexity and fail to capture the dynamic, localized nature of wavefront propagation. For instance, CFE mapping, while conceptually appealing as a marker of slow conduction and potential reentry, is highly susceptible to noise, far-field signals, and variations in catheter contact and orientation, leading to poor reproducibility and questionable clinical utility. Similarly, dominant frequency analysis, which identifies the peak frequency in the power spectrum of an electrogram, is typically performed over long time windows (e.g., 10–30 seconds) to ensure spectral stability. While this yields a robust measure of the average rate of electrical activity, it inherently obscures transient, high-frequency events that may be mechanistically significant, such as the passage of a stable rotor or the occurrence of a wave break.

A wave break, defined as a point or line of conduction block where a propagating wavefront fragments into multiple daughter wavelets, is a central tenet of the multiple wavelet hypothesis of AF and is also a key feature in models of localized rotational drivers (rotors). The ability to reliably detect and quantify wave breaks in human atrial tissue could therefore provide a direct window into the arrhythmogenic substrate. However, direct visualization of wave breaks requires high-resolution, simultaneous mapping of a large atrial area—a feat that is technically challenging with current clinical mapping systems. Basket catheters, which offer panoramic coverage, suffer from poor electrode-tissue contact, limited spatial resolution due to wide inter-electrode spacing, and susceptibility to artifacts. Moreover, advanced mapping techniques like phase mapping, which attempt to reconstruct wavefront trajectories from activation times, are critically dependent on the accurate identification of local activation times—a task that is notoriously difficult in the context of fractionated, low-amplitude, or continuous electrograms typical of AF.

Consequently, there exists a critical unmet need for a robust, computationally efficient, and clinically practical method to characterize the local dynamics of wavefront propagation during AF. Such a method should be capable of operating on data acquired with standard, high-density mapping catheters used in routine electrophysiology laboratories, without requiring simultaneous acquisition from a large atrial area. It should provide a quantitative measure that is sensitive to the transient disruptions in wavefront coherence that signify wave breaks, while also being stable enough to allow for meaningful comparison across different atrial sites and patients. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a novel signal processing paradigm that synthesizes information from all electrodes of a high-density catheter to generate a regional signal whose spectral properties directly reflect the quality of wavefront propagation. This approach bypasses the pitfalls of local activation time estimation and instead focuses on the collective behavior of the electrode array, yielding two powerful new metrics: Regional Dominant Frequency (RDF) and Wave Break Rate (WBR).

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a system, method, and associated computer-readable medium for identifying and characterizing critical sites for atrial fibrillation (AF) perpetuation to guide targeted catheter ablation therapy. The core of the invention is a novel computational algorithm that processes intracardiac electrograms (IEGMs) recorded from a high-density mapping catheter to generate two key quantitative metrics: Regional Dominant Frequency (RDF) and Wave Break Rate (WBR). These metrics are derived from a synthesized regional signal that represents the collective electrical activity of a localized atrial region, thereby providing a robust assessment of local wavefront dynamics without relying on the error-prone estimation of individual local activation times.

The method begins by acquiring a segment of IEGMs from a high-density catheter, such as a circular mapping catheter with 20 or more closely spaced electrodes, positioned at a specific site within the atrium. Each bipolar electrogram from the catheter is independently preprocessed using a signal transformation that converts its complex morphology into a smooth, pulse-like waveform, effectively highlighting the timing of electrical activity without attempting to pinpoint a single activation time. These preprocessed signals from all electrode pairs are then averaged to create a single, composite regional signal. This composite signal is subsequently filtered using a specialized two-sided exponential finite impulse response (FIR) filter, which is designed to attenuate high-frequency components that arise from incoherent, temporally dispersed electrical activity—a hallmark of wave break.

The power spectrum of this filtered regional signal is then computed using a short-time Fourier transform (STFT) with a high temporal resolution (e.g., a 1-second window with 95% overlap). From this time-varying power spectrum, the instantaneous Regional Dominant Frequency (iRDF) is calculated as the frequency with the highest power at each time point. The RDF for the recording site is defined as the upper quartile of the iRDF values over the entire segment, providing a robust measure of the highest sustained frequency of coherent wavefront activity in that region. Concurrently, a wave break is identified as any transient event where the iRDF drops by more than a predefined threshold (e.g., 3 Hz or below 0.5 Hz) and persists for a minimum duration (e.g., 100 ms). The Wave Break Rate (WBR) is then calculated as the number of such wave break events per second, serving as a direct quantitative measure of the instability and fragmentation of wavefront propagation at that site.

The invention further establishes that the combination of a high RDF and a low WBR at a given atrial site is a strong predictor of a critical driver for AF perpetuation. Clinical validation has shown that ablation lesions placed at sites exhibiting this specific signature—high RDF and low WBR—are significantly associated with the acute termination of AF, either to sinus rhythm or to a more organized atrial tachycardia. The method provides a clear, objective, and quantifiable target for ablation that is superior to traditional metrics like CFE-mean or global DF, as it directly probes the underlying mechanism of wavefront stability versus fragmentation.

The system implementing this method can be integrated into an existing electroanatomic mapping (EAM) system or operate as a standalone software module. It receives raw or preprocessed IEGM data, performs the aforementioned signal processing steps, and outputs the RDF and WBR values for each mapped site. These values can be displayed on a three-dimensional reconstruction of the patient's atrial geometry as color-coded maps, allowing the electrophysiologist to visually identify candidate ablation targets in real-time or near-real-time. The invention also specifies the minimum data requirements for reliable metric estimation: a segment duration of at least 4 seconds is sufficient for a stable RDF estimate, while a segment of at least 25 seconds is required for a reliable WBR estimate. This knowledge allows for efficient data collection during a clinical procedure, minimizing mapping time while ensuring diagnostic accuracy.

In summary, the invention provides a transformative approach to AF substrate mapping by introducing a novel, mechanism-based framework for identifying critical arrhythmogenic sites. By quantifying the dynamic interplay between the frequency of electrical activity and the stability of its propagation, the RDF and WBR metrics offer a powerful new tool for guiding precision ablation therapy, with the potential to significantly improve procedural success rates and long-term outcomes for patients suffering from atrial fibrillation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### 1A. Regional Dominant Frequency and Wave Break Rate

The present invention introduces a novel signal processing methodology for the analysis of intracardiac electrograms (IEGMs) to derive two key electrophysiological metrics: Regional Dominant Frequency (RDF) and Wave Break Rate (WBR). These metrics are designed to provide a quantitative and mechanistic assessment of the local atrial substrate during atrial fibrillation (AF), specifically focusing on the characteristics of wavefront propagation within a localized region of the atrial endocardium. The core innovation of this approach lies in its ability to synthesize information from a high-density array of electrodes to generate a representative regional signal, the spectral properties of which are directly indicative of the coherence and stability of the underlying electrical wavefronts. This method deliberately circumvents the traditional and often problematic step of estimating local activation times (ATs) for individual electrograms, which is known to be highly unreliable in the context of the complex, fractionated, and rapidly changing signals typical of AF. Instead, the invention leverages the collective temporal behavior of the entire electrode array to infer the quality of wavefront propagation.

The process begins with the acquisition of IEGMs from a high-density mapping catheter, such as a circular catheter with 20 or more electrodes arranged in a close configuration (e.g., with inter-electrode spacing of 1-2 mm). The catheter is positioned at a specific site within the left atrium, and a segment of electrical activity is recorded while the patient is in AF. The duration of this segment is a critical parameter, which will be discussed in detail in subsequent sections, but is typically on the order of several seconds to tens of seconds. The raw bipolar electrograms from each adjacent pair of electrodes on the catheter are first subjected to a preprocessing step. This preprocessing involves a signal transformation that converts the complex, multi-phasic morphology of a typical AF electrogram into a smooth, unimodal, pulse-like waveform. A common and effective method for this transformation is to apply a filter that emphasizes the sharp transitions and peaks in the original signal, effectively creating a series of Gaussian-shaped pulses that correspond to the primary periods of electrical activity detected by that electrode pair. The purpose of this step is not to isolate a single "activation" time, but rather to create a robust indicator of when the electrode is actively sensing electrical depolarization, regardless of the signal's complexity.

Once all the individual bipolar electrograms have been preprocessed into their respective pulse trains, the next step is a spatial synthesis. The preprocessed signals from all electrode pairs of the catheter are averaged together to produce a single, composite signal. This composite signal is a crucial construct of the invention, as it represents the aggregate electrical activity of the entire region of myocardium in contact with or near the catheter. In a region where a stable, coherent wavefront is propagating, the electrical activity sensed by all electrodes will be highly synchronized. Consequently, the peaks of the individual preprocessed pulse trains will occur nearly simultaneously, and their average will result in a composite signal with large, well-defined peaks corresponding to the passage of each wavefront. Conversely, in a region characterized by wave break or other forms of disorganized activity, the electrical activity sensed by the different electrodes will be temporally dispersed. The peaks of the individual pulse trains will be spread out over time, and their average will produce a composite signal with smaller, more numerous, and higher-frequency fluctuations, reflecting the lack of a unified wavefront.

To enhance the sensitivity of the composite signal to these differences in wavefront coherence, it is passed through a specialized low-pass filter. In the preferred embodiment, this is a two-sided exponential finite impulse response (FIR) filter with a specific kernel designed to strongly attenuate high-frequency components while preserving lower-frequency, more coherent signals. The mathematical form of this filter is given by \(h_{n} = \exp{(\left. - \middle| - 1 + \frac{n - 1}{90} \right|)}\) for \(n = 1,\cdots,180\), which creates a smooth, bell-shaped weighting function. This filter is particularly effective at smoothing out the high-frequency "noise" generated by the temporal dispersion of activations during a wave break, causing a pronounced drop in the amplitude of the composite signal during such events. The mean amplitude of the resulting filtered signal is then removed to create a zero-mean signal suitable for spectral analysis.

The final step in the computation is the spectral analysis of this filtered, zero-mean, composite regional signal. To capture the dynamic nature of AF, a short-time Fourier transform (STFT) is employed. The STFT divides the signal into short, overlapping segments (e.g., 1-second segments with 95% overlap) and computes the power spectrum for each segment using a fast Fourier transform (FFT) after applying a windowing function, such as a Hanning window, to reduce spectral leakage. This process yields a time-varying power spectrum, \(P(f, t)\), which shows how the power distribution across different frequencies changes over the course of the recording. From this, the instantaneous Regional Dominant Frequency (iRDF) is calculated at each time point \(t\) as the frequency \(f\) that maximizes the power spectrum, i.e., \(\text{iRDF}(t) = \arg\max_{f}P(f, t)\). The iRDF is a dynamic measure that fluctuates with the changing nature of the local electrical activity. The RDF for the entire recording site is then derived as a stable, representative value from this time series; in the preferred embodiment, it is defined as the upper quartile of the iRDF values over the entire segment duration. This choice of the upper quartile ensures that the RDF reflects the highest sustained frequency of coherent activity, which is hypothesized to be associated with a potential driver or source.

Simultaneously, the time series of iRDF is analyzed to detect wave break events. A wave break is defined by a specific pattern in the iRDF: a transient drop in frequency that is both significant in magnitude and duration. In the preferred embodiment, a wave break is identified when the iRDF falls more than 3 Hz below the site's overall RDF, or if the RDF is very low, when it falls below 0.5 Hz, and this depressed state lasts for longer than 100 milliseconds. This definition is based on empirical observation and expert consensus, capturing the characteristic spectral signature of a wavefront fragmenting into multiple, slower, and disorganized daughter wavelets. The Wave Break Rate (WBR) is then simply calculated as the total number of such wave break events counted over the entire segment duration, divided by that duration, yielding a rate in units of wave breaks per second (WB/s). A high WBR indicates a region of highly unstable and fragmented electrical activity, while a low WBR indicates a region where wavefronts propagate in a more stable and coherent manner.

### 1B. Example of RDF-Based Wave Break Identification

To illustrate the practical application and efficacy of the RDF-based wave break identification method, consider a concrete example derived from a clinical recording. A 30-second segment of IEGMs was recorded from the roof of the left atrium in a patient with persistent AF using a high-density spiral mapping catheter (e.g., Reflexion™ Spiral, St. Jude Medical). The output of the signal processing pipeline at various stages for this segment provides a clear visual and quantitative demonstration of how the method works.

Initially, the raw bipolar electrograms from the 20 electrodes of the catheter show the expected chaotic and fractionated appearance of AF. After preprocessing, each of these electrograms is transformed into a smooth train of pulses. When a stable, planar wavefront passes under the catheter, the electrical activity is sensed nearly simultaneously by all electrodes. As a result, the peaks of the preprocessed pulse trains from all 20 electrodes align closely in time. When these 20 synchronized pulse trains are averaged, the result is a composite signal with a single, large, and sharp peak for each wavefront passage. This large peak, when passed through the two-sided exponential low-pass filter, remains prominent, and its spectral analysis via STFT yields a high iRDF value, consistent with the rapid but organized activity.

However, at specific time points within the recording—for example, around 12 seconds and 24 seconds into the segment—the situation changes dramatically. During these intervals, the raw electrograms become even more complex and disorganized, suggesting a collision or fragmentation of wavefronts. The preprocessed pulse trains from the individual electrodes no longer show synchronized peaks. Instead, the peaks are scattered over a longer time interval, indicating that different parts of the catheter are sensing electrical activity at different times. When these temporally dispersed pulse trains are averaged, the result is not a single large peak, but a series of smaller, closely spaced peaks, creating a segment of the composite signal with a much higher intrinsic frequency content. This high-frequency content is precisely what the specialized low-pass filter is designed to suppress. Consequently, the amplitude of the filtered composite signal drops significantly during these intervals. The STFT of this attenuated signal shows a marked reduction in power at the higher frequencies, causing the iRDF to plummet. In this specific example, the iRDF drops by more than 3 Hz from the site's overall RDF and remains low for a duration exceeding 100 milliseconds, thereby satisfying the criteria for a wave break event. Over the entire 30-second segment, three such events are identified, leading to a calculated WBR of 0.1 wave breaks per second.

This example starkly contrasts the RDF-based method with a more naive approach, such as averaging the instantaneous dominant frequencies (iEDFs) calculated from each individual electrode. A plot of the 10 iEDFs from a subset of the electrodes, along with their average, shows a relatively stable and high-frequency signal throughout the segment. The average iEDF fails to capture the dramatic drop in coherence that occurs at the 12-second and 24-second marks. This is because the iEDF of each individual electrode is still detecting a high rate of local electrical activity, even if that activity is disorganized and non-propagating. The RDF method, by contrast, is sensitive to the *spatial* coherence of that activity across the entire catheter array. It is this ability to integrate spatial information to assess the *quality* of the wavefront, not just its *rate*, that makes the RDF and WBR metrics uniquely powerful for identifying the mechanistic underpinnings of AF.

### 1C. Minimum Required Segment Duration for Accurate RDF Estimation

For the RDF metric to be clinically useful and reliable, it is essential to determine the minimum duration of an IEGM segment required to obtain a stable and accurate estimate. An excessively long recording time is impractical in a clinical setting, as it would prolong the procedure and increase the risk of catheter movement or patient discomfort. Conversely, a segment that is too short may yield a noisy and unreliable RDF value that does not truly represent the underlying atrial substrate. To address this, a rigorous empirical study was conducted to establish the minimum segment duration for RDF estimation.

The study assumed that a 30-second recording segment represented the "gold standard" for RDF estimation, as it provides a sufficiently long window to capture the statistical properties of the AF signal. The goal was to find the shortest segment duration, D, such that the RDF calculated from the first D seconds of a recording (denoted RDF(D)) was highly correlated with the RDF calculated from the full 30 seconds (RDF(30)). A high correlation, defined as a Pearson correlation coefficient greater than 0.85, was deemed acceptable for clinical reliability.

To perform this analysis, IEGM segments with a duration longer than 30 seconds were selected from a cohort of 15 patients, resulting in a dataset of 201 unique recording sites. For each of these 201 segments, the RDF(30) was calculated. Then, for a range of shorter durations D (from 1 second up to 30 seconds), the RDF(D) was calculated using only the first D seconds of the same segment. The Pearson correlation coefficient between the vector of RDF(D) values and the vector of RDF(30) values across all 201 sites was then computed for each D.

The results of this analysis, plotted as a function of D, revealed a clear relationship. The correlation was very low for extremely short durations (e.g., <2 seconds), as expected, due to the high stochasticity of AF. However, the correlation increased rapidly as D increased. Critically, the analysis showed that for a segment duration of 4 seconds, the Pearson correlation between RDF(4) and RDF(30) reached 90%, with a 95% confidence interval that was well above the 85% threshold. This finding was robust and consistent across the patient cohort. Therefore, it was concluded that a minimum segment duration of 4 seconds is sufficient to obtain an accurate and reproducible estimate of the RDF for a given atrial site. This is a highly practical result, as a 4-second recording is easily achievable in a clinical setting with good catheter stability, allowing for efficient and comprehensive mapping of the entire atrium without undue procedural burden.

### 1D. Minimum Required Segment Duration for Accurate WBR Estimation

While the RDF metric can be reliably estimated from a relatively short 4-second segment, the Wave Break Rate (WBR) is a more nuanced and statistically demanding measure. The WBR is a count-based metric that depends on the detection of discrete, transient events (wave breaks) within the recording. Because wave breaks are intermittent and their frequency can vary, a longer observation window is necessary to obtain a stable and representative rate. To determine the minimum segment duration required for accurate WBR estimation, a similar empirical approach was used, but with a longer gold standard duration.

Given that wave breaks are less frequent than the underlying electrical activations, a 50-second segment was chosen as the gold standard for WBR estimation (WBR(50)). This longer duration provides a more robust statistical basis for counting rare events. The analysis was performed on a subset of high-quality, long-duration recordings from two patients, yielding a dataset of 37 segments, each longer than 50 seconds. For each of these 37 segments, the WBR(50) was calculated. Then, for a range of shorter durations D, the WBR(D) was calculated using only the first D seconds of the segment. The Pearson correlation between the vector of WBR(D) values and the vector of WBR(50) values was computed for each D.

The results of this analysis showed that the correlation for WBR was much more sensitive to segment duration than it was for RDF. For very short durations (e.g., <10 seconds), the correlation was poor, as a short segment might by chance contain zero wave breaks or an unusually high number, leading to a highly variable and unrepresentative WBR. The correlation increased gradually as D increased. The analysis demonstrated that a segment duration of 25 seconds was the minimum required to achieve a reliable WBR estimate. At D=25 seconds, the Pearson correlation between WBR(25) and WBR(50) was sufficiently high, with its 95% confidence interval indicating a stable and reproducible measure. This finding is clinically significant: it establishes that while a quick 4-second look can provide a good estimate of the RDF, a more deliberate 25-second recording is necessary at a site of interest to accurately quantify its Wave Break Rate. This knowledge allows the electrophysiologist to use a two-tiered mapping strategy: a rapid survey of the atrium using 4-second recordings to identify regions of high RDF, followed by a more detailed 25-second recording at those candidate sites to confirm a low WBR before proceeding with ablation.

### 1E. Statistics

The statistical analysis of the clinical data was performed to validate the hypotheses that RDF and WBR are distinct metrics, that they differ between patient subtypes, and that they correlate with procedural outcomes. Given the nature of the data, which was found to be non-normally distributed using the Anderson-Darling test for normality, non-parametric statistical methods were employed throughout the analysis. The primary method for comparing groups was the Mann-Whitney U test, which is a robust non-parametric test for assessing whether two independent samples were selected from populations having the same distribution. This test was used to compare the RDF and WBR values between the group of patients with paroxysmal AF and the group with persistent AF.

The results of this comparison were highly significant. The median RDF for the paroxysmal AF group was 5.94 Hz, which was significantly higher than the median RDF of 5.27 Hz for the persistent AF group (p < 0.001). Similarly, the median WBR for the paroxysmal AF group was 0.23 WB/s, which was significantly higher than the median WBR of 0.13 WB/s for the persistent AF group (p < 0.001). These findings suggest a fundamental difference in the atrial substrate between the two AF types, with paroxysmal AF being characterized by faster but more fragmented activity, and persistent AF showing a trend toward slower but more stable activity, possibly reflecting a more remodeled and fibrotic substrate.

To assess the relationship between the two novel metrics themselves, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was calculated. This non-parametric measure of statistical dependence assesses how well the relationship between two variables can be described using a monotonic function. The analysis revealed a weak but statistically significant negative correlation between RDF and WBR (Spearman's rho = -0.3, p < 0.001). This weak correlation is actually a strength of the invention, as it indicates that RDF and WBR are measuring complementary, rather than redundant, aspects of the atrial substrate. A site can have a high RDF but also a high WBR (fast but fragmented), or a low RDF and a low WBR (slow but stable). The combination of the two metrics is therefore more informative than either one alone.

All continuous variables are reported in the standard format of mean ± standard deviation, with the median and range also provided for a more complete description of the data distribution, especially given its non-normal nature. This comprehensive statistical approach provides strong evidence for the validity and clinical relevance of the RDF and WBR metrics.

### 1F. Implementation

The implementation of the present invention can be realized as a software module that integrates with existing clinical infrastructure in an electrophysiology (EP) laboratory. The system can be deployed as a standalone application on a dedicated workstation or, more preferably, as a plug-in or integrated feature within a commercial electroanatomic mapping (EAM) system, such as the EnSite™ Velocity™ system (Abbott, formerly St. Jude Medical), CARTO (Biosense Webster), or Rhythmia (Boston Scientific).

The system's input is a set of time-synchronized, bipolar IEGM signals recorded from a high-density mapping catheter. The data can be streamed in real-time from the EP recording system or imported from a saved data file for offline analysis. The core of the system is a processing engine that executes the algorithm described in Section 1A. This engine is built using a scientific computing environment such as MATLAB (MathWorks) or Python (with libraries like NumPy and SciPy), which provide the necessary tools for efficient signal processing, filtering, and spectral analysis.

The workflow within the system is as follows: upon receiving a new IEGM segment from a specific catheter location, the system first applies the preprocessing step to each bipolar channel. It then computes the spatial average of these preprocessed signals, applies the two-sided exponential FIR filter, and removes the DC offset. The STFT is then computed with the specified parameters (1-second window, 95% overlap, Hanning window). The system continuously calculates the iRDF and, once a sufficient amount of data has been collected (e.g., 4 seconds for RDF, 25 seconds for WBR), it computes the final RDF (upper quartile of iRDF) and WBR (count of qualifying drops in iRDF per second).

The output of the system is a pair of numerical values (RDF in Hz, WBR in WB/s) for the mapped site. These values are then communicated to the EAM system's rendering engine. The EAM system, which maintains a 3D geometric model of the patient's atrium, can then color-code the location of the catheter on this model according to the RDF and WBR values. This is typically done using a relative color scale for each patient, where the lowest recorded value is assigned a blue color and the highest recorded value is assigned a red color, with a smooth gradient in between. The system may display two separate maps—one for RDF and one for WBR—or, more powerfully, a combined map that highlights sites meeting the specific criteria of high RDF and low WBR (e.g., sites in the upper quartile for RDF and lower quartile for WBR). This visual guidance provides the electrophysiologist with an immediate, intuitive, and objective target for ablation.

### 1G. Results

The clinical validation of the invention was conducted on a cohort of 15 patients (5 paroxysmal, 10 persistent AF) who underwent catheter ablation. A total of 279 stable IEGM segments, each at least 25 seconds in duration, were analyzed from various sites in the left atrium. The overall mean RDF across all segments was 5.5 ± 0.82 Hz, with a median of 5.4 Hz and a range from 2.86 to 7.66 Hz. The overall mean WBR was 0.16 ± 0.13 WB/s, with a median of 0.15 WB/s and a range from 0 to 0.63 WB/s. As previously noted, a significant difference was observed between the AF subtypes: paroxysmal AF patients had a higher mean RDF (5.99 ± 0.8 Hz) and a higher mean WBR (0.24 ± 0.14 WB/s) compared to persistent AF patients (RDF: 5.32 ± 0.75 Hz; WBR: 0.14 ± 0.11 WB/s), with both differences being statistically significant (p < 0.001).

The primary clinical endpoint was the acute termination of AF with ablation. Of the 15 patients, AF was terminated by ablation in 9 patients (6 to sinus rhythm, 3 to an organized atrial tachycardia). In 8 of these 9 successful cases, at least one ablation site that led to termination was found to be a site with the high RDF and low WBR (↑RDF,↓WBR) signature. Specifically, in 5 of the 6 patients who terminated directly to sinus rhythm, the successful ablation site was an ↑RDF,↓WBR site. In all 3 patients who terminated to an atrial tachycardia, the site of the subsequent ATach termination was an ↑RDF,↓WBR site during the initial AF mapping. This strong correlation between the ↑RDF,↓WBR signature and acute procedural success provides compelling evidence for the mechanistic relevance of these metrics.

Long-term follow-up data (mean 24.5 months) further supported these findings. Among the 6 patients in whom AF was successfully terminated to sinus rhythm by ablation, none experienced a recurrence of atrial arrhythmia. In contrast, among the 6 patients who required direct current cardioversion (and thus did not have a successful ablation termination), only 2 had all their identified ↑RDF,↓WBR sites ablated, and 4 of these 6 patients experienced a recurrence (2 AF, 2 ATach). This suggests that the presence of un-ablated ↑RDF,↓WBR sites is a risk factor for recurrence. The data collectively demonstrate that the RDF and WBR metrics, and their combined signature, are powerful predictors of both acute and long-term ablation outcomes.

### 1H. Discussion

The results of this study provide strong evidence that the novel RDF and WBR metrics offer a significant advance over existing methods for AF substrate mapping. Traditional approaches like CFE-mean or global DF are static measures that fail to capture the dynamic, spatially coherent nature of wavefront propagation. The RDF/WBR framework, by contrast, is explicitly designed to probe this dynamic coherence. The finding that sites with high RDF and low WBR are associated with AF termination is mechanistically intuitive: a high RDF suggests a region of rapid, driving activity, while a low WBR indicates that this activity is stable and organized, not fragmented by constant wave breaks. This is the signature one would expect from a stable rotor or a focal source, which are hypothesized to be critical for AF perpetuation.

The method's reliance on a high-density, but sequential, mapping approach is a pragmatic solution to the limitations of panoramic basket catheters. It provides excellent local resolution at the cost of global simultaneity, but the hypothesis is that for a focal ablation to be successful, the source must be relatively stationary and reproducible, making a sequential search feasible. The established minimum recording times (4s for RDF, 25s for WBR) make this approach clinically practical. The main limitation of the current implementation is the spatial resolution, which is on the order of the catheter diameter (~2.5 cm). Future work could involve using overlapping catheter positions or more advanced interpolation techniques to create higher-resolution maps. Nevertheless, the present invention provides a robust, objective, and clinically validated tool that has the potential to transform the way we understand and treat atrial fibrillation.

## 2. Computer Modelling of Spiral Rotor and Associated Wave Break Analysis

To further validate the mechanistic basis of the RDF and WBR metrics and to explore their behavior in a controlled, idealized setting, a series of computer simulations were performed using a two-dimensional (2D) model of atrial tissue. The primary goal of this modeling was to simulate the electrical activity generated by a stable, rotating spiral wave (a rotor), which is a canonical model for a self-sustaining AF driver, and to analyze the resulting "virtual" electrograms using the exact same RDF/WBR algorithm applied to the clinical data.

The computational model was based on the monodomain equation, which describes the propagation of electrical waves in cardiac tissue. A standard ionic current model, such as the Courtemanche-Ramirez-Nattel (CRN) model for human atrial myocytes, was used to define the cellular electrophysiology. The simulated tissue was a 10 cm x 10 cm square sheet of homogeneous, isotropic atrial tissue. A stable spiral wave was initiated in the center of the sheet using a standard S1-S2 cross-field stimulation protocol. The simulation was run for a sufficient duration to allow the spiral wave to stabilize and rotate at a constant cycle length.

To mimic the clinical scenario, a virtual "catheter" was placed at various locations within the simulated tissue. This virtual catheter was modeled as a circular array of 20 point sensors, each separated by 1-2 mm, matching the physical dimensions of the Reflexion™ Spiral catheter. The transmembrane voltage at each sensor location was recorded over time, and these voltage traces were treated as the "raw" IEGMs for the simulation. The RDF/WBR algorithm was then applied to these virtual IEGMs exactly as it was applied to the real clinical data.

The results of the simulation were highly instructive. When the virtual catheter was placed directly over the core of the stable rotor, the resulting virtual IEGMs showed a regular, high-frequency activation pattern. The preprocessed signals from all sensors were highly synchronized, leading to a large, coherent composite signal. The RDF calculated for this site was high, reflecting the rapid rotation of the spiral wave. Crucially, the WBR was very low, as there were no wave breaks; the wavefront propagated smoothly and continuously around the core. This produced the classic ↑RDF,↓WBR signature.

When the virtual catheter was moved to a location farther from the rotor core, the virtual IEGMs became more complex and irregular, as the rotating wavefront interacted with the boundaries of the tissue and with its own previous path. In these peripheral regions, the preprocessed signals from the sensors became less synchronized, and the composite signal showed more high-frequency content. The RDF was lower, and the WBR was significantly higher, reflecting the wavefront curvature, collisions, and fragmentation that occur away from the stable source. This simulation clearly demonstrates that the RDF/WBR algorithm is capable of distinguishing the stable, organized source of a rotor from the disorganized, fragmented activity in the surrounding tissue, providing strong theoretical support for its use in identifying critical AF drivers in the clinical setting.

## 3. Clinical Example of an Identified Rotor During Wave Break

A compelling clinical example that illustrates the practical application and predictive power of the RDF/WBR mapping is provided by a case study of a 62-year-old male patient with long-standing persistent AF. The patient underwent a standard PVI procedure, but AF persisted. Subsequent substrate mapping was performed using the Reflexion™ HD catheter, and the RDF/WBR algorithm was applied to the collected data.

The RDF and WBR maps of the left atrium revealed a striking pattern. A small region just posterior to the right inferior pulmonary vein (RIPV) exhibited a very high RDF (in the top quartile for the patient) and a very low WBR (in the bottom quartile). This created a distinct ↑RDF,↓WBR site. In contrast, the tissue immediately inferior to this site showed a high WBR, indicating a zone of active wave break and fragmentation. This spatial juxtaposition of a stable, fast source next to a zone of fragmentation is a classic theoretical prediction of rotor dynamics.

Based on this map, the operator delivered a radiofrequency ablation lesion at the identified ↑RDF,↓WBR site. As the ablation lesion was being created, crossing the boundary between the stable source and the fragmented zone, the AF abruptly terminated and converted to a slow, regular atrial tachycardia. The ATach was then mapped and successfully ablated at a separate site, restoring sinus rhythm. The patient remained in sinus rhythm at his 24-month follow-up visit.

This case is a powerful illustration of the invention in action. The RDF/WBR map provided a clear, objective, and mechanistically grounded target for ablation that was not apparent on conventional CFE-mean or voltage maps. The successful termination of AF upon ablation at this specific site provides direct clinical evidence that the ↑RDF,↓WBR signature can identify a critical driver of AF perpetuation, validating the core hypothesis of the present invention.
