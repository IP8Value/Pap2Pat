# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of biotechnology and pharmaceutical development, specifically to genetically encoded microbial systems for the discovery and biosynthesis of biologically active natural products that function as inhibitors of disease-relevant enzymes. More particularly, the invention provides a novel bacterial two-hybrid (B2H) system integrated with heterologous metabolic pathways in Escherichia coli to enable the identification, production, and functional validation of selective inhibitors of protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs), with a primary focus on protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B). The invention further encompasses the terpenoid compounds discovered through this system—specifically amorphadiene and β-bisabolene—as well as related analogs and derivatives, which exhibit allosteric inhibition of PTP1B, enhanced selectivity over closely related phosphatases such as TC-PTP, and demonstrable biological activity in mammalian cells by increasing insulin receptor phosphorylation. The invention also includes methods for scaling the discovery platform to uncharacterized biosynthetic gene clusters, particularly terpene synthases from diverse phylogenetic origins, and for adapting the genetically encoded detection system to other members of the PTP family or additional enzyme classes by modular exchange of target genes. Additionally, the invention provides a framework for secondary screening of inhibitor selectivity through comparative use of multiple B2H systems, each tuned to a specific phosphatase target. The disclosed platform represents a paradigm shift in drug discovery by coupling molecular biosynthesis directly to a selectable phenotypic output—cell survival under antibiotic pressure—thereby enabling high-throughput, growth-coupled screening of complex natural product libraries without the need for prior purification or in vitro biochemical assays. This approach leverages the synthetic capacity of microbial hosts to access structurally diverse, nonpolar, and conformationally flexible small molecules that are often overlooked in conventional structure-based drug design due to their inability to form canonical hydrogen bonds or electrostatic interactions, yet which can engage dynamic, hydrophobic pockets on protein surfaces through induced-fit mechanisms. The invention thus bridges synthetic biology, enzymology, structural biology, and medicinal chemistry to create a versatile and scalable pipeline for the discovery of next-generation therapeutics targeting historically “undruggable” enzymes.

## BACKGROUND

The development of small-molecule inhibitors for disease-relevant proteins remains a central challenge in modern pharmacology, particularly for enzyme families that possess highly conserved, charged active sites that impede the design of selective and membrane-permeable compounds. Protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) exemplify this challenge. These enzymes, which catalyze the dephosphorylation of tyrosine residues on signaling proteins, function in concert with protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs) to regulate a vast array of cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, and immune response. Dysregulation of PTP activity has been implicated in numerous pathologies, including type 2 diabetes, obesity, various cancers, autoimmune disorders, and cardiovascular diseases. Despite their clear therapeutic relevance, PTPs have proven exceptionally difficult to drug. In contrast to PTKs, which have yielded over thirty FDA-approved inhibitors, no clinically approved PTP-targeted therapeutics currently exist. This disparity stems largely from the structural features of the PTP catalytic domain: a deep, positively charged active site pocket that is highly conserved across the family, which favors binding of polar, phosphate-mimicking compounds that suffer from poor cell permeability, metabolic instability, and lack of selectivity. Consequently, efforts to develop competitive inhibitors that bind within the active site have largely failed to produce viable drug candidates.

Allosteric inhibition has emerged as a promising alternative strategy to overcome these limitations. By targeting less conserved regions outside the catalytic cleft, allosteric inhibitors can achieve greater selectivity and improved pharmacokinetic properties. A notable example is the benzbromarone derivative series, which binds to a C-terminal allosteric site on PTP1B, stabilizing the WPD loop in an open conformation and thereby inhibiting catalysis. However, despite extensive research over the past fifteen years, this class remains the only publicly reported set of allosteric PTP1B inhibitors with crystallographically confirmed binding modes. The scarcity of such molecules underscores the difficulty of identifying novel allosteric modulators using conventional screening approaches, which often rely on purified proteins, in vitro assays, and static structural models that fail to capture the dynamic conformational landscapes of proteins or the complex solvation effects that govern molecular recognition.

Natural products represent a rich source of bioactive scaffolds, having contributed to nearly half of all clinically approved drugs. Terpenoids, in particular, constitute one of the largest and most structurally diverse classes of natural products, with over 80,000 known compounds spanning monoterpenes (C10), sesquiterpenes (C15), diterpenes (C20), and higher oligomers. Their biosynthesis is mediated by terpene synthases, which convert linear isoprenoid diphosphates into complex cyclic or acyclic hydrocarbons through carbocation-driven cyclization cascades. While some terpenoids have been shown to inhibit PTP1B, their mechanisms of action are poorly understood, and systematic exploration of terpenoid chemical space for PTP inhibition has been hindered by low natural abundance, difficulties in chemical synthesis, and the labor-intensive nature of traditional bioassay-guided fractionation. Moreover, computational methods for predicting binding affinities remain limited in their ability to model the entropic contributions of water displacement, protein flexibility, and hydrophobic interactions—factors that are especially critical for nonpolar ligands like terpenoids.

Recent advances in synthetic biology have enabled the reconstruction of natural product pathways in heterologous microbial hosts such as Escherichia coli, facilitating the production and diversification of complex molecules. However, linking biosynthesis to functional screening in a way that selects for desired biological activity—rather than mere production—has remained elusive. Existing microbial screening platforms typically decouple production from activity, requiring downstream extraction and in vitro testing. This bottleneck severely limits throughput and scalability, especially when dealing with large libraries of uncharacterized biosynthetic genes or engineered variants. There is therefore a critical need for integrated systems that couple the genetic encoding of both biosynthetic capability and functional detection within a single living cell, such that only those cells producing a molecule with the desired inhibitory activity survive under selective pressure.

Prior art includes bacterial two-hybrid systems that detect protein-protein interactions via transcriptional activation, but these have not been adapted to sense the enzymatic activity of a human phosphatase in a manner that links inhibition to a selectable phenotype. Similarly, while optogenetic tools and genetically encoded biosensors exist for monitoring intracellular signaling, none have been configured to drive the discovery of enzyme inhibitors through growth-coupled selection. The present invention addresses these gaps by introducing a novel genetically encoded objective—a B2H circuit in E. coli wherein cell survival depends on the inhibition of a co-expressed human PTP. This system exploits the orthogonality of bacterial and human proteomes to minimize off-target effects while enabling real-time, in vivo detection of phosphatase inactivation. When combined with modular terpenoid biosynthetic pathways, this platform allows for the direct screening of genetic libraries for novel inhibitors, bypassing the need for compound purification and enabling the discovery of molecules that exploit dynamic, hydrophobic binding pockets inaccessible to rational design. The invention thus represents a significant advancement over existing methods by integrating biosynthesis, detection, and selection into a single, scalable, and genetically programmable microbial chassis.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a genetically encoded microbial platform for the discovery and biosynthesis of selective, biologically active inhibitors of protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs), particularly protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B). The core of the invention is a novel bacterial two-hybrid (B2H) system engineered in Escherichia coli, wherein the transcription of a selectable marker gene—specifically, spectinomycin resistance (SpecR)—is dependent upon the phosphorylation state of a synthetic substrate domain. This system comprises a chimeric RNA polymerase omega subunit fused to a kinase substrate domain, a 434 phage cI repressor fused to a Src homology 2 (SH2) domain with enhanced affinity for phosphotyrosine, and co-expressed human Src kinase and PTP1B. In the absence of PTP1B activity, Src phosphorylates the substrate domain, enabling SH2-mediated binding to the phosphorylated substrate, which relieves repression of the SpecR gene and permits cell survival in the presence of spectinomycin. Conversely, active PTP1B dephosphorylates the substrate, preventing the SH2-substrate interaction and maintaining repression of SpecR, leading to cell death under antibiotic selection. Critically, when a molecule inhibits PTP1B, the system is reactivated, allowing growth—a direct linkage between enzymatic inhibition and a selectable phenotype.

This B2H detection system is coupled with heterologous metabolic pathways for the biosynthesis of terpenoids, a class of natural products chosen for their structural diversity, hydrophobic character, and potential to engage allosteric sites on PTPs. The invention discloses the integration of the mevalonate-dependent isoprenoid pathway from Saccharomyces cerevisiae with various terpene synthases to produce sesquiterpenes (C15) and diterpenes (C20) in E. coli. Using this integrated platform, the inventors identified two previously unknown inhibitors of PTP1B: amorphadiene and β-bisabolene. Both compounds were shown to inhibit PTP1B with IC50 values of 53 ± 8 μM and 13 ± 2 μM, respectively, in vitro, and to increase insulin receptor phosphorylation in HEK293T/17 mammalian cells, confirming their biological relevance. X-ray crystallography revealed that amorphadiene binds to the same C-terminal allosteric site as benzbromarone derivatives, stabilizing the WPD loop in an open conformation, but does so through a unique mechanism involving reorganization of the α7 helix to form a hydrophobic cleft and adopting multiple conformations within the binding pocket. Kinetic and selectivity analyses demonstrated that both terpenoids exhibit 5- to 6-fold selectivity for PTP1B over its closest homolog, TC-PTP, and minimal inhibition of other PTPs such as SHP1 and SHP2, indicating target specificity.

The invention further provides a scalable method for screening uncharacterized biosynthetic genes. By constructing a phylogenetically diverse library of 24 uncharacterized terpene synthases from fungi, plants, and bacteria—selected from eight clades of the PF03936 family—the platform identified six additional hits that conferred spectinomycin resistance, including a gene encoding (+)-δ-cadinene synthase. This demonstrates the utility of the system for mining cryptic or orphan biosynthetic pathways for novel bioactive compounds without prior knowledge of their products. Moreover, the B2H system is modular: the gene for PTP1B can be replaced with genes encoding other PTPs (e.g., PTPN2, PTPN6, PTPN12) to create target-specific detection systems, enabling the discovery of inhibitors tailored to different disease contexts. The invention also includes a secondary screening strategy wherein parallel B2H systems—each specific to a different PTP—are used to assess inhibitor selectivity directly in vivo by comparing relative antibiotic resistance.

In summary, the invention offers a comprehensive solution to the challenges of PTP inhibitor discovery by combining (1) a genetically encoded, growth-coupled detection system that reports PTP inactivation through antibiotic resistance; (2) a modular biosynthetic chassis for producing diverse terpenoid scaffolds; (3) the identification of novel, selective, and biologically active PTP1B inhibitors with unique allosteric mechanisms; (4) a scalable pipeline for screening uncharacterized biosynthetic gene clusters; and (5) a versatile platform adaptable to other enzyme targets. This approach circumvents the limitations of traditional drug discovery by leveraging the synthetic and evolutionary capabilities of microbial systems to access chemical space that is refractory to rational design and conventional screening.

## DEFINITIONS

As used herein, the term “protein tyrosine phosphatase” or “PTP” refers to any enzyme belonging to the protein tyrosine phosphatase superfamily that catalyzes the hydrolysis of phosphate groups from phosphorylated tyrosine residues on substrate proteins. This includes classical receptor-like and non-receptor PTPs, dual-specificity phosphatases (DUSPs), and low molecular weight PTPs, unless otherwise specified. The term encompasses full-length proteins as well as catalytically active fragments, such as the isolated catalytic domain (e.g., PTP1B residues 1–321).

The term “PTP1B” specifically denotes human protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B, encoded by the PTPN1 gene, and includes any naturally occurring isoform or recombinant variant thereof that retains catalytic activity toward phosphotyrosine-containing substrates.

An “inhibitor” of a PTP, as used herein, is any molecule that reduces the enzymatic activity of the PTP, either by direct binding to the enzyme or by indirect mechanisms that result in decreased dephosphorylation of its substrates. Inhibition may be competitive, noncompetitive, uncompetitive, or allosteric, and is quantified by metrics such as IC50 (half-maximal inhibitory concentration) or Ki (inhibition constant).

The phrase “allosteric inhibitor” refers to an inhibitor that binds to a site on the PTP distinct from the catalytic active site, inducing a conformational change that reduces enzymatic activity. The “allosteric site” of PTP1B, as referenced herein, specifically denotes the C-terminal pocket adjacent to the α7 helix, which, when occupied, stabilizes the WPD loop in an open, catalytically inactive conformation.

A “terpenoid” or “isoprenoid” is a natural product derived from the assembly of isoprene (C5) units, typically classified by the number of such units: monoterpenes (C10), sesquiterpenes (C15), diterpenes (C20), etc. The term includes hydrocarbons as well as oxygenated derivatives (e.g., alcohols, aldehydes, acids), and encompasses both naturally occurring compounds and synthetic analogs.

“Amorphadiene” refers to the sesquiterpene hydrocarbon (C15H24) with the IUPAC name (4S,7R)-4,7-dimethyl-1-(propan-2-ylidene)-1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8-octahydronaphthalene, and includes enantiomerically pure or racemic forms.

“β-Bisabolene” refers to the sesquiterpene (C15H24) with the IUPAC name (E)-7,11-dimethyl-3-methylene-1,6,10-dodecatriene, and includes stereoisomers and geometric isomers where applicable.

A “bacterial two-hybrid (B2H) system” as used herein describes a genetic circuit in bacteria wherein a protein-protein interaction modulates transcription of a reporter or selectable gene. In the context of the present invention, the B2H system is specifically engineered to detect the phosphorylation state of a synthetic substrate, with transcriptional output dependent on the balance between kinase and phosphatase activities.

The term “genetically encoded objective” refers to a biological function or state (e.g., inhibition of a specific enzyme) that is hardwired into the genome or plasmids of a host cell such that it directly controls a selectable phenotype (e.g., antibiotic resistance). In this invention, the objective is the inactivation of PTP1B, which is linked to cell survival.

“Growth-coupled assay” means a screening method wherein the production of a desired molecule confers a growth advantage under selective conditions, allowing enrichment of productive strains without the need for external detection reagents.

“Selectivity” in the context of PTP inhibition refers to the preferential inhibition of one PTP over another, typically expressed as the ratio of IC50 values (e.g., IC50(TC-PTP)/IC50(PTP1B)).

“Metabolic pathway” denotes a series of enzymatic reactions encoded by one or more genes that convert a starting metabolite into a final product. In this invention, it refers to the heterologous expression of the mevalonate pathway and terpene synthases in E. coli to produce terpenoids.

“Uncharacterized biosynthetic gene” means a gene predicted to encode a biosynthetic enzyme (e.g., based on sequence homology) but for which the enzymatic product or activity has not been experimentally verified.

All other terms used herein have their ordinary meanings as understood by those skilled in the relevant art, unless otherwise defined.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF INVENTION

### I. Protein Tyrosine Phosphatases (PTPs) and Protein Tyrosine Kinases (PTKs) in Relation to Disease.

Protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) and protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs) constitute a fundamental regulatory axis in eukaryotic signal transduction, governing the reversible phosphorylation of tyrosine residues on a wide array of substrate proteins. This post-translational modification serves as a molecular switch that modulates protein activity, localization, stability, and interactions, thereby influencing virtually every aspect of cellular physiology. PTKs catalyze the transfer of a phosphate group from ATP to tyrosine residues, while PTPs reverse this process through hydrolytic dephosphorylation. The precise balance between these opposing activities is critical for maintaining homeostasis, and its dysregulation is a hallmark of numerous human diseases. For instance, hyperactivation of PTKs such as EGFR, HER2, or BCR-ABL drives oncogenic signaling in various cancers, leading to the successful development of targeted kinase inhibitors like imatinib and erlotinib. In contrast, aberrant PTP activity—either through loss-of-function or gain-of-function mutations—can similarly disrupt signaling networks. PTP1B, for example, negatively regulates insulin and leptin signaling by dephosphorylating the insulin receptor (IR) and JAK2, respectively, making it a compelling therapeutic target for type 2 diabetes and obesity. Genetic deletion of PTP1B in mice results in enhanced insulin sensitivity, resistance to diet-induced obesity, and improved glucose tolerance, validating its role in metabolic regulation. Moreover, PTP1B has been shown to promote HER2-positive breast cancer by stabilizing the HER2 receptor and enhancing downstream signaling, further expanding its therapeutic relevance. Other PTPs are implicated in distinct pathologies: TC-PTP (PTPN2) modulates immune responses and is associated with autoimmune disorders; SHP2 (PTPN11) is a key node in RAS/MAPK signaling and is mutated in Noonan syndrome and leukemias; and PTPN12 acts as a tumor suppressor in breast cancer by dephosphorylating oncogenic kinases like EGFR and HER2. Despite this well-established disease linkage, the development of PTP-targeted therapeutics has lagged far behind that of PTK inhibitors. The primary obstacle lies in the structural conservation of the PTP catalytic domain, which features a deep, positively charged pocket that recognizes the phosphate group of substrates. This pocket is highly similar across the >100 human PTPs, making it difficult to design inhibitors that selectively target one member without affecting others. Furthermore, molecules that mimic the phosphate group—such as carboxylic acids, sulfonates, or phosphonates—tend to be highly polar, resulting in poor cell permeability and oral bioavailability. Consequently, most early PTP inhibitors failed in clinical development due to lack of selectivity and unfavorable pharmacokinetics. Allosteric inhibition, which targets less conserved regions outside the catalytic cleft, offers a promising alternative. The discovery of benzbromarone-derived allosteric inhibitors of PTP1B, which bind to a site near the α7 helix and stabilize the WPD loop in an open conformation, provided proof-of-concept that selective PTP inhibition is achievable. However, the rarity of such compounds highlights the need for innovative discovery platforms capable of identifying novel chemotypes that engage dynamic, hydrophobic pockets on PTPs—precisely the gap addressed by the present invention.

### II. Optogenetic Actuators.

While the present invention primarily focuses on genetically encoded biosensors for enzyme inhibition, it is noteworthy that the underlying principles of modular genetic circuit design are also applicable to the field of optogenetics. Optogenetic actuators are light-sensitive proteins that can be engineered to control cellular processes with high spatial and temporal precision. Although not directly implemented in the disclosed PTP screening platform, the concept of photoswitchable constructs—wherein protein activity is regulated by light—represents a complementary approach to probing enzyme function and could be integrated into future iterations of the system. For example, a photoswitchable PTP or kinase could be incorporated into the B2H circuit to allow light-gated control of the phosphorylation-dephosphorylation equilibrium, thereby enabling dynamic interrogation of inhibitor efficacy under varying signaling conditions. Such hybrid systems would combine the selectivity of genetic encoding with the precision of optical control, offering new avenues for studying signaling dynamics and screening context-dependent inhibitors. However, the current invention does not rely on optogenetic components; rather, it establishes a foundational framework for activity-based selection that could be extended to include such advanced regulatory elements in subsequent developments.

### III. Genetically Encoded System for Constructing and Detecting Biologically Active Agents: Microbial Inhibitor Screening Systems.

The cornerstone of the present invention is a genetically encoded microbial system that integrates the biosynthesis of candidate inhibitors with their functional detection in a single living cell. This system, implemented in Escherichia coli, consists of two modular components: (1) a biosynthetic pathway for producing structurally diverse natural products, and (2) a detection circuit that links the inhibition of a target enzyme to a selectable phenotype—specifically, antibiotic resistance. The detection circuit is a refined bacterial two-hybrid (B2H) system designed to report the activity of human PTP1B in vivo. It comprises a chimeric transcriptional regulator in which the omega subunit of RNA polymerase is fused to a kinase substrate domain (e.g., MidT), and the 434 phage cI repressor is fused to an SH2 domain engineered for high affinity to phosphotyrosine (SH2*). Co-expression of human Src kinase and PTP1B creates a tunable phosphorylation-dephosphorylation cycle on the substrate domain. When PTP1B is active, it dephosphorylates the substrate, preventing SH2*-mediated binding to the cI repressor, which remains bound to its operator and represses transcription of the spectinomycin resistance (SpecR) gene. Conversely, when PTP1B is inhibited, Src-mediated phosphorylation predominates, enabling SH2*-substrate binding, which sequesters the cI repressor away from the operator and derepresses SpecR expression, allowing cell survival under spectinomycin selection. This design ensures that only cells producing a molecule capable of inhibiting PTP1B will grow under antibiotic pressure, creating a direct, growth-coupled link between biosynthesis and function. The system was optimized through iterative engineering: initial luminescent reporters (LuxAB) were used to tune expression levels of Src, PTP1B, and SH2*; promoter and ribosome binding site strengths were adjusted to maximize dynamic range; and the GOI was switched from luminescence to SpecR to enable high-throughput selection. Critically, E. coli was chosen as the host due to its proteomic orthogonality to humans, minimizing off-target effects of the heterologous kinases and phosphatases. This genetically encoded objective—cell survival contingent on PTP1B inhibition—provides a powerful alternative to traditional screening methods, which require compound purification and in vitro assays. By embedding the detection logic directly into the cellular machinery, the system enables the screening of complex metabolic libraries in a format compatible with standard microbiological techniques, such as plating and colony picking, thereby dramatically increasing throughput and scalability.

### IV. Evolving High-Affinity Terpenoid Inhibitors of PTP1B.

The invention leverages the genetically encoded B2H system to discover novel terpenoid inhibitors of PTP1B. Terpenoids were selected as the chemical scaffold due to their structural diversity, hydrophobic nature, and potential to engage allosteric sites on PTPs that are inaccessible to polar, active-site-directed inhibitors. The biosynthetic module consists of the mevalonate-dependent isoprenoid pathway from Saccharomyces cerevisiae, optimized for expression in E. coli, coupled with various terpene synthases that convert farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) or geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP) into sesquiterpenes (C15) or diterpenes (C20), respectively. Initial screening of five well-characterized terpenoid pathways—amorphadiene, β-humulene, β-bisabolene, abietadiene, and taxadiene—revealed that only amorphadiene and β-bisabolene conferred robust spectinomycin resistance, indicating PTP1B inhibition. Subsequent in vitro kinetic assays confirmed that both compounds inhibit PTP1B with IC50 values of 53 ± 8 μM and 13 ± 2 μM, respectively, in 10% DMSO. These potencies are remarkable for unfunctionalized hydrocarbons, suggesting high ligand efficiency. X-ray crystallography of the PTP1B–amorphadiene complex revealed binding at the C-terminal allosteric site, overlapping with the benzbromarone pocket, but with a distinct mechanism: amorphadiene induces a conformational rearrangement of the α7 helix to form a hydrophobic cleft and exhibits conformational flexibility within the binding site, as evidenced by disordered electron density and molecular dynamics simulations. Kinetic analyses further demonstrated that both terpenoids are noncompetitive with substrate and selective for PTP1B over TC-PTP (5- to 6-fold) and other PTPs like SHP1/SHP2. Crucially, both compounds increased insulin receptor phosphorylation in HEK293T/17 cells, confirming their biological activity in a mammalian context. Structure-activity relationships were probed using analogs: dihydroartemisinic acid, which bears a carboxyl group, showed 8-fold reduced potency, consistent with steric clash in the hydrophobic cleft, while β-bisabolol (a hydroxylated analog) exhibited 20-fold lower activity. These findings validate the hypothesis that nonpolar terpenoids can selectively target allosteric sites on PTPs, providing new lead scaffolds for drug development. The invention thus establishes a pipeline for evolving high-affinity terpenoid inhibitors through microbial biosynthesis and selection, bypassing the need for synthetic chemistry and enabling the exploration of natural product chemical space in a function-driven manner.

### VI. Evolving Optogenetic Actuators: Photoswitchable Constructs.

Although the primary embodiment of the invention does not incorporate optogenetic elements, the modular architecture of the genetically encoded system is inherently compatible with the integration of light-responsive domains. Future extensions of the platform could involve the development of photoswitchable PTP or kinase constructs, wherein enzymatic activity is controlled by illumination with specific wavelengths of light. For example, a PTP1B variant fused to a light-oxygen-voltage (LOV) domain or a phytochrome could be engineered such that its catalytic activity is reversibly inhibited or activated by blue or red light, respectively. Incorporating such a photoswitchable PTP into the B2H circuit would allow researchers to dynamically modulate the phosphorylation state of the substrate domain in real time, enabling the study of inhibitor kinetics under varying signaling conditions or the screening of compounds that exhibit context-dependent activity. Additionally, light-gated control could be used to synchronize the induction of biosynthetic pathways with the activation of the detection circuit, minimizing basal expression and improving signal-to-noise ratios. While these applications are speculative and not part of the current disclosure, they illustrate the versatility of the genetically encoded framework and its potential to interface with emerging technologies in synthetic biology and optogenetics. The core innovation remains the growth-coupled selection of enzyme inhibitors, but the system’s design principles pave the way for more sophisticated, multi-input regulatory circuits in the future.

## Examples

**Example 1: Construction and Optimization of the B2H Detection System**  
The bacterial two-hybrid system was assembled in E. coli s1030 by cloning genes for Src kinase, CDC37 chaperone, PTP1B, a MidT substrate domain fused to RpoZ (omega subunit), and an SH2* domain (K15L, T8V, C10A mutations) fused to 434cI repressor into compatible plasmids under inducible promoters. The gene of interest was spectinomycin resistance (SpecR). Initial luminescent versions (using LuxAB) were used to optimize expression levels; maximal luminescence was achieved with MidT substrate and secondary induction of Src. The final system required co-expression of all components and showed >100-fold difference in spectinomycin resistance between PTP1B-active and PTP1B-inhibited states.

**Example 2: Discovery of Amorphadiene and β-Bisabolene as PTP1B Inhibitors**  
E. coli strains harboring the B2H system and terpenoid pathways (amorphadiene synthase or β-bisabolene synthase plus mevalonate pathway) were grown in TB media with IPTG and mevalonate. After 20 hours, cultures were plated on LB agar with 1000 μg/mL spectinomycin. Only amorphadiene- and β-bisabolene-producing strains formed colonies. GC-MS confirmed terpenoid production (titers: 0.3–18 mg/L). Purified compounds inhibited PTP1B with IC50 = 53 ± 8 μM and 13 ± 2 μM, respectively.

**Example 3: Structural and Mechanistic Characterization of Amorphadiene**  
Co-crystallization of PTP1B with amorphadiene yielded a structure (PDB: 7LFO) showing binding at the C-terminal allosteric site. The α7 helix (residues 287–298) underwent a conformational shift to form a hydrophobic cleft accommodating the terpenoid. Electron density indicated multiple conformations. Molecular dynamics simulations confirmed ligand flexibility. Competition assays showed amorphadiene did not compete with orthovanadate but antagonized TCS401, consistent with WPD loop stabilization in the open state.

**Example 4: Biological Activity in Mammalian Cells**  
HEK293T/17 cells were treated with 930 μM amorphadiene or 405 μM β-bisabolene for 10 minutes. ELISA using PathScan® kit showed 2.1-fold and 1.8-fold increases in IR phosphorylation, respectively, versus DMSO control. Dihydroartemisinic acid (8-fold weaker PTP1B inhibitor) showed no significant effect, confirming on-target activity.

**Example 5: Scalable Screening of Uncharacterized Terpene Synthases**  
A cladogram of 4,464 PF03936-family terpene synthases was constructed. Twenty-four uncharacterized genes (8 clades, 3 per clade) were synthesized and cloned. Co-expression with B2H system and FPP pathway identified six hits conferring spectinomycin resistance >400 μg/mL. One hit, A0A0C9VSL7, produced (+)-δ-cadinene (IC50 = 165 ± 33 μM), demonstrating detection of weak inhibitors.

**Example 6: Adaptation to Other PTP Targets**  
The B2H system was reconfigured by replacing PTP1B with PTPN2, PTPN6, or PTPN12. All supported growth under spectinomycin upon inhibition. Selectivity was assessed by comparing resistance of PTP1B- vs. TC-PTP-specific systems to amorphadiene: PTP1B system showed 4-fold higher resistance, confirming selectivity in vivo.
