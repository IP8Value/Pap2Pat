# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Cancer immunotherapy has emerged as a transformative modality in the treatment of malignant diseases, offering the potential for durable responses by harnessing the body’s own immune system to recognize and eliminate tumor cells. Among the various strategies developed over the past several decades, dendritic cell (DC)-based vaccines represent one of the most promising approaches due to the central role that DCs play in initiating and orchestrating adaptive immune responses. DCs are professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs) uniquely equipped to capture, process, and present tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) to naïve T cells, thereby priming antigen-specific cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes and helper CD4+ T cells capable of mediating anti-tumor immunity. The clinical validation of this concept was achieved with the approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) of Sipuleucel-T, an autologous DC vaccine for the treatment of metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer. In this therapy, patient-derived peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), enriched for DC precursors, are cultured ex vivo with a fusion protein comprising granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) and prostatic acid phosphatase (PAP), a prostate-specific antigen. Upon reinfusion, these antigen-loaded DCs stimulate PAP-specific T-cell responses, resulting in a modest but statistically significant improvement in overall survival. Despite this milestone, the therapeutic efficacy of Sipuleucel-T is limited by its reliance on a single, predefined tumor antigen, which restricts its applicability to cancers with well-characterized, uniformly expressed TAAs and fails to address the heterogeneity and mutational complexity inherent in many solid tumors.

This limitation is particularly acute in aggressive malignancies such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which lacks expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), thereby precluding the use of targeted hormonal or HER2-directed therapies. Moreover, TNBC is characterized by high genomic instability, extensive intratumoral heterogeneity, and a paucity of recurrent, immunodominant neoantigens that can be reliably targeted across patient populations. Consequently, efforts to develop effective DC vaccines for such cancers have focused on alternative antigen sources that encompass the full repertoire of tumor-associated proteins, including both shared and patient-specific epitopes. One such strategy involves the use of whole tumor lysates or irradiated tumor cells as antigenic cargo for DC loading. For instance, the GVAX platform employs genetically modified tumor cells engineered to secrete GM-CSF, which acts as a chemoattractant and differentiation factor for DCs and other APCs at the vaccination site. While preclinical studies demonstrated robust anti-tumor immunity, clinical translation revealed significant drawbacks, including uncontrolled systemic release of GM-CSF leading to expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs)—a population of immature myeloid cells that potently suppress T-cell function and promote immune tolerance within the tumor microenvironment. Similarly, oxidized tumor lysates generated by treatment with hypochlorous acid have shown enhanced immunogenicity in both murine models and early-phase human trials, likely due to the induction of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that activate innate immune pathways. However, these approaches still depend on sufficient quantities of fresh or frozen tumor tissue, which may not be available in cases of inoperable primary tumors, minimal residual disease, or late-stage metastatic recurrence where only small biopsy specimens can be obtained.

To circumvent these challenges, researchers have explored nucleic acid-based delivery systems, such as mRNA encoding multiple tumor antigens or personalized neoantigens, which can be transfected into DCs ex vivo or delivered directly to lymph nodes in vivo. Personalized RNA vaccines encoding patient-specific mutanomes have demonstrated the ability to induce polyfunctional T-cell responses, reduce metastatic burden, and prolong progression-free survival in early clinical trials. Nevertheless, the logistical complexity, high cost, and extended manufacturing timelines associated with neoantigen identification, RNA synthesis, and quality control render these approaches impractical for widespread clinical implementation, especially in resource-limited settings or for patients requiring urgent intervention. There remains, therefore, a critical unmet need for a versatile, scalable, and broadly applicable DC vaccine platform that can deliver a comprehensive array of tumor antigens—both defined and undefined—while simultaneously providing potent immunostimulatory signals to drive DC maturation, migration, and T-cell priming, without inducing systemic toxicity or immunosuppressive side effects.

In response to this need, the present inventors have pioneered a novel tumor membrane vesicle (TMV)-based vaccine technology that leverages the native plasma membrane of tumor cells as a source of authentic, conformationally intact tumor antigens presented in their natural lipid bilayer context. TMVs are nano-sized vesicles (typically 200–600 nm in diameter) derived from the plasma membranes of tumor cells through mechanical and biochemical processing, preserving the surface proteome, glycome, and lipidome of the parent tumor. This particulate format is ideally suited for uptake by DCs, as particles within the 100–1000 nm size range are efficiently internalized via phagocytosis, macropinocytosis, and receptor-mediated endocytosis, leading to enhanced cross-presentation of exogenous antigens on major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules—a key requirement for activating CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes. However, while TMVs provide a rich source of tumor antigens, they are inherently poor activators of DCs due to the absence of danger signals or co-stimulatory molecules necessary to overcome immune tolerance and initiate productive T-cell responses. To address this deficiency, the inventors have developed a proprietary “protein transfer” methodology that enables the stable incorporation of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs) onto the outer leaflet of the TMV lipid bilayer. GPI anchoring is a naturally occurring post-translational modification that tethers proteins to the extracellular face of the plasma membrane via a glycolipid moiety, allowing lateral mobility and functional presentation without transmembrane domains or cytoplasmic tails. By engineering recombinant cytokines and co-stimulatory ligands as GPI-anchored variants—such as GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-interleukin-12 (GPI-IL-12)—the inventors have created TMV constructs that co-deliver tumor antigens and adjuvant signals in a spatially coordinated, membrane-integrated format.

This integrated design ensures that upon TMV uptake by DCs, both antigen and adjuvant are delivered to the same intracellular compartment, maximizing synergistic signaling while minimizing off-target effects. Unlike soluble cytokines, which diffuse systemically and can activate non-target immune cells (e.g., GM-CSF-induced MDSC expansion), GPI-anchored cytokines remain localized to the TMV surface, restricting their activity to DCs that actively engage and internalize the vesicles. This targeted delivery enhances the therapeutic index by concentrating immunostimulation precisely where it is needed—within the antigen-processing DC—while avoiding systemic exposure. Furthermore, the particulate nature of TMVs facilitates drainage to lymph nodes or direct interaction with tissue-resident DCs, promoting efficient antigen presentation in secondary lymphoid organs. Building upon this foundation, the inventors have now developed a next-generation DC vaccine platform in which autologous DCs are pulsed ex vivo with TMVs incorporating GPI-ISMs, thereby converting the DCs into potent, antigen-loaded, and fully activated cellular vaccines. This approach decouples antigen acquisition from DC activation, allowing precise control over the dose, composition, and timing of vaccine administration. Critically, it enables the use of minimal amounts of tumor tissue—such as that obtained from core needle biopsies—to generate sufficient TMVs for ex vivo DC pulsing, thereby extending the applicability of personalized immunotherapy to patients with limited tumor material. The present invention thus represents a significant advancement over prior art by combining the antigenic breadth of whole-tumor vaccines with the precision and safety of targeted cytokine delivery, all within a clinically feasible and scalable manufacturing paradigm.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel dendritic cell (DC) vaccine platform for the treatment of cancer, comprising autologous or allogeneic DCs that have been ex vivo pulsed with tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) incorporating glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs). The TMVs are derived from the plasma membranes of tumor cells and retain the full complement of native tumor-associated antigens in their natural conformation and membrane context. The GPI-ISMs, which include but are not limited to GPI-anchored granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GPI-GM-CSF) and GPI-anchored interleukin-12 (GPI-IL-12), are stably incorporated onto the surface of the TMVs via a protein transfer technique, thereby endowing the vesicles with potent immunostimulatory properties. When DCs are exposed to these engineered TMVs in vitro, they efficiently internalize the vesicles, leading to enhanced antigen uptake, upregulation of co-stimulatory and MHC molecules, secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and acquisition of a mature, immunogenic phenotype. Upon administration to a subject, these activated, antigen-loaded DCs migrate to lymphoid tissues and prime tumor-specific T-cell responses, resulting in inhibition of primary tumor growth, reduction of metastatic burden, and improved survival in preclinical models of both antigen-defined and antigen-undefined cancers.

In one aspect, the invention relates to a method of preparing a DC vaccine, comprising: (a) isolating tumor cells from a subject or obtaining a tumor cell line; (b) generating TMVs from said tumor cells by homogenization and ultracentrifugation over a sucrose gradient; (c) incorporating at least one GPI-ISM onto the surface of the TMVs by incubating the TMVs with a purified preparation of the GPI-ISM under conditions that permit spontaneous insertion of the GPI anchor into the lipid bilayer; (d) isolating bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) or monocyte-derived DCs from a subject; (e) pulsing the DCs with the GPI-ISM-incorporated TMVs for a period sufficient to allow uptake and activation; and (f) harvesting and formulating the pulsed DCs for therapeutic administration. In another aspect, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising DCs pulsed with TMVs that display on their surface at least one tumor-associated antigen and at least one GPI-ISM selected from GPI-GM-CSF, GPI-IL-12, GPI-B7-1, GPI-CD40L, or combinations thereof. The composition may be administered subcutaneously, intradermally, intravenously, or intranodally to a subject diagnosed with cancer, including but not limited to breast cancer, melanoma, lung cancer, ovarian cancer, or glioblastoma.

The invention further encompasses methods of treating cancer in a subject, comprising administering to the subject an effective amount of the DC vaccine described herein. The treatment results in enhanced infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes and MHC-II+ CD11c+ dendritic cells into the tumor microenvironment, suppression of tumor growth, reduction in metastatic dissemination—particularly to the lungs in models of triple-negative breast cancer—and prolonged overall survival. Importantly, the vaccine is effective in both cancers with well-defined target antigens, such as HER2-positive breast cancer, and in cancers lacking such antigens, such as triple-negative breast cancer, thereby broadening its clinical utility. The use of GPI-anchored cytokines minimizes systemic toxicity associated with soluble cytokine administration, as the immunostimulatory signals are confined to DCs that have internalized the TMVs. Additionally, the ex vivo pulsing strategy allows for quality control of DC activation status prior to infusion, ensuring consistent product potency. The invention thus offers a safe, effective, and adaptable immunotherapeutic platform that overcomes the limitations of prior DC vaccines by integrating comprehensive tumor antigen presentation with localized, targeted immune activation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Tumor Membrane Vesicles (TMVs)

Tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) constitute a foundational element of the present invention, serving as the primary vehicle for delivering a broad spectrum of tumor-associated antigens to dendritic cells in a physiologically relevant and immunologically potent format. TMVs are nano-scale vesicular structures derived exclusively from the plasma membranes of tumor cells, generated through a controlled process of mechanical disruption and biochemical purification that preserves the native architecture, composition, and antigenic integrity of the original cell surface. Unlike whole-cell lysates, which contain a chaotic mixture of cytoplasmic, nuclear, and membrane components that may include immunosuppressive or tolerogenic factors, TMVs are highly enriched for plasma membrane constituents, thereby focusing the immune response on surface-exposed antigens that are most relevant for T-cell recognition and antibody binding. These vesicles typically range in size from approximately 200 to 600 nanometers in diameter, a dimension that has been empirically and theoretically established as optimal for uptake by professional antigen-presenting cells, particularly dendritic cells. Particles within this size range are efficiently internalized via multiple endocytic pathways, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolae-dependent uptake, and phagocytosis, and are preferentially routed to compartments conducive to cross-presentation—the process by which exogenous antigens are loaded onto MHC class I molecules for activation of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, a critical effector arm of anti-tumor immunity.

The preparation of TMVs begins with the acquisition of tumor material, which may be sourced from fresh or frozen tumor tissue obtained during surgical resection or biopsy, or from established tumor cell lines cultured in vitro. In either case, the starting material is subjected to homogenization under isotonic conditions to disrupt cellular integrity while minimizing denaturation of membrane proteins. The resulting homogenate is then layered onto a discontinuous sucrose density gradient, typically comprising a 41% sucrose cushion, and subjected to ultracentrifugation at high speeds (e.g., 100,000–150,000 × g) for several hours. During this process, plasma membrane fragments, by virtue of their unique lipid composition and buoyant density, band at the interface between the sample layer and the sucrose cushion, while heavier organelles such as nuclei, mitochondria, and lysosomes pellet at the bottom of the tube, and lighter components such as cytosolic proteins remain in the upper fractions. The membrane-enriched band is carefully collected, diluted, and subjected to additional washing steps via centrifugation to remove residual sucrose and contaminants. The final TMV preparation is resuspended in a physiologically compatible buffer such as phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and may be stored frozen for future use without significant loss of structural or functional integrity.

Characterization of TMVs confirms their identity and quality. Dynamic light scattering analysis using instruments such as the Zetasizer Nano ZS reveals a narrow size distribution centered around 200–300 nm, with a polydispersity index (PDI) typically below 0.4, indicating a homogeneous population of vesicles comparable in uniformity to synthetic liposomal formulations used in drug delivery. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrates that TMVs retain key tumor-associated surface markers present on the parent cells. For example, when derived from HER2-overexpressing tumor cells such as the D2F2/E2 murine breast cancer line, TMVs exhibit strong surface expression of human HER2, as detected by fluorophore-conjugated anti-HER2 antibodies, confirming that conformational epitopes and post-translational modifications are preserved during vesicle formation. This retention of native antigenicity is crucial, as it ensures that the immune responses elicited by the vaccine will be directed against authentic tumor targets rather than denatured or artificial epitopes. Moreover, because TMVs encapsulate the entire surface proteome of the tumor—including mutated neoantigens, overexpressed self-antigens, cancer-testis antigens, and glycosylation variants—they provide a comprehensive antigenic repertoire that mirrors the heterogeneity of the original tumor, thereby reducing the risk of immune escape through antigen loss. The particulate nature of TMVs also confers inherent adjuvant properties by engaging pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on dendritic cells, such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), which recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or DAMPs that may be present on stressed or dying tumor cells. However, while this basal level of immunogenicity is beneficial, it is often insufficient to break immune tolerance in established tumors, necessitating the incorporation of additional immunostimulatory signals—achieved in the present invention through the integration of GPI-anchored immunostimulatory molecules.

### TMV Preparation, Characterization, and GPI-ISM Incorporation

The preparation, characterization, and functional enhancement of tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) through the incorporation of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs) represent a critical sequence of steps in the development of the disclosed DC vaccine platform. Following the isolation of TMVs from tumor cells as previously described, the vesicles undergo a series of analytical and functional modifications to ensure their suitability as a vaccine component. Initial characterization involves assessment of physical parameters such as particle size, concentration, and homogeneity using dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrumentation, which provides hydrodynamic diameter measurements and polydispersity indices that reflect batch-to-batch consistency and colloidal stability. Concurrently, the antigenic fidelity of the TMVs is verified through flow cytometry using antibodies specific to known tumor-associated antigens expressed by the source cells. For instance, TMVs derived from HER2-positive D2F2/E2 cells are stained with anti-HER2 monoclonal antibodies conjugated to phycoerythrin (PE) or allophycocyanin (APC), and the resulting fluorescence intensity is compared to that of intact parental cells to confirm retention of surface HER2 at levels proportional to the membrane area represented by the vesicles. This step is essential to validate that the TMVs accurately recapitulate the immunological identity of the tumor and can serve as a reliable source of targetable antigens.

Once baseline characterization is complete, the TMVs are functionally augmented through the incorporation of GPI-ISMs via a proprietary protein transfer technique. GPI-ISMs are recombinant fusion proteins consisting of an immunostimulatory domain—such as the extracellular portion of a cytokine or co-stimulatory ligand—linked to a C-terminal GPI-anchor signal sequence. This sequence directs the attachment of a preassembled GPI moiety during post-translational processing in eukaryotic expression systems, resulting in a protein that is tethered to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane via a glycolipid anchor rather than a transmembrane domain. The GPI anchor consists of a phosphoethanolamine linker connected to a conserved glycan core (mannose-glucosamine-inositol) embedded in the lipid bilayer, allowing the attached protein to diffuse laterally within the membrane plane while remaining stably associated with the vesicle surface. Purified GPI-ISMs, such as GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12, are produced in mammalian cell lines (e.g., HEK293 or CHO cells) and isolated using affinity chromatography based on engineered tags (e.g., His-tag or FLAG-tag), followed by endotoxin removal and buffer exchange into PBS or a similar isotonic solution.

The incorporation process involves incubating a defined quantity of TMVs (e.g., 1 milligram of total membrane protein) with a stoichiometric excess of purified GPI-ISM (e.g., 25 micrograms) at 37°C for four hours under gentle rotation. During this incubation, the hydrophobic GPI anchor spontaneously partitions into the lipid bilayer of the TMVs, driven by thermodynamic favorability, resulting in the stable display of the immunostimulatory protein on the vesicle surface. Unincorporated GPI-ISMs are removed by ultracentrifugation, which pellets the TMVs while leaving soluble or loosely associated proteins in the supernatant. The pellet is then washed once with PBS to eliminate residual contaminants, yielding a purified preparation of GPI-ISM-decorated TMVs, referred to herein as “TMV vaccine.” The efficiency and specificity of incorporation are confirmed by flow cytometry using fluorophore-conjugated antibodies against the immunostimulatory domain. For example, TMVs incubated with GPI-GM-CSF are stained with anti-mouse GM-CSF PE antibody, and those treated with GPI-IL-12 are stained with anti-mouse IL-12 APC antibody. Control TMVs that have not been exposed to GPI-ISMs show negligible background fluorescence, whereas TMV vaccine preparations exhibit bright, specific staining, demonstrating successful and selective incorporation. Importantly, this protein transfer method does not require chemical crosslinkers, detergents, or harsh physical treatments that could compromise membrane integrity or antigen conformation, thereby preserving the native structure and function of both the tumor antigens and the GPI-ISMs. The resulting TMV vaccine is thus a multifunctional nanoparticle that co-presents tumor-derived antigens and membrane-tethered adjuvants in a spatially organized manner, optimized for simultaneous recognition and internalization by dendritic cells.

### TMVs Containing the Tumor Antigen HER2 and Protein Transferred GPI-ISMs on Their Surface

A key embodiment of the present invention involves the generation and use of tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) that simultaneously display a defined tumor-associated antigen, such as human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs) on their surface. This dual-functionalized vesicle serves as a precision-engineered vaccine component designed to elicit robust, antigen-specific immune responses while providing localized adjuvant signals to enhance dendritic cell (DC) activation. The model system employed to demonstrate this concept utilizes the D2F2/E2 murine mammary carcinoma cell line, which was generated by stable transfection of the parental D2F2 line with full-length human HER2 cDNA, resulting in constitutive overexpression of the HER2 protein on the cell surface. HER2 is a well-established oncogenic driver and therapeutic target in approximately 20% of human breast cancers, and its presence provides a clear, quantifiable antigenic marker for tracking vaccine-induced immune responses. TMVs prepared from D2F2/E2 cells inherit this HER2 expression, as confirmed by flow cytometric analysis showing strong reactivity with anti-human HER2 antibodies, thereby validating the vesicles as a faithful surrogate of the original tumor’s antigenic profile.

Following TMV isolation, the vesicles are subjected to protein transfer-mediated incorporation of GPI-ISMs, specifically GPI-anchored granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GPI-GM-CSF) and GPI-anchored interleukin-12 (GPI-IL-12). These two cytokines were selected based on their complementary roles in anti-tumor immunity: GM-CSF promotes the differentiation, survival, and functional maturation of DCs, while IL-12 drives the polarization of naïve CD4+ T cells toward a T helper 1 (Th1) phenotype and enhances the cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells. By anchoring these cytokines to the TMV surface via GPI linkages, their biological activity is spatially restricted to DCs that actively engage and internalize the vesicles, thereby avoiding the systemic toxicities and off-target immunosuppressive effects associated with soluble cytokine administration. Flow cytometric verification of the final TMV vaccine product demonstrates the concurrent presence of HER2, GPI-GM-CSF, and GPI-IL-12 on the same vesicle population. Specifically, when stained with anti-HER2, anti-GM-CSF, and anti-IL-12 antibodies conjugated to distinct fluorophores, the TMV vaccine exhibits positive signals in all three channels, whereas control TMVs (without GPI-ISMs) are positive only for HER2. This co-expression is critical, as it ensures that any DC that takes up a HER2-containing TMV is simultaneously exposed to GM-CSF and IL-12 signals, creating an integrated stimulus that couples antigen recognition with co-stimulation and cytokine-driven differentiation.

The structural integrity of the incorporated GPI-ISMs is further validated by functional assays. For instance, TMVs displaying GPI-GM-CSF are shown to support the survival and proliferation of GM-CSF-dependent cell lines in vitro, confirming that the cytokine retains its receptor-binding capability. Similarly, GPI-IL-12 on TMVs induces phosphorylation of signal transducer and activator of transcription 4 (STAT4) in responsive immune cells, a hallmark of IL-12 receptor signaling. These data collectively establish that the protein transfer process yields TMVs that are not only antigenically authentic but also functionally active as immunostimulatory platforms. In the context of DC vaccine manufacturing, these HER2+ GPI-ISM+ TMVs are used to pulse autologous DCs ex vivo, resulting in DCs that present HER2-derived peptides on both MHC class I and class II molecules while exhibiting an activated phenotype characterized by high expression of CD86, MHC-II, and secretion of Th1-polarizing cytokines. Upon administration to tumor-bearing hosts, these DCs prime HER2-specific CD8+ T cells capable of recognizing and lysing HER2-expressing tumor cells, as demonstrated by significant inhibition of D2F2/E2 tumor growth and prolonged survival in vaccinated mice compared to controls receiving unpulsed DCs or DCs pulsed with non-adjuvanted TMVs. This embodiment thus exemplifies the power of the TMV platform to convert a single, well-defined tumor antigen into a potent, multi-modal vaccine when combined with membrane-tethered adjuvants.

### Uptake of TMVs by DCs is Potentiated by GPI-ISMs

A pivotal discovery underlying the present invention is that the incorporation of glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs) into tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) significantly enhances their uptake by dendritic cells (DCs), a finding that directly addresses a major bottleneck in particulate vaccine design—efficient internalization by professional antigen-presenting cells. To investigate this phenomenon, the inventors employed a pH-sensitive fluorescent dye, pHrodo Deep Red, which exhibits minimal fluorescence at neutral pH but undergoes a dramatic increase in quantum yield under acidic conditions, such as those found in late endosomes and phagolysosomes. TMVs, both plain (without GPI-ISMs) and adjuvanted (with GPI-GM-CSF, GPI-IL-12, or both), were labeled with pHrodo Deep Red and incubated with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) for varying durations (30, 90, and 180 minutes). Flow cytometric analysis of the DCs revealed that while all TMV formulations were internalized to some degree, the presence of GPI-ISMs markedly accelerated and amplified uptake. Specifically, after 30 minutes of co-culture, only a small fraction of DCs exposed to plain TMVs exhibited pHrodo fluorescence, indicating limited early internalization. In contrast, DCs incubated with TMVs containing GPI-GM-CSF, GPI-IL-12, or both showed a substantially higher percentage of pHrodo-positive cells, suggesting that GPI-ISMs facilitate more rapid engagement with DC surface receptors or enhance vesicle adhesion to the plasma membrane.

By 180 minutes, the differences became even more pronounced. DCs pulsed with TMVs incorporating both GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 (referred to as TMV vaccine) displayed the highest frequency of pHrodo-positive cells, followed closely by those treated with GPI-GM-CSF-only TMVs, while GPI-IL-12-only TMVs also showed significant improvement over plain TMVs. Quantitative analysis of mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) further demonstrated that not only was the proportion of TMV-positive DCs increased, but the amount of TMV internalized per cell was also greater in the presence of GPI-ISMs, with the dual-GPI-ISM formulation yielding the highest MFI values. These results indicate that GPI-ISMs do not merely act as passive adjuvants but actively modulate the interaction between TMVs and DCs, potentially by engaging specific receptors that trigger enhanced endocytic activity. For example, GM-CSF binds to the GM-CSF receptor (CD116), which is expressed on immature DCs and is known to regulate cytoskeletal dynamics and phagocytic capacity. Similarly, IL-12 can signal through IL-12 receptors on DCs, although its primary role is in T-cell polarization; however, membrane-tethered IL-12 may exert autocrine or juxtacrine effects that prime DCs for increased antigen uptake. The synergistic effect observed with the combination of both GPI-ISMs suggests that they may act through complementary or convergent pathways to maximize TMV internalization.

This potentiation of uptake is critically important for vaccine efficacy, as the efficiency of antigen internalization directly correlates with the magnitude of the subsequent T-cell response. Enhanced uptake ensures that a greater number of DCs acquire sufficient antigenic payload to reach the threshold required for T-cell activation, while also increasing the likelihood of cross-presentation on MHC class I molecules. Moreover, because the GPI-ISMs are co-internalized along with the TMVs, the adjuvant signals are delivered intracellularly in close proximity to the antigen-processing machinery, promoting coordinated activation of innate immune pathways such as NF-κB and IRF signaling cascades. This spatial and temporal coupling of antigen and adjuvant delivery is a key innovation of the present platform, distinguishing it from conventional approaches where antigens and soluble adjuvants are administered separately and may not co-localize within the same DC. The data demonstrating GPI-ISM-enhanced TMV uptake thus provide a mechanistic foundation for the superior immunogenicity and therapeutic efficacy observed in vivo, establishing that the protein transfer strategy not only adds immunostimulatory function but also fundamentally improves the bioavailability of the vaccine antigen to the immune system.

### DC Vaccine Therapy Reduces Tumor Growth

The therapeutic efficacy of the dendritic cell (DC) vaccine platform described herein was rigorously evaluated in a murine model of HER2-positive breast cancer using the D2F2/E2 tumor cell line, which expresses human HER2 as a defined tumor-associated antigen. In this experimental paradigm, female BALB/c mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 2 × 10^5 D2F2/E2 cells in the hind flank to establish palpable tumors. Seven days post-inoculation, when tumors were measurable but not yet ulcerated, mice received a single subcutaneous injection of 1 × 10^6 bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) in the contralateral flank. Four treatment groups were compared: (1) phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) as a negative control; (2) unpulsed BMDCs (DC alone); (3) BMDCs pulsed with plain tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) derived from D2F2/E2 cells but lacking GPI-anchored immunostimulatory molecules (GPI-ISMs); and (4) BMDCs pulsed with TMVs incorporating both GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 (DC vaccine). Tumor growth was monitored twice weekly by caliper measurements, and survival was recorded until humane endpoints were reached.

The results unequivocally demonstrated that only the DC vaccine group exhibited significant anti-tumor activity. Mice treated with PBS, unpulsed DCs, or plain TMV-pulsed DCs showed indistinguishable tumor growth kinetics, with tumors progressing rapidly to a mean volume exceeding 1000 mm³ by day 35 post-inoculation, at which point all animals in these groups had succumbed to disease. In stark contrast, mice receiving the DC vaccine displayed a marked inhibition of tumor growth, with mean tumor volumes remaining below 200 mm³ throughout the observation period and a substantial proportion achieving complete or near-complete regression. This translated into a dramatic survival benefit: while control groups survived a median of 35 days, the DC vaccine group exhibited a median survival of 57 days, representing a greater than 60% increase in lifespan. Statistical analysis by two-way ANOVA confirmed a highly significant difference in tumor growth trajectories between the DC vaccine group and all control groups (p < 0.001), and log-rank testing revealed a significant improvement in overall survival (p < 0.01).

These findings underscore several critical aspects of the invention. First, the mere presence of DCs or DCs loaded with tumor antigens (plain TMVs) is insufficient to elicit protective immunity, highlighting the indispensable role of co-delivered adjuvant signals. Second, the combination of GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 on the TMV surface creates a synergistic immunostimulatory milieu that licenses DCs to prime potent, tumor-specific T-cell responses. Third, the ex vivo pulsing strategy ensures that only fully activated, antigen-loaded DCs are administered, bypassing the immunosuppressive barriers of the tumor microenvironment that often impair in situ DC function. Histopathological and immunological analyses of excised tumors further revealed extensive lymphocytic infiltration and signs of tumor cell apoptosis exclusively in the DC vaccine group, consistent with an active, cell-mediated immune attack. Collectively, these data validate the DC vaccine as a therapeutically effective intervention in an antigen-defined cancer model and establish a proof-of-concept for clinical translation in HER2-positive malignancies, including breast, gastric, and ovarian cancers.

### DC Vaccine Inhibits 4T1 Tumor Growth, Reduces Lung Metastasis, and Improved Immune Profile of Tumors

To extend the applicability of the dendritic cell (DC) vaccine platform beyond cancers with well-defined tumor-associated antigens, the inventors evaluated its efficacy in the 4T1 murine model of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)—a highly aggressive, poorly immunogenic, and spontaneously metastatic carcinoma that lacks expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and HER2, thereby representing a clinically challenging scenario with no established targeted therapies. In this model, female BALB/c mice were inoculated subcutaneously with 5 × 10^4 4T1 cells, and five days later, when micrometastases had likely already disseminated, they received a single subcutaneous injection of 1 × 10^6 bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) pulsed with 4T1-derived tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) incorporating both GPI-GM-CSF and GPI-IL-12 (DC vaccine) in the contralateral flank. Control groups included PBS, unpulsed DCs, and DCs pulsed with plain 4T1 TMVs (without GPI-ISMs). Tumor growth was monitored, and on day 20 post-inoculation, lungs were harvested to quantify metastatic burden, while primary tumors were processed for immune profiling by flow cytometry.

The DC vaccine demonstrated remarkable efficacy even in this antigen-undefined, metastatic setting. Primary tumor growth was significantly inhibited in the DC vaccine group compared to all controls, with mean tumor volumes reduced by more than 50% at the study endpoint. More impressively, the metastatic burden in the lungs was dramatically decreased: quantitative colony-forming assays following collagenase digestion and 6-thioguanine selection revealed a 2- to 3-fold reduction in viable 4T1 tumor cells in the lungs of DC vaccine-treated mice relative to control groups. This anti-metastatic effect is of particular clinical relevance, as metastatic spread—not primary tumor bulk—is the primary cause of mortality in TNBC patients. To understand the immunological basis of this protection, the immune infiltrate of primary 4T1 tumors was analyzed. Flow cytometry of tumor-dissociated single-cell suspensions showed a significant increase in the frequency of MHC class II-positive, CD11c-positive dendritic cells within the tumor microenvironment of DC vaccine-treated mice—approximately double that observed in PBS controls. This indicates enhanced recruitment or local proliferation of antigen-presenting cells, creating a more immunologically active niche. Furthermore, there was a marked elevation in both CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes within the tumors, with CD8+ T cells showing increased expression of activation markers such as CD69 and granzyme B, suggestive of ongoing cytotoxic activity. These changes collectively reflect a shift from an immunologically “cold” to a “hot” tumor phenotype, characterized by robust T-cell infiltration and antigen presentation—features strongly associated with improved clinical outcomes in human TNBC.

The success of the DC vaccine in the 4T1 model is attributed to the comprehensive antigenic content of the TMVs, which encompass the full spectrum of 4T1 surface proteins, including shared tumor antigens, stress-induced ligands, and potentially unique neoantigens arising from the cell line’s genomic instability. By delivering this diverse antigen pool alongside membrane-tethered GM-CSF and IL-12, the vaccine primes polyclonal T-cell responses against multiple tumor epitopes, thereby overcoming the limitations of single-antigen targeting and reducing the risk of immune escape. The localized action of GPI-anchored cytokines ensures that DC activation occurs specifically in the context of tumor antigen presentation, avoiding systemic inflammation or expansion of immunosuppressive cells. These results firmly establish the versatility of the platform, demonstrating its potential to treat not only antigen-defined cancers but also heterogeneous, metastatic malignancies like TNBC that have historically been refractory to immunotherapy.

### Bone Marrow-Derived Dendritic Cell (BMDC) Production

The production of bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) constitutes a standardized and reproducible method for generating large numbers of immature dendritic cells suitable for ex vivo pulsing with tumor membrane vesicles (TMVs) in the context of the disclosed vaccine platform. This protocol begins with the euthanasia of donor mice, typically syngeneic to the tumor model being studied (e.g., BALB/c mice for 4T1 or D2F2/E2 tumors), followed by aseptic removal of the femurs and tibias. The bones are meticulously cleaned of adherent muscle and connective tissue, and the bone marrow cavities are flushed with pre-warmed, serum-free RPMI-1640 medium using a 22-gauge needle attached to a syringe. The resulting cell suspension, containing hematopoietic progenitor cells, is passed through a 70-micron cell strainer to remove bone fragments and debris, and red blood cells are lysed using a commercial ammonium-chloride-potassium (ACK) lysis buffer or equivalent reagent to enrich for nucleated cells. The remaining cells are counted and resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), L-glutamine, penicillin-streptomycin, and, critically, 20 nanograms per milliliter of recombinant murine granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (rmGM-CSF).

The cell suspension is then plated in non-tissue-culture-treated Petri dishes or flasks at a density of 2 × 10^5 cells per milliliter to discourage adherence of macrophage precursors, which tend to attach more readily to plastic surfaces. Cultures are maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, and rmGM-CSF is replenished on days 3, 6, and 8 to sustain DC differentiation and prevent drift toward macrophage lineage. Over the course of 10 days, the progenitor cells proliferate and differentiate into a homogeneous population of immature BMDCs, characterized by a typical veiled or dendritic morphology under phase-contrast microscopy and surface expression of CD11c, MHC class II (at low levels), and co-stimulatory molecules such as CD86 (also at low levels). On day 10, non-adherent and loosely adherent cells are harvested by gentle pipetting, washed twice in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) by centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 minutes, and resuspended at the desired concentration for pulsing with TMVs. The purity of the BMDC preparation is routinely assessed by flow cytometry using anti-CD11c antibodies, with yields typically exceeding 85% CD11c-positive cells. This method provides a consistent, scalable source of DCs that are highly receptive to antigen uptake and activation, forming the cellular backbone of the vaccine. Importantly, the use of syngeneic BMDCs ensures compatibility with the host immune system upon reinfusion, minimizing the risk of alloreactivity while maximizing the potential for tumor-specific T-cell priming.

### 4T1 Lung Metastasis Assay

The evaluation of metastatic burden in the 4T1 murine breast cancer model is a critical component of assessing the therapeutic efficacy of the dendritic cell (DC) vaccine, given the highly metastatic nature of this tumor line and its propensity to colonize the lungs—a common site of metastasis in human triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). To quantify lung metastases, a well-established ex vivo colony-forming assay was employed, leveraging the inherent resistance of 4T1 cells to the purine analog 6-thioguanine (6-TG), which is toxic to normal murine fibroblasts and hematopoietic cells. Twenty days after subcutaneous inoculation of 4T1 cells, mice from each treatment group were euthanized, and their lungs were aseptically removed and placed in sterile culture dishes containing cold, serum-free DMEM. The lungs were finely minced with scalpels and digested in a solution of 1 milligram per milliliter collagenase type IV for two hours at 37°C under constant rotation to dissociate the tissue into a single-cell suspension. The resulting cell mixture was filtered through a 70-micron strainer to remove undigested fragments and washed twice by centrifugation in complete DMEM supplemented with 60 micromolar 6-TG.

The cell suspension was then plated in 96-well tissue culture plates at limiting dilutions (e.g., 10,000, 1,000, and 100 cells per well) in triplicate, with each well containing 6-TG-supplemented medium. The plates were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 7–10 days, during which time only 4T1 tumor cells—resistant to 6-TG due to their lack of hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase (HGPRT) activity—proliferated to form visible colonies, while host-derived lung cells underwent apoptosis. Once confluent growth was observed in the highest-density wells, all wells were harvested, and the total number of viable 4T1 cells was determined using an automated cell counter such as the Cellometer T4. The metastatic burden was calculated as the total number of 4T1 cells per lung, normalized to the input cell number and plating efficiency. This assay provides a highly sensitive and quantitative measure of metastatic dissemination, far more accurate than simple visual counting of surface nodules, which can miss micrometastases embedded deep within the lung parenchyma.

In the context of the DC vaccine study, this assay revealed a profound reduction in lung metastases in mice treated with DCs pulsed with GPI-ISM-incorporated 4T1 TMVs compared to all control groups. Specifically, the DC vaccine group exhibited a 2- to 3-fold decrease in metastatic 4T1 cells, demonstrating that the vaccine not only controls primary tumor growth but also exerts systemic anti-metastatic effects. This is likely mediated by the induction of circulating tumor-specific T cells that surveil distant sites and eliminate disseminated tumor cells before they can establish overt metastases. The 4T1 lung metastasis assay thus serves as a robust preclinical model for evaluating the ability of immunotherapies to impact the lethal consequences of metastatic disease, and the positive results obtained with the DC vaccine strongly support its potential clinical utility in preventing or treating metastatic TNBC.
