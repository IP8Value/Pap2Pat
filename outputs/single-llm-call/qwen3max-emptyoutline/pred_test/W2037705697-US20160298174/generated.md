# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE PRESENT INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular biology and diagnostic enzymology, specifically to engineered variants of DNA polymerases with enhanced mismatch extension selectivity. More particularly, the invention provides mutant forms of the Klenow fragment of Thermus aquaticus (Taq) DNA polymerase, herein referred to as KlenTaq, wherein specific amino acid residues that interact with the phosphate backbone of the primer strand have been substituted to increase the enzyme’s ability to discriminate against extension of primers containing a 3′-terminal mismatch relative to the template strand. These engineered polymerases exhibit superior performance in applications requiring high-fidelity allele discrimination, including but not limited to allele-specific amplification (ASA), methylation-specific PCR (MSP), and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing via sequence-specific primed PCR (PCR-SSP). The invention further encompasses methods for detecting single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), epigenetic modifications such as 5-methylcytosine, and HLA alleles using these mutant polymerases, as well as kits and diagnostic assays incorporating them. The disclosed variants enable robust, cost-effective, and highly specific nucleic acid amplification without the need for modified primers or complex instrumentation, thereby facilitating point-of-care and high-throughput genetic and epigenetic diagnostics in clinical, forensic, and research settings.

## BACKGROUND OF THE PRESENT INVENTION

The advent of personalized medicine has underscored the critical importance of accurately identifying genetic and epigenetic variations that influence disease susceptibility, drug response, and therapeutic outcomes. Central to this endeavor is the detection of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which represent the most abundant form of genetic variation in the human genome. SNPs can reside in coding or non-coding regions and may exert profound effects on protein structure, gene regulation, or serve as neutral markers for linkage and association studies. In clinical diagnostics, SNPs such as the prothrombin G20210A mutation are well-established risk factors for venous thromboembolism, necessitating reliable genotyping methods for risk stratification and preventive care. Similarly, in transfusion and transplantation medicine, the extreme polymorphism of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genes demands high-resolution typing to ensure donor-recipient compatibility and minimize graft rejection or graft-versus-host disease. Beyond genetics, epigenetic alterations—particularly DNA methylation at CpG dinucleotides—have emerged as pivotal biomarkers in oncology, with hypermethylation of promoter regions in genes like Septin 9 serving as early indicators of colorectal cancer.

To address these diagnostic needs, several PCR-based methodologies have been developed that rely fundamentally on the ability of a DNA polymerase to discriminate between perfectly matched and mismatched primer-template duplexes. Allele-specific amplification (ASA) is one such technique, wherein amplification proceeds efficiently only when the 3′-end of the primer is perfectly complementary to the target allele. In real-time ASA, this discrimination is monitored through the accumulation of double-stranded DNA, typically detected by intercalating dyes such as SYBR Green I, with mismatched reactions exhibiting delayed or absent fluorescence signals. Methylation-specific PCR (MSP) exploits a similar principle: following bisulfite conversion of genomic DNA—which deaminates unmethylated cytosines to uracils while leaving 5-methylcytosines intact—primers are designed to anneal specifically to either methylated or unmethylated sequences. Efficient amplification occurs only when the primer matches the methylation state of the template, again contingent upon the polymerase’s inability to extend mismatched termini. Likewise, in HLA typing by PCR-SSP, allele assignment is based on the presence or absence of amplification products from a panel of reactions, each employing primers specific to particular HLA alleles; the fidelity of this method is directly dependent on the polymerase’s capacity to suppress non-specific amplification arising from cross-reactive priming.

Despite their utility, conventional DNA polymerases such as wild-type Taq or its Klenow fragment (KlenTaq) often exhibit insufficient mismatch discrimination, leading to false-positive signals and reduced assay specificity. This limitation stems from the inherent biochemical properties of these enzymes, which, while optimized for processivity and thermostability, lack stringent geometric or energetic constraints that would prevent extension of mispaired 3′-ends. To overcome this, various strategies have been employed, including the use of modified primers with destabilizing bases near the 3′-terminus, locked nucleic acids, or chemical modifications. However, these approaches increase reagent costs and complicate assay design. An alternative and more elegant solution involves engineering the DNA polymerase itself to enhance its intrinsic selectivity. Prior work has demonstrated that mutations in conserved motifs—particularly in the palm domain, such as motif C (YGDTD)—can modulate fidelity. For instance, substituting polar residues like glutamine or histidine with hydrophobic leucine in Taq polymerase was shown to improve mismatch extension selectivity, a concept later extended to Pfu polymerase.

Nevertheless, a systematic exploration of residues beyond the canonical active site motifs—particularly those involved in stabilizing the primer-template duplex through electrostatic interactions with the phosphate backbone—has remained underexplored. Structural studies of KlenTaq reveal that in its closed conformation, five basic amino acids—R487, K508, R536, R587, and R660—make direct contact with the primer strand. These residues, while not part of the catalytic center, are positioned to influence the conformational dynamics and stability of the primer-template complex, thereby potentially affecting the enzyme’s ability to sense and reject mismatches. The hypothesis that altering these interactions could tune selectivity without compromising overall activity represents a rational yet untested approach to polymerase engineering. Moreover, while combinatorial methods such as directed evolution and gene shuffling have been used to optimize enzyme properties, the additive or synergistic effects of multiple selectivity-enhancing mutations remain uncertain, especially given potential trade-offs with protein stability and expression yield.

Commercially available high-fidelity polymerases, though improved in replication accuracy, are often not optimized for the specific demands of ASA or MSP, where the critical parameter is not general error rate but rather the binary discrimination at the 3′-primer end. Furthermore, many clinical samples—such as whole blood—contain PCR inhibitors that can compromise amplification efficiency, necessitating polymerases with both high selectivity and robustness in complex matrices. The development of a DNA polymerase variant that simultaneously offers enhanced mismatch discrimination, retained or improved thermostability, compatibility with crude samples, and applicability across multiple diagnostic modalities (SNP genotyping, methylation analysis, HLA typing) would represent a significant advance in molecular diagnostics. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing a novel class of KlenTaq mutants with precisely engineered substitutions at primer-backbone-interacting residues, validated across a range of clinically relevant assays and demonstrating superior performance over existing wild-type and commercial enzymes.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE PRESENT INVENTION

The present invention provides engineered variants of the Klenow fragment of Thermus aquaticus DNA polymerase (KlenTaq) that exhibit enhanced mismatch extension selectivity, enabling highly specific nucleic acid amplification in applications requiring discrimination of single-base differences. The invention is based on the discovery that substitution of specific basic amino acid residues—namely arginine and lysine—that interact with the phosphate backbone of the primer strand in the closed conformation of KlenTaq can significantly increase the enzyme’s ability to discriminate against extension of primers with a 3′-terminal mismatch relative to the template strand. These residues, identified as R487, K508, R536, R587, and R660 in the KlenTaq sequence, are located at varying distances from the catalytic active site and contribute to the structural stabilization of the primer-template duplex during polymerization. By systematically replacing these residues with alternative amino acids through saturation mutagenesis, the inventors identified multiple mutant variants that display markedly improved selectivity while maintaining sufficient catalytic activity for robust PCR amplification.

The engineered KlenTaq variants of the present invention are characterized by one or more amino acid substitutions selected from the group consisting of R487V, R487H, K508Y, K508W, R536K, R587K, R587I, R660T, and R660V. Among these, the R660V mutant has been identified as particularly advantageous due to its combination of high mismatch extension selectivity, retained thermostability, and robust performance in diverse diagnostic contexts. The substitution of arginine at position 660 with valine—a hydrophobic, non-polar residue—disrupts the electrostatic interaction between the enzyme and the primer backbone, thereby increasing the energetic penalty for accommodating a mispaired 3′-end and reducing the rate of nucleotide incorporation in mismatched complexes. This effect is quantifiable as an increased ratio of extension rates for matched versus mismatched substrates (k<sub>match</sub>/k<sub>mismatch</sub>), with the R660V mutant exhibiting up to a 36-fold improvement in selectivity over wild-type KlenTaq under pre-steady-state conditions.

The KlenTaq variants of the present invention are recombinantly expressed in Escherichia coli and purified using standard chromatographic techniques, such as immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) via a hexahistidine tag, yielding preparations of high purity suitable for diagnostic applications. The enzymes are stable when stored in buffered glycerol solutions at −20°C for extended periods, with no loss of activity or selectivity observed over six months. Importantly, the mutants retain the thermostability characteristic of the KlenTaq scaffold, enabling their use in standard thermal cycling protocols without modification.

The enhanced selectivity of these variants renders them particularly suitable for allele-specific amplification (ASA), wherein the presence or absence of amplification indicates the genotype at a specific SNP locus. In ASA, forward primers are designed with their 3′-terminal nucleotide complementary to one allele of the SNP, such that only perfect matches support efficient extension and exponential amplification. When used in real-time PCR with dsDNA-binding dyes such as SYBR Green I, the mutant polymerases produce a clear distinction between matched and mismatched reactions, as evidenced by a significantly higher threshold cycle (C<sub>t</sub>) value or complete absence of signal in the latter. For example, in genotyping the prothrombin G20210A SNP, the R660V mutant achieves a ΔC<sub>t</sub> of 18 cycles between matched and mismatched templates, compared to only 8.5 cycles with wild-type KlenTaq, thereby improving genotyping confidence and reducing ambiguity in heterozygous calls.

Furthermore, the invention enables multiplexed ASA in a single reaction tube by incorporating differential-length amplicons or melting temperature tags into allele-specific primers. For instance, by appending a 30-nucleotide non-complementary tail to one allele-specific primer, two distinct amplicons are generated whose melting temperatures can be resolved by post-amplification melt curve analysis, allowing simultaneous detection of both alleles in homozygous and heterozygous samples. This approach, implemented with the R660V mutant, successfully discriminates all three possible genotypes (G/G, A/A, G/A) of the Factor II SNP in a single closed-tube assay, enhancing throughput and reducing reagent consumption.

The utility of the KlenTaq variants extends to methylation-specific PCR (MSP), a technique for detecting 5-methylcytosine at specific CpG sites following bisulfite conversion of genomic DNA. In MSP, primers are designed to anneal to sequences where cytosines have been converted to uracils (unmethylated) or remain as cytosines (methylated). The 3′-end of the primer is positioned opposite the interrogated CpG, such that a mismatch arises if the methylation state does not match the primer design. The R660V mutant demonstrates superior discrimination in MSP assays targeting the Septin 9 promoter, a known colorectal cancer biomarker, producing strong amplification only with the correctly matched template and minimal background with the mismatched counterpart. This performance exceeds that of commercial MSP kits, offering a more sensitive and specific alternative for epigenetic diagnostics.

In the context of HLA typing by sequence-specific primed PCR (PCR-SSP), the invention provides a polymerase with enhanced specificity for discriminating between closely related HLA alleles that differ by only one or two nucleotides at the 3′-end of the primer. Using a primer mix specific for HLA-DRB1*03, *11, *13, and *14 alleles, the R660V mutant produced clean, specific amplification only in samples containing the target alleles, whereas the commercially used AmpliTaq Gold exhibited non-specific amplification in negative controls, indicating inferior selectivity. This improved specificity reduces the risk of misassignment and increases the reliability of HLA typing, which is critical in transplantation medicine.

Notably, the R660V mutant also exhibits resistance to inhibitors present in whole blood, enabling direct SNP genotyping from minimally processed samples. By increasing the concentration of SYBR Green I to counteract fluorescence quenching and using a higher enzyme dose, the mutant successfully amplified the Factor II SNP from dried blood spots without prior DNA extraction, opening avenues for point-of-care testing in resource-limited settings.

Finally, the overall fidelity of the R660V mutant was assessed using a lacZ-based error rate assay, revealing an error rate of 2.8 × 10<sup>−5</sup> mutations per base per duplication, approximately three-fold lower than wild-type KlenTaq (8.8 × 10<sup>−5</sup>). This improvement is primarily due to a reduction in transition mutations, consistent with the enzyme’s enhanced ability to reject mispaired purine-pyrimidine combinations.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Saturation mutagenesis was performed at five basic amino acid positions in KlenTaq—R487, K508, R536, R587, and R660—using degenerate NNK or MNN primers to generate comprehensive mutant libraries. Each library contained at least 320 clones, ensuring >99.9% coverage of all 20 amino acids at the targeted position. Libraries were screened in 384-well plates using heat-treated E. coli lysates expressing the mutant enzymes. Screening reactions included matched and mismatched primer-template pairs, with amplification monitored in real-time by SYBR Green I fluorescence. Mutants exhibiting delayed or absent amplification in the mismatch condition relative to the match condition were identified as hits with enhanced selectivity. Sequencing of hits revealed that substitutions to hydrophobic residues (e.g., valine, isoleucine, tryptophan) or polar uncharged residues (e.g., tyrosine, threonine) were common among selective variants, supporting the hypothesis that reducing positive charge at the primer backbone interface increases mismatch discrimination.

### Example 2

Selected mutants (R487V, K508Y, R536K, R587K, R587I, R660T, R660V) were purified and subjected to primer extension assays. A 20-nt radiolabeled primer was annealed to 33-nt templates differing only at the position opposite the primer’s 3′-end (A for match, G for mismatch). Reactions were initiated by dNTP addition and quenched after 10 minutes at 55°C. PAGE analysis showed that all mutants failed to extend the mismatched primer, while wild-type KlenTaq produced detectable full-length product. Among mutants, R660V and R587K displayed full-length extension in the match condition comparable to wild-type, indicating retained activity, whereas R487V and K508Y showed partial extension, suggesting reduced catalytic efficiency.

### Example 3

Pre-steady-state kinetics were performed to quantify mismatch extension selectivity. Reactions used saturating enzyme and dNTP concentrations, with time points collected from 0.1 to 600 seconds. The first-order rate constant for nucleotide incorporation (k<sub>pol</sub>) was determined for matched and mismatched substrates. The R660V mutant exhibited k<sub>A</sub>/k<sub>G</sub> = 360 s<sup>−1</sup>/0.01 s<sup>−1</sup>, yielding a selectivity ratio 36-fold higher than wild-type KlenTaq (k<sub>A</sub>/k<sub>G</sub> = 10). This confirms that the R660V substitution dramatically slows mismatch extension while preserving rapid matched extension.

### Example 4

ASA assays were conducted on HeLa genomic DNA targeting the prothrombin G20210A SNP. Reactions with R660V showed C<sub>t</sub> = 30 for matched primers and no amplification (C<sub>t</sub> > 50) for mismatched primers, giving ΔC<sub>t</sub> = 20. Wild-type KlenTaq gave C<sub>t</sub> = 28 and 36.5, respectively (ΔC<sub>t</sub> = 8.5). Commercial Taq polymerases (Platinum Taq, Taq 2X Master Mix) yielded ΔC<sub>t</sub> values of 9–11, confirming the superior performance of R660V.

### Example 5

Multiplex ASA was developed by adding a 30-nt tail to the F2forG primer, creating a 94-bp amplicon for the G-allele versus a 64-bp amplicon for the A-allele. Using R660V, melt curve analysis of reactions with gDNA standards clearly distinguished homozygous G/G (T<sub>m</sub> = 82°C), A/A (T<sub>m</sub> = 78°C), and heterozygous G/A (both peaks) samples in a single tube, demonstrating the feasibility of closed-tube, multiplexed genotyping.

### Example 6

Direct genotyping from whole blood was achieved by spotting 0.5 µL blood into wells, drying, and adding PCR mix containing 30× SYBR Green I and 500 nM R660V. The assay correctly identified a blood sample as G/G (wild-type) by a single melt peak at 82°C, with no amplification from the A-specific primer, proving compatibility with crude clinical samples.

### Example 7

HLA typing was performed using a primer mix for HLA-DRB1*03/11/13/14. R660V produced a 465-bp band only in positive control samples (rows 1–3), while AmpliTaq Gold showed faint bands in negative controls (rows 4–5), indicating non-specific amplification. This demonstrates the superior specificity of R660V in complex, multi-allele discrimination tasks.

### Example 8

Error rate determination using a lacZ reversion assay showed R660V has an error rate of 2.8 × 10<sup>−5</sup>, compared to 8.8 × 10<sup>−5</sup> for wild-type KlenTaq. Sequencing of mutants revealed a 3-fold reduction in G→A and C→T transitions, consistent with improved rejection of A:C and G:T mismatches.

### Example 9

MSP was performed on bisulfite-converted templates mimicking methylated (5mC) and unmethylated (U) Septin 9 sequences. R660V showed strong amplification only with matched primer-template pairs (C<sub>t</sub> ≈ 32) and no signal with mismatches (C<sub>t</sub> > 50), outperforming a commercial EpiTect MSP kit, which exhibited higher background and later C<sub>t</sub> values. This confirms the utility of R660V in epigenetic diagnostics.
