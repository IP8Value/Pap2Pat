# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of molecular biology and biotechnology, particularly to genetically encoded systems for recording and storing biological information within living cells. More specifically, the invention provides a novel protein-based molecular recorder that enables the continuous, time-resolved capture of cellular physiological signals through the formation of linear, self-assembling protein chains that incorporate distinct molecular labels in a temporally ordered manner. The invention further encompasses methods for reading out such recorded information post hoc through standard immunostaining and imaging techniques, thereby enabling the retrospective analysis of dynamic cellular processes without the need for live imaging or cell lysis. This technology is particularly applicable to the study of gene expression dynamics, signal transduction pathways, and other time-dependent biological phenomena in intact cells, tissues, and whole organisms, including the mammalian brain.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The ability to monitor biological processes as they unfold over time is fundamental to understanding the mechanisms underlying cellular function, development, disease, and response to environmental stimuli. Traditional approaches to observing dynamic cellular events have relied heavily on live-cell imaging using fluorescent reporters, such as green fluorescent protein (GFP) and its variants, which can be genetically fused to proteins of interest or placed under the control of specific promoters to report on gene expression or signaling activity in real time. While powerful, these methods suffer from significant limitations. Long-term live imaging is technically demanding, requiring specialized and expensive microscopy equipment that must be dedicated to a single experiment for extended periods. Moreover, the number of cells that can be simultaneously monitored is constrained by the field of view and resolution of the imaging system, limiting the scalability and statistical power of such experiments. Additionally, prolonged exposure to excitation light can induce phototoxicity, perturbing normal cellular physiology and potentially confounding experimental results.

In contrast, fixed-tissue imaging methods offer substantial advantages in terms of scalability, resolution, and multiplexing capability. Techniques such as RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and protein immunostaining allow for the simultaneous detection of multiple molecular species across large tissue volumes, including entire organs like the mammalian brain, especially when combined with tissue clearing, sectioning, or expansion microscopy. However, these snapshot methods are inherently limited to capturing biological states at one or, at best, two discrete time points. They cannot provide a continuous record of how a cellular process evolved over time within the same cell or tissue sample. This temporal limitation represents a major gap in the biologist’s toolkit, as many critical biological questions—such as the precise timing and duration of gene activation in response to a stimulus, or the sequence of molecular events during cellular differentiation—require knowledge of the full timecourse of a process, not just its endpoint.

To bridge this gap, researchers have developed molecular recording systems that enable cells to autonomously store a history of their internal states or external experiences in a stable, readable format. A prominent class of such systems utilizes nucleic acids as the storage medium. These DNA- or RNA-based recorders exploit the cell’s own machinery to write information into the genome or transcriptome in response to specific signals, often using integrases, recombinases, or CRISPR-associated enzymes to make permanent, sequence-level changes that can later be read out by high-throughput sequencing. While these nucleic acid recorders have demonstrated impressive capabilities for capturing complex event histories, they share a critical drawback: the readout process necessitates the dissociation and/or lysis of the cells or tissues being studied. This destruction of the sample precludes the correlation of the recorded molecular history with the spatial context, morphology, or other structural features of the original cells. It also prevents the integration of the recorded data with other multimodal measurements that require intact tissue, such as conventional histology or spatial proteomics.

There has therefore been a long-felt but unmet need for a molecular recording technology that combines the continuous, time-resolved recording capability of live imaging with the scalability, spatial preservation, and compatibility with standard histological workflows of fixed-tissue methods. Such a system would need to be fully genetically encodable, so that it can be introduced into any cell type of interest, and its readout should rely on widely available laboratory techniques like immunofluorescence, rather than specialized sequencing infrastructure. Furthermore, the recording medium itself should be a stable, macromolecular structure that grows continuously over time, incorporating new information in a strictly temporal order that can be physically mapped along its length. Prior art in the field of protein-based self-assembly has produced various filamentous structures, but these have typically been used as static scaffolds or for spatial organization, not as dynamic, information-encoding “molecular tapes” capable of preserving a high-fidelity temporal record of cellular physiology. The present invention addresses this need by providing a novel, engineered protein assembly system that functions precisely as such a molecular tape recorder, enabling the retrospective, high-resolution decoding of cellular event histories from fixed samples.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a genetically encoded, protein-based molecular recording system, termed an Expression Recording Island (XRI), which enables the continuous, time-resolved capture and storage of cellular physiological information within living cells. The core of the XRI system is a self-assembling protein monomer that is engineered to form a linear, unbranched polymer chain within the cytosol of a host cell. This monomer is designed to incorporate distinct, user-defined molecular labels—such as epitope tags—in a manner that reflects the temporal dynamics of their availability within the cell. The availability of a given labeled monomer is controlled by linking its expression to a specific biological signal of interest, for example, by placing its gene under the control of an inducible promoter. As the XRI polymer chain grows bidirectionally from a nucleation point over time, it incorporates newly synthesized monomers at its growing ends. Consequently, the local density and order of different molecular labels along the length of the mature polymer chain directly encode the timecourse of the corresponding biological signals that were active during the period of chain growth.

A key innovation of the XRI system is its use of a “constitutive” monomer, which is expressed at a steady, baseline rate throughout the recording period, as an internal reference or “clock.” This constitutive monomer, typically bearing a first epitope tag (e.g., HA), provides a continuous backbone against which the incorporation of “signal” monomers, bearing a second, distinct epitope tag (e.g., FLAG, V5), can be measured. Because the XRI chain grows by the sequential addition of monomers, the position along the chain where a signal monomer is incorporated corresponds to the time at which that monomer was synthesized. By performing quantitative immunostaining for both the constitutive and signal epitope tags on fixed cells or tissues, and then analyzing the intensity profiles of these stains along the length of the XRI polymer, it is possible to reconstruct the precise timecourse of the biological signal that drove the expression of the signal monomer.

The invention further provides a specific, optimized XRI monomer design that ensures faithful temporal encoding. This design is based on a human-engineered variant of an *Escherichia coli* isoaspartyl dipeptidase, 1POK(E239Y), which has been fused to a highly monomeric “insulator” protein, such as Maltose Binding Protein (MBP). The insulator component sterically blocks unwanted lateral interactions between adjacent filaments, thereby preventing the formation of unstructured aggregates and ensuring that the polymer grows exclusively in a longitudinal, linear fashion. This linear geometry is essential for maintaining the strict temporal order of monomer incorporation, as any lateral growth or branching would scramble the spatial encoding of time. The resulting XRI polymer is a stable, fiber-like structure that is compatible with standard cell fixation and immunostaining protocols.

The invention encompasses a comprehensive set of tools and methods for implementing the XRI system. This includes nucleic acid constructs encoding the XRI monomer, which can be delivered to cells via viral vectors (e.g., AAV) or plasmid transfection. The system is designed for multiplexing, allowing for the simultaneous recording of multiple independent biological signals by using a unique epitope tag for each signal of interest. The invention also provides detailed methods for calibrating the XRI system to convert spatial measurements along the polymer into absolute time, as well as computational algorithms for extracting and interpreting the recorded signal timecourses from imaging data. The utility of the XRI system has been demonstrated in both cultured neurons and in the living mouse brain, where it successfully recorded the timecourse of chemically induced gene expression and neuronal activity-dependent gene expression (via the c-fos promoter), respectively. The invention thus provides a powerful and versatile platform for retrospective, high-resolution analysis of dynamic cellular processes in their native spatial and structural context.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Compositions and Components

The present invention is centered on a novel composition of matter: a genetically encoded, self-assembling protein monomer that forms a linear polymer chain within a living cell, wherein the monomer is engineered to incorporate distinct molecular identifiers that encode temporal information about cellular physiology. This monomer, and the polymer it forms, constitute the core of the Expression Recording Island (XRI) system. The fundamental composition of the XRI monomer comprises three essential functional domains: a filament-forming domain, an insulator domain, and a molecular label domain. The filament-forming domain is responsible for the self-assembly property of the monomer, driving its polymerization into a stable, linear chain. In a preferred embodiment, this domain is derived from a human-engineered variant of an *Escherichia coli* isoaspartyl dipeptidase, specifically the 1POK(E239Y) protein. This protein was selected from a panel of candidate self-assembling proteins due to its ability to form clear, stable fiber-like structures in the cytosol of mammalian neurons with minimal background of unstructured aggregates or free-floating monomers. The E239Y mutation is a critical feature that enhances the protein’s propensity for ordered, longitudinal assembly in the complex intracellular environment.

Fused to the filament-forming domain is an insulator domain. This domain is a crucial engineering feature that solves a key problem encountered with the native 1POK(E239Y) protein: the tendency to form unwanted lateral aggregates. The insulator domain is a highly monomeric protein that projects outwards from the lateral surface of the growing filament, creating a steric barrier that prevents adjacent filaments or monomers from binding to the sides of the polymer chain. This forces all new monomer additions to occur only at the two free ends of the chain, ensuring strictly longitudinal, bidirectional growth. This linear geometry is paramount for the temporal fidelity of the recording, as it guarantees that the order of monomers along the chain directly corresponds to the order of their synthesis in time. In the most effective embodiment of the invention, the insulator domain is Maltose Binding Protein (MBP), an *E. coli* protein widely used in biochemistry for its excellent solubility and monomeric properties. Alternative insulator domains, such as monomeric enhanced Green Fluorescent Protein (mEGFP), have been tested but were found to be less effective at completely blocking lateral growth, leading to a slight blurring of the temporal signal. The choice of a truly monomeric insulator is critical; oligomeric or dimeric proteins would themselves promote lateral interactions, defeating the purpose of the insulator.

The third essential component of the XRI monomer is a molecular label domain, which serves as a user-readable identifier. This domain is a short peptide sequence that can be specifically recognized by a commercially available antibody, enabling its detection via standard immunostaining techniques after cell fixation. The most common and preferred labels are small, well-characterized epitope tags such as the hemagglutinin (HA) tag, the FLAG tag, and the V5 tag. The HA tag is typically used for the “constitutive” XRI monomer, which is expressed at a constant, baseline level throughout the recording period. This constitutive monomer forms the continuous backbone of the polymer and serves as an internal reference for both visualizing the entire XRI structure and for calibrating the time axis. The FLAG and V5 tags, or other distinct epitopes, are used for “signal” XRI monomers, whose expression is linked to a biological event of interest. By using a unique tag for each signal to be recorded, the system is inherently multiplexable, allowing for the simultaneous monitoring of multiple independent cellular processes within the same cell. The molecular label is positioned at the terminus of the monomer, distal to the filament-forming interface, to ensure it does not interfere with the self-assembly process and remains fully accessible for antibody binding.

### Expression and Use of XRI Components

The XRI system is deployed by introducing the genetic instructions for the XRI monomers into the target cells. This is achieved through the use of expression vectors, which can be either plasmid DNA for transient transfection or viral vectors, such as adeno-associated virus (AAV), for stable, long-term expression. The choice of promoter within the expression vector dictates the expression dynamics of the XRI monomer and is a key aspect of its use. For the constitutive XRI-HA monomer, a strong, ubiquitous promoter like the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter is used to ensure a steady, high-level supply of monomers throughout the recording window. This constant flux of HA-tagged monomers is what allows the XRI polymer to grow continuously and provides the stable reference signal needed for temporal calibration.

For recording a specific biological signal, a second XRI monomer, for example XRI-FLAG, is co-expressed in the same cell. The gene for this monomer is placed under the control of a promoter that is responsive to the signal of interest. In a preferred embodiment for recording gene expression dynamics, this is a chemically inducible system. Specifically, the XRI-FLAG gene is cloned into a Cre-dependent FLEX (flip-excision) switch vector. In this configuration, the gene is initially in an inverted, non-expressible orientation, flanked by pairs of heterotypic lox sites. The cell is also made to express a tamoxifen-inducible Cre recombinase, ERT2-iCre-ERT2. Upon administration of 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT), the Cre recombinase translocates to the nucleus and catalyzes a recombination event that flips the XRI-FLAG gene into its correct, expressible orientation. This results in a rapid and synchronous induction of XRI-FLAG monomer production at a user-defined time point. The newly synthesized FLAG-tagged monomers are then incorporated into the growing ends of the pre-existing XRI-HA polymer, creating a localized zone of high FLAG density that marks the time of 4-OHT administration.

In another preferred embodiment, the XRI system is used to record endogenous cellular activity, such as neuronal firing. Here, the signal monomer (e.g., XRI-V5) is placed under the control of an activity-dependent promoter, such as the murine c-fos promoter. The c-fos gene is a classic immediate early gene (IEG) whose transcription is rapidly and transiently upregulated in neurons in response to depolarization and other forms of synaptic activity. By linking XRI-V5 expression to the c-fos promoter, any burst of neuronal activity will lead to a pulse of V5-tagged monomer synthesis. These monomers are incorporated into the growing XRI polymer, creating a peak in V5 immunofluorescence whose position along the chain corresponds to the time of the activity event. This allows researchers to retrospectively map the history of a neuron’s activity patterns from a single, fixed tissue sample. The system can be adapted to record any biological process for which a suitable promoter or regulatory element exists, making it a highly versatile tool for a wide range of biological questions.

### Molecules and Compounds

The molecules and compounds central to the XRI system are the genetically encoded protein monomers themselves, as described in detail in the preceding sections. However, the practical implementation of the system also relies on several key exogenous compounds that are used to control and manipulate the recording process. The primary compound is 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT), which is used as the chemical inducer in the Cre/loxP-based recording scheme. 4-OHT is a metabolite of the drug tamoxifen and functions by binding to the mutated estrogen receptor ligand-binding domain (ERT2) that is fused to the Cre recombinase. This binding causes a conformational change that releases the Cre-ERT2 fusion protein from cytoplasmic chaperones, allowing it to translocate into the nucleus where it can act on its loxP target sites. The use of 4-OHT provides a high degree of temporal precision, as the induction of the signal monomer can be triggered at an exact, user-specified time by simply adding the compound to the cell culture medium or administering it to a live animal via intraperitoneal injection. The concentration and duration of 4-OHT treatment can be titrated to achieve the desired level of induction, with typical protocols using a 15-minute pulse at a concentration of 1 µM in cell culture.

Another important class of compounds are the depolarizing agents used to stimulate neuronal activity in experiments that employ the c-fos promoter. Potassium chloride (KCl) is the most commonly used compound for this purpose. By elevating the extracellular concentration of K+ ions, the resting membrane potential of neurons is depolarized, leading to the opening of voltage-gated calcium channels, an influx of Ca2+, and the subsequent activation of downstream signaling cascades that culminate in the transcription of IEGs like c-fos. In the research paper, a final K+ concentration of 55 mM was used for a 3-hour stimulation period to robustly induce c-fos-driven XRI-V5 expression. The system’s sensitivity was further demonstrated by showing that it could detect responses to shorter (10 minutes to 1 hour) and weaker (20 mM KCl) stimulation protocols, highlighting its utility for probing a range of physiological activity levels.

Finally, a variety of standard laboratory compounds are used in the workflow for reading out the XRI recordings. These include fixatives like paraformaldehyde, which cross-links proteins and preserves the cellular architecture and the XRI polymer structure at the moment of fixation. Permeabilization agents like Triton X-100 are used to allow antibodies to access the intracellular XRI filaments. Blocking agents such as bovine serum albumin (BSA) or commercial blocking buffers are used to prevent non-specific antibody binding. The primary antibodies themselves—anti-HA, anti-FLAG, anti-V5—are critical reagents that specifically recognize their respective epitope tags on the XRI monomers. These are typically used in conjunction with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies that enable visualization by fluorescence microscopy. All of these compounds are standard, off-the-shelf reagents, which contributes to the accessibility and ease of adoption of the XRI technology by the broader biological research community.

### Delivery of XRI-Based Recorder Components

The successful deployment of the XRI system requires the efficient and reliable delivery of its genetic components into the target cells. The invention provides for multiple delivery strategies, each suited to different experimental contexts. For in vitro studies using cultured cells, such as primary mouse hippocampal neurons, both transient plasmid transfection and viral transduction are viable options. Calcium phosphate co-precipitation is a cost-effective method for transiently introducing plasmid DNA encoding the XRI components into neurons. This method is suitable for proof-of-concept experiments and for testing different construct designs. However, for long-term recording experiments that span several days or weeks, viral delivery is strongly preferred due to its higher efficiency, more consistent expression levels across a population of cells, and lower cellular toxicity.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) is the viral vector of choice for delivering XRI components, both in vitro and in vivo. AAV is a non-pathogenic, single-stranded DNA virus that has become a gold standard for gene delivery in neuroscience and other fields due to its excellent safety profile, ability to infect both dividing and non-dividing cells, and potential for long-term transgene expression. In the described research, AAV serotype 9 (AAV9) was used, which has a broad tropism and is known to efficiently transduce neurons. The XRI expression cassettes—whether for the constitutive XRI-HA, the inducible XRI-FLAG, or the activity-dependent XRI-V5—are cloned into AAV transfer plasmids, which are then used to produce high-titer viral stocks. These stocks are added directly to the culture medium of neurons for in vitro work, or injected stereotaxically into specific brain regions for in vivo applications.

For in vivo delivery to the mammalian brain, the process is more complex but follows established neurosurgical protocols. In the research paper, adult wild-type mice were anesthetized and placed in a stereotaxic frame. A small craniotomy was performed above the target region, the dorsal CA1 area of the hippocampus. A mixture of AAVs, each encoding a different component of the XRI system (e.g., AAV-UBC-XRI-HA, AAV-UBC-FLEX-XRI-FLAG, and AAV-Syn-ERT2-iCre-ERT2), was loaded into a microsyringe and slowly infused into the brain tissue at precise coordinates relative to bregma. The use of a neuron-specific promoter, such as the human synapsin (Syn) promoter, to drive the expression of the ERT2-iCre-ERT2 recombinase ensures that the inducible system is only active in neurons, providing cell-type specificity. Following the AAV injection, the animals are allowed to recover, and the XRI components are expressed over a period of days to weeks. The 4-OHT inducer can then be administered systemically via intraperitoneal injection to trigger the recording of a specific event at a defined time in the animal’s life. This in vivo delivery strategy demonstrates the robustness and practicality of the XRI system for recording physiological events in the intact, living brain.

### Vectors, Plasmids, and Molecules

The genetic foundation of the XRI system is built upon a suite of carefully designed vectors and plasmids that enable the modular construction and flexible deployment of its components. The core vector backbones are mammalian expression plasmids that have been adapted for both transient transfection and AAV production. A key feature of these vectors is the presence of a multiple cloning site (MCS) downstream of a chosen promoter, allowing for the seamless insertion of the XRI monomer coding sequence. For the constitutive reporter, the human ubiquitin C (UBC) promoter is used in a standard expression vector, such as pAAV-UBC. This vector provides strong, ubiquitous expression in a wide variety of mammalian cell types.

For recording inducible events, a more sophisticated vector architecture is employed: the Cre-dependent FLEX (flip-excision) switch. The pAAV-UBC-FLEX vector contains a pair of heterotypic lox sites (e.g., loxP and lox2272) arranged in a specific orientation that initially places the XRI monomer gene in an inverted, non-functional state. Only upon Cre-mediated recombination does the gene flip into its correct orientation, allowing for transcription and translation. This design provides an extremely low basal expression level (“leakiness”) in the absence of Cre, which is critical for achieving a high signal-to-noise ratio in the recording. The FLEX switch is a well-established and reliable genetic tool that has been widely used in neuroscience for conditional gene expression.

For recording endogenous cellular activity, a promoter-responsive vector is used. The pAAV-cFos vector contains the murine c-fos promoter, which is a ~350 base pair fragment of the c-fos gene’s upstream regulatory region that is sufficient to confer activity-dependent transcription. This promoter is known to be responsive to a variety of stimuli that increase intracellular calcium, including neuronal depolarization, growth factor signaling, and stress responses. By cloning the XRI-V5 coding sequence into this vector, the system becomes a direct sensor of c-fos promoter activity.

An additional, useful vector design incorporates a self-cleaving peptide, such as P2A, to create a bicistronic expression cassette. For example, the pAAV-UBC-mEGFP-P2A-XRI-HA vector expresses both mEGFP and the XRI-HA monomer from a single mRNA transcript. The P2A peptide mediates a ribosomal “skip” event during translation, resulting in the production of two separate proteins from one open reading frame. This design is invaluable for live-cell imaging and electrophysiology experiments, as the mEGFP fluorescence serves as a bright, cytosolic marker that allows for easy identification of successfully transduced neurons for patch-clamp recording or time-lapse imaging of XRI growth. Although a small fraction of the XRI monomers may remain fused to mEGFP due to the imperfect cleavage efficiency of P2A (~90%), this does not appear to significantly compromise the structural integrity or recording fidelity of the XRI polymer.

All of these plasmid vectors are designed with AAV inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) flanking the expression cassette, which allows them to be packaged into AAV virions by standard triple-transfection methods in HEK293 cells. The resulting AAV stocks are then purified and titered for use in both in vitro and in vivo experiments. This modular vector system provides researchers with a flexible and powerful toolkit for constructing custom XRI recording experiments tailored to their specific biological questions.

### Cells and Subjects

The XRI system has been validated in a range of cellular and organismal models, demonstrating its broad applicability. The primary cell type used for its development and characterization is the primary mouse hippocampal neuron. These post-mitotic cells are an ideal testbed for the technology because they are highly relevant to neuroscience, are amenable to long-term culture, and exhibit complex, dynamic physiology that can be easily manipulated and recorded. The research paper shows that XRI expression in these neurons does not alter their fundamental health or function, as confirmed by comprehensive assessments including whole-cell patch-clamp electrophysiology, RNA sequencing, and immunohistochemical staining for a panel of markers of neuronal identity, apoptosis, glial activation, synaptic integrity, DNA damage, and cellular stress. This lack of perturbation is a critical feature of the invention, as it ensures that the recorded signals reflect genuine physiological processes rather than artifacts of the recording system itself.

The XRI system has also been successfully deployed in vivo in the brains of adult wild-type mice. Stereotaxic injection of AAVs encoding the XRI components into the hippocampal CA1 region resulted in robust and specific expression in neurons, with over 95% of targeted cells showing clear XRI formation. The system was able to faithfully record a temporally defined event (4-OHT administration) in the living brain, demonstrating its utility for studying complex physiological processes in their native, intact environment. This in vivo application is a major advance, as it opens the door to using XRI to record the history of neural activity, drug responses, or disease progression in behaving animals.

It is important to note a current limitation of the XRI system: it is not well-suited for use in actively dividing cells. The research paper observed that expression of the current XRI design in dividing cell lines, such as U2OS cells, resulted in the formation of aggregate-like structures alongside the desired filaments. This is likely because the large, stable XRI polymer cannot be faithfully segregated between daughter cells during mitosis. Either the polymer remains in one daughter cell, leaving the other without a recorder, or it is fragmented, leading to a loss of the temporal information it encodes. Therefore, the invention is primarily intended for use in post-mitotic or slowly dividing cell populations, such as neurons, cardiomyocytes, or certain immune cells. Future engineering efforts may focus on developing XRI variants that can be dynamically disassembled and reassembled during cell division to overcome this limitation, but for now, the system’s strength lies in its application to stable, long-lived cells.

### Controls

The rigorous validation of the XRI system required a comprehensive set of control experiments to establish its specificity, sensitivity, and lack of cellular perturbation. A fundamental control for any inducible recording system is the assessment of basal “leak” expression in the absence of the inducing signal. In the chemically inducible (4-OHT) experiments, a control group of neurons was transduced with the full set of AAVs (including the FLEX-XRI-FLAG and ERT2-iCre-ERT2 constructs) but was never treated with 4-OHT. Immunostaining of these control neurons showed no detectable FLAG signal above background, confirming the tight regulation of the FLEX switch and the negligible leakiness of the system. This is essential for ensuring that any observed FLAG signal in experimental groups is a true positive result of the 4-OHT induction.

Another critical set of controls involved verifying that the XRI system itself does not alter the normal physiology of the host cell. To this end, a battery of assays was performed on neurons expressing the XRI-HA monomer. Electrophysiological recordings showed that key intrinsic properties, such as resting membrane potential, input resistance, and action potential firing patterns, were indistinguishable between XRI-expressing neurons and control neurons expressing only a cytosolic GFP. RNA sequencing was used to perform a global transcriptomic analysis, comparing the gene expression profiles of XRI-expressing neurons to control neurons. This analysis revealed no significant differential expression of any mouse genes, indicating that XRI expression does not trigger a widespread stress response or alter the cell’s transcriptional program. Finally, a panel of immunohistochemical markers was used to assess cellular health in both cultured neurons and in vivo in the mouse brain. Staining for NeuN (a neuronal nuclear marker), cleaved Caspase-3 (an apoptosis marker), GFAP and Iba1 (markers for reactive astrocytes and microglia, respectively), Synaptophysin (a presynaptic marker), γH2AX (a DNA damage marker), and Hsp70/Hsp27 (heat shock protein stress markers) all showed no differences between XRI-expressing and control tissue. Together, these controls provide strong evidence that the XRI system is a passive, non-perturbative recorder of cellular physiology.

For the activity-dependent recording experiments using the c-fos promoter, the primary control was a group of neurons that were not subjected to KCl depolarization. These “No Stim” neurons showed a flat, baseline level of V5 immunofluorescence along the XRI polymer, in stark contrast to the clear peak of V5 signal seen in stimulated neurons. This control confirms that the V5 signal is specifically reporting on c-fos promoter activity induced by the depolarizing stimulus and is not due to constitutive expression or non-specific antibody binding. Additional controls included titrating the strength and duration of the KCl stimulus to demonstrate the system’s sensitivity and dynamic range, and performing sequential stimulations to show that the system can resolve multiple, temporally distinct events.

### Administration Means

The administration of the XRI system and its associated reagents involves a series of well-defined procedures that are standard in molecular and cellular biology, adapted for the specific requirements of this technology. The initial step is the introduction of the genetic material into the target cells. For in vitro work with cultured neurons, this is accomplished by adding the AAV vector stock directly to the culture medium. The neurons are incubated with the virus for a period of time (often overnight) to allow for infection and subsequent transgene expression. The titer of the AAV stock is carefully calibrated to achieve the desired level of expression; too low a titer results in insufficient XRI formation, while too high a titer can be cytotoxic. The research paper provides specific genomic copy (GC) per milliliter concentrations that were found to be optimal for different constructs.

For in vivo work in mice, the administration of the XRI genetic components is performed via stereotaxic surgery. Under deep anesthesia, the animal’s head is fixed in a stereotaxic frame, and a small hole is drilled in the skull at coordinates that correspond to the target brain region. A fine-gauge needle attached to a microsyringe pump is then slowly lowered to the correct depth, and a small volume (e.g., 0.6-1.0 µl) of the AAV mixture is infused at a controlled rate (e.g., 100 nl/min). The needle is left in place for several minutes after the infusion to prevent backflow of the virus up the injection track. Post-operative care, including analgesia, is provided to ensure the animal’s welfare.

Once the XRI components are expressed and the polymer has begun to form, the next key administration step is the delivery of the recording trigger, if using an inducible system. For the 4-OHT/Cre system, 4-OHT is prepared as a stock solution in ethanol and then diluted in the cell culture medium to a final concentration of 1 µM for in vitro experiments. The neurons are exposed to this solution for a short, defined period (e.g., 15 minutes), after which the medium is replaced with the original culture medium. For in vivo experiments, 4-OHT is formulated in a vehicle of ethanol and corn oil and administered to the mouse via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at a dose of 40 mg/kg. This systemic delivery ensures that the inducer reaches the brain and activates the Cre recombinase in the targeted neurons.

Finally, the recording is terminated, and the sample is prepared for readout by administering a fixative. For both in vitro and in vivo samples, this is typically done by perfusing the animal transcardially with a solution of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), or by immersing cultured cells in a PFA solution. This chemical fixation cross-links all proteins in place, permanently preserving the state of the XRI polymer and the cellular architecture at the exact moment of fixation. The fixed tissue can then be processed for sectioning, immunostaining, and imaging at a later time, decoupling the recording phase from the readout phase of the experiment.

### Assessment Methods

The readout and interpretation of the information stored in the XRI polymer is a multi-step process that combines advanced microscopy with sophisticated image analysis algorithms. The first step is the acquisition of high-quality, multichannel fluorescence images of the fixed sample. This is typically performed using a spinning disk confocal microscope, which provides optical sectioning to eliminate out-of-focus light and generate sharp, high-resolution images of the XRI filaments within the complex cellular environment. The somata of neurons are identified using a counterstain like NeuroTrace Blue Nissl stain or an antibody against NeuN. Within each soma, the XRI polymers are clearly visible as elongated structures in the channel corresponding to the constitutive HA tag.

The core of the assessment method is the quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity profiles along the length of each XRI polymer. Using image analysis software like ImageJ or MATLAB, a curved centerline is manually or semi-automatically drawn along the longitudinal axis of the XRI in the HA channel. The fluorescence intensities for both the HA (constitutive) and the signal tag (e.g., FLAG or V5) are then sampled at each point along this centerline, generating two parallel intensity profiles. Because the XRI grows bidirectionally from a central nucleation point, the intensity profile is symmetric. To analyze it, the profile is split into two halves at its geometric center. For each half, the HA intensity profile is converted into a cumulative line integral, which represents the total amount of HA-tagged monomer incorporated from the center out to any given point along the half-filament. This cumulative HA integral is then normalized to a scale from 0 (at the center) to 1 (at the end), creating a dimensionless metric that serves as a proxy for time.

The signal intensity profile (e.g., FLAG) is then re-sampled and plotted against this normalized cumulative HA integral axis. This transformation effectively converts the spatial measurement along the XRI into a temporal measurement. The resulting plot shows how the signal of interest changed over the course of the recording period. To extract a precise time of event onset, a specific algorithm is applied. The baseline signal level at the center of the XRI (time zero) is subtracted from the entire signal profile. The initial rising phase of the signal (e.g., from 10% to 50% of its peak) is fitted with a linear regression. This fitted line is then extrapolated backward until it intersects the baseline (zero signal) axis. The value of the normalized cumulative HA integral at this intersection point is taken as the calibrated time of event onset. This method is robust because it is independent of the absolute length, width, or curvature of the individual XRI polymer, relying only on the relative order of monomer incorporation.

For more complex signals, such as the transient peak of c-fos activity, the entire waveform of the signal relative to the baseline is analyzed. The system’s sensitivity is assessed by quantifying metrics like the average post-stimulus amplitude, the peak amplitude, and the rising slope of the signal. Statistical analysis, using non-parametric tests like the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test, is used to compare these metrics across different experimental conditions (e.g., different KCl doses or durations) to confirm that the XRI system can reliably distinguish between different levels of biological activity. This comprehensive assessment pipeline transforms the raw fluorescence images of the XRI polymer into a quantitative, time-resolved record of the cellular events that occurred during its growth.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The following example describes the development, calibration, and application of the XRI system for recording gene expression dynamics in both cultured neurons and the living mouse brain. The process began with the screening of a library of 14 human-engineered, filament-forming proteins for their ability to form stable, linear assemblies in the cytosol of primary mouse hippocampal neurons. Each candidate protein was fused to an HA epitope tag and expressed from a plasmid under the control of a constitutive promoter. After fixation and immunostaining, two candidates, 1POK(E239Y) and DHF40, produced discernible fiber-like structures. However, 1POK(E239Y) was selected for further development due to its lower background of unstructured aggregates and free-floating monomers. To improve the linearity and temporal fidelity of the assembly, an insulator domain was fused to the 1POK(E239Y) core. Both monomeric EGFP (mEGFP) and Maltose Binding Protein (MBP) were tested as insulators. While both reduced aggregate formation, only the MBP-fused variant (hereafter designated as XRI) demonstrated the ability to preserve temporal information. This was demonstrated in a chemically inducible experiment where a FLAG-tagged XRI monomer was expressed from a Cre-dependent FLEX vector. Neurons co-expressing constitutive XRI-HA and the FLEX-XRI-FLAG, along with an ERT2-iCre-ERT2 recombinase, were treated with 4-OHT at a defined time. In neurons expressing the MBP-XRI, immunostaining revealed a polarized FLAG signal—bright at the ends and dim in the middle—indicating that the FLAG monomers were incorporated only during the growth phase that followed the 4-OHT pulse. In contrast, the mEGFP-XRI and the un-insulated 1POK(E239Y) showed uniform FLAG staining, indicating a failure to preserve temporal order.

The XRI system was then characterized in detail. A bicistronic AAV vector, mEGFP-P2A-XRI-HA, was created to allow live imaging of XRI formation. Time-lapse imaging over 7 days post-transduction revealed that XRI growth was bidirectional and slightly accelerating over time, with the width stabilizing by day 3. Electrophysiology and RNA-seq confirmed that XRI expression did not alter neuronal health or gene expression. A comprehensive calibration experiment was performed by treating separate cultures of XRI-expressing neurons with 4-OHT at different times (days 1-6) after AAV transduction and fixing them all on day 7. Quantitative image analysis of the HA and FLAG intensity profiles showed that the system could only reliably record events that occurred from day 3 onward, consistent with the time needed for the XRI polymer to stabilize. A robust mathematical relationship was established between the time of 4-OHT treatment and the position along the XRI (measured as the fraction of the cumulative HA line integral) where the FLAG signal began to rise. This calibration curve allows future experiments to convert spatial measurements on the XRI into absolute time.

The calibrated XRI system was then applied to record endogenous gene expression. Neurons were co-transduced with AAV-UBC-XRI-HA and AAV-cFos-XRI-V5. A 3-hour depolarization with 55 mM KCl on day 5 induced a pulse of c-fos promoter activity, which was recorded as a peak in V5 immunofluorescence on the XRI polymer. Using the previously established calibration curve, the timecourse of c-fos activity was successfully reconstructed from the fixed sample, and this reconstructed waveform matched the timecourse obtained from live imaging of a c-fos-GFP reporter in a parallel experiment. The system’s sensitivity was further demonstrated by its ability to detect responses to shorter and weaker KCl stimuli, and its capacity for multiplexing was shown by its ability to resolve two sequential KCl stimulations separated by 24 hours.

Finally, the XRI system was tested in vivo. AAVs encoding XRI-HA, FLEX-XRI-FLAG, and Syn-ERT2-iCre-ERT2 were co-injected into the CA1 region of the mouse hippocampus. Ten days later, 4-OHT was administered via i.p. injection, and the brain was fixed four days after that. Immunostaining of brain sections revealed abundant XRI expression in CA1 neurons, and the FLAG signal showed the same polarized pattern as in cultured neurons, confirming that the XRI system can preserve temporal information in the intact, living mammalian brain. This example comprehensively demonstrates the XRI system as a powerful, genetically encoded platform for retrospective, time-resolved recording of cellular physiology.

### Equivalents

The present invention is not limited to the specific embodiments, sequences, or methods described herein. It will be apparent to those skilled in the art that various modifications and variations can be made in the compositions and methods of the invention without departing from the spirit or scope of the invention. For instance, while the 1POK(E239Y) protein is the preferred filament-forming domain, other self-assembling protein domains that can be engineered to form linear, non-aggregating polymers in mammalian cells could be used as a substitute. Similarly, while MBP is the preferred insulator domain, other highly monomeric, soluble protein domains with a large hydrodynamic radius could serve the same steric blocking function. The molecular labels are not restricted to HA, FLAG, and V5; any number of distinct, well-characterized epitope tags or even small fluorescent proteins (if they do not interfere with assembly) could be employed to enable multiplexed recording of numerous signals.

The promoters used to drive XRI monomer expression can be varied widely to target the system to different cell types or to make it responsive to different biological signals. Instead of the UBC promoter, other constitutive promoters like CAG or EF1α could be used. Instead of the c-fos promoter, other activity-dependent promoters (e.g., Arc, Egr1) or promoters responsive to specific signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB, STAT, CREB) could be used to record a vast array of cellular events. The inducible system is not limited to the Cre/loxP-4-OHT combination; other chemically inducible dimerization systems (e.g., rapamycin-induced FKBP/FRB) or optogenetic systems (e.g., light-inducible Cre) could be integrated to provide alternative means of triggering a recording event with high temporal precision.

The delivery methods can also be adapted. While AAV is the preferred vector, other viral vectors like lentivirus or non-viral methods like electroporation or lipid nanoparticles could be used depending on the target tissue and experimental needs. The assessment methods are not limited to confocal microscopy; other high-resolution imaging modalities like expansion microscopy (ExM), as demonstrated in the research paper, or super-resolution microscopy could be used to achieve even greater spatial (and thus temporal) resolution in reading out the XRI recordings. All such equivalents and modifications that fall within the spirit and scope of the appended claims are considered to be part of the present invention.
