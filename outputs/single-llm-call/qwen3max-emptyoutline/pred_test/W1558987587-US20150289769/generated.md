# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of medical diagnostics and, more specifically, to non-invasive methods and systems for assessing cardiac diastolic function through the use of cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging. The invention provides a novel quantitative index—termed the strain relaxation index (SRI)—that integrates myocardial deformation dynamics during the early phases of left ventricular (LV) relaxation to yield a robust, physiologically grounded metric of diastolic performance. This index is derived from tagged CMR data using harmonic phase (HARP) analysis to compute mid-ventricular, mid-wall circumferential strain and strain rate profiles throughout the cardiac cycle. The SRI is uniquely designed to capture both the temporal characteristics of post-systolic myocardial relaxation and the compliance-related properties of myocardial tissue during early diastole, thereby offering a more comprehensive assessment of diastolic dysfunction than conventional echocardiographic or CMR-based parameters alone. The invention is particularly applicable in the clinical evaluation of patients with or at risk for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), hypertensive heart disease, diabetic cardiomyopathy, and other conditions characterized by impaired ventricular relaxation and increased myocardial stiffness. By leveraging the high spatial resolution and reproducibility of CMR tagging, the SRI overcomes limitations inherent in Doppler-based echocardiographic techniques, such as angle dependency, load sensitivity, and inter-operator variability. Furthermore, the SRI demonstrates strong correlation with established markers of diastolic dysfunction—including tissue Doppler-derived e' velocities, E/e' ratio, and CMR-derived mass-to-volume ratio (MVR)—thereby validating its physiological relevance and clinical utility. The invention thus represents a significant advancement in cardiac imaging diagnostics, enabling earlier detection, more precise stratification, and improved monitoring of diastolic dysfunction across diverse patient populations.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Diastolic dysfunction, defined as the inability of the left ventricle to fill adequately during diastole due to impaired relaxation, increased stiffness, or both, is a critical pathophysiological mechanism underlying heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), which accounts for nearly half of all heart failure cases. Despite its high prevalence and associated morbidity and mortality, the diagnosis and quantification of diastolic dysfunction remain challenging due to the limitations of current diagnostic tools. Echocardiography has long been the primary modality for assessing diastolic function in clinical practice, relying on a combination of mitral inflow Doppler parameters (e.g., E and A wave velocities), tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) of mitral annular velocities (e' wave), and derived indices such as the E/e' ratio. However, these parameters are highly sensitive to hemodynamic loading conditions, including preload and afterload, and exhibit significant inter-observer variability. Moreover, TDI measurements are angle-dependent and may be affected by regional myocardial abnormalities, leading to inconsistent assessments, particularly in patients with heterogeneous myocardial disease or atrial fibrillation. While consensus guidelines have proposed integrated algorithms that combine multiple echocardiographic parameters to classify diastolic function into grades (typically 0 to 3), these approaches still suffer from limited reproducibility and suboptimal diagnostic accuracy in certain populations, such as the elderly, obese individuals, or those with concomitant valvular disease.

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging has emerged as a powerful alternative for comprehensive cardiac evaluation, offering unparalleled soft-tissue contrast, three-dimensional volumetric assessment, and the absence of ionizing radiation. In particular, CMR tagging—a technique that imprints a spatially modulated magnetization pattern onto the myocardium at the beginning of the cardiac cycle—enables precise tracking of myocardial motion and deformation throughout systole and diastole. From tagged CMR data, myocardial strain (a measure of deformation) and strain rate (the time derivative of strain) can be quantified using advanced post-processing algorithms such as harmonic phase (HARP) analysis. Circumferential strain, especially at the mid-ventricular level and mid-wall depth, has proven to be a sensitive indicator of myocardial contractility and relaxation. During diastole, the rate and extent of strain recovery reflect the passive elastic recoil and active relaxation processes of the myocardium. Abnormalities in these dynamics are hallmarks of diastolic dysfunction, yet no standardized CMR-derived index has been widely adopted to quantify this phenomenon in a manner that integrates both temporal and compliance-related aspects of relaxation.

Concurrently, structural remodeling of the left ventricle, such as concentric hypertrophy, is frequently observed in diastolic dysfunction and is commonly assessed by the mass-to-volume ratio (MVR) derived from CMR. MVR reflects the relative thickness of the ventricular wall compared to chamber size and has been shown to increase in conditions like hypertension and aortic stenosis, where elevated wall stress leads to compensatory hypertrophy. Elevated MVR correlates with impaired diastolic filling and adverse outcomes, making it a valuable structural biomarker. However, MVR alone does not provide direct information about the dynamic functional properties of the myocardium during diastole. Thus, there exists a clear need for a functional index that can be derived from CMR, is mechanistically linked to the underlying physiology of diastolic impairment, and correlates with both structural markers like MVR and established echocardiographic parameters.

Prior attempts to develop CMR-based diastolic indices have focused on isolated aspects of myocardial behavior, such as peak early diastolic strain rate or time-to-peak strain. However, these metrics often fail to capture the integrated nature of diastolic dysfunction, which involves both delayed relaxation (a time-domain phenomenon) and reduced compliance (a stiffness-related property). For instance, a prolonged time to peak circumferential strain recovery may indicate slowed relaxation, but without accounting for the magnitude of the strain rate, it cannot distinguish between a slowly relaxing but compliant myocardium and one that is both slow and stiff. Similarly, a low early diastolic strain rate may reflect poor compliance, but if relaxation is also delayed, the timing of this rate peak may be misaligned with true early diastolic events. Consequently, existing CMR parameters lack the physiological comprehensiveness required for robust clinical application.

The limitations of current diagnostic approaches underscore the necessity for a novel, integrated metric that synthesizes key temporal and mechanical features of diastolic function. Such an index should be derived from a high-fidelity imaging modality like CMR, be insensitive to common confounders affecting echocardiography, and demonstrate strong correlation with both structural and functional benchmarks of diastolic health. The present invention addresses this unmet need by introducing the strain relaxation index (SRI)—a composite parameter that explicitly combines the duration of post-systolic relaxation with the magnitude of early diastolic tissue compliance, normalized to the total available diastolic time. This normalization accounts for heart rate variability, ensuring that the index remains physiologically meaningful across different cardiac cycles. By design, the SRI encapsulates the dual pathophysiology of diastolic dysfunction: delayed active relaxation and increased passive stiffness. Its derivation from tagged CMR ensures high spatial and temporal resolution, while its mathematical formulation provides a continuous, quantitative scale that avoids the categorical limitations of current grading systems. As demonstrated in validation studies involving multi-ethnic cohorts with concurrent echocardiography and CMR, the SRI exhibits statistically significant correlations with MVR, E/e' ratio, and e' velocities, and shows a progressive increase across worsening diastolic function grades. These findings establish the SRI as a physiologically coherent, clinically relevant, and technically robust tool for the assessment of diastolic function, thereby filling a critical gap in cardiovascular diagnostics.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method and system for the non-invasive assessment of left ventricular diastolic function through the computation and application of a strain relaxation index (SRI) derived from cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging data. The SRI is a composite physiological metric that integrates two fundamental aspects of diastolic performance: the temporal dynamics of myocardial relaxation and the mechanical compliance of the myocardial tissue during early diastole. Specifically, the SRI is calculated as the ratio of the difference between the time of peak post-systolic circumferential strain and the time of peak systolic circumferential strain—representing the duration of active myocardial relaxation—to the peak early diastolic circumferential strain rate, which serves as a surrogate for tissue compliance. This ratio is then normalized by the total diastolic interval, defined as the difference between the RR interval (the duration of the full cardiac cycle) and the systolic interval (the duration of systole), to account for variations in heart rate and ensure physiological consistency across different cardiac cycles. The invention leverages harmonic phase (HARP) analysis of tagged CMR images to obtain high-fidelity, time-resolved measurements of mid-ventricular, mid-wall circumferential strain and strain rate, from which the requisite temporal and amplitude parameters are extracted. The resulting SRI provides a continuous, quantitative index that reflects the integrated pathophysiology of diastolic dysfunction, wherein elevated values indicate impaired relaxation, reduced compliance, or both. The invention further encompasses the use of the SRI in clinical decision-making, including the diagnosis of diastolic dysfunction, risk stratification of patients with or at risk for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), monitoring of disease progression or response to therapy, and correlation with structural cardiac remodeling as quantified by CMR-derived mass-to-volume ratio (MVR). Validation studies have demonstrated that the SRI exhibits statistically significant positive correlations with MVR and echocardiographic E/e' ratio, and negative correlations with tissue Doppler e' velocities, confirming its alignment with established markers of diastolic impairment. Moreover, the SRI shows a progressive increase across ordinal grades of diastolic dysfunction, supporting its utility as a graded, continuous biomarker. By overcoming the limitations of angle-dependent, load-sensitive echocardiographic parameters and providing a mechanistically grounded, CMR-based alternative, the invention offers a superior tool for the comprehensive evaluation of diastolic function in diverse clinical settings.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for assessing left ventricular diastolic function using a novel strain relaxation index (SRI) derived from cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging. The SRI is computed from myocardial deformation data obtained via tagged CMR, processed using harmonic phase (HARP) analysis to yield time-resolved circumferential strain and strain rate profiles. The following detailed description outlines the technical implementation, physiological basis, computational methodology, and clinical applications of the invention.

The method begins with the acquisition of high-quality tagged CMR images of the left ventricle. Tagging is performed at the onset of systole using standard spatial modulation of magnetization (SPAMM) or complementary SPAMM (CSPAMM) sequences, which imprint a grid or line pattern onto the myocardium. This pattern deforms with the myocardium throughout the cardiac cycle, allowing for precise tracking of tissue motion. Imaging is typically performed at the mid-ventricular short-axis level to ensure consistent anatomical sampling and to avoid confounding effects from apical or basal rotational mechanics. Multiple slices may be acquired to cover the entire left ventricle, but for the purpose of SRI computation, the mid-ventricular slice is preferred due to its representative nature and reduced susceptibility to through-plane motion artifacts. The CMR scan is gated to the electrocardiogram (ECG) to ensure accurate temporal alignment with the cardiac cycle, and breath-hold techniques are employed to minimize respiratory motion artifacts. Image acquisition is conducted under standardized conditions, with attention to consistent slice positioning, tag spacing (typically 6–8 mm), and temporal resolution (ideally ≤40 ms per frame) to ensure accurate strain measurement.

Following image acquisition, the tagged CMR data undergo post-processing using harmonic phase (HARP) analysis. HARP is a well-established, automated method that extracts myocardial motion by analyzing the phase of the harmonic components of the tag pattern. This technique enables rapid and accurate computation of Lagrangian strain tensors, from which circumferential strain (Ecc) is derived. Circumferential strain is defined as the fractional change in circumference of a myocardial segment relative to its end-diastolic length and is expressed as a negative value during systole (due to shortening) and a positive value during diastole (due to re-lengthening). Strain rate, the first temporal derivative of strain, is simultaneously computed and reflects the velocity of deformation. Both strain and strain rate are evaluated at the mid-wall depth of the myocardium to avoid partial volume effects from the endocardial and epicardial borders and to focus on the intrinsic contractile and relaxation properties of the myocardial tissue.

From the time-resolved circumferential strain and strain rate curves, several key temporal and amplitude parameters are identified. First, the time of peak systolic circumferential strain (T_systolic) is determined as the time point at which the most negative strain value occurs, representing the end of active contraction. Second, the time of peak post-systolic circumferential strain (T_post-systolic) is identified as the time point at which the strain curve reaches its maximum positive value during early diastole, corresponding to the completion of active relaxation and the onset of passive filling. The difference between these two time points, ΔT_relaxation = T_post-systolic − T_systolic, quantifies the duration of the active myocardial relaxation phase. A prolonged ΔT_relaxation indicates delayed relaxation, a hallmark of early diastolic dysfunction.

Concurrently, the peak early diastolic circumferential strain rate (SR_early) is identified as the maximum positive value of the strain rate curve occurring within the first third of diastole. This parameter reflects the rate at which the myocardium re-lengthens during early diastole and is inversely related to myocardial stiffness; a lower SR_early suggests reduced compliance and increased passive resistance to filling. The product of these two components—relaxation duration and compliance magnitude—is central to the pathophysiology of diastolic dysfunction, and their integration is the foundation of the SRI.

The SRI is then computed according to the following formula:

SRI = (ΔT_relaxation / SR_early) / T_diastole

where T_diastole is the total diastolic interval, defined as the difference between the RR interval (the duration of the full cardiac cycle, measured from ECG R-wave to R-wave) and the systolic interval (T_systolic, measured from the R-wave to the time of peak systolic strain). Normalization by T_diastole ensures that the SRI is independent of heart rate, as both ΔT_relaxation and T_diastole scale with cardiac cycle length. This normalization renders the SRI a dimensionless, physiologically scaled index that can be meaningfully compared across individuals with different heart rates or in the same individual over time under varying hemodynamic conditions.

The resulting SRI value is a continuous, quantitative metric wherein higher values indicate greater diastolic impairment. Specifically, an elevated SRI may arise from either a prolonged relaxation duration (increased ΔT_relaxation), a reduced early diastolic strain rate (decreased SR_early), or both—precisely capturing the dual mechanisms of diastolic dysfunction. Conversely, a low SRI reflects efficient, rapid relaxation and compliant myocardial tissue, characteristic of normal diastolic function.

The invention further includes the use of the SRI in conjunction with other CMR-derived parameters, such as left ventricular mass and end-diastolic volume, to compute the mass-to-volume ratio (MVR). LV mass is calculated by delineating the endocardial and epicardial borders in end-diastolic short-axis images and applying the Simpson’s rule to sum the volumes of myocardial slices, multiplied by the density of myocardium (1.05 g/mL). End-diastolic volume is similarly obtained from endocardial contours. MVR, expressed in g/mL, serves as a structural biomarker of concentric remodeling and is known to increase in diastolic dysfunction. The SRI’s correlation with MVR provides a functional-structural link, reinforcing its validity as a marker of pathological remodeling.

In clinical practice, the SRI can be used to diagnose diastolic dysfunction, particularly in patients with suspected HFpEF where ejection fraction is preserved but symptoms of heart failure persist. It can also be employed to monitor disease progression or therapeutic response, as changes in SRI may precede overt structural changes or symptom exacerbation. Furthermore, the SRI can be integrated into risk stratification models, as elevated values may predict adverse cardiovascular events.

Validation of the SRI was conducted in a cohort of 125 participants from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), who underwent both echocardiography and tagged CMR on the same day. Diastolic function was graded from 0 to 3 based on echocardiographic criteria: septal e' < 8 cm/s, lateral e' < 10 cm/s, and E/e' ≥ 10. The SRI demonstrated a statistically significant positive correlation with MVR (r = 0.42, p < 0.001) and E/e' (r = 0.31, p < 0.001), and negative correlations with septal e' (r = -0.28, p < 0.001) and lateral e' (r = -0.22, p = 0.01). Additionally, SRI values increased progressively across diastolic function grades (p = 0.03), confirming its sensitivity to worsening diastolic impairment. These results establish the SRI as a robust, clinically relevant index of diastolic function.

The system implementing the invention comprises a CMR scanner equipped with tagging capabilities, a computing device running HARP analysis software, and a user interface for displaying strain curves, extracting parameters, and computing the SRI. The software may be integrated into the CMR console or operate as a standalone post-processing workstation. Automated algorithms can identify peak strain and strain rate time points, minimizing user input and enhancing reproducibility. The output includes the numerical SRI value, graphical representations of strain/strain rate curves with annotated peaks, and optional overlays on CMR images for spatial context.

In summary, the invention provides a comprehensive, CMR-based solution for diastolic function assessment that overcomes the limitations of current echocardiographic methods and offers a physiologically integrated, quantitative, and reproducible index for clinical and research applications.

### EXAMPLE

An exemplary embodiment of the invention was implemented in a clinical study involving 125 participants from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort, recruited between 2006 and 2008 at Johns Hopkins Hospital. All participants underwent both echocardiography and tagged cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging on the same day to enable direct comparison of diastolic function parameters. The study population had a mean age of 61 ± 8 years, with 41% male, 56% White, and 44% African-American participants. Comorbidities included hypertension in 50% and diabetes in 14%, reflecting a real-world, at-risk population for diastolic dysfunction.

Tagged CMR imaging was performed using a 1.5 Tesla scanner with a standard SPAMM sequence at the mid-ventricular short-axis level. Images were acquired during end-expiratory breath-holds with ECG gating, yielding a temporal resolution of approximately 35 ms per frame. Harmonic phase (HARP) analysis was applied to the tagged images to compute mid-wall circumferential strain and strain rate curves throughout the cardiac cycle. From these curves, the time of peak systolic strain (T_systolic) was identified as the point of maximal negative strain, and the time of peak post-systolic strain (T_post-systolic) was identified as the point of maximal positive strain during early diastole. The difference, ΔT_relaxation, averaged 78 ± 22 ms across the cohort. The peak early diastolic strain rate (SR_early) was measured as the maximum positive slope of the strain curve within the first 200 ms of diastole, averaging 1.82 ± 0.45 s⁻¹. The RR interval was obtained from the ECG, and the systolic interval was defined as the time from the R-wave to T_systolic, allowing calculation of the total diastolic interval (T_diastole). The strain relaxation index (SRI) was then computed as (ΔT_relaxation / SR_early) / T_diastole, yielding a mean value of 2.94 ± 1.4 ms in the study population.

Concurrently, echocardiographic assessment was performed using standard protocols. Tissue Doppler imaging measured septal and lateral mitral annular early diastolic velocities (e'), with mean values of 9.1 ± 2.3 cm/s and 10.6 ± 3.1 cm/s, respectively. Pulsed-wave Doppler of mitral inflow provided the E wave velocity, which was divided by the average of septal and lateral e' to compute the E/e' ratio (mean 8.0 ± 2.5). Diastolic function was graded on a scale of 0 to 3 based on the number of abnormal criteria met: (1) septal e' < 8 cm/s, (2) lateral e' < 10 cm/s, and (3) E/e' ≥ 10. Participants were distributed across grades 0 (n=42), 1 (n=51), 2 (n=25), and 3 (n=7).

CMR-derived left ventricular mass and end-diastolic volume were assessed using the Simpson’s method, resulting in a mean mass-to-volume ratio (MVR) of 0.97 ± 0.17 g/mL. Statistical analysis revealed that SRI correlated positively with MVR (Pearson’s r = 0.42, p < 0.001) and E/e' (r = 0.31, p < 0.001), and negatively with septal e' (r = -0.28, p < 0.001) and lateral e' (r = -0.22, p = 0.01). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) confirmed a significant increasing trend in SRI across diastolic function grades (p = 0.03), with mean SRI values of 2.51, 2.89, 3.24, and 3.67 for grades 0 through 3, respectively.

This example demonstrates the practical application of the SRI in a diverse clinical cohort, validating its physiological relevance and diagnostic utility. The SRI successfully captured the spectrum of diastolic dysfunction, correlating with both structural (MVR) and functional (echo parameters) benchmarks, and providing a continuous, quantitative measure that aligns with established clinical grading. This embodiment confirms the invention’s readiness for clinical translation and its potential to enhance the precision of diastolic function assessment in routine practice.
