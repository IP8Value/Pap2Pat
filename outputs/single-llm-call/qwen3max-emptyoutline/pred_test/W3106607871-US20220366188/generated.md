# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Semantic role labeling (SRL) constitutes a foundational task in natural language understanding, aimed at identifying the predicate-argument structure of sentences by mapping linguistic expressions to semantic frames and their associated roles. In practical terms, SRL seeks to answer fundamental questions about events described in text: who did what to whom, when, where, why, and how. This capability is essential for a wide range of downstream applications, including question answering, information extraction, machine translation, dialogue systems, and text summarization. The standard formulation of SRL involves decomposing a sentence into four interrelated subtasks: predicate identification, which determines which words in the sentence function as predicates; predicate sense disambiguation, which assigns a specific sense or meaning to each identified predicate based on a predefined inventory such as PropBank; argument identification, which locates the spans or dependency heads that serve as arguments to each predicate; and role classification, which assigns a specific semantic role label—such as ARG0 (agent), ARG1 (theme), or various adjunct roles like AM-LOC (location) or AM-TMP (temporal)—to each identified argument. Historically, early approaches to SRL relied heavily on syntactic parsing, leveraging constituency or dependency trees to guide the identification and classification of arguments. These syntax-aware methods often achieved strong performance under the assumption that high-quality syntactic parsers were available, but they suffered from brittleness when applied to domains or languages lacking robust parsing resources.

In recent years, the advent of deep neural networks has enabled significant progress in SRL through end-to-end learning paradigms that do not require explicit syntactic input. These syntax-agnostic models typically frame SRL as a sequence tagging problem, where each token in a sentence is assigned a label indicating its role with respect to a given predicate. Architectures based on recurrent neural networks (RNNs), particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, have proven highly effective in capturing contextual dependencies necessary for accurate role labeling. More recently, the integration of large-scale pretrained language models—such as ELMo and BERT—has further elevated performance by providing rich, context-sensitive word representations that encode deep semantic and syntactic knowledge learned from massive corpora. Despite these advances, even the most sophisticated neural models exhibit limitations, particularly in handling low-frequency predicates or rare argument-role configurations that are underrepresented in training data. These “low-frequency exceptions” pose a persistent challenge because neural models, which rely on statistical generalization across the entire dataset, may fail to learn reliable patterns for infrequent cases. Consequently, there remains a critical need for mechanisms that can adapt model predictions at inference time by leveraging local, instance-specific information without compromising the generalization power of deep learning.

Instance-based or memory-augmented learning offers a promising direction to address this gap. Unlike parametric models that compress all training knowledge into fixed weights, memory-based methods retain explicit representations of training examples and use them directly during inference. A notable example is K-SRL, which uses handcrafted syntactic features to compute distances between test instances and stored training examples, then predicts labels via majority vote among the nearest neighbors. While effective, such methods are limited by the expressiveness of manually designed features and do not benefit from the representational power of deep neural networks. Concurrently, memory-augmented neural architectures have emerged in other domains, such as language modeling, where models like the continuous cache store hidden activations from previous contexts and use them to adjust predictions dynamically. These approaches demonstrate that combining global parametric knowledge with local non-parametric memory can yield more robust and adaptive systems. However, direct application of such techniques to SRL is non-trivial due to the structured, multi-label nature of the task and the need for efficient, scalable neighbor retrieval during both training and inference. The present invention addresses these challenges by introducing a novel parameterized neighborhood memory adaptation framework that seamlessly integrates the strengths of deep neural representations with instance-based learning, enabling significant improvements in SRL performance—particularly on out-of-domain and low-frequency cases—without reliance on syntactic parsers or handcrafted features.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a Parameterized Neighborhood Memory Adaptive (PNMA) method for semantic role labeling that enhances the performance of deep neural base models by adaptively incorporating information from a memory of training token representations. The PNMA method operates in two distinct phases: a memory generation phase and a memory-adaptive retraining phase. In the first phase, a base SRL model—such as an Alternating LSTM or a transformer-based architecture—is trained on a standard SRL dataset using conventional supervised learning. Once trained, this base model is used to generate dense vector representations for each token in a subset of the training data, which are then stored in a memory bank. For each token in the training set, the system computes its K nearest neighbors in this memory based on Euclidean distance in the representation space, thereby constructing a neighborhood of semantically and contextually similar tokens. Empirical analysis confirms that these neighborhoods frequently contain tokens bearing the correct semantic role label, even when the base model’s own prediction for the query token is incorrect, thus validating the utility of the memory for corrective adaptation.

In the second phase, the parameters of the base model’s embedding and contextual encoding layers (e.g., LSTM or transformer layers) are frozen, and only the classification head—including any conditional random field (CRF) layer—is retrained. Crucially, during this retraining, the input to the classification head is not the original token representation from the base model, but rather a new, parameterized neighborhood representation. This representation is computed as a weighted combination of the K nearest neighbor vectors, where the weights are determined by a learned scoring function that depends on the relative distances between the query token and each neighbor. Specifically, for each neighbor, a learnable parameter vector is used to compute a score via inner product with the distance vector between the query and the neighbor; these scores are then passed through a softmax function to produce a probability distribution over the neighbors, which is used to form a convex combination of the neighbor representations. This yields a single, compact vector that encapsulates the most relevant information from the local neighborhood in a manner optimized for the SRL task. By retraining the classification layers on this enhanced representation, the model learns to leverage the corrective signals present in the memory, leading to improved generalization—especially for low-frequency predicates and out-of-domain examples.

The PNMA method is fully compatible with any deep neural base model for SRL and can be combined with pretrained contextualized embeddings such as those from ELMo or BERT. It does not require syntactic parse trees or handcrafted features, making it applicable to a wide range of languages and domains. Experimental results demonstrate that PNMA consistently improves upon the base model across multiple benchmark datasets, including CoNLL2005, CoNLL2012, and CoNLL2009, achieving new state-of-the-art results for single-model systems. Notably, the largest gains are observed on out-of-domain test sets and for low-frequency predicates, confirming the method’s effectiveness in addressing the long-tail problem in SRL. The computational overhead of PNMA is minimal, as neighbor retrieval can be efficiently parallelized on GPUs, and the second training phase is significantly faster than the initial base model training due to the frozen lower layers. Overall, the invention provides a robust, scalable, and syntax-agnostic framework for enhancing semantic role labeling through adaptive memory utilization, with broad applicability to natural language understanding tasks.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a computer-implemented method and system for semantic role labeling using a Parameterized Neighborhood Memory Adaptive (PNMA) architecture. The method enhances the accuracy and robustness of semantic role labeling by integrating a memory-augmented adaptation mechanism into a deep neural network base model. The following detailed description outlines the components, procedures, and operational principles of the invention, with reference to its application in processing natural language sentences to identify and classify semantic roles of arguments with respect to predicates.

The invention begins with the selection and training of a base semantic role labeling model. This base model may be any deep neural architecture capable of producing contextualized token representations and assigning semantic role labels. In one embodiment, the base model comprises an Alternating LSTM architecture, which processes input sequences bidirectionally through multiple stacked LSTM layers to capture rich contextual dependencies. The input to the base model is a sequence of tokens representing a sentence, wherein each token is augmented with a binary indicator specifying whether it corresponds to a predicate in the current SRL task. This predicate indicator is embedded into a dense vector space using a small embedding layer, and combined with the word embedding of the token. The word embeddings may be initialized randomly or derived from a pretrained language model such as ELMo or BERT. In the case of BERT, the input sentence is processed through the BERT encoder, and the resulting contextualized embeddings from one or more layers are combined via a learnable weighted sum to produce the final word representation for each token. These combined embeddings are then fed into the LSTM layers (or alternative contextual encoders such as transformers) to produce a sequence of hidden representations, one per token. The final hidden representation for each token is passed through a linear classification layer followed by a conditional random field (CRF) layer, which jointly models the sequence of role labels to ensure globally consistent predictions. The base model is trained end-to-end using standard maximum likelihood estimation with respect to the ground truth semantic role labels, typically encoded in BIO (Beginning, Inside, Outside) format for span-based SRL or as dependency labels for dependency-based SRL.

Upon completion of base model training, the first phase of the PNMA method—memory generation—is initiated. During this phase, the trained base model is used to process a subset of the original training data to populate a memory bank. Specifically, for each token in the selected subset of training sentences, the base model computes its final hidden representation (e.g., the output of the top LSTM layer). These representations, which are dense vectors in a high-dimensional space (e.g., 300 or 1024 dimensions), are stored in a memory structure M along with their corresponding ground truth semantic role labels. The size of the memory is configurable; in practice, using 15% of the training tokens has been found to provide sufficient coverage without excessive computational cost. Once the memory M is populated, for each token w in the full training set (including those not in the memory subset), the system retrieves its K nearest neighbors from M based on Euclidean distance in the representation space. The value of K is a hyperparameter; empirical studies have shown that K=64 yields optimal performance. This neighbor retrieval is performed efficiently using GPU-accelerated batched distance computation, enabling scalability to large datasets. The result is, for each training token w, a set N_K(w) = {m_1(w), m_2(w), ..., m_K(w)} of K neighbor representations, each associated with a ground truth label.

The second phase of the PNMA method—memory-adaptive retraining—leverages the precomputed neighborhoods to refine the classification capabilities of the base model. In this phase, the parameters of the base model’s embedding layers and contextual encoder (e.g., LSTM or transformer layers) are frozen; only the parameters of the classification head (including the linear projection and CRF layers) are updated. Additionally, a set of learnable parameter vectors {n_i ∈ ℝ^d | i = 1, ..., K} is introduced, one for each neighbor position. For each token w in the training set, the system computes a parameterized neighborhood representation n_K(w) as follows. First, the distance vector between the base model’s representation h(w) of w and each neighbor m_i(w) is computed as d_i(w) = m_i(w) - h(w). Then, a score s_i(w) is computed for each neighbor as the inner product s_i(w) = n_i · d_i(w). These scores are passed through a softmax function to produce a weight distribution η_i(w) = exp(s_i(w)) / Σ_j exp(s_j(w)). The final neighborhood representation is then formed as the weighted sum n_K(w) = Σ_i η_i(w) · m_i(w). This representation effectively aggregates information from the K nearest neighbors in a manner that is adaptive to the specific context of w, with the weights η_i(w) being optimized during training to emphasize neighbors that are most informative for predicting the correct semantic role label.

The retraining process proceeds by feeding n_K(w) into the classification head instead of h(w). The model is trained to minimize the negative log-likelihood of the ground truth labels given these neighborhood representations, using the same optimization procedure as in the base model training (e.g., Adam optimizer with learning rate scheduling and dropout regularization). Importantly, the base model’s contextual representations h(w) are not used during this phase; the entire predictive signal comes from the parameterized neighborhood representation. This design ensures that the model learns to rely on the corrective information present in the memory, rather than simply reinforcing the original base model’s biases. After retraining, the resulting model constitutes the final PNMA-enhanced SRL system. At inference time, for a new test sentence, the base model first computes h(w) for each token w. Then, for each w, the system retrieves its K nearest neighbors from the precomputed memory M using the same Euclidean distance metric. The parameterized neighborhood representation n_K(w) is computed using the learned parameters {n_i}, and this representation is passed through the retrained classification head to produce the final semantic role label prediction.

The PNMA method offers several key advantages over prior approaches. First, it eliminates the need for handcrafted features or syntactic parsers, relying instead on the rich representations learned by deep neural networks. Second, it provides a principled way to incorporate local, instance-specific information into the prediction process without sacrificing the generalization benefits of global parametric learning. Third, it is particularly effective for low-frequency predicates and out-of-domain examples, as demonstrated by empirical analysis showing that PNMA disproportionately corrects errors made by the base model on these challenging cases. Fourth, the method is computationally efficient, with neighbor retrieval and representation computation adding less than 10% overhead compared to base model inference when implemented on modern hardware. Finally, PNMA is modular and can be applied to any base SRL model, including those based on state-of-the-art pretrained language models like BERT, enabling straightforward integration into existing NLP pipelines.

Experimental validation of the invention was conducted on standard SRL benchmarks, including the CoNLL2005 and CoNLL2012 datasets for span-based SRL and the CoNLL2009 dataset for dependency-based SRL. Results showed consistent improvements across all datasets and base model configurations. For example, when combined with a BERT-based base model, PNMA achieved F1 scores of 87.2 on the CoNLL2005 in-domain test set and 82.5 on the out-of-domain Brown test set, representing absolute improvements of 0.3 and 2.0 points, respectively, over the base model alone. On CoNLL2009, the improvement on the Brown test set was even more pronounced, with a 5.0 point gain. Role-level analysis revealed that PNMA particularly benefits core argument roles such as ARG1 and ARG2, which are often confused due to semantic overlap, by reducing misclassifications through the influence of correctly labeled neighbors. Instance-level analysis confirmed that PNMA is far more likely to correct a base model error than to introduce a new error, with correction rates of 68% and 48% on CoNLL2005 and CoNLL2009, respectively, in cases of disagreement. Furthermore, the method was shown to be especially effective for low-frequency predicates, where the base model’s generalization is weakest, thereby addressing a critical limitation of current SRL systems.

In summary, the PNMA method represents a significant advancement in semantic role labeling by bridging the gap between global parametric learning and local instance-based adaptation. Through its two-phase architecture—memory generation and parameterized neighborhood retraining—the invention provides a robust, efficient, and syntax-agnostic solution that enhances SRL performance across diverse domains and linguistic phenomena, setting new standards for accuracy and reliability in natural language understanding systems.
