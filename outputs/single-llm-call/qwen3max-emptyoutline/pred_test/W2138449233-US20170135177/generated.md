# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of intelligent lighting systems, and more specifically to a privacy-preserving occupancy sensing method and system for smart lighting applications that utilizes perturbation-modulated illumination from color-controllable light-emitting diode (LED) fixtures in conjunction with non-imaging color sensors to estimate spatial occupancy distribution within an indoor environment. The invention further encompasses methods for recovering a light transport matrix that characterizes the optical properties of the illuminated space, and techniques for reconstructing either three-dimensional occupancy volumes or two-dimensional floor-plane occupancy maps based on analysis of changes in this light transport matrix relative to a reference state. The technical domain spans multiple disciplines including solid-state lighting, photometry, computer vision, signal processing, and building automation systems, with particular emphasis on energy-efficient lighting control strategies that enhance human comfort, well-being, and productivity while maintaining occupant privacy through the use of non-imaging sensing modalities that do not capture recognizable visual information.

## BACKGROUND

Traditional lighting systems have evolved significantly from incandescent and fluorescent technologies to modern solid-state lighting solutions based on light-emitting diodes (LEDs), which offer superior energy efficiency, longer operational lifespans, and enhanced controllability. This technological advancement has enabled the development of smart lighting systems capable of dynamically adjusting illumination parameters based on real-time environmental conditions and occupancy patterns. Conventional approaches to occupancy detection in lighting control applications have primarily relied upon either imaging sensors such as cameras and depth sensors, or non-imaging sensors including passive infrared (PIR) detectors, ultrasonic sensors, microwave sensors, and various other motion detection technologies. While imaging sensors can provide rich spatial information about room occupancy through high-resolution visual data, their deployment in private spaces raises significant privacy concerns, as they inherently capture detailed visual representations of occupants that could potentially be misused if security protocols are compromised. Non-imaging alternatives like PIR sensors address these privacy issues but suffer from limitations in spatial resolution and detection accuracy, often failing to distinguish between multiple occupants or accurately localize their positions within a space.

The emergence of color-controllable LED fixtures has opened new possibilities for dual-use lighting systems that simultaneously provide illumination and enable communication or sensing capabilities through visible light. Visible light communication (VLC) and light fidelity (Li-Fi) technologies have demonstrated that rapid modulation of LED light output can transmit data while maintaining adequate illumination levels. However, prior art has not adequately addressed the challenge of using these capabilities for privacy-preserving occupancy sensing in practical lighting applications. Existing occupancy-based lighting control systems typically employ simple binary detection mechanisms that indicate presence or absence of occupants without providing sufficient spatial information to enable sophisticated lighting strategies that could optimize both energy consumption and human-centric lighting parameters. Furthermore, conventional approaches fail to leverage the full potential of modern LED fixtures' ability to independently control multiple color channels, which could provide additional degrees of freedom for both illumination quality and sensing resolution.

The fundamental challenge in developing effective occupancy sensing for smart lighting lies in the trade-off between information richness and privacy preservation. High-resolution imaging provides detailed spatial information but compromises privacy, while simple presence detection preserves privacy but lacks the spatial granularity needed for advanced lighting control. Additionally, existing non-imaging sensors such as PIR detectors are limited to detecting thermal radiation from living beings and cannot sense inanimate objects or provide detailed spatial mapping. Ultrasonic and microwave sensors, while capable of distance measurement, suffer from false alarms due to environmental interference and cannot provide the fine-grained spatial resolution required for optimal lighting control. The need remains for a sensing methodology that can provide sufficient spatial information about occupancy distribution without compromising occupant privacy, while operating within the constraints of practical lighting system deployment including cost, installation complexity, and compatibility with existing infrastructure.

Previous attempts to address this challenge have focused on either enhancing traditional sensor technologies or developing complex multi-sensor fusion approaches that combine multiple sensing modalities. However, these solutions often increase system complexity, cost, and potential points of failure while still failing to achieve the optimal balance between spatial information richness and privacy preservation. The computer graphics community has extensively studied light transport matrices in projector-camera systems for applications such as relighting and radiometric compensation, but these approaches assume high-resolution, high-dimensional systems with numerous light sources and sensors, making them impractical for real-world lighting applications where hardware costs and installation constraints limit the number of available fixtures and sensors. The medical imaging field has developed sophisticated reconstruction algorithms for computed tomography and other modalities, but these techniques require either rotating equipment around the subject or dense sampling geometries that are incompatible with fixed-installation lighting systems. What is needed is a novel approach that leverages the unique capabilities of modern LED lighting systems to enable privacy-preserving occupancy sensing with sufficient spatial resolution for practical lighting control applications, while operating within realistic hardware constraints and maintaining compatibility with existing building infrastructure.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution for privacy-preserving occupancy sensing in smart lighting systems through the integration of perturbation-modulated illumination from color-controllable LED fixtures with non-imaging color sensors to estimate spatial occupancy distribution within indoor environments. The core innovation lies in the systematic application of imperceptible perturbations to the base illumination provided by LED fixtures, followed by measurement of corresponding changes in sensor outputs to recover a light transport matrix that characterizes the optical properties of the illuminated space. This light transport matrix serves as a rich signature of the current occupancy state, enabling the estimation of spatial occupancy distribution through analysis of differences between the current light transport matrix and a reference matrix obtained during a known baseline condition.

The invention comprises two distinct but complementary approaches for occupancy distribution estimation: a three-dimensional reconstruction method based on a light blockage model that requires wall-mounted sensors, and a two-dimensional floor-plane mapping technique based on a light reflection model that utilizes ceiling-mounted sensors. The light blockage model exploits the attenuation of direct light paths between fixtures and sensors caused by occupying objects, while the light reflection model analyzes changes in diffuse reflection patterns from floor surfaces. Both approaches operate within the constraints of practical lighting system deployment, utilizing a small number of fixtures and sensors while providing sufficient spatial information for effective lighting control without compromising occupant privacy.

A key aspect of the invention is the perturbation-modulation technique that enables the recovery of the light transport matrix without disrupting normal illumination functions. By applying small, imperceptible changes to the base lighting conditions, the system can gather sufficient information to characterize the light transport properties of the space while maintaining comfortable illumination levels for occupants. The perturbation patterns are carefully designed to balance information richness with human comfort, ensuring that the magnitude of changes is large enough to be accurately measured by sensors but small enough to remain imperceptible to human observers. Additionally, the temporal ordering of perturbation patterns is optimized using traveling salesman problem algorithms to minimize perceptible flicker and maximize human comfort during the sensing phase.

The invention further includes methods for aggregating multi-channel sensor data to improve robustness against noise and channel-specific variations, as well as techniques for accelerating reconstruction computations through pre-computation and efficient algorithmic implementations. The system operates under a quasi-static assumption that allows for rapid data collection during brief sensing intervals, ensuring that occupancy and ambient lighting conditions remain effectively constant during measurement periods. This approach enables real-time or near-real-time occupancy estimation suitable for dynamic lighting control applications.

The disclosed methodology represents a significant advancement over existing occupancy sensing technologies by providing a privacy-preserving solution that delivers sufficient spatial information for sophisticated lighting control strategies while operating within practical hardware constraints. Unlike imaging-based approaches that capture detailed visual information, the non-imaging color sensors used in this invention output only numerical values representing luminous flux measurements across different color channels, eliminating privacy concerns while still providing the spatial information necessary for effective occupancy-sensitive lighting control. The invention thus bridges the gap between privacy preservation and spatial information richness that has characterized previous approaches to occupancy sensing in smart lighting systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Rank Minimization

The rank minimization aspect of the present invention addresses the fundamental challenge of recovering the light transport matrix from limited measurements while ensuring computational efficiency and robustness against noise. In the context of smart lighting systems, the light transport matrix A represents the linear relationship between inputs to LED fixtures and outputs from color sensors, capturing the complete optical characteristics of the illuminated space including direct illumination, diffuse reflections, specular reflections, and interreflections. Given the practical constraints of real-world lighting installations, which typically involve a limited number of fixtures and sensors due to cost and installation considerations, the light transport matrix is relatively small compared to those encountered in computer graphics applications involving high-resolution projectors and cameras. However, even with this reduced dimensionality, the recovery of the light transport matrix presents significant challenges due to measurement noise, sensor limitations, and the inherent ill-posed nature of the inverse problem.

The rank minimization approach leverages the observation that the light transport matrix in typical indoor environments exhibits low-rank structure due to the smooth and continuous nature of light propagation and reflection phenomena. This low-rank property arises because the optical characteristics of most indoor spaces are governed by a limited number of dominant light paths and reflection patterns, rather than completely random or unstructured light transport. By exploiting this low-rank structure, the invention enables more robust and accurate recovery of the light transport matrix from limited measurements, particularly in scenarios where the number of available measurements is insufficient to fully determine all matrix elements through standard least-squares approaches.

The mathematical formulation of the rank minimization problem involves finding the light transport matrix A that best explains the observed relationship between perturbation inputs δx and corresponding sensor response changes δy, while simultaneously minimizing the rank of A. This can be expressed as an optimization problem:

minimize rank(A)
subject to ||Y - AX||_F ≤ ε

where Y represents the matrix of observed sensor response changes, X represents the matrix of applied perturbation inputs, ||·||_F denotes the Frobenius norm, and ε represents a tolerance parameter that accounts for measurement noise and modeling errors. The rank minimization objective encourages solutions that capture the essential low-dimensional structure of the light transport phenomenon while filtering out noise and irrelevant details.

In practice, the rank minimization problem is computationally challenging due to the non-convex nature of the rank function. Therefore, the invention employs convex relaxation techniques that replace the rank function with the nuclear norm (sum of singular values), which serves as the tightest convex approximation to the rank function. This leads to the following convex optimization problem:

minimize ||A||_*
subject to ||Y - AX||_F ≤ ε

where ||A||_* denotes the nuclear norm of A. This convex relaxation can be solved efficiently using established optimization algorithms such as proximal gradient methods or alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM).

The rank minimization approach provides several advantages in the context of smart lighting systems. First, it enhances robustness against measurement noise by filtering out high-frequency components that are likely to represent noise rather than genuine light transport characteristics. Second, it enables accurate recovery of the light transport matrix even when the number of measurements is less than the number of unknown parameters, which is particularly valuable in scenarios with limited sensing capabilities or time constraints. Third, the low-rank approximation captures the essential features of the light transport phenomenon while discarding irrelevant details, leading to more stable and interpretable occupancy estimates.

The effectiveness of the rank minimization approach depends on appropriate selection of the regularization parameter that balances the trade-off between data fidelity and rank reduction. The invention includes methods for automatically determining this parameter based on statistical properties of the measurement data and expected noise levels. Additionally, the approach can be combined with other regularization techniques such as sparsity constraints or smoothness priors to further enhance performance in specific application scenarios.

In the context of the overall occupancy sensing system, the rank-minimized light transport matrix serves as a more reliable basis for subsequent occupancy estimation steps. By providing a cleaner, more structured representation of the light transport characteristics, the rank minimization approach improves the accuracy and robustness of both the light blockage model and the light reflection model described in subsequent sections. This leads to more reliable occupancy estimates that better support sophisticated lighting control strategies while maintaining the privacy-preserving nature of the non-imaging sensing approach.

### Perturbation-Modulated Lighting

The perturbation-modulated lighting technique constitutes a critical component of the present invention, enabling the recovery of the light transport matrix without disrupting normal illumination functions or compromising occupant comfort. This approach involves the systematic application of small, imperceptible changes to the base illumination provided by color-controllable LED fixtures, followed by precise measurement of corresponding changes in non-imaging color sensor outputs. The perturbation patterns are carefully designed to balance multiple competing requirements: they must be sufficiently rich in variation to capture comprehensive information about the light transport characteristics of the space, while remaining small enough in magnitude to avoid perceptible changes in illumination that might disturb occupants.

The mathematical foundation of perturbation-modulated lighting rests on the affine relationship between fixture inputs and sensor outputs, expressed as y = Ax + b, where y represents the sensor measurements, x represents the fixture inputs, A represents the light transport matrix, and b represents the sensor response to ambient light sources. By applying a reference illumination level x₀ and then introducing small perturbations δx around this reference level, the system can eliminate the ambient light component b through simple subtraction, yielding the relationship δy = Aδx. This differential measurement approach effectively isolates the light transport characteristics of the controlled lighting system from external influences, enabling accurate recovery of the light transport matrix A.

The design of perturbation patterns follows three primary requirements established by the invention. First, the patterns must exhibit sufficient variation to ensure that the resulting measurement matrix X has full column rank, thereby enabling unique recovery of the light transport matrix. Randomly generated patterns typically satisfy this requirement, as they provide diverse illumination conditions that probe different aspects of the light transport phenomenon. Second, the magnitude of perturbations must remain below the threshold of human perception to maintain occupant comfort during the sensing phase. Extensive sensitivity analyses have determined that perturbation magnitudes in the range of 0.01 to 0.05 (relative to the full input range of [0,1]) represent an optimal balance between detectability and imperceptibility for typical indoor lighting conditions. Third, the perturbation magnitude must be sufficiently large to overcome sensor noise and measurement uncertainties, ensuring that the resulting signal changes can be accurately captured by the color sensors.

The temporal ordering of perturbation patterns represents another critical aspect of the perturbation-modulated lighting technique. To minimize perceptible flicker and maximize human comfort, the invention employs optimization algorithms that arrange the perturbation patterns in sequences that minimize abrupt changes in illumination. This problem is mathematically equivalent to the traveling salesman problem (TSP), where each perturbation pattern represents a city to be visited, and the distance between cities represents the magnitude of change required to transition between corresponding illumination states. The invention utilizes genetic algorithms and other TSP solvers to find near-optimal sequences that minimize the total variation in illumination over the sensing period.

The perturbation-modulated lighting technique operates within a quasi-static assumption that treats both the occupancy distribution and ambient lighting conditions as effectively constant during the brief sensing interval. This assumption is justified by the rapid response capabilities of modern LED fixtures and photodiodes, which enable the collection of sufficient measurements within timeframes much shorter than typical occupancy movement timescales. For example, with state-of-the-art components capable of megabit-per-second data rates, dozens of perturbation measurements can be collected within fractions of a second, ensuring that the quasi-static assumption holds for practical occupancy scenarios.

The implementation of perturbation-modulated lighting involves careful coordination between the lighting control system and sensor network. The invention specifies protocols for synchronizing perturbation application with sensor measurements, ensuring that each perturbation pattern is fully established before corresponding measurements are taken. This synchronization accounts for fixture response delays and sensor integration times, which may vary depending on specific hardware implementations. Advanced implementations may employ adaptive perturbation strategies that adjust pattern characteristics based on real-time feedback from sensor measurements, optimizing the information content of subsequent measurements based on initial results.

The perturbation-modulated lighting technique provides several key advantages over alternative approaches to light transport characterization. Unlike structured light methods that require specialized high-resolution projection equipment, this approach leverages existing lighting infrastructure, making it compatible with standard LED fixture installations. Compared to static measurement approaches that rely on natural variations in occupancy or ambient lighting, the active perturbation strategy provides controlled, repeatable conditions that enable more reliable and accurate light transport matrix recovery. Furthermore, the imperceptible nature of the perturbations ensures that the sensing process does not interfere with normal lighting functions or occupant activities, making it suitable for continuous or frequent operation in occupied spaces.

### Analysis of the Light Transport Matrix

The analysis of the light transport matrix represents the core computational component of the present invention, transforming raw sensor measurements into meaningful occupancy information through systematic examination of the recovered light transport matrix A. This analysis process involves multiple stages, beginning with preprocessing and aggregation of the raw matrix elements, followed by comparison with a reference matrix to identify occupancy-induced changes, and culminating in the application of specialized reconstruction algorithms to generate spatial occupancy estimates.

The initial preprocessing stage addresses the multi-channel nature of both LED fixtures and color sensors. Each LED fixture typically controls three independent color channels (red, green, and blue), while each color sensor measures four output channels (red, green, blue, and white/unfiltered). This results in a light transport matrix A with dimensions m₂ × m₁, where m₁ = 3N_L (with N_L representing the number of LED fixtures) and m₂ = 4N_S (with N_S representing the number of color sensors). The invention recognizes that while individual color channels provide valuable information about spectral characteristics of the light transport, the primary goal of occupancy estimation focuses on spatial rather than spectral information. Therefore, the analysis process includes an aggregation step that combines information across color channels to produce a more robust and interpretable representation.

The aggregation process computes a weighted sum of the red, green, and blue channels for each fixture-sensor pair, resulting in an N_S × N_L matrix Ê that establishes a one-to-one correspondence between matrix elements and direct light paths. The weights w_R, w_G, and w_B can be adjusted to compensate for differences in sensor sensitivity across color channels or to emphasize particular spectral bands that may be more informative for occupancy detection. This aggregation not only reduces the dimensionality of the problem but also provides noise reduction benefits through the averaging effect of combining multiple measurements.

The central analytical step involves comparing the current light transport matrix A with a reference matrix A₀ obtained during a known baseline condition (typically an empty or reference occupancy state). The difference matrix E = A₀ - A captures the changes in light transport characteristics induced by the current occupancy configuration. Each element of E represents the change in light transport efficiency between a specific fixture-sensor pair, with positive values indicating attenuation (reduced light transmission) and negative values indicating enhancement (increased light transmission, though this is less common in typical occupancy scenarios).

The interpretation of the difference matrix E depends on the physical configuration of the sensor network. When sensors are mounted on walls, creating direct line-of-sight paths between fixtures and sensors, the primary mechanism for light transport changes is blockage of direct illumination paths by occupying objects. In this configuration, large positive values in E strongly suggest that the corresponding direct path has been partially or completely blocked by an occupant or object. When sensors are mounted on the ceiling alongside fixtures, eliminating direct line-of-sight paths, the primary mechanism becomes changes in diffuse reflection patterns from floor and other surfaces, requiring different analytical approaches.

The analysis process incorporates several important considerations to ensure robust performance in real-world conditions. First, it accounts for the non-uniform spatial distribution of fixtures and sensors, which can lead to biased occupancy estimates if not properly normalized. Second, it handles the inherent ill-posed nature of the inverse problem by incorporating appropriate regularization terms that reflect prior knowledge about expected occupancy patterns (such as smoothness and continuity constraints). Third, it provides mechanisms for handling missing or corrupted data, such as malfunctioning sensors or temporarily occluded fixtures, through robust statistical methods and redundancy exploitation.

The analysis of the light transport matrix also includes quality assessment metrics that evaluate the reliability of the recovered matrix and the resulting occupancy estimates. These metrics consider factors such as measurement noise levels, condition number of the measurement matrix X, and consistency of results across different perturbation patterns. When quality metrics fall below acceptable thresholds, the system can trigger adaptive responses such as increasing the number of measurements, adjusting perturbation magnitudes, or requesting recalibration of the reference matrix.

The final output of the light transport matrix analysis is a set of processed data structures that serve as input to the subsequent reconstruction algorithms. These include the aggregated difference matrix Ê, quality assessment metrics, and any additional metadata required by the specific reconstruction approach being employed. The analysis process is designed to be computationally efficient, enabling real-time or near-real-time operation in practical lighting control applications, while maintaining sufficient accuracy and robustness for reliable occupancy estimation.

### Volume Rendering

The volume rendering component of the present invention transforms the abstract mathematical representations generated by the light transport matrix analysis into intuitive visual representations of the estimated occupancy distribution, facilitating both human interpretation and automated decision-making in lighting control applications. This process involves converting the confidence values computed by the reconstruction algorithms into three-dimensional volumetric data structures that can be visualized, analyzed, and utilized by downstream lighting control modules.

For the light blockage model approach, which produces three-dimensional occupancy estimates, the volume rendering process begins with a discretized representation of the indoor space divided into voxels of specified dimensions (typically 1 inch³ in experimental implementations). Each voxel is assigned a confidence value C(P) representing the likelihood that the corresponding spatial location is occupied, computed according to the reconstruction algorithm described in the light blockage model section. The volume rendering process converts these confidence values into visual representations through various techniques including direct volume rendering, isosurface extraction, and maximum intensity projection.

Direct volume rendering employs transfer functions that map confidence values to optical properties such as color and opacity, enabling the creation of semi-transparent visualizations that reveal the internal structure of the occupancy distribution. Isosurface extraction identifies surfaces of constant confidence value, producing mesh representations that can be rendered using standard computer graphics techniques. Maximum intensity projection creates two-dimensional projections by selecting the maximum confidence value along each viewing ray, providing compact representations suitable for display on conventional monitors.

The volume rendering process incorporates several important features to enhance the utility and interpretability of the resulting visualizations. First, it supports interactive visualization capabilities that allow users to explore the three-dimensional occupancy distribution from different viewpoints and at different levels of detail. Second, it includes annotation features that highlight regions of high confidence or significant changes relative to previous states, drawing attention to important occupancy events. Third, it provides quantitative analysis tools that extract numerical metrics from the volumetric data, such as total occupied volume, centroid locations, and spatial distribution statistics.

For the light reflection model approach, which produces two-dimensional floor-plane occupancy maps, the volume rendering process is simplified but still incorporates important visualization features. The confidence map is represented as a two-dimensional grid of pixel values, with each pixel corresponding to a specific location on the floor plane. The rendering process applies color mapping techniques to convert confidence values into visually distinguishable colors, typically using warm colors (reds, oranges) for high-confidence regions and cool colors (blues, greens) for low-confidence regions. Additional visualization features include contour lines that delineate regions of similar confidence levels and transparency effects that allow underlying floor plans to remain visible.

The volume rendering component also includes optimization techniques to ensure efficient performance in real-time applications. These optimizations include level-of-detail management that adjusts rendering complexity based on available computational resources and user requirements, caching mechanisms that store frequently accessed visual representations to reduce redundant computation, and parallel processing implementations that leverage modern graphics processing units (GPUs) for accelerated rendering.

The rendered visualizations serve multiple purposes in the overall smart lighting system. For human operators, they provide intuitive representations of the current occupancy state that facilitate system monitoring and troubleshooting. For automated lighting control modules, they provide structured data representations that can be easily processed to determine appropriate lighting adjustments. The volume rendering process ensures that the rich spatial information captured by the occupancy sensing system is effectively communicated to both human and machine consumers, supporting sophisticated lighting control strategies that optimize energy efficiency, human comfort, and productivity while maintaining occupant privacy.

The volume rendering component is designed to be flexible and extensible, supporting various output formats and integration interfaces that accommodate different application requirements and system architectures. It can generate static images for archival purposes, animated sequences for temporal analysis, and interactive visualizations for real-time monitoring. The component also supports customization options that allow users to adjust visualization parameters such as color schemes, transparency levels, and display resolutions to suit specific application needs and preferences.
