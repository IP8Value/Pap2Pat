# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The serine/threonine kinase Akt, also known as protein kinase B (PKB), is a central node in the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt/mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway, which governs critical cellular processes including cell survival, proliferation, metabolism, and growth. Aberrant activation of this pathway is one of the most frequently observed molecular alterations in human malignancies, with estimates suggesting that up to fifty percent of all human cancers exhibit dysregulation at some level of the PI3K/Akt axis. This dysregulation may arise from multiple mechanisms, including loss-of-function mutations or epigenetic silencing of the tumor suppressor phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), activating mutations in the catalytic subunit of PI3K (p110α), amplification or overexpression of receptor tyrosine kinases such as HER2 or EGFR, or gain-of-function mutations in the AKT genes themselves. Among these, the AKT1-E17K mutation—resulting from a glutamic acid-to-lysine substitution at codon 17 in the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of AKT1—has been identified in a range of solid tumors, including breast, ovarian, colorectal, and lung cancers, and leads to constitutive membrane localization and hyperactivation of Akt independent of upstream PI3K signaling.

Given the pivotal role of Akt in oncogenesis and tumor maintenance, substantial efforts have been directed toward the development of pharmacological inhibitors targeting this kinase. Early strategies focused on lipid-based compounds designed to interfere with the PH domain of Akt, which normally binds to phosphatidylinositol (3,4,5)-trisphosphate (PIP3) generated by PI3K at the plasma membrane—a critical step required for Akt recruitment, phosphorylation, and full activation. Perifosine, a first-generation alkylphospholipid, exemplifies this class; however, clinical trials revealed significant limitations, including poor pharmacokinetic properties, dose-limiting toxicities, and a lack of demonstrable target modulation in patient samples. Moreover, perifosine exhibits limited selectivity and affects multiple membrane-associated signaling pathways beyond Akt, complicating interpretation of its mechanism of action and therapeutic efficacy. A second approach has yielded ATP-competitive small molecule inhibitors that bind to the catalytic kinase domain of Akt. While potent in biochemical assays, many of these compounds suffer from off-target effects due to the high degree of structural conservation among the ATP-binding pockets of the broader AGC kinase family (which includes PKA, PKC, SGK, S6K, and RSK). Such lack of selectivity can lead to unintended biological consequences and increased toxicity, thereby narrowing the therapeutic window.

A third category includes allosteric inhibitors that bind to a site distinct from the ATP pocket, often at the interface between the PH and kinase domains, stabilizing an inactive conformation of Akt. These agents, such as MK-2206, demonstrate improved selectivity but have shown variable efficacy against clinically relevant Akt mutants, particularly those residing within the PH domain like E17K, which disrupt the autoinhibitory interaction between the PH and kinase domains and promote constitutive membrane association. Notably, the naturally occurring AKT1-E17K mutant has proven resistant to several allosteric inhibitors, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic modalities capable of effectively neutralizing this oncogenic variant. Additionally, triciribine (also known as API-2), a tricyclic nucleoside analog previously evaluated in clinical trials decades ago for its antitumor activity without knowledge of its molecular target, was later discovered to function as a pan-Akt inhibitor. Although it demonstrated preclinical efficacy and is currently undergoing re-evaluation in phase I trials, its historical use was associated with significant hematological and hepatic toxicities, likely due to non-selective effects or suboptimal dosing regimens not informed by modern biomarker strategies.

Despite these advances, there remains a critical unmet medical need for a potent, selective, and well-tolerated Akt inhibitor that can effectively suppress both wild-type and mutant forms of Akt—particularly those driven by PH domain alterations—and that demonstrates robust antitumor activity in vivo with a favorable safety profile. Such an agent would not only serve as a valuable therapeutic tool for cancers harboring PI3K/Akt pathway activation but also as a chemical probe to dissect the nuanced biology of Akt isoforms and their contributions to tumorigenesis. The ideal inhibitor should operate through a mechanism that circumvents the limitations of ATP-competitive agents (e.g., selectivity issues) and lipid mimetics (e.g., poor bioavailability), while retaining the ability to block Akt membrane translocation—a fundamental step in its activation cascade. Furthermore, it should exhibit preferential cytotoxicity toward cancer cells dependent on Akt signaling ("oncogene addiction") while sparing normal cells, thereby enhancing therapeutic index. Prior to the present invention, no small molecule had been described that fulfills all these criteria: direct binding to the Akt PH domain, inhibition of membrane translocation, suppression of both Thr308 and Ser473 phosphorylation, activity against constitutively active mutants including AKT1-E17K and myristoylated Akt constructs, selectivity over other AGC kinases and major oncogenic pathways, and demonstrable in vivo antitumor efficacy in xenograft models with minimal systemic toxicity. The identification and characterization of such a compound represent a significant advancement in the field of targeted cancer therapy and provide a foundation for the development of next-generation Akt inhibitors with improved clinical potential.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel small molecule compound, designated Akt/PKB Inhibitor-1 (API-1), and its pharmaceutically acceptable salts, solvates, hydrates, prodrugs, and stereoisomers, which functions as a potent, selective, and orally bioavailable inhibitor of the Akt signaling pathway. API-1, chemically identified as pyrido[2,3-d]pyrimidine (NSC 177223), exerts its inhibitory effect through a unique mechanism involving direct binding to the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of Akt isoforms (Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3), thereby preventing the critical step of Akt translocation from the cytosol to the plasma membrane. This blockade of membrane localization consequently abrogates the phosphorylation of Akt at both Thr308 (by PDK1) and Ser473 (by mTORC2), leading to complete inactivation of Akt kinase activity in living cells. Importantly, API-1 does not function as an ATP-competitive or substrate-competitive inhibitor, as evidenced by its inability to suppress recombinant Akt kinase activity in cell-free systems at concentrations that fully inhibit Akt phosphorylation in intact cells. This mechanism distinguishes API-1 from conventional kinase inhibitors and underlies its exceptional selectivity.

The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising API-1 in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, diluent, or excipient, formulated for administration via oral, intravenous, intraperitoneal, subcutaneous, or other suitable routes. These compositions are particularly useful for the treatment of cancers characterized by hyperactivation of the Akt pathway, whether due to upstream genetic alterations (e.g., PTEN loss, PI3K mutations, receptor tyrosine kinase amplification) or intrinsic Akt mutations (e.g., AKT1-E17K). API-1 demonstrates potent antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects selectively in human cancer cell lines exhibiting elevated phospho-Akt levels, while showing minimal activity in cells with low basal Akt signaling. This selectivity translates into significant in vivo antitumor efficacy in nude mouse xenograft models bearing tumors with constitutively active Akt, such as OVCAR3 (ovarian carcinoma) and PANC-1 (pancreatic adenocarcinoma), where daily intraperitoneal administration of API-1 at 10 mg/kg results in 50–70% tumor growth inhibition without observable systemic toxicity, including no alterations in body weight, food intake, activity, or blood glucose levels.

Moreover, the invention includes methods of treating a subject suffering from a neoplastic disease associated with aberrant Akt activation, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of API-1 or a pharmaceutical composition thereof. The compound is effective against both wild-type Akt activated by extracellular stimuli (e.g., EGF, IGF-1) and constitutively active Akt mutants, including the clinically relevant AKT1-E17K mutant and artificially myristoylated Akt constructs, which are often resistant to other classes of Akt inhibitors. API-1 exhibits superior potency compared to the previously known Akt inhibitor triciribine (API-2), particularly in suppressing the kinase activity of mutant AKT1-E17K. Additionally, API-1 shows no significant inhibitory activity against key upstream regulators of Akt (PI3K, PDK1, mTORC1/2 complexes) or other major oncogenic signaling pathways (ERK, JNK, p38 MAPK, STAT3), nor does it inhibit closely related AGC kinases such as PKA, PKC, or SGK, thereby confirming its high degree of target specificity. The invention thus provides a powerful new therapeutic agent for precision oncology, enabling the targeting of Akt-dependent tumors with enhanced efficacy and reduced off-target effects.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### MATERIALS AND METHODS

The present invention is based on the identification and comprehensive characterization of a novel small molecule, API-1 (NSC 177223), as a selective inhibitor of the Akt signaling pathway. The experimental procedures employed to discover, validate, and mechanistically elucidate the action of API-1 are detailed herein to enable the skilled artisan to practice the invention without undue experimentation. All cell lines utilized in the studies were either obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) or have been previously described in the scientific literature. Specifically, NIH3T3 murine fibroblasts stably transfected with either AKT2 or the empty LXSN vector served as isogenic models for Akt-transformed versus control cells. Human cancer cell lines, including OVCAR3, OVCAR5, OVCAR8, MCF7, MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-435s, PANC1, H661, and COLO357, were selected based on their known Akt activation status—either due to PTEN deficiency (e.g., MDA-MB-468), Akt overexpression (e.g., OVCAR3), or low basal Akt signaling (e.g., OVCAR5)—to assess the selectivity of API-1. HEK293 human embryonic kidney cells were used for transient transfection experiments due to their high transfection efficiency and robust protein expression.

The compound library screened consisted of 2,300 structurally diverse small molecules sourced from the National Cancer Institute’s Developmental Therapeutics Program (NCI/DTP) Open Chemical Repository. API-1, corresponding to NSC 177223, is a pyrido[2,3-d]pyrimidine derivative structurally related to the antibiotic sangivamycin but previously untested in cancer models. Expression plasmids encoding HA-tagged or Myc-tagged wild-type human Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3 were constructed by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) using RNA isolated from MCF10A mammary epithelial cells as template, followed by cloning into the BamHI-EcoRI sites of the pCMV-Myc-Tag2 vector (Stratagene). Site-directed mutagenesis using the QuikChange kit (Stratagene) was employed to generate the AKT1-E17K mutant by introducing a G-to-A transition at nucleotide 49 of the wild-type AKT1 cDNA. GFP-tagged constructs of full-length Akt and the isolated PH domain were created by ligating the respective cDNAs into the pEGFP-C1 vector (Clontech).

Primary screening for Akt inhibitors was performed by seeding AKT2-transformed NIH3T3 cells and LXSN-transfected control cells into 96-well plates and treating them with each compound at a final concentration of 5 μM. After 72 hours, cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter 96 One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay (Promega), which measures mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity as a surrogate for viable cell number. Compounds that selectively inhibited the growth of AKT2-transformed cells without affecting control cells were prioritized for secondary validation. In vitro kinase assays were conducted as previously established: cells were lysed, and Akt or other kinases were immunoprecipitated using epitope-specific antibodies (e.g., anti-HA for transfected constructs). The immunoprecipitates were then incubated with kinase buffer containing [γ-32P]ATP and appropriate substrates (e.g., GSK3 fusion protein for Akt, Kemptide for PKA, SGK substrate peptide for SGK). Phosphorylated products were resolved by SDS-PAGE, visualized by autoradiography, and quantified by densitometry.

To evaluate direct binding between API-1 and Akt, an affinity purification assay was developed. NHS-activated Sepharose beads (GE Healthcare) were coupled to API-1 via its primary amino group in the presence of triethylamine in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Unreacted NHS esters were quenched with aminoethanol, and the beads were extensively washed with alternating buffers (0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 0.1 M sodium acetate, 0.5 M NaCl, pH 4.0). The API-1-conjugated beads were then incubated overnight at 4°C with recombinant Akt1 protein or GST-fusion proteins encompassing the PH domain, kinase domain (KD), or C-terminal region (CT) of Akt in lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After washing, bound proteins were eluted by boiling in Laemmli sample buffer, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-Akt or anti-GST antibodies. Control beads were coupled to BMS-354825 (a Bcr-Abl inhibitor serving as a negative control) or compound E (a pan-kinase inhibitor serving as a positive control).

For in vivo studies, female athymic nude mice (8 weeks old) were implanted subcutaneously in both flanks with 2 × 10^6 human tumor cells suspended in phosphate-buffered saline. Once tumors reached a volume of approximately 100 mm³, mice were randomized into treatment groups (n = 6–8 per group). API-1 was administered daily via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 10 mg/kg dissolved in 20% DMSO, while control animals received vehicle alone. Tumor dimensions were measured twice weekly with calipers, and volumes were calculated using the formula (length × width²)/2. At study termination, tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for immunohistochemical or Western blot analysis of Akt phosphorylation status. Animal body weights, food consumption, and general behavior were monitored throughout the study to assess systemic toxicity. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and conducted in accordance with NIH guidelines for the humane treatment of laboratory animals.

### EXAMPLE 1

Identification and initial characterization of API-1 as a selective inhibitor of Akt-transformed cell growth began with a phenotypic screen of 2,300 compounds from the NCI/DTP repository. The screening strategy exploited the differential dependency of AKT2-transformed NIH3T3 cells versus their LXSN vector-transfected counterparts on Akt signaling for proliferation and survival. Cells were plated in 96-well format and exposed to each compound at 5 μM for 72 hours, after which viability was quantified using the CellTiter 96 assay. This approach yielded 32 hit compounds that selectively suppressed the growth of AKT2-transformed cells without significantly affecting control cells across three independent replicates. Among these, API-1 (NSC 177223) emerged as a lead candidate due to its structural novelty and absence of prior evaluation in oncology contexts. Subsequent validation confirmed that API-1 inhibits the kinase activity of all three Akt isoforms—Akt1, Akt2, and Akt3—in HEK293 cells transiently expressing HA-tagged versions of each. Following serum starvation and EGF stimulation, immunoprecipitated Akt isoforms exhibited dose-dependent reductions in kinase activity upon API-1 treatment, with near-complete inhibition observed at 10 μM. Concurrently, API-1 treatment of OVCAR3 ovarian cancer cells, which harbor constitutively elevated phospho-Akt due to PTEN loss, resulted in a marked decrease in Akt phosphorylation at both Thr308 and Ser473, with an IC50 of approximately 0.8 μM, while total Akt protein levels remained unchanged. Crucially, when tested in a cell-free system using recombinant, constitutively active Akt protein and a synthetic peptide substrate, API-1 failed to inhibit kinase activity at concentrations up to 10 μM, and only achieved ~20% inhibition at 50 μM—concentrations far exceeding those required for cellular efficacy. This dissociation between cellular and biochemical activity strongly suggested that API-1 does not act as a classical ATP- or substrate-competitive inhibitor but rather interferes with an upstream regulatory step essential for Akt activation in vivo.

### EXAMPLE 2

To elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying API-1’s cellular activity, a direct binding assay was developed using NHS-activated Sepharose beads covalently conjugated to API-1 via its primary amine group. Incubation of these API-1-beads with recombinant Akt1 protein resulted in robust pulldown of Akt, as detected by anti-Akt immunoblotting, whereas beads coupled to the unrelated compound BMS-354825 showed no binding. To map the interaction domain, GST-fusion proteins representing the PH domain, kinase domain (KD), and C-terminal tail (CT) of Akt were generated and subjected to the same binding assay. Only the GST-PH domain construct was efficiently retained by API-1-beads across a range of physiological salt concentrations (50–150 mM NaCl), indicating that API-1 specifically engages the PH domain of Akt. This finding prompted investigation into the functional consequence of PH domain binding. Since Akt activation requires PH domain-mediated recruitment to PIP3-enriched membranes, HeLa cells were transfected with Myc-tagged Akt and treated with API-1 prior to IGF-1 stimulation. Immunofluorescence microscopy revealed that while IGF-1 induced clear translocation of Akt to the plasma membrane in vehicle-treated cells, this redistribution was completely abrogated in API-1-treated cells. Parallel experiments using GFP-tagged full-length Akt or isolated GFP-PH domain constructs confirmed that API-1 blocks both basal and growth factor-induced membrane localization. These data collectively demonstrate that API-1 inhibits Akt by binding directly to its PH domain and preventing the essential step of membrane translocation, thereby uncoupling Akt from its upstream activators PDK1 and mTORC2.

### EXAMPLE 3

To confirm that API-1’s effects are specific to Akt and not mediated through inhibition of upstream signaling components, a series of experiments assessed the impact of API-1 on PI3K, PDK1, and mTOR complexes. In an in vitro PI3K lipid kinase assay, HEK293 cells were serum-starved, treated with API-1 or the PI3K inhibitor wortmannin, stimulated with EGF, and PI3K activity was measured by immunoprecipitating p110α and quantifying its ability to phosphorylate phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate. While wortmannin potently suppressed PI3K activity, API-1 had no effect, indicating it does not target PI3K. Similarly, an in vitro PDK1 kinase assay using SGK as a substrate showed that API-1 did not inhibit PDK1 enzymatic activity, unlike the known PDK1 inhibitor UCN-01. Furthermore, in OVCAR3 cells, API-1 treatment did not reduce phosphorylation of PDK1 at Ser241—an autophosphorylation site critical for its activation—whereas UCN-01 markedly decreased this modification. Finally, co-immunoprecipitation experiments demonstrated that API-1 does not disrupt the integrity of mTORC1 (Raptor-mTOR) or mTORC2 (Rictor-mTOR) complexes, although it reduced phosphorylation of mTOR at Ser2448, a downstream consequence of Akt inhibition via the TSC2-Rheb axis. These findings conclusively establish that API-1 acts directly on Akt itself and not through modulation of its canonical upstream regulators.

### EXAMPLE 4

Selectivity profiling of API-1 against other kinases and signaling pathways was conducted to assess potential off-target effects. In vitro kinase assays revealed that API-1 (up to 10 μM) did not inhibit the enzymatic activities of PKA or SGK, two AGC kinases closely related to Akt in structure and function, whereas specific inhibitors of these kinases (PKI for PKA, compound E for SGK) showed expected suppression. In cellular contexts, API-1 treatment of OVCAR3 cells did not alter the phosphorylation status of PKC or PKA activation loop residues. Moreover, immunoblot analysis of key mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways and the JAK-STAT axis showed no reduction in phosphorylated ERK1/2, JNK, p38, or STAT3 following API-1 exposure, indicating that these major oncogenic survival pathways remain intact. This high degree of selectivity is attributed to API-1’s unique mechanism of action—targeting the PH domain rather than the highly conserved kinase domain—and underscores its potential for a favorable safety profile in clinical applications.

### EXAMPLE 5

The ability of API-1 to inhibit constitutively active Akt mutants, particularly those resistant to other inhibitor classes, was rigorously evaluated. HEK293 cells were transfected with constructs encoding myristoylated Akt1 (myr-Akt1), myristoylated Akt2 (myr-Akt2), the PH domain mutant Akt1-E40K, or the clinically relevant AKT1-E17K mutant. Despite the expectation that myristoylation might bypass the need for PH domain–mediated membrane recruitment, API-1 effectively suppressed the kinase activity and phosphorylation of all four constitutively active forms. This unexpected result suggests that API-1 binding to the PH domain may induce a conformational change or steric hindrance that impairs kinase function even when membrane localization is artificially enforced. Notably, API-1 demonstrated superior potency compared to triciribine (API-2) in inhibiting both wild-type Akt and the AKT1-E17K mutant in head-to-head comparisons, highlighting its enhanced efficacy against this therapeutically challenging variant. Downstream, API-1 treatment of OVCAR3 and H661 cancer cells led to dephosphorylation of canonical Akt substrates, including GSK3β, FOXO3a, Bad, and components of the mTOR/p70S6K axis, confirming functional pathway blockade.

### EXAMPLE 6

The therapeutic potential of API-1 was validated in preclinical in vivo models. Nude mice bearing bilateral xenografts of OVCAR3/PANC1 (Akt-hyperactivated) and OVCAR5/COLO357 (low Akt activity) tumors were treated daily with API-1 (10 mg/kg, i.p.) or vehicle. API-1 induced significant tumor growth inhibition—70% in OVCAR3 and 50% in PANC1 xenografts—while exerting minimal effects on OVCAR5 and COLO357 tumors. Immunohistochemical analysis of excised tumors confirmed a ~70% reduction in phospho-Akt (Ser473) levels without changes in total Akt, verifying target engagement. Critically, no signs of systemic toxicity were observed: body weight, food intake, locomotor activity, and fasting blood glucose levels remained stable throughout the treatment period. In parallel in vitro studies, API-1 selectively induced apoptosis (measured by annexin V staining and PARP cleavage) and suppressed proliferation in Akt-dependent cell lines (e.g., 9-fold increase in apoptosis in OVCAR3), but not in Akt-independent lines. These data collectively demonstrate that API-1 is a potent, selective, and well-tolerated Akt inhibitor with compelling preclinical efficacy, supporting its advancement as a candidate for clinical development in cancers driven by Akt pathway activation.
