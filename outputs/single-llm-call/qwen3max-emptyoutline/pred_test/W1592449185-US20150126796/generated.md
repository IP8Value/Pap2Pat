# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of radiation therapy, and more particularly to systems and methods for ensuring accurate patient positioning during external beam radiotherapy treatments. Specifically, the invention provides an automated, general-purpose patient safety system (PSS) that utilizes optical tracking technology to continuously verify patient setup accuracy in real time, thereby preventing gross geometric setup errors that could lead to severe treatment complications or misadministration of radiation dose. The system is designed to function as a complementary safeguard to existing image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) technologies by offering independent, non-ionizing, and continuous verification of patient position relative to the treatment isocenter throughout the entire treatment process, from initial setup through delivery. The invention is applicable across all anatomical sites and treatment modalities, including intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT), stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), and conventional fractionated radiotherapy, and is compatible with both coplanar and noncoplanar beam arrangements. By integrating seamlessly into existing clinical workflows and interfacing with standard record-and-verify (R&V) and treatment planning systems (TPS), the invention enhances patient safety without imposing significant additional burden on clinical staff.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Radiation therapy is a cornerstone of modern cancer treatment, involving the precise delivery of ionizing radiation to malignant tumors while sparing surrounding healthy tissues. The efficacy and safety of this modality critically depend on the accurate spatial alignment of the patient relative to the radiation beam at each treatment session. Over the past two decades, technological advancements such as intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) and volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT) have enabled highly conformal dose distributions that tightly wrap around complex tumor geometries. While these techniques improve therapeutic ratios, they also increase the sensitivity of treatment outcomes to even minor deviations in patient positioning. A geometric miss—defined as a spatial discrepancy of more than 1 cm between the intended and actual treatment location—can result in catastrophic consequences, including tumor recurrence due to underdosing of the target volume and severe normal tissue toxicity from overdosing of critical organs. In hypofractionated regimens like stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), where large doses are delivered in few fractions, the margin for error is especially narrow, and setup inaccuracies can lead to life-threatening complications.

Despite significant progress in reducing overall incident rates in radiotherapy, severe errors continue to occur, often stemming from incorrect patient setup. Epidemiological studies have consistently identified geometric miss as a leading cause of radiotherapy incidents. For instance, among 100 internationally reported radiotherapy incidents, 21% were attributed to incorrect patient setup; this proportion rises to 37.5% when excluding brachytherapy cases, where setup precision is less critical. In another institutional analysis of 41 serious incidents over a three-year period, 14 involved geographic misses, with 13 directly resulting from shifting errors during patient setup. Similarly, a review of 230 misadministrations reported to the New York State Department of Health between 2001 and 2009 revealed that “incorrect body part treated” accounted for 46% of errors, predominantly due to setup inaccuracies. These findings underscore that, despite technological sophistication, the human-dependent nature of patient setup remains a persistent vulnerability in the radiotherapy chain.

To mitigate setup errors, the field has adopted various technological aids. Immobilization devices with couch indexing capabilities provide reproducible patient fixation and reduce initial setup uncertainty. More significantly, image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) systems—such as cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)—have become standard in modern clinics. CBCT enables visualization of internal anatomy prior to treatment, allowing for corrections based on registration with the reference planning CT. Additionally, continuous optical tracking systems like AlignRT, C-Rad Sentinel, SonArray, and ExacTrac Optical-Tracking System offer real-time monitoring of surface or fiducial positions. These tools have demonstrably reduced setup-related incidents; for example, one institution reported a 50% decrease in localization errors after implementing IGRT on select linear accelerators, and none of the geometric misses in another study occurred on units equipped with daily image guidance.

However, reliance on these advanced systems does not eliminate the risk of gross setup errors. Several interrelated factors contribute to this residual risk. First, human interpretation and operation remain central to the setup process. Personnel may lack sufficient training in using or interpreting data from complex IGRT devices, leading to misregistration—particularly in anatomically homogeneous regions like the thoracic spine, where vertebral bodies appear similar on imaging. Second, the increasing complexity of treatment delivery systems has led to cognitive overload among therapists, who must manage multiple monitors, software interfaces, and procedural steps. This heightened workload can compromise attention to detail, especially when standardized workflows are absent. Third, inherent limitations of current IGRT technologies create blind spots. CBCT, for instance, captures a static snapshot of patient position at the time of acquisition but cannot monitor subsequent movements. After CBCT imaging, the treatment couch may be inadvertently displaced—for example, during clearance checks—and not returned to the corrected position. Although modern linear accelerators include interlocks to prevent treatment under such conditions, these safeguards can be overridden by users, potentially leading to treatment at entirely incorrect sites. Moreover, CBCT and most radiographic IGRT systems cannot be used during noncoplanar treatments, as the imaging geometry differs from the treatment geometry, necessitating manual couch repositioning that introduces error opportunities. Fourth, and most critically, there is often no immediate, independent means of verifying that the patient is positioned exactly as planned, particularly when the limitations of primary IGRT systems are encountered. This lack of real-time, secondary confirmation leaves room for undetected errors to propagate into treatment delivery.

Existing continuous tracking systems, while valuable, are not universally applicable or optimally integrated into routine clinical practice. Systems like ExacTrac require multiple infrared reflective markers (IRRM) affixed to the patient per session, increasing therapist workload and potential for placement errors. Others, such as Frameless SonArray, are restricted to specific anatomical sites (e.g., brain via bite blocks). Surface imaging systems like AlignRT and C-Rad Sentinel are predominantly used for head and neck, brain, or breast treatments and are not routinely deployed across all disease sites. Furthermore, these systems typically require manual therapist intervention to load patient data, select treatment sites, and input couch angles for noncoplanar beams—steps that disrupt workflow and introduce human error. Consequently, despite their technical capabilities, these systems are often underutilized as comprehensive safety nets in busy clinical environments.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a patient safety system that is general-purpose, fully automated, minimally burdensome, and capable of providing continuous, independent verification of patient position across all treatment scenarios. Such a system must overcome the limitations of current IGRT technologies by offering real-time monitoring that is decoupled from imaging constraints, couch movement uncertainties, and human operational variability. It should integrate seamlessly with existing clinical infrastructure—including TPS and R&V systems—to ensure adoption without disrupting established workflows. Most importantly, it must reliably detect gross setup errors (>1 cm) that could lead to severe clinical consequences, thereby serving as a robust final checkpoint before and during radiation delivery.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a fully automated, general-purpose patient safety system (PSS) for real-time, independent verification of patient setup accuracy in external beam radiotherapy. The system is designed to prevent gross geometric setup errors by continuously monitoring the three-dimensional position of a single infrared reflective marker (IRRM) affixed either to the patient’s skin or, preferably, to a patient-specific immobilization device. The PSS operates independently of the primary image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) system, treatment machine, couch design, or internal patient anatomy, thereby serving as a complementary safeguard against a wide range of setup-related errors, including those arising from human oversight, equipment limitations, or unusual clinical circumstances.

The core components of the system include a pair of ceiling-mounted charge-coupled device (CCD) cameras configured to detect infrared light reflected from the IRRM, and a dedicated software platform that processes camera data to determine the real-time 3D coordinates of the marker in the treatment room coordinate system. Through a rigorous calibration procedure, the native coordinate system of the camera array is transformed into the absolute room coordinate system aligned with the machine isocenter. This calibration employs a jig containing multiple IRRMs whose relative positions are precisely known from CT imaging; by co-registering this jig to the imaging isocenter using CBCT and solving the relative pose problem via singular value decomposition (SVD), a rotation matrix and translation vector are derived to enable accurate spatial mapping.

A key innovation of the invention lies in its seamless integration with existing clinical workflows. During the preparation stage, a small radiopaque marker (CT BB) is placed on or near the patient during simulation CT scanning at a location proximal to the treatment target. The coordinates of this point of interest (POI), along with the treatment isocenter, are exported from the treatment planning system (TPS) to a designated network folder. Concurrently, the approved treatment plan—including beam configurations and associated anatomical sites—is exported from the record-and-verify (R&V) system in standard radiation treatment plan (RTP) format. The PSS software automatically imports and correlates this information, establishing a database entry that links each treatment site to its corresponding reference IRRM position relative to the isocenter.

At the treatment stage, the system operates with minimal or no therapist intervention. When a treatment beam is loaded into the R&V system, an external trigger file is generated containing patient and beam identifiers. The PSS automatically retrieves the associated treatment site and reference IRRM coordinates from its database. If the IRRM is permanently mounted on an immobilization device—such as an aquaplastic face mask for head and neck patients or a custom body mold for other sites—the system immediately begins continuous tracking upon patient placement. The real-time position of the IRRM is compared against its expected reference location, and any deviation exceeding user-defined thresholds is displayed prominently on monitors in both the treatment room and control area, prompting corrective action.

For noncoplanar treatments involving couch rotation, the system dynamically adjusts the expected IRRM position by mathematically rotating the vector connecting the isocenter to the IRRM according to the actual couch angle, ensuring accurate verification regardless of beam arrangement. In cases where permanent mounting is impractical—such as for abdominal-pelvic patients during initial sessions—the IRRM is temporarily placed on a skin tattoo corresponding to the original CT BB location. After successful verification, the IRRM is transferred to an optimal location on the immobilization device, and this new position is captured as the reference for subsequent fractions, thereby eliminating daily placement variability.

The invention further includes a streamlined daily quality assurance (QA) protocol. An IRRM is permanently affixed to a standard daily QA phantom, which is routinely aligned to the room isocenter using CBCT as part of existing QA procedures. When the corresponding QA beam is loaded in the R&V system, the PSS automatically displays the positional consistency of the IRRM, providing immediate feedback on system performance without additional steps.

Extensive validation demonstrates that the PSS achieves sub-millimeter short-term stability and long-term accuracy within 2 mm, with end-to-end phantom tests confirming detection of intentional couch shifts as small as 5 mm with high fidelity. Clinical implementation across head and neck and abdominal-pelvic patient cohorts shows that the system reliably identifies setup discrepancies greater than 1 cm, while maintaining compatibility with breathing motion through appropriate signal averaging or strategic marker placement. Critically, since its deployment, the system has intercepted multiple near-miss events—including incorrect indexing bar placement, machine substitution errors, and post-imaging couch displacements—that would otherwise have resulted in treatment of wrong anatomical sites.

By utilizing only a single, disposable, flat-surface IRRM and automating all data handling through integration with R&V systems, the invention minimizes therapist workload and eliminates common sources of human error. Its general applicability across all disease sites, independence from imaging modalities, and ability to provide continuous, real-time feedback make it an ideal complement to existing IGRT technologies. The PSS thus fulfills a crucial role in the radiotherapy safety ecosystem: not as a primary setup tool, but as an independent, automated sentinel that ensures the final treatment position matches the intended plan, thereby preventing catastrophic setup errors before they reach the patient.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a patient safety system (PSS) for use in external beam radiotherapy that provides continuous, real-time, and independent verification of patient setup accuracy to prevent gross geometric errors. The system is engineered to be general-purpose, applicable to all anatomical treatment sites, and fully integrated into standard clinical workflows with minimal disruption to existing practices. Below is a detailed description of the system architecture, calibration methodology, operational workflow, quality assurance protocols, and performance characteristics.

The hardware foundation of the PSS consists of a pair of high-precision Polaris CCD cameras, commercially available from Northern Digital Inc., mounted on the ceiling of the radiotherapy treatment room in a fixed, opposing configuration to ensure overlapping fields of view. These cameras are sensitive to infrared light and are specifically designed to detect reflections from passive infrared reflective markers (IRRM). The cameras are connected to a dedicated processing computer via a serial cable and a proprietary data interface, enabling high-frequency data acquisition at approximately 15 frames per second. The system is capable of simultaneously tracking up to 50 IRRMs, though the present invention utilizes only a single marker per patient to simplify workflow and reduce potential sources of error.

The IRRM employed in the preferred embodiment is a custom-designed, disposable, flat-surface marker fabricated by applying a layer of infrared-reflective tape to one side of a circular double-sided adhesive disc measuring 6 millimeters in diameter and 1 millimeter in height. The opposite side is protected by a peelable backing, facilitating easy and consistent application to either patient skin or immobilization devices. This flat design offers advantages in terms of low profile and ease of placement, though it requires careful orientation to maintain visibility to the ceiling-mounted cameras. For patients treated with rigid immobilization such as aquaplastic face masks, the IRRM is affixed to a location on the mask—typically over the chin—that ensures unobstructed line-of-sight to both cameras throughout treatment. For body mold immobilization, the IRRM is strategically placed on a stable, non-flexing region of the mold that remains visible during all treatment positions and is unlikely to be dislodged during patient entry or exit.

System calibration is a critical step that establishes the mathematical transformation between the camera-native coordinate system and the absolute room coordinate system aligned with the linear accelerator’s mechanical isocenter. This is achieved through a multi-stage procedure using a calibration jig composed of five commercial IRRMs arranged in a known geometric configuration. Prior to calibration, the jig is scanned using the same CT simulator used for patient imaging, and the three-dimensional coordinates of each IRRM relative to the jig’s centroid are extracted and stored in a 5×3 matrix denoted as A. The jig is then physically placed on the treatment couch and aligned to the imaging isocenter using cone-beam CT (CBCT) guidance, with confirmation that the imaging isocenter coincides with the machine isocenter via a ball-bearing phantom test as per standard commissioning protocols. The Polaris cameras then sample the real-time positions of the five IRRMs, and these coordinates are compiled into a second 5×3 matrix B in the camera-native frame.

The transformation from the camera frame to the room frame is determined by solving the classical relative pose problem in computer vision, which seeks a rotation matrix R and a translation vector S that minimize the least-squares error ε² = ||A − BR − S||. This is accomplished using a robust analytical solution based on singular value decomposition (SVD). Specifically, the centroids Ac and Bc of matrices A and B are first computed and subtracted to center both coordinate systems at their respective origins. The SVD of the matrix product BᵀA is then calculated, yielding unitary matrices U and V and a diagonal matrix W of singular values such that BᵀA = UWVᵀ. The optimal rotation matrix is given by R = U diag(1, 1, det(UVᵀ)) Vᵀ, which ensures a proper rotation (i.e., determinant +1), and the translation vector is S = Ac − BcR. This calibrated transformation is stored in the PSS software and applied in real time to convert all subsequent IRRM observations into absolute room coordinates.

The clinical workflow of the PSS is meticulously designed to minimize therapist burden and maximize automation. It is divided into two phases: preparation and treatment. During the preparation phase, which occurs at the time of CT simulation, a small radiopaque marker known as a CT BB is placed on the patient’s skin or directly onto the immobilization device at a location adjacent to the intended treatment volume. This ensures inclusion of the marker within the CT scan field of view. Following image acquisition, the CT BB is removed, and its location is marked with a permanent skin tattoo if placed on the body surface. In the treatment planning system (TPS), such as Pinnacle³ from Philips, the CT BB is contoured as a point of interest (POI), and its coordinates—along with those of the treatment isocenter—are exported to a shared network directory. Concurrently, the finalized treatment plan, including beam parameters and anatomical site labels, is transferred from the record-and-verify (R&V) system (e.g., MOSAIQ from Elekta) to the same directory in standard RTP format. A dedicated data import module within the PSS software automatically parses these files, associates each treatment site with its corresponding POIs, and computes the expected room coordinates of the IRRM (which will replace the CT BB at treatment) relative to the isocenter for each beam arrangement.

At the treatment stage, the system leverages the existing R&V infrastructure to achieve full automation. Modern R&V systems are configured to generate an external trigger file whenever a treatment beam is loaded for delivery; this file contains unique patient and beam identifiers. The PSS continuously monitors this trigger file and, upon detection of a new beam, automatically queries its internal database to retrieve the associated treatment site and reference IRRM coordinates. No manual data entry or patient selection is required by the therapist.

For patients with immobilization devices amenable to permanent marker attachment—such as head and neck patients using indexed face masks—the IRRM is affixed to the mask during the initial setup and remains in place for all subsequent fractions. Once the mask is positioned on the patient and a setup beam is loaded in the R&V system, the PSS immediately initiates continuous tracking. The real-time 3D position of the IRRM is displayed on dual monitors—one in the treatment room and one in the control area—with numerical and graphical representations of deviations from the reference position along lateral, longitudinal, and anteroposterior axes. Predefined tolerance thresholds (e.g., 5 mm) can be set to trigger visual or audible alerts if exceeded.

In the case of noncoplanar treatments involving couch rotation, the system dynamically recalculates the expected IRRM position. Since the IRRM is fixed relative to the patient (or immobilization device), its room coordinates change as the couch rotates. The PSS accounts for this by applying the same rotational transformation to the vector connecting the isocenter to the IRRM as is applied to the couch. Specifically, if the couch is rotated by an angle θ about the vertical axis, the reference IRRM position is updated by rotating the original offset vector by θ in the room coordinate system. This ensures accurate verification regardless of beam gantry or couch angle combinations.

For anatomical sites where permanent immobilization marker placement is initially challenging—such as abdominal-pelvic regions—the workflow incorporates a two-step initialization. During the first treatment fraction, the therapist locates the skin tattoo corresponding to the CT BB and places a temporary IRRM for initial verification. Once correct setup is confirmed via the PSS and standard IGRT, an optimal location on the body mold is selected for permanent IRRM mounting, taking into account visibility, stability, and resistance to displacement. The PSS then captures this new IRRM position as the reference for all future fractions, eliminating the need for daily tattoo-based placement and its associated uncertainties.

Daily quality assurance is seamlessly integrated into existing clinic routines. A permanent IRRM is affixed to the end of a standard daily QA phantom, such as the Daily QA³ from Sun Nuclear Corp., oriented toward the ceiling cameras. As part of routine morning QA, the phantom is aligned to the room isocenter using CBCT, and a dedicated “QA beam” is created in the R&V system. When this beam is loaded, the PSS automatically displays the positional deviation of the IRRM from its baseline, providing immediate feedback on system calibration stability. This process adds no extra steps to the therapist’s daily duties.

Performance validation of the PSS has been conducted through extensive phantom and clinical studies. Short-term stability tests, involving 10-minute continuous monitoring of a stationary IRRM on the QA phantom, demonstrated positional variations with standard deviations near zero and maximum excursions of only 0.3 mm, primarily attributable to ambient ceiling vibrations. Long-term stability, assessed weekly over 12 weeks using CBCT-aligned phantom placements, showed mean deviations of 0.5–0.6 mm per axis and a combined 3D error of 1.0 ± 0.6 mm, consistent with the known uncertainties of CBCT registration and couch positioning.

Phantom accuracy was evaluated using an anthropomorphic head model with five IRRMs placed at various surface locations. End-to-end testing revealed mean errors within 1.5 mm per axis and a maximum 3D deviation of 3.1 mm, with the chin-positioned IRRM showing superior accuracy (≤0.8 mm per axis). The system accurately detected intentional couch shifts ranging from ±0.5 cm to ±10 cm, with observed shifts differing from nominal values by less than 1.5 mm in coplanar setups and less than 1.7 mm with 60° couch rotation—deviations attributable to the lever-arm effect of rotational uncertainty on distal markers.

Clinical validation included 20 head and neck patients immobilized with indexed systems. The PSS showed mean deviations of <2.5 mm relative to both couch indexing and CBCT systems, with residual errors after CBCT correction averaging <1.0 mm per axis, confirming its ability to track automatic couch motions accurately. In 20 abdominal-pelvic patients, where the IRRM was placed on the breathing abdomen, average deviations increased slightly (2.8–3.9 mm) due to respiratory motion and placement uncertainty, but residual couch shift errors remained below 2.0 mm. Breathing-induced motion ranged up to 17.2 mm in 3D, informing appropriate threshold settings to avoid false alarms.

Critically, the PSS has intercepted multiple near-miss events in clinical practice. These include: (1) a 7 cm longitudinal offset due to incorrect indexing bar placement during CT simulation, detected before CBCT; (2) a 14 cm error from treating a patient on a substitute linac with different couch parameters, caught prior to imaging; and (3) an inadvertent 5 cm lateral couch shift after CBCT for collision avoidance, which was not reverted before treatment—a scenario that would have gone undetected without continuous monitoring.

The system’s design philosophy prioritizes simplicity and automation over ultra-high precision. By using only one IRRM, it avoids the complexity of multi-marker registration and rotational error decomposition, focusing instead on detecting gross translational discrepancies (>1 cm) that pose the greatest clinical risk. Its independence from imaging, couch mechanics, and internal anatomy makes it uniquely robust against a wide spectrum of failure modes. Combined with its seamless R&V integration and minimal workflow impact, the PSS represents a practical, scalable solution for enhancing patient safety in modern radiotherapy.
