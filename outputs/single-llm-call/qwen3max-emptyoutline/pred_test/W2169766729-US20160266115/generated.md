# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of immunology has long grappled with the challenge of accurately characterizing the human immune system in health and disease. Traditional methods for assessing immune status have relied heavily on relative frequencies of specific immune cell subsets—such as expressing regulatory T cells (Tregs) as a percentage of CD4+ T cells or total lymphocytes—without accounting for absolute cell concentrations or systemic interactions among diverse leukocyte populations. This approach, while useful in certain contexts, fails to capture the dynamic and interconnected nature of immune responses, particularly in complex clinical settings such as cancer, acute inflammatory syndromes, or immunosuppressive conditions. Moreover, conventional techniques often involve sample processing steps—such as density gradient centrifugation—that may alter cellular composition or viability, thereby introducing artifacts that compromise data integrity.

In clinical trials involving immunotherapies, this limitation becomes especially problematic. Despite significant advances in therapeutic modalities that modulate immune function—ranging from checkpoint inhibitors to adoptive cell transfer—the lack of robust, reproducible biomarkers correlating immune status with clinical outcomes remains a major obstacle. Investigators frequently observe discordance between expected immunological changes and actual patient responses, suggesting that current paradigms for immune monitoring are insufficiently comprehensive. For instance, a patient may exhibit an increase in a particular immune subset deemed “favorable” by conventional metrics, yet still experience disease progression or poor survival. Conversely, some patients with seemingly “unfavorable” immune profiles may respond exceptionally well to treatment. These inconsistencies underscore the need for a more holistic, quantitative, and systems-level approach to immune assessment.

Efforts to address this gap have included the application of high-dimensional technologies such as gene expression microarrays, cytokine profiling, multiplexed immunohistochemistry, and high-parameter flow cytometry. While these tools offer greater resolution, they often remain siloed within specific analytical frameworks that do not integrate across modalities or account for the physical reality of immune cell interactions in vivo. Critically, immune responses depend not only on the identity and functional state of individual cell types but also on their spatial proximity and likelihood of interaction—factors inherently governed by absolute cell concentrations within a given biological compartment, such as peripheral blood. Thus, a methodology that quantifies immune cells in absolute terms (e.g., cells per microliter of whole blood) and evaluates their collective configuration as an integrated system holds significant promise for improving prognostic accuracy, guiding therapeutic decisions, and uncovering novel biological relationships.

Prior art in immune monitoring has largely neglected this systems perspective. Most clinical flow cytometry protocols report immune subsets as percentages of parent populations, which can be misleading in conditions characterized by leukopenia, lymphopenia, or myeloid expansion—common features in advanced malignancy, sepsis, or post-chemotherapy states. Furthermore, existing classification schemes rarely consider cross-lineage interactions or emergent properties arising from the combined presence or absence of multiple cell types. As a result, the field lacks a standardized framework for defining what constitutes a “normal” versus “dysregulated” immune state across diverse pathologies.

The present invention arises from the recognition that immunity is best understood not as a collection of isolated cell types but as a dynamic network whose functional output depends on the absolute abundance and relational architecture of its components. By leveraging quantitative whole blood flow cytometry—bypassing sample manipulation that could distort cellular representation—and applying unsupervised bioinformatic clustering to absolute cell counts, it becomes possible to define reproducible, disease-transcendent immune configurations, herein termed “immune profiles.” These profiles reflect stable, systemic states of immune organization that correlate with clinical outcomes independent of underlying diagnosis. The invention thus provides a transformative methodology for immune phenotyping that overcomes the limitations of prior approaches and enables precision immunology in both research and clinical practice.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel, comprehensive method for characterizing the human immune system through the quantitative analysis of absolute immune cell concentrations in peripheral whole blood, followed by unsupervised hierarchical clustering to identify reproducible immune configurations, referred to herein as “immune profiles.” Unlike conventional immune monitoring techniques that rely on relative frequencies or require extensive sample processing, the disclosed method utilizes direct staining of unmanipulated whole blood with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies targeting defined immune markers, coupled with calibrated flow cytometry to determine absolute cell counts per unit volume (e.g., cells/μL). This approach preserves the native cellular composition of the sample and enables accurate enumeration of key leukocyte subsets, including but not limited to T lymphocytes (CD3+, CD4+, CD8+), B lymphocytes (CD19+), natural killer (NK) cells (CD56+CD16+), regulatory T cells (CD4+CD25+CD127lo), granulocytes, monocytes (including CD14+HLA-DR+ and CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg subsets), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (Lineage−HLA-DR−CD33+).

The absolute cell counts for each immune marker are normalized relative to the mean values observed in a reference cohort of healthy volunteers, thereby establishing a standardized scale for inter-individual comparison. These normalized values are then subjected to unsupervised agglomerative hierarchical clustering using Euclidean distance and average linkage criteria, which groups individuals with similar immune phenotypes into discrete immune profiles. Each immune profile represents a distinct systemic configuration of the immune system, characterized by unique combinations of elevated, suppressed, or balanced immune cell populations. Critically, these profiles are not disease-specific; rather, they are shared across diverse pathological conditions—including glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), renal cell carcinoma (RCC), ovarian cancer (OVA), and acute lung injury (ALI)—and reflect fundamental states of immune organization that transcend etiology.

The invention further discloses that these immune profiles are strongly associated with clinical outcomes. Specifically, patients whose immune phenotypes cluster within profiles resembling those of healthy volunteers (e.g., Profile 1 and Profile 2 as defined herein) exhibit significantly longer overall survival compared to those assigned to other profiles, regardless of their underlying disease. This prognostic power is independent of age, treatment history, or specific diagnosis, demonstrating that immune status—as captured by the disclosed profiling method—is a primary determinant of patient trajectory.

Additionally, the hierarchical clustering process reveals novel biological relationships among immune cell populations. For example, the analysis identifies that CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes—a known immunosuppressive subset—cluster independently from total monocytes and exhibit an inverse correlation with CD4+ T cell counts. This observation led to the development of a novel prognostic biomarker: the ratio of absolute CD4+ T cells to CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes (cells/μL). A low ratio (e.g., <2.0) is strongly predictive of poor survival in cancer patients, whereas a high ratio correlates with favorable outcomes. This ratio integrates information from two disparate lineages—adaptive T cells and innate myeloid cells—thereby capturing a critical axis of immune dysregulation.

The invention also encompasses the reconstruction of the average leukocyte composition for each immune profile, enabling visualization of both absolute and relative shifts in cellular abundance. Pie charts and scatter plots derived from principal component analysis illustrate how total leukocyte and peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) counts vary systematically across profiles, providing intuitive representations of immune status.

In summary, the present invention offers a paradigm-shifting approach to immune monitoring that is quantitative, unbiased, reproducible, and clinically actionable. It enables the identification of shared immune states across diseases, facilitates risk stratification, reveals previously unrecognized cellular relationships, and provides a foundation for the development of immune profile-guided therapies. The method is readily implementable in clinical laboratories using standard flow cytometers and commercially available reagents, making it suitable for widespread adoption in both research and routine patient care.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for comprehensively characterizing the human immune system by determining the absolute concentrations of multiple immune cell populations in peripheral whole blood and using unsupervised bioinformatic clustering to define reproducible immune configurations, termed “immune profiles,” that correlate with clinical outcomes independent of underlying disease. The method overcomes significant limitations of prior art by avoiding sample manipulation, reporting absolute cell counts, and analyzing the immune system as an integrated network rather than a collection of isolated subsets.

In one embodiment, the method comprises collecting a peripheral whole blood sample from a human subject. The sample is obtained via standard venipuncture and is processed without density gradient centrifugation, red blood cell lysis prior to staining, or other manipulations that may selectively deplete or activate cell populations. Instead, fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies specific for a panel of immune markers are added directly to a defined volume (e.g., 50–100 μL) of whole blood. The antibody panel includes markers that define major leukocyte lineages and functionally relevant subsets. Exemplary markers include, but are not limited to: CD45 (pan-leukocyte), CD3 (T cells), CD4 (helper T cells), CD8 (cytotoxic T cells), CD19 (B cells), CD56 and CD16 (NK cells), CD25 and CD127 (for identification of regulatory T cells as CD4+CD25+CD127lo), CD14 (monocytes), HLA-DR (major histocompatibility complex class II), CD33 (myeloid lineage), and lineage cocktail markers (e.g., CD3, CD19, CD56) to exclude mature lymphoid and NK cells when identifying immature or suppressive myeloid populations such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs).

In a preferred embodiment, absolute cell counts are determined using calibrated flow cytometry. This is achieved by employing internal counting standards, such as BD TruCount™ tubes, which contain a known number of fluorescent beads. When a fixed volume of stained whole blood is acquired on a flow cytometer, the ratio of cell events to bead events allows calculation of absolute cell concentration (cells/μL). Alternatively, volumetric acquisition systems that measure exact sample volumes can be used. The use of absolute counts eliminates dependence on assumptions about total white blood cell counts or lymphocyte percentages, which may be highly variable in diseased states.

Following staining, red blood cells are lysed using a commercially available lysing solution (e.g., BD FACS™ Lysing Solution) under standardized conditions to preserve leukocyte integrity. The sample is then washed and optionally fixed in paraformaldehyde to stabilize fluorescence signals. Data acquisition is performed on a calibrated flow cytometer (e.g., BD FACSCalibur™), with daily quality control to ensure instrument stability. Data analysis is conducted using standard flow cytometry software (e.g., FlowJo, CellQuest) with predefined, consensus gating strategies to ensure reproducibility. Gating hierarchies are established to first identify broad populations (e.g., lymphocytes, monocytes, granulocytes) based on forward and side scatter, followed by sequential gating on specific markers to define subsets. For example, regulatory T cells are identified as CD4+ cells that are CD25+ and CD127lo; CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes are defined as CD14+ cells with low or absent HLA-DR expression.

The absolute cell counts (cells/μL) for each immune marker are then compiled for each subject. To enable cross-subject comparison, these values are normalized relative to a reference cohort of healthy volunteers. Specifically, the count for each marker in a given subject is divided by the mean count of that same marker in the healthy volunteer cohort. This yields a dimensionless “marker ratio” that indicates how many times higher or lower a subject’s value is compared to the healthy average. Normalization accounts for inter-assay variability and establishes a common scale for clustering.

The normalized marker ratios for all subjects—comprising both healthy volunteers and patients with various diseases—are imported into bioinformatic software capable of unsupervised hierarchical clustering (e.g., Partek Genomics Suite). The data are log-transformed to reduce skewness and improve clustering performance. Hierarchical clustering is performed using agglomerative average linkage with Euclidean distance as the similarity metric. This algorithm iteratively merges the most similar subjects (or clusters) until a dendrogram representing the full dataset is constructed. Immune profiles are defined as branches of the dendrogram that contain a minimum number of subjects (e.g., at least seven) and exhibit minimal internal branching, indicating high homogeneity within the group.

Principal component analysis (PCA) is optionally performed to visualize the major sources of variation in the dataset. PCA reduces dimensionality by identifying orthogonal axes (principal components) that capture the greatest variance in the data. Scatter plots of subjects along the first two or three principal components often reveal clear separation by immune profile, confirming the robustness of the clustering.

Each immune profile is characterized by its unique combination of elevated, suppressed, or normal immune cell populations. For instance, one profile may exhibit high granulocytes, high monocytes, and low lymphocytes, while another shows balanced counts across lineages. Critically, these profiles are not defined by disease diagnosis but emerge from the data in an unbiased manner. Healthy volunteers typically cluster into one or two profiles (e.g., Profile 1 and Profile 2), while patients distribute across multiple profiles depending on their immune status.

The clinical utility of the method is demonstrated by its ability to predict patient outcomes. In studies involving cancer patients (GBM, NHL, RCC) and critically ill patients (ALI), assignment to an immune profile resembling that of healthy volunteers is associated with significantly longer overall survival. Multivariate Cox regression models, adjusted for age and stratified by disease, confirm that immune profile is an independent prognostic factor. This finding suggests that the method captures a fundamental aspect of immune competence that influences disease trajectory regardless of etiology.

Furthermore, the clustering process reveals novel biological insights. Immune markers that co-vary across profiles are likely subject to shared regulatory mechanisms, while those that segregate independently may be controlled by distinct pathways. For example, CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes—known mediators of immunosuppression—cluster separately from total monocytes and show a strong inverse correlation with CD4+ T cell counts. This relationship is leveraged to create a composite biomarker: the CD4+/CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg ratio. Using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis or the Contal and O’Quigley method, an optimal cut-point (e.g., ratio = 2.0) is established to dichotomize patients into high- and low-risk groups. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrates that patients with a ratio above the cut-point have markedly better survival than those below it, validating the ratio as a powerful prognostic tool.

The method also enables reconstruction of the average immune landscape for each profile. By aggregating absolute cell counts across all subjects within a profile, the total leukocyte and PBMC concentrations can be calculated and visualized using pie charts or bar graphs. These visualizations highlight both quantitative (absolute cell numbers) and qualitative (relative proportions) differences between profiles. For example, one profile may have twice the total leukocytes of another but a lower proportion of lymphocytes, indicating a myeloid-skewed inflammation.

The invention is not limited to the specific markers or diseases described herein. The panel of immune markers can be expanded to include additional subsets (e.g., dendritic cells, γδ T cells, exhausted T cells) or functional markers (e.g., PD-1, Ki-67). Similarly, the method can be applied to any condition where immune status is relevant, including autoimmune diseases, infectious diseases, transplantation, and vaccine responses. Longitudinal sampling can track immune profile transitions over time, offering insights into immune dynamics during therapy or recovery.

In implementation, the method requires only standard clinical laboratory equipment: a flow cytometer, refrigerated centrifuge, and basic reagents. Antibody panels can be customized but should include core markers that define major lineages and key regulatory subsets. Data analysis pipelines can be automated to facilitate high-throughput profiling in multicenter studies.

Overall, the disclosed method represents a significant advance in immune monitoring by providing a quantitative, systems-level view of human immunity that is both biologically insightful and clinically actionable.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Identification of Immune Profiles in Glioblastoma and Healthy Volunteers**

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 27 newly diagnosed glioblastoma (GBM) patients and 40 age-matched healthy volunteers (HV). Samples were processed using the method described herein: 100 μL of whole blood was stained with a 4-color antibody panel including CD3-FITC, CD4-PE, CD8-PerCP, and CD19-APC, and absolute T, B, and NK cell counts were determined using BD TruCount™ tubes. Additional markers (CD25, CD127, CD14, HLA-DR) were measured as percentages of parent populations and converted to absolute counts using the TruCount-derived lymphocyte and monocyte totals. All values were normalized to the HV mean and subjected to hierarchical clustering.

Three immune profiles emerged: Profile 1 contained 32 HV, 5 untreated GBM patients, and 5 dexamethasone (DEX)-treated GBM patients; Profile 2 contained 8 HV and 4 untreated GBM patients; Profile 3 contained 13 DEX-treated GBM patients exclusively (p < 0.0001, Fisher’s exact test). This segregation by DEX treatment confirmed the method’s sensitivity to known immunosuppressive interventions. Profile 3 exhibited markedly reduced lymphocytes and elevated CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes, consistent with glucocorticoid-induced immunosuppression.

**Example 2: Cross-Disease Immune Profiling Reveals Shared Immune States**

To identify immune profiles transcending specific diagnoses, data from 40 HV, 27 GBM, 30 NHL, 25 RCC, 20 OVA, and 25 ALI patients were combined (n = 167 total). Hierarchical clustering identified five major immune profiles (Profiles 1–5), each containing at least 10 subjects. Profile 1 and 2 predominantly included HV (85% of HV clustered here), while Profiles 3–5 were enriched for diseased subjects. Notably, each disease showed a distinct distribution across profiles: GBM patients were mostly in Profile 3; NHL and RCC in Profile 4; ALI in Profile 2 or 3; OVA distributed broadly.

Principal component analysis confirmed separation by profile, not disease. Profile 3 was characterized by elevated granulocytes, monocytes, lymphocytes, Tregs, and CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes. Profile 4 showed high granulocytes and monocytes but low lymphocytes and CD4+ T cells. Profile 5 exhibited profound leukopenia. These findings demonstrate that diverse diseases can converge on common immune configurations.

**Example 3: Immune Profiles Predict Survival Independent of Diagnosis**

Survival data were available for 76 cancer patients (GBM, NHL, RCC). Patients in Profile 1 or 2 (resembling HV) had a median overall survival of 915 days (n = 42), compared to 379 days for those in Profiles 3–5 (n = 34; p = 0.009, log-rank test). Multivariate Cox regression, adjusting for age and cancer type, confirmed immune profile as an independent prognostic factor (hazard ratio = 2.1, p = 0.012). This illustrates the method’s ability to stratify risk across heterogeneous cancer populations.

**Example 4: Discovery of the CD4+/CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg Ratio as a Prognostic Biomarker**

Analysis of clustering patterns revealed that CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg monocytes segregated distally from CD4+ T cells. Spearman correlation confirmed a significant inverse relationship (r = −0.3244, p < 0.001). The CD4+/CD14+HLA-DRlo/neg ratio was calculated for all cancer patients. Using a cut-point of 2.0 (determined by Contal and O’Quigley method), patients with a high ratio (n = 68) had a median survival of 30 months, versus 9 months for low-ratio patients (n = 39; p = 0.006, multivariate analysis). This ratio outperformed individual markers and provided a simple, clinically applicable biomarker.

**Example 5: Reconstruction of Immune Landscapes by Profile**

Average absolute cell counts were computed for each profile. Profile 3 had 1.9-fold higher total leukocytes than Profile 1 (p < 0.0001), driven by increases in granulocytes and monocytes. Conversely, Profile 5 had 58% fewer PBMCs than Profile 1 (p < 0.0001). Pie charts visualizing PBMC composition showed Profile 4 had a markedly reduced CD4+ T cell fraction, while Profile 3 had expanded Tregs. These reconstructions provide intuitive, quantitative summaries of immune status for each profile.

These examples collectively validate the invention’s core claims: that quantitative whole blood immune profiling identifies reproducible, cross-disease immune states that are prognostically significant and biologically informative.
