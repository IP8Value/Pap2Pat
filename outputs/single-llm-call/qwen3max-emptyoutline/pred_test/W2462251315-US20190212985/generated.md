# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The generation of high-quality random numbers has become increasingly critical across a broad spectrum of modern technological domains, including but not limited to cryptography, scientific simulations such as Monte Carlo methods, secure communications, financial modeling, and even regulated applications such as online gambling. In particular, cryptographic systems—ranging from symmetric key encryption to public-key infrastructure and digital signatures—rely fundamentally on the availability of truly unpredictable and unbiased random numbers for the secure generation of keys, initialization vectors, nonces, and other cryptographic primitives. The security of such systems is only as strong as the randomness underlying their operations; any predictability or bias in the random number source can be exploited by adversaries to compromise confidentiality, integrity, or authenticity. Consequently, the demand for robust, high-throughput, and scalable sources of true randomness has intensified alongside the proliferation of digital communication and computation.

Traditional approaches to random number generation have largely relied on pseudo-random number generators (PRNGs), which are deterministic algorithms that produce sequences of numbers that appear statistically random but are entirely determined by an initial seed value. While PRNGs are computationally efficient and suitable for many non-security-critical applications, they are inherently unsuitable for cryptographic purposes unless seeded with sufficient entropy from a true random source. Even then, the quality of the output depends critically on the unpredictability of the seed. True random number generators (TRNGs), by contrast, derive randomness from physical processes that are fundamentally non-deterministic. Among these, quantum mechanical processes stand out as the only known sources of intrinsic randomness in nature, as dictated by the principles of quantum theory—specifically, the indeterminacy of measurement outcomes for quantum states.

Over the past several decades, various hardware-based TRNGs leveraging quantum phenomena have been developed. One prominent class exploits the quantum nature of photon emission and detection. For example, single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) operating in Geiger mode have been used to detect individual photons emitted from a light source, where the timing or presence of a photon arrival is treated as a random event. Another approach involves homodyne detection of vacuum fluctuations in quantum optics, which also yields quantum-random outcomes. More recently, conventional CMOS image sensors (CIS) have been repurposed for random number generation by capturing the statistical variation in photon arrivals under low-light conditions. However, while these technologies demonstrate the feasibility of optical quantum random number generation (QRNG), they suffer from significant limitations in terms of scalability, power consumption, data rate, and randomness quality.

Conventional CMOS image sensors, for instance, are not designed for single-photon sensitivity. Their read noise typically exceeds one electron root mean square (e− r.m.s.), which obscures the discrete nature of photon arrivals and blends the underlying Poisson statistics into a near-Gaussian distribution. This degradation in signal fidelity reduces the entropy per bit of the raw output and necessitates aggressive post-processing via randomness extractors, thereby diminishing the effective output data rate. Moreover, thermal noise sources such as dark current and 1/f noise introduce correlations and biases that further compromise randomness unless carefully mitigated through cooling or complex calibration—both of which are impractical in consumer-grade or portable devices.

SPAD-based QRNGs, while capable of true single-photon detection, face different challenges. They require high bias voltages (often 20–30 V) to operate above the breakdown voltage, which increases power consumption and complicates integration with standard low-voltage CMOS circuitry. Additionally, SPADs suffer from after-pulsing and dead time effects, which introduce temporal correlations between successive detections and reduce the maximum sustainable count rate. Pixel sizes in SPAD arrays are relatively large (typically tens of micrometers), limiting spatial density and thus the total parallelism achievable in a given chip area. As a result, despite their high per-pixel randomness quality, SPAD-based systems struggle to scale to the gigabit-per-second throughput levels required by emerging applications such as post-quantum cryptography or real-time secure communications in data centers.

These limitations highlight the need for a new class of quantum random number generator that combines the single-photon sensitivity of SPADs with the scalability, low power, and CMOS compatibility of conventional image sensors. Such a solution must exhibit deep sub-electron read noise to resolve individual photoelectrons, operate at high frame rates to enable high data throughput, and integrate millions to billions of independent randomness sources on a single chip to support massive parallelism. Furthermore, the system should be manufacturable using standard semiconductor fabrication processes to ensure cost-effectiveness and widespread adoption.

It is in this context that the Quanta Image Sensor (QIS) emerges as a compelling candidate. Originally proposed in 2005 as a “digital film” sensor, the QIS represents a paradigm shift in imaging technology by treating each pixel—not as an analog integrator of charge—but as a binary or multi-bit photon counter. Each pixel, referred to as a “jot,” is engineered to detect the presence or absence of individual photons with high fidelity. With sub-micron pitch dimensions, a QIS can incorporate over one billion jots on a single chip, enabling unprecedented spatial resolution and parallelism. Critically, the QIS achieves deep sub-electron read noise—less than 0.5 e− r.m.s., and ideally below 0.15 e− r.m.s.—which preserves the quantized nature of photon arrivals and enables accurate photon counting even at room temperature. This capability, combined with high-speed readout architectures (e.g., 1000 frames per second) and ultra-low power consumption (as low as 2.5 picojoules per bit), positions the QIS as a uniquely suited platform for high-performance, scalable, and energy-efficient quantum random number generation.

Despite its potential, the application of QIS technology to QRNG has not been previously realized or disclosed in the prior art. Existing literature on QIS focuses primarily on imaging applications, such as low-light photography, scientific imaging, and medical diagnostics, with no recognition of its utility in cryptographic randomness generation. Similarly, prior QRNG implementations using optical detectors have not leveraged the specific architectural and operational advantages of the QIS, particularly its ability to generate high-entropy bits from the binary discrimination of zero versus one-or-more photoelectrons under optimized quanta exposure conditions. The present invention bridges this gap by providing a novel method and system for generating quantum random numbers using a QIS, wherein the intrinsic randomness of photon emission is harnessed through precise control of illumination, readout thresholding, and entropy extraction to produce certified, high-throughput, and scalable true random bits.

## SUMMARY OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The present invention provides a quantum random number generator (QRNG) based on a Quanta Image Sensor (QIS), which leverages the fundamental quantum randomness of photon emission and detection to generate high-quality, high-throughput, and scalable true random numbers. In accordance with some embodiments, the QRNG system comprises a QIS device having an array of photon-counting pixels, each referred to as a “jot,” wherein each jot is configured to detect incident photons and output a digital signal corresponding to the number of photoelectrons generated during a defined exposure period. The QIS is characterized by deep sub-electron read noise—preferably less than 0.25 e− r.m.s., and more preferably less than 0.15 e− r.m.s.—which enables the resolution of discrete photon arrival events and preserves the quantum-statistical nature of the photocurrent.

In operation, the QIS is illuminated by a stable light source, such as a light-emitting diode (LED) or laser diode, operated under conditions that yield a controlled average number of photoelectrons per jot per frame, referred to as the quanta exposure \( H \). The quanta exposure is deliberately set to a value that maximizes the entropy of the binary output derived from the jot signals. Specifically, in some embodiments, \( H \) is selected to be approximately 0.7 photoelectrons per jot per frame, which, when combined with a readout threshold \( U_t \) set at approximately 0.5 e−, results in a near-equal probability of observing zero photoelectrons (“0” state) versus one or more photoelectrons (“1” state). This configuration ensures that the raw binary sequence exhibits maximum Shannon entropy, approaching 1 bit per symbol, while simultaneously maintaining robustness against minor fluctuations in light intensity—a critical requirement for long-term stability in practical deployments.

The analog signal from each jot is read out through a low-noise readout circuit, which may include correlated double sampling (CDS) to suppress reset noise and other fixed-pattern noise components. The readout signal is normalized by the conversion gain of the jot to express the signal in units of electrons, and a binary decision is made by comparing the normalized signal to the predetermined threshold \( U_t \). The resulting stream of binary bits constitutes a raw random sequence whose entropy is quantified based on the known statistical model of the photon detection process, which combines Poisson-distributed photon arrivals with Gaussian-distributed read noise. The minimum quantum entropy of the raw sequence is computed using established information-theoretic methods, and this entropy value is used to determine the appropriate compression ratio for a subsequent randomness extraction step.

In some embodiments, the raw binary sequence is processed by a non-deterministic randomness extractor based on Universal-2 hash functions. This extractor takes a block of \( n \) raw bits and compresses them into \( m \) output bits, where \( m < n \), such that the output sequence is statistically indistinguishable from a perfectly uniform random string, up to a negligible deviation probability \( \varepsilon_{\text{hash}} \). The compression ratio \( n/m \) is chosen based on the measured or estimated entropy per raw bit to ensure that \( \varepsilon_{\text{hash}} \) is sufficiently small—for example, less than \( 2^{-100} \)—thereby guaranteeing cryptographic-grade randomness even under adversarial assumptions. The extracted random bits are then subjected to standardized statistical test suites, such as the NIST Special Publication 800-22, to verify compliance with randomness specifications.

The QRNG system of the present invention offers several advantages over existing technologies. First, by utilizing the QIS’s single-photon sensitivity and deep sub-electron read noise, it achieves higher entropy per raw bit compared to conventional CMOS image sensors, reducing the need for aggressive post-processing and thereby increasing the effective output data rate. Second, the QIS operates at low supply voltages compatible with standard CMOS processes, eliminating the need for high-voltage biasing and enabling seamless integration into consumer electronics, mobile devices, and embedded systems. Third, the massively parallel architecture of the QIS—with hundreds of millions to billions of independent jots—allows for extreme scalability in throughput; by reading out multiple jots simultaneously, the system can generate gigabits per second of random data without increasing the per-pixel readout speed. Fourth, the system is inherently resistant to classical noise sources such as dark current and 1/f noise due to the low operating temperature requirements and the use of entropy-aware thresholding that minimizes sensitivity to thermal drift.

In further embodiments, the QRNG system may include calibration circuitry to dynamically adjust the light intensity or readout threshold in response to environmental changes, ensuring consistent entropy performance over time and across operating conditions. Additionally, the system may incorporate multiple independent QIS arrays or jots to provide redundancy and enhance the total entropy rate. The extracted random bits may be output through a secure interface compliant with cryptographic standards such as those defined by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) or the Common Criteria for Information Technology Security Evaluation.

Overall, the present invention provides a novel, practical, and high-performance solution for quantum random number generation that addresses the shortcomings of prior art technologies while offering unprecedented scalability, efficiency, and randomness quality. By harnessing the quantum randomness of light through the advanced photon-counting capabilities of the QIS, the invention enables the deployment of true random number generators in a wide range of applications where security, speed, and reliability are paramount.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF SOME EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to a quantum random number generator (QRNG) that utilizes a Quanta Image Sensor (QIS) as the primary source of entropy. The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, including structural, functional, and operational aspects, while emphasizing the novel integration of QIS technology into the field of cryptographic randomness generation. It should be understood that the embodiments described herein are illustrative and not exhaustive; numerous variations and modifications will be apparent to those skilled in the art without departing from the scope of the invention as defined by the appended claims.

A Quanta Image Sensor (QIS) is a specialized solid-state image sensor composed of a dense array of nanoscale pixels, each referred to as a “jot.” Unlike conventional CMOS image sensors (CIS), which integrate photocurrent over an exposure period and output an analog voltage proportional to the total charge, each jot in a QIS is designed to function as a photon counter. This is achieved through the combination of extremely small pixel capacitance, high conversion gain, and deep sub-electron read noise (DSERN). The conversion gain, expressed in volts per electron (V/e−), determines how much the output voltage changes per unit of charge; a high conversion gain amplifies the signal from a single photoelectron above the noise floor, enabling its detection. Read noise, which arises from thermal and flicker noise in the readout electronics, must be sufficiently low—ideally below 0.15 e− r.m.s.—to prevent the smearing of discrete photon peaks in the output signal distribution. When these conditions are met, the histogram of jot outputs under low-light illumination exhibits distinct peaks corresponding to 0, 1, 2, … photoelectrons, a phenomenon known as the photon counting histogram (PCH).

In the context of the present invention, the QIS is not used for imaging but as a massively parallel array of quantum random bit generators. Each jot independently samples the quantum-random process of photon arrival during a defined exposure window. The randomness stems from the fact that, under coherent or thermal illumination, the number of photons arriving at a jot in a given time interval follows a Poisson distribution governed by the average quanta exposure \( H \), defined as the expected number of photoelectrons collected per jot per frame. Because photon emission and absorption are quantum mechanical events, this process is fundamentally unpredictable and serves as a source of true entropy.

To convert the analog jot output into a binary random bit, a thresholding operation is applied. Specifically, the normalized readout signal \( U \) (in units of electrons) is compared to a threshold \( U_t \). If \( U < U_t \), the output is assigned a “0”; if \( U \geq U_t \), the output is assigned a “1”. The choice of \( U_t \) and \( H \) is critical to maximizing the entropy of the resulting binary sequence. Entropy, as defined by Shannon, quantifies the uncertainty in a random variable and is given by \( S = -p_0 \log_2 p_0 - p_1 \log_2 p_1 \), where \( p_0 \) and \( p_1 \) are the probabilities of “0” and “1”, respectively. Maximum entropy (1 bit) occurs when \( p_0 = p_1 = 0.5 \).

However, due to the presence of read noise, the ideal condition \( p_0 = p_1 = 0.5 \) cannot be achieved for arbitrary \( H \) and \( U_t \). The readout signal PDF is a convolution of the Poisson distribution for photon arrivals and a Gaussian distribution representing read noise. Through analysis and empirical validation, it has been determined that setting \( H \approx 0.7 \) and \( U_t = 0.5 \) e− yields a near-optimal trade-off between entropy and stability. At this operating point, the threshold lies in the valley between the 0-photoelectron and 1-photoelectron peaks of the PCH, making the binary decision robust against small variations in \( H \) caused by light source drift or temperature changes. For example, a 2% fluctuation in \( H \) around 0.7 results in minimal change in entropy, whereas the same fluctuation at \( H = 1.2 \) (with \( U_t = 1.0 \)) causes a significant entropy drop due to the threshold being positioned at a peak rather than a valley.

In a prototype implementation, a pump-gate (PG) jot device fabricated using standard CMOS processes was employed. The PG jot achieved a read noise of 0.24 e− r.m.s. with single correlated double sampling (CDS) at room temperature. A 3×3 array of green LEDs served as the light source, positioned 2 cm from the sensor chip. The LED intensity was controlled by a precision voltage source to maintain a stable average photon flux. During calibration, 20,000 samples were collected from a single jot to estimate \( H \) using the PCH method, and the threshold \( U_t \) was set to the median of the sample distribution, which corresponded to 27.5 digital numbers (DN) or 0.5 e−. Subsequent data collection involved reading the same jot at 10 ksamples/s, yielding a raw binary sequence.

The raw sequence exhibited a minimum quantum entropy of 0.9845 bits per bit, calculated using the formula \( S_{\text{min}} = -\log_2(\max(p_0, p_1)) \). This high entropy value reflects the effectiveness of the chosen \( H \) and \( U_t \) in balancing randomness and stability. To distill this raw sequence into cryptographically secure random bits, a non-deterministic randomness extractor based on Universal-2 hash functions was applied. The extractor uses a randomly generated matrix \( M \) of size \( n \times m \) (where \( n > m \)) to perform a linear transformation: \( y = x \cdot M \mod 2 \), where \( x \) is the input vector of \( n \) raw bits and \( y \) is the output vector of \( m \) extracted bits. The compression ratio \( n/m \) was set to 1.23 (i.e., \( m = 1024 \), \( n = 1260 \)) to ensure that the deviation probability \( \varepsilon_{\text{hash}} = 2^{-(\bar{S}n - m)/2} \) remained below \( 2^{-100} \), a level considered secure for all practical purposes.

The extracted random bits were subjected to the NIST SP 800-22 statistical test suite, which includes tests for frequency, runs, block frequency, cumulative sums, and serial correlation, among others. The QIS-based QRNG passed all tests, confirming the absence of detectable statistical biases or patterns. Over 200 hours of continuous operation, 500 megabytes of extracted random data were generated, demonstrating the system’s reliability and long-term stability.

Compared to SPAD-based QRNGs, the QIS approach offers superior scalability and power efficiency. SPAD arrays typically achieve data rates of 200–600 Mbits/s over several square millimeters of die area, whereas a QIS with one billion jots operating at 1000 fps can theoretically generate terabits per second of raw data. Even accounting for entropy extraction overhead, the effective throughput far exceeds that of SPADs. Moreover, the QIS operates at standard CMOS voltages (e.g., 1.8 V or 3.3 V), avoiding the high-voltage requirements of SPADs. Compared to CIS-based QRNGs, the QIS provides significantly higher entropy per bit due to its single-photon resolution, reducing the need for heavy post-processing and enabling higher net output rates.

In alternative embodiments, the QRNG system may employ multi-bit jots, where each jot outputs a 2-bit or 3-bit value indicating 0, 1, 2, or 3+ photoelectrons. In such cases, entropy extraction can be applied to the multi-bit symbols directly, or the symbols can be decomposed into multiple binary streams. Additionally, the system may utilize spatial or temporal oversampling—reading the same jot multiple times or aggregating outputs from neighboring jots—to increase entropy or mitigate localized defects.

The invention also contemplates integration with cryptographic modules, such as hardware security modules (HSMs) or trusted platform modules (TPMs), where the extracted random bits serve as seeds for PRNGs or direct inputs for key generation. The QIS QRNG can be implemented as a standalone chip or co-integrated with processing units on a system-on-chip (SoC), enabling on-demand randomness generation in smartphones, IoT devices, servers, and network appliances.

In summary, the detailed embodiments of the present invention demonstrate a practical, high-performance, and scalable quantum random number generator that leverages the unique photon-counting capabilities of the Quanta Image Sensor. By optimizing the operating parameters of quanta exposure and thresholding, and combining them with rigorous entropy extraction and statistical validation, the invention provides a robust solution for next-generation cryptographic and security applications.
