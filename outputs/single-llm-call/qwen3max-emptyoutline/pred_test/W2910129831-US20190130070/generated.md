# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of computational modeling and data-driven analytics in healthcare, and more specifically to systems and methods for constructing probabilistic disease progression models from longitudinal observational data. The invention is particularly applicable to chronic diseases that exhibit slow, multifaceted symptom progression over extended time periods, including but not limited to neurodegenerative disorders such as Huntington’s Disease (HD). The technical domain encompasses machine learning, statistical inference, continuous-time stochastic processes, and clinical informatics, with specific emphasis on the integration of heterogeneous clinical assessments into unified progression frameworks that can capture the complex temporal dynamics of disease evolution. The invention provides a novel framework based on Continuous-Time Hidden Markov Models (CTHMMs) that enables the identification of discrete yet clinically meaningful disease states, estimation of transition probabilities between these states, and individualized patient staging across the entire disease trajectory—including both premanifest and manifest phases. This approach addresses critical limitations in existing clinical staging systems, which often rely on single-domain biomarkers, subjective diagnostic criteria, or are restricted to post-diagnosis periods, thereby offering a more comprehensive, objective, and temporally granular representation of disease progression. The technical field further extends to applications in personalized medicine, clinical trial design, early intervention strategies, and digital health platforms where accurate modeling of disease trajectories is essential for risk stratification, prognosis, and therapeutic evaluation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive and data-driven framework for modeling the progression of chronic diseases using longitudinal observational data, particularly suited for conditions lacking well-established biomarkers or staging systems. At the core of the invention is a two-stage methodology based on a Continuous-Time Hidden Markov Model (CTHMM) that overcomes the inherent challenges of real-world clinical data—namely, irregular observation times, incomplete individual trajectories, high-dimensional and heterogeneous symptom manifestations, and the absence of ground-truth disease labels. In the first stage, the optimal number of latent disease states is determined through a data-driven grid-search procedure that evaluates model fit on held-out test data using log-likelihood as a fitness criterion. In the second stage, a CTHMM is trained with the selected number of states, incorporating domain-specific constraints on the transition structure to reflect biological plausibility—for instance, enforcing unidirectional or limited-order progression in irreversible neurodegenerative conditions. The model assumes that the underlying disease process evolves continuously in time according to a Markovian generator matrix, while observed clinical features at discrete time points are conditionally independent emissions from Gaussian distributions parameterized by state-specific means and variances. Through an Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, the invention simultaneously estimates the hidden state sequences for each patient, the transition dynamics between states, and the emission parameters that map latent states to observable clinical manifestations. The resulting disease progression model yields a finite set of interpretable disease states that span the entire course of the illness—from pre-symptomatic to advanced stages—each characterized by multidimensional symptom profiles across motor, cognitive, and functional domains. Moreover, the model provides quantitative estimates of expected state durations, annual transition probabilities, and individualized staging trajectories, enabling nuanced insights into disease heterogeneity and progression patterns. When applied to an integrated dataset of Huntington’s Disease Gene Expansion Carriers (HDGECs) derived from four large-scale observational studies, the invention identified a nine-state progression model that reveals three distinct phases: Prodromal (states 1–2), Transition (states 3–5), and Manifest (states 6–9). This model not only aligns with established clinical knowledge—such as the long premanifest period and post-diagnosis life expectancy—but also uncovers previously underappreciated dynamics, including early functional decline in occupational capacity, non-monotonic chorea progression, and potential branching pathways during the critical transition to motor diagnosis. Unlike conventional staging systems like the Shoulson and Fahn stages, which are limited to post-diagnosis functional capacity and ignore premanifest changes, the invention offers a holistic, continuous, and multidimensional view of disease evolution. The framework is generalizable to other chronic conditions, provided appropriate clinical constraints are imposed on the transition structure and relevant clinical domains are selected. Ultimately, the invention serves as a powerful tool for enhancing disease understanding, refining diagnostic criteria, enabling early intervention, optimizing patient recruitment for clinical trials, and supporting personalized care planning through precise, dynamic, and data-informed disease staging.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention addresses a critical gap in the management and understanding of chronic progressive diseases—particularly those that are rare, multifactorial, or lack definitive biomarkers—by providing a robust, scalable, and clinically interpretable method for constructing probabilistic disease progression models from real-world longitudinal data. Chronic diseases such as Huntington’s Disease (HD), Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and certain forms of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) are characterized by slow, insidious onset and heterogeneous symptom trajectories that unfold over years or even decades. Traditional approaches to modeling such progression often rely on cross-sectional snapshots, linear regression on single endpoints, or staging systems derived from expert consensus rather than empirical data. These methods fail to capture the dynamic, nonlinear, and multidimensional nature of disease evolution, especially during the crucial premanifest phase when early intervention may be most effective. The invention overcomes these limitations through a novel application of Continuous-Time Hidden Markov Models (CTHMMs) tailored to the unique challenges of observational healthcare data.

At the foundation of the invention is the recognition that disease progression, while continuous in biological reality, can be effectively approximated by a finite number of discrete, yet clinically meaningful, latent states. Each state represents a prototypical configuration of symptoms across multiple domains—such as motor function, cognitive ability, and daily living activities—and transitions between states occur stochastically over time according to a continuous-time Markov process. The key innovation lies in the integration of temporal continuity, multidimensional symptomatology, and data-driven state discovery within a single probabilistic framework. Unlike discrete-time Hidden Markov Models (HMMs), which assume fixed observation intervals and cannot naturally handle irregularly spaced clinical visits, the CTHMM explicitly models the underlying process as evolving in continuous time, with observations occurring at arbitrary time points. This is essential for real-world datasets derived from electronic health records (EHRs) or observational registries, where patient visit schedules are dictated by clinical need rather than experimental protocol.

The invention proceeds in two principal stages. The first stage involves determining the optimal number of latent disease states, denoted as \( M \). For well-characterized diseases with established staging systems—such as chronic kidney disease with its five-stage classification based on glomerular filtration rate—\( M \) may be set a priori. However, for less understood or rare conditions like HD, where no universally accepted staging system exists for the premanifest phase, the invention employs a data-driven model selection procedure. Specifically, the available cohort is partitioned into training and testing subsets. A series of candidate CTHMMs, each with a different value of \( M \) (e.g., ranging from 6 to 12), is trained on the training data. Each model is then evaluated on the held-out test set by computing the log-likelihood of the observed data under the model. The value of \( M \) that maximizes this out-of-sample log-likelihood is selected as optimal, balancing model complexity against explanatory power and avoiding overfitting. This approach ensures that the number of states is grounded in the statistical structure of the data rather than arbitrary clinical conventions.

Once \( M \) is determined, the second stage constructs the full disease progression model. The CTHMM is defined by three sets of parameters: (1) an initial state probability vector \( \pi \in \mathbb{R}^M \), representing the distribution of patients across states at baseline; (2) a transition generator matrix \( Q \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times M} \), where off-diagonal elements \( Q_{i,j} \) (for \( i \neq j \)) denote the instantaneous transition rate from state \( i \) to state \( j \), and diagonal elements are set such that each row sums to zero; and (3) emission parameters that link latent states to observed clinical features. In the preferred embodiment, the observed features are assumed to follow independent Gaussian distributions conditional on the latent state, with state-specific means \( \mu_{m,k} \) and variances \( \sigma_{m,k}^2 \) for each feature \( k \) in state \( m \). However, the framework is flexible and can accommodate other exponential family distributions as needed.

A critical aspect of the invention is the incorporation of domain knowledge through structural constraints on the transition matrix \( Q \). For irreversible neurodegenerative diseases like HD, where no known treatment can reverse or halt progression, the model enforces a forward-chain progression structure. In the specific implementation described herein, a second-order forward-chain constraint is applied: from any state \( i \), a patient may remain in state \( i \), transition to state \( i+1 \), or “skip” directly to state \( i+2 \), but cannot regress to earlier states or jump beyond \( i+2 \) in a single infinitesimal time step. The final state is designated as absorbing, reflecting the terminal nature of advanced disease. These constraints ensure that the learned model aligns with biological reality, improving interpretability and generalizability.

Parameter estimation is performed via the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm, which iteratively alternates between an E-step and an M-step. In the E-step, the posterior distribution over hidden state sequences is computed using the forward-backward algorithm adapted for continuous-time processes, along with expectations of sufficient statistics such as the expected number of transitions between states and the expected sojourn time in each state over observed intervals. In the M-step, the parameters \( Q \), \( \pi \), \( \mu \), and \( \sigma^2 \) are updated to maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood. The Viterbi algorithm is used to decode the most likely sequence of states for each individual patient, enabling personalized staging.

To handle the high dimensionality and heterogeneity of clinical assessments, the invention employs a preprocessing step based on Bayesian Latent Variable Analysis. Raw clinical scores—such as those from the Unified Huntington’s Disease Rating Scale (UHDRS)—are often numerous, correlated, and subject to missingness. Instead of using all raw features directly, the invention extracts a small number of latent factors from each major clinical domain (e.g., motor, cognitive, functional). In the HD application, three leading factors per domain are retained, ensuring balanced representation across domains and reducing noise and redundancy. These latent factors serve as the observed emission variables \( Z \) in the CTHMM, providing a low-dimensional, information-rich summary of the underlying disease process.

When applied to an integrated dataset comprising 16,653 Huntington’s Disease Gene Expansion Carriers (HDGECs) from four major observational studies (Enroll-HD, REGISTRY, TRACK-HD/TRACK-ON, and PREDICT-HD), the invention yielded a nine-state Integrated Huntington’s Disease Progression Model (IHDPM). Validation of the model selection procedure confirmed that \( M = 9 \) achieved the highest test-set log-likelihood. The resulting states were clinically interpretable and aligned with known HD natural history. States 1 and 2 corresponded to the Prodromal phase, where most patients had not yet reached motor onset (as defined by Diagnostic Confidence Level < 4). States 3 to 5 formed a Transition phase, during which the majority of patients crossed the threshold into clinical diagnosis. States 6 to 9 represented the Manifest phase, with progressive functional and cognitive decline.

The IHDPM revealed several novel insights. First, it demonstrated that functional impairment—specifically in occupational capacity—begins as early as state 2, well before motor diagnosis, suggesting that work-related difficulties may be an early warning sign. Second, during the Transition phase, the model identified a potential branching pattern: some patients progressed directly from state 3 to 5, bypassing state 4, indicating heterogeneity in the path to motor onset. Third, motor symptoms, particularly chorea, followed a non-monotonic trajectory, peaking around state 7 and then attenuating—a pattern consistent with clinical observations but not captured by linear models. Fourth, the model provided quantitative estimates of state durations: the Prodromal phase lasted approximately 19 years on average, while the Manifest phase (states 6–8) spanned about 10 years, aligning with known post-diagnosis life expectancy.

Individual patient staging under IHDPM offers significant advantages over traditional systems. For example, the Shoulson and Fahn stages classify all pre-diagnosis patients as “premanifest,” providing no granularity. In contrast, IHDPM can identify patients in state 3 as being on the cusp of motor onset, enabling timely intervention or trial enrollment. Similarly, within the manifest phase, IHDPM distinguishes subtle differences in symptom profiles that may inform care planning.

The invention is not limited to HD. The framework is general and can be adapted to other chronic conditions by selecting appropriate clinical domains, defining relevant constraints on \( Q \) (e.g., allowing recovery in relapsing-remitting diseases), and adjusting the number of states based on data characteristics. Potential applications include staging of pre-symptomatic Alzheimer’s using cognitive and imaging biomarkers, modeling progression in multiple sclerosis based on relapse frequency and disability scores, or tracking functional decline in heart failure using laboratory and echocardiographic data.

In summary, the invention provides a principled, flexible, and clinically actionable method for transforming sparse, irregular, and high-dimensional longitudinal data into a coherent model of disease progression. By integrating continuous-time dynamics, multidimensional symptomatology, and data-driven state discovery, it enables a more precise, individualized, and comprehensive understanding of chronic disease trajectories—ultimately supporting better diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic decision-making.
