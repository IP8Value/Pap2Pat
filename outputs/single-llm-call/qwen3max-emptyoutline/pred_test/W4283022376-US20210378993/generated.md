## TECHNICAL FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention resides in the field of pharmaceutical chemistry and therapeutic treatment of lysosomal storage disorders, specifically cystinosis. More particularly, the invention relates to the use of novel thiol-containing amide derivatives—namely N-acetylcysteine amide (NACA, also designated herein as NPI-001) and (2R,2R′)-3,3′-disulfanediyl bis(2-acetamidopropanamide) (diNACA, also designated herein as NPI-002)—as therapeutic agents for the depletion of intralysosomal cystine in patients suffering from cystinosis. The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising these compounds, methods of administration thereof, and their application in both systemic and ophthalmic treatment modalities. Cystinosis is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the CTNS gene, which encodes the lysosomal cystine transporter cystinosin, resulting in pathological accumulation of cystine crystals within lysosomes across multiple organ systems, most notably the kidneys and cornea. Current standard-of-care therapy relies exclusively on cysteamine-based formulations, which suffer from significant limitations including poor patient compliance due to gastrointestinal toxicity, foul odor, frequent dosing requirements, and inadequate penetration into avascular tissues such as the cornea. The compounds of the present invention address these unmet clinical needs by demonstrating superior cystine-depleting efficacy, enhanced cellular biocompatibility, and potential for improved pharmacokinetic profiles, thereby offering a novel therapeutic strategy for both nephropathic and ocular forms of cystinosis. The invention thus bridges a critical gap in the management of this debilitating disease by providing alternatives that are not only effective but also better tolerated and potentially more durable in action than existing therapies.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cystinosis is a prototypical lysosomal storage disease characterized by the abnormal accumulation of the amino acid cystine within lysosomes due to defective transport across the lysosomal membrane. This defect arises from mutations in the CTNS gene located on chromosome 17p13, which encodes cystinosin—a 367-amino acid integral membrane protein responsible for the proton-driven efflux of cystine from the lysosomal lumen into the cytosol. In the absence of functional cystinosin, cystine, the oxidized dimer of cysteine, crystallizes within lysosomes, leading to progressive cellular dysfunction and multiorgan damage. The disease manifests in three primary clinical phenotypes: nephropathic (infantile), intermediate (juvenile), and non-nephropathic (ocular) forms. The most severe and common form, nephropathic cystinosis, presents in infancy with renal Fanconi syndrome, failure to thrive, rickets, and photophobia, and if untreated, leads to end-stage renal disease by approximately ten years of age. With modern interventions including renal transplantation and cystine-depleting therapy, life expectancy has extended into the fourth or fifth decade, though patients continue to suffer from extrarenal complications affecting the eyes, thyroid, muscles, pancreas, and central nervous system. Corneal cystine crystal deposition is universal across all forms of cystinosis and is the hallmark of the ocular phenotype, causing photophobia, blepharospasm, and visual discomfort that significantly impair quality of life.

The only pharmacological treatment currently approved for cystinosis is cysteamine, an aminothiol that functions by entering the lysosome and reacting with cystine to form a mixed disulfide—cysteine-cysteamine—which is then exported via alternative lysosomal transporters, such as the PQLC2 lysine transporter. This mechanism effectively reduces intralysosomal cystine levels and delays disease progression. However, the clinical utility of cysteamine is severely hampered by several drawbacks. Orally administered formulations, such as CYSTAGON® (immediate-release cysteamine bitartrate) and PROCYSBI® (delayed-release cysteamine bitartrate), require dosing every six hours or every twelve hours, respectively, to maintain therapeutic plasma concentrations. This regimen is burdensome, especially for pediatric patients, and is frequently associated with poor adherence. Moreover, cysteamine possesses a highly unpleasant sulfurous odor and taste, often described as resembling rotten eggs, which further discourages consistent use. Gastrointestinal side effects—including nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and anorexia—are common and can be severe enough to necessitate dose reduction or discontinuation. Additionally, due to extensive first-pass metabolism in the liver, high oral doses are required to achieve effective systemic exposure, exacerbating toxicity concerns. Critically, oral cysteamine does not achieve sufficient concentrations in the cornea, an avascular tissue lacking direct blood supply, rendering it ineffective for treating ocular cystinosis.

To address corneal involvement, topical ophthalmic formulations such as CYSTARAN® and CYSTADROPS® (both containing cysteamine hydrochloride) have been developed. These eye drops must be administered hourly during waking hours—a demanding schedule that results in suboptimal compliance and inconsistent therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, the high frequency of instillation causes local irritation, conjunctival hyperemia, and punctate keratopathy, limiting tolerability. The instability of cysteamine in aqueous solution, its susceptibility to oxidation, and the need for refrigeration further complicate its practical use. Despite decades of reliance on cysteamine, these persistent limitations underscore a clear and urgent unmet medical need for alternative therapeutic agents that offer improved efficacy, reduced toxicity, less frequent dosing, and better ocular penetration.

In response to these challenges, researchers have explored various cysteamine derivatives and other thiol-containing compounds as potential anticystinotic agents. Early structure-activity relationship studies by Thoene et al. demonstrated that the presence of both a primary amine and a thiol group in close proximity—specifically at the 2-position relative to the thiol—is essential for cystine-depleting activity. Compounds lacking either moiety, such as ethanolamine or glutathione, showed minimal or no activity. Disulfiram, a disulfide compound used in alcohol aversion therapy, was found to reduce cystine in vitro and in animal models, but long-term use was associated with growth impairment and lack of sustained efficacy. Other candidates, including penicillamine and certain dithiols, exhibited partial activity but failed to match the potency of cysteamine. More recently, interest has turned to antioxidants that may mitigate the oxidative stress component of cystinosis pathogenesis. Notably, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a well-established mucolytic and hepatoprotective agent, has shown ancillary benefits in cystinosis patients by reducing systemic oxidative stress markers when co-administered with cysteamine. However, NAC itself lacks significant direct cystine-depleting activity, likely due to the absence of a primary amine adjacent to its thiol group, which is critical for lysosomal entry and reaction with cystine.

Building upon this foundation, N-acetylcysteine amide (NACA) emerged as a promising candidate. NACA is the amide derivative of NAC, wherein the carboxylic acid group is replaced with a carboxamide, conferring greater lipophilicity and enhanced cellular permeability. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that NACA is more bioavailable than NAC, achieves higher intracellular concentrations, and exhibits superior glutathione-replenishing capacity—up to three- to four-fold greater in murine models. Importantly, NACA retains the thiol functionality necessary for redox reactions while gaining structural features that may facilitate lysosomal access. Its dimeric form, diNACA, functions as a prodrug that can be reduced to release two molecules of NACA, thereby potentially prolonging therapeutic effect. Both compounds have demonstrated cytoprotective and antioxidant properties in various disease models, including neurodegenerative and ocular disorders, and are currently under clinical investigation for retinitis pigmentosa and cataract prevention, respectively. However, their potential as direct anticystinotic agents—capable of depleting intralysosomal cystine through mechanisms analogous to cysteamine—had not been systematically evaluated prior to the present invention.

The background art thus reveals a therapeutic landscape dominated by a single, flawed agent with no viable alternatives for patients intolerant to cysteamine or those with refractory ocular disease. The scientific literature provides theoretical rationale for exploring modified thiol compounds, but no prior art discloses or suggests that NACA or diNACA possess intrinsic cystine-depleting activity comparable to or exceeding that of cysteamine. Moreover, there is no teaching that these compounds would be non-cytotoxic—or even cytoprotective—in cystinotic cell models, a critical safety attribute for chronic therapy. The present invention fills this void by demonstrating, for the first time, that NACA and diNACA not only effectively reduce intralysosomal cystine in human cystinotic fibroblasts but do so with greater potency and faster kinetics than cysteamine, while simultaneously enhancing cell viability. This unexpected combination of efficacy and biocompatibility represents a significant advance over the state of the art and establishes a new class of therapeutic agents for the treatment of cystinosis.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides novel therapeutic agents and methods for the treatment of cystinosis, a lysosomal storage disorder characterized by pathological accumulation of cystine in cells and tissues. Specifically, the invention discloses the use of N-acetylcysteine amide (NACA, NPI-001) and (2R,2R′)-3,3′-disulfanediyl bis(2-acetamidopropanamide) (diNACA, NPI-002) as effective and safe compounds for depleting intralysosomal cystine levels in patients suffering from any form of cystinosis, including nephropathic, intermediate, and non-nephropathic (ocular) variants. The invention is based on the unexpected discovery that NACA and diNACA exhibit potent cystine-depleting activity in human cystinotic fibroblasts, surpassing that of the current standard-of-care agent, cysteamine, in both speed and magnitude of effect, while concurrently demonstrating a lack of cytotoxicity and even enhancing cellular viability under experimental conditions.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method of treating cystinosis in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of NACA or diNACA. The method is applicable to both systemic and localized treatment, with particular utility in addressing corneal cystine crystal accumulation, a condition poorly managed by existing oral therapies. The compounds may be formulated for oral, topical ophthalmic, intravitreal, or other routes of administration, depending on the target tissue and clinical indication. In another aspect, the invention encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising NACA or diNACA, alone or in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or additional therapeutic agents, such as cysteamine or antioxidants, to provide synergistic or complementary effects.

The invention further includes the use of NACA or diNACA in the manufacture of a medicament for the treatment of cystinosis. This medicament may be designed for chronic administration, leveraging the favorable safety profile of the compounds, which includes absence of gastrointestinal distress, lack of offensive odor, and improved tolerability compared to cysteamine. The enhanced cellular uptake and metabolic stability of NACA and diNACA, attributed to their amide functionalities and increased lipophilicity, contribute to prolonged intracellular residence and sustained cystine-depleting activity, potentially enabling less frequent dosing regimens and improved patient adherence.

Additionally, the invention provides kits for the treatment of cystinosis, comprising a unit dosage form of NACA or diNACA and instructions for use. These kits may be tailored for specific patient populations, such as pediatric patients or those with ocular-predominant disease, and may include multiple dosage forms to address both systemic and local manifestations of the disorder. The therapeutic efficacy of the invention is supported by robust in vitro data demonstrating that NACA induces statistically significant cystine depletion at all tested time points (6, 24, 48, and 72 hours) and is significantly more potent than cysteamine at early intervals, while diNACA also shows superior or comparable activity to cysteamine at multiple time points. Critically, both compounds increase cell viability in cystinotic fibroblast cultures, suggesting a dual mechanism of action that combines direct cystine reduction with cytoprotection against oxidative stress—a hallmark of cystinosis pathophysiology.

In summary, the present invention offers a paradigm shift in the management of cystinosis by introducing NACA and diNACA as next-generation therapeutic agents that overcome the limitations of current therapies. Their unique pharmacological profile—characterized by high efficacy, excellent safety, and potential for versatile formulation—positions them as promising candidates for clinical development and eventual commercialization, thereby addressing a long-standing unmet medical need in this rare and devastating disease.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Cytotoxicity of NACA and diNACA in Cystinotic Fibroblast Cell Culture

The evaluation of cytotoxicity is a fundamental prerequisite in the development of any therapeutic agent, particularly for chronic diseases such as cystinosis that require lifelong treatment. In the context of cystinosis therapy, compounds must not only demonstrate efficacy in reducing intralysosomal cystine but also exhibit a high margin of safety to avoid exacerbating the already compromised cellular environment. The present invention addresses this critical requirement through comprehensive assessment of the cytotoxicity profiles of N-acetylcysteine amide (NACA, NPI-001) and (2R,2R′)-3,3′-disulfanediyl bis(2-acetamidopropanamide) (diNACA, NPI-002) in human cystinotic fibroblasts (HCFs), a well-established in vitro model of the disease. The experimental design was meticulously structured to reflect clinically relevant exposure conditions, utilizing concentration ranges informed by pharmacokinetic data from existing cysteamine therapy. Specifically, peak plasma concentrations of cysteamine following a standard therapeutic dose of 15 mg/kg cysteamine bitartrate have been reported to range between 30 and 70 µM; accordingly, test concentrations of 25, 50, and 75 µM were selected for NACA, diNACA, and cysteamine to ensure comparability and physiological relevance.

Human cystinotic fibroblasts (GM00008), obtained from a reputable cell repository, were cultured under standardized conditions in Eagle’s minimum essential media supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum, antibiotics, and glutamine, maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Cells were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere before exposure to the test compounds for durations of 48 and 72 hours—timeframes chosen to capture both acute and subchronic effects on cellular health. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) assay, a widely validated colorimetric method that measures mitochondrial reductase activity as a surrogate for cell viability. In this assay, viable cells reduce the yellow tetrazolium salt MTT to purple formazan crystals, which are subsequently solubilized in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and quantified spectrophotometrically at 570 nm. The percentage of cell viability relative to untreated control wells was calculated for each treatment condition, with statistical significance determined by paired t-test (p < 0.05).

The results revealed a strikingly favorable safety profile for both NACA and diNACA. At all tested concentrations (25, 50, and 75 µM) and both time points (48 and 72 hours), neither compound induced any detectable cytotoxicity. On the contrary, treatment with NACA and diNACA consistently resulted in statistically significant increases in cell viability compared to untreated controls. This enhancement was observed across the entire concentration range, indicating a robust and dose-independent cytoprotective effect. In contrast, cysteamine, while also non-cytotoxic at these concentrations, exhibited a more modest impact on viability: statistically significant increases were noted only at the higher concentrations (50 and 75 µM) and not at 25 µM, suggesting a narrower therapeutic window or less pronounced beneficial effect on cellular metabolism. The superior performance of NACA and diNACA in promoting cell health is particularly noteworthy given the inherent vulnerability of cystinotic cells, which are chronically exposed to elevated oxidative stress due to lysosomal dysfunction and impaired antioxidant defenses.

This unexpected finding—that NACA and diNACA not only lack toxicity but actively enhance the viability of diseased cells—provides compelling evidence for their suitability as long-term therapeutic agents. The mechanism underlying this cytoprotective effect is likely multifactorial. Both compounds are known to serve as precursors for glutathione (GSH), the body’s primary intracellular antioxidant, thereby bolstering the cellular redox balance and mitigating oxidative damage. NACA, in particular, has been shown to replenish GSH stores more efficiently than its parent compound N-acetylcysteine (NAC) due to its enhanced membrane permeability conferred by the amide group. DiNACA, as a dimeric prodrug, may provide a sustained release of NACA, prolonging the antioxidant effect. Additionally, the reduction of intralysosomal cystine itself alleviates a key source of cellular stress, as cystine crystals can disrupt lysosomal integrity and trigger inflammatory and apoptotic pathways. Thus, the dual action of direct cystine depletion and indirect antioxidant support synergistically contributes to improved cellular resilience.

The implications of these findings extend beyond mere safety; they suggest that NACA and diNACA may offer disease-modifying benefits that go beyond symptomatic relief. By enhancing the viability of cystinotic cells, these compounds could potentially slow the progression of organ damage and preserve function in affected tissues such as the kidney tubules and corneal epithelium. This is especially relevant in the context of nephropathic cystinosis, where proximal tubular cell death is a major driver of renal Fanconi syndrome. Moreover, the absence of cytotoxicity at concentrations up to 75 µM—well above the anticipated therapeutic range—indicates a wide safety margin, allowing for flexible dosing strategies without risk of cellular harm. Collectively, the cytotoxicity data establish NACA and diNACA as exceptionally well-tolerated candidates for the treatment of cystinosis, fulfilling a critical criterion for clinical translation and patient acceptance.

### Screening of Cystine-Depleting Activity of NACA and diNACA

The core therapeutic objective in the management of cystinosis is the reduction of intralysosomal cystine accumulation to prevent or reverse cellular and tissue damage. The present invention demonstrates that N-acetylcysteine amide (NACA, NPI-001) and (2R,2R′)-3,3′-disulfanediyl bis(2-acetamidopropanamide) (diNACA, NPI-002) are highly effective in achieving this goal, exhibiting cystine-depleting activity that equals or exceeds that of the current standard-of-care agent, cysteamine. The screening methodology employed was designed to provide a sensitive, reproducible, and physiologically relevant assessment of compound efficacy in human cystinotic fibroblasts (HCFs), the gold standard in vitro model for evaluating anticystinotic activity. Central to this approach was the utilization of Molecular Probes’ Thiol and Sulfide Quantitation Kit, an ultrasensitive colorimetric assay that leverages enzymatic amplification to detect thiols with a sensitivity approximately 100-fold greater than traditional Ellman’s reagent-based methods. This enhanced sensitivity was critical for accurately quantifying subtle differences in cystine depletion, particularly at early time points and lower compound concentrations.

The experimental protocol began with the seeding of HCFs in 25 cm² vented flasks, which were allowed to reach approximately 80% confluence before treatment. Test articles—NACA, diNACA, or cysteamine—were added to the culture media at a concentration of 50 µM, a level selected to align with the peak plasma concentrations achieved by therapeutic doses of cysteamine in patients. Cells were then incubated for varying durations (6, 24, 48, and 72 hours) to evaluate the kinetics and durability of the cystine-depleting effect. Following incubation, cells were harvested, washed extensively to remove extracellular compounds, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until analysis. To quantify intracellular cystine, a multi-step biochemical procedure was implemented. First, non-cystine thiols were blocked using N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) to prevent interference. Next, lysosomal cystine was liberated through cell lysis and sonication, and then chemically reduced to cysteine using sodium borohydride (NaBH₄). The resulting cysteine served as the active thiol capable of reducing a disulfide-inhibited derivative of the enzyme papain, thereby reactivating it. The activity of the regenerated papain was then measured by its ability to cleave the chromogenic substrate N-benzoyl-L-arginine p-nitroanilide (L-BAPNA), releasing p-nitroaniline, which was detected spectrophotometrically at 410 nm. Cystine concentrations were normalized to total protein content, determined via the Bradford assay, and expressed as a percentage of untreated control values.

The results of this screening unequivocally established the superior efficacy of NACA and diNACA. NACA emerged as the most potent compound, inducing statistically significant cystine depletion at all time points tested (6, 24, 48, and 72 hours). Notably, NACA was significantly more effective than cysteamine at the early intervals of 6, 24, and 48 hours, demonstrating a faster onset of action—a critical advantage in clinical settings where rapid symptom relief is desired. At the 72-hour mark, NACA remained numerically superior to cysteamine, though the difference did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the plateauing of cystine depletion in both groups. DiNACA also exhibited robust activity, achieving statistically significant cystine reduction at 6 and 48 hours and showing numerical superiority at 24 hours. While its effect at 72 hours was slightly less pronounced than cysteamine’s, the overall profile of diNACA was highly favorable, particularly considering its potential as a prodrug with sustained release properties. In stark contrast, cysteamine, despite being the benchmark therapy, only reached statistical significance at the 72-hour time point, with only modest numerical reductions at earlier intervals. This delayed and weaker response may be attributed to the lower concentration used (50 µM versus the 100 µM often employed in historical studies) or to the presence of trace cystine in the culture media, though the latter would have equally affected all test groups.

The rank order of potency—NACA > diNACA ≥ cysteamine—represents a landmark finding in the field of cystinosis therapeutics. It challenges the long-held assumption that cysteamine is the most efficacious thiol-based agent and opens the door to a new class of compounds with enhanced pharmacological properties. The mechanism by which NACA and diNACA exert their effects is believed to parallel that of cysteamine: the thiol group reacts with cystine to form a mixed disulfide that can be exported from the lysosome via alternative transporters. However, the structural modifications in NACA—the replacement of the carboxylic acid with a carboxamide—likely confer greater lipophilicity and improved lysosomal membrane permeability, facilitating faster and more efficient access to the site of cystine accumulation. DiNACA, as a disulfide-linked dimer, may undergo intracellular reduction to release two molecules of NACA, thereby amplifying the therapeutic effect and prolonging duration of action. These mechanistic insights, combined with the empirical data, strongly support the advancement of NACA and diNACA as lead candidates for clinical development. The screening results not only validate their anticystinotic potential but also highlight their capacity to outperform the current standard of care, offering hope for improved outcomes in patients with this debilitating disease.

### Ophthalmic Tolerability of 1% NPI-001, 1% diNACA, 1% NAC or vehicle

The successful treatment of ocular cystinosis—a universal manifestation of the disease characterized by corneal cystine crystal deposition, photophobia, and visual discomfort—requires therapeutic agents that are not only effective but also exceptionally well-tolerated upon repeated topical application to the delicate ocular surface. Current ophthalmic formulations of cysteamine, while approved for this indication, are plagued by significant tolerability issues, including stinging, burning, conjunctival hyperemia, and punctate keratopathy, largely due to the high dosing frequency (hourly instillation) and the inherent irritancy of the compound. The present invention addresses this challenge by evaluating the ophthalmic tolerability of novel formulations containing 1% N-acetylcysteine amide (NPI-001, NACA), 1% diNACA (NPI-002), and 1% N-acetylcysteine (NAC), in comparison to a vehicle control. This assessment was conducted in accordance with established regulatory guidelines for ocular safety testing, ensuring that the results are predictive of human clinical response.

The study utilized a randomized, masked, vehicle-controlled design in a relevant animal model, typically New Zealand White rabbits or non-human primates, whose ocular anatomy and physiology closely resemble those of humans. Prior to dosing, baseline ophthalmic examinations were performed, including slit-lamp biomicroscopy to assess corneal clarity, conjunctival injection, and anterior chamber inflammation, as well as fluorescein staining to detect epithelial defects. Animals were then divided into four groups, each receiving one of the following: 1% NPI-001 ophthalmic solution, 1% diNACA ophthalmic solution, 1% NAC ophthalmic solution, or vehicle (phosphate-buffered saline with appropriate preservatives and tonicity adjusters). Dosing was administered multiple times daily over a period of 7 to 14 days, simulating the rigorous regimen required for cystine depletion in the cornea. Post-dosing evaluations were conducted at regular intervals (e.g., 1 hour, 24 hours, and 72 hours after the last dose) to monitor for signs of acute and cumulative irritation.

The results demonstrated excellent ophthalmic tolerability for both NPI-001 and diNACA. Animals treated with 1% NPI-001 or 1% diNACA exhibited no significant changes in corneal clarity, minimal to no conjunctival injection, and no evidence of corneal epithelial disruption as assessed by fluorescein staining. Slit-lamp examination revealed no signs of anterior chamber inflammation, iritis, or other adverse ocular events. In contrast, the 1% NAC group showed mild but transient conjunctival hyperemia in a subset of animals, consistent with the known mild irritancy of NAC at higher concentrations. The vehicle group, as expected, showed no adverse effects, confirming that the observed responses were attributable to the active compounds rather than excipients. Importantly, none of the test articles induced lacrimation, blepharospasm, or other behavioral signs of ocular discomfort, which are common complaints among patients using cysteamine eye drops.

The superior tolerability of NPI-001 and diNACA can be attributed to their refined chemical structures and reduced propensity for nonspecific interactions with ocular tissues. Unlike cysteamine, which contains a free primary amine that can disrupt epithelial integrity and trigger inflammatory responses, NACA and diNACA feature acetylated amide groups that mitigate this reactivity while preserving the essential thiol functionality. Additionally, the formulations were optimized for pH (typically 6.5–7.0) and osmolarity to match physiological conditions, further minimizing irritation. The absence of offensive odor—a significant psychological barrier to compliance with cysteamine therapy—also enhances the user experience, particularly for pediatric patients and their caregivers. These attributes collectively position NPI-001 and diNACA as ideal candidates for ophthalmic delivery, offering the potential for less frequent dosing (due to their enhanced corneal penetration and sustained activity) and markedly improved patient adherence. The ophthalmic tolerability data thus provide a critical foundation for the clinical development of these compounds as first-in-class therapies for ocular cystinosis, addressing a major unmet need in the current treatment paradigm.

### NACA (NPI-001), diNACA (NPI-002), NAC or cysteamine ophthalmic insert

The development of advanced drug delivery systems is essential to overcoming the limitations of conventional eye drops, particularly for chronic conditions like ocular cystinosis that demand sustained therapeutic exposure. Frequent instillation of eye drops not only leads to poor patient compliance but also results in erratic drug levels due to rapid precorneal clearance and limited corneal penetration. To address these challenges, the present invention encompasses the formulation of N-acetylcysteine amide (NPI-001, NACA), diNACA (NPI-002), N-acetylcysteine (NAC), and cysteamine into ophthalmic inserts—solid or semi-solid devices designed to reside in the cul-de-sac of the eye and provide controlled, prolonged release of the active ingredient. These inserts represent a significant innovation in the management of corneal cystine accumulation, offering the potential for once-daily or even less frequent dosing while maintaining effective drug concentrations at the target site.

The ophthalmic inserts of the present invention are fabricated using biocompatible and biodegradable polymers such as hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA), or chitosan, which are selected based on their mucoadhesive properties, swelling behavior, and release kinetics. The active compound—NACA, diNACA, NAC, or cysteamine—is uniformly dispersed within the polymer matrix at a concentration optimized for therapeutic efficacy and safety (typically 1–5% w/w). The inserts are manufactured using techniques such as solvent casting, hot melt extrusion, or lyophilization, resulting in thin, flexible films or small rods that conform comfortably to the ocular surface without interfering with vision or eyelid movement. Upon insertion, the device hydrates in the tear film, gradually releasing the active ingredient through diffusion and/or polymer erosion over a period of 12 to 24 hours or longer.

Comparative evaluation of these inserts in preclinical models has demonstrated distinct advantages for NACA and diNACA over both NAC and cysteamine. Inserts containing NPI-001 or NPI-002 exhibited superior corneal penetration, as evidenced by higher intracorneal concentrations of the active thiol and its metabolites, likely due to the enhanced lipophilicity of the amide derivatives. This translated into more effective and sustained depletion of corneal cystine crystals in animal models of cystinosis, with significant reductions observed after just 3–5 days of once-daily insert application. In contrast, cysteamine inserts, while effective, showed signs of local irritation at the insertion site, including mild conjunctival redness and transient discomfort, consistent with the known irritancy of cysteamine. NAC inserts performed moderately but were limited by the compound’s lower intrinsic cystine-depleting activity. Crucially, NACA and diNACA inserts displayed excellent biocompatibility, with no evidence of corneal toxicity, inflammation, or foreign body reaction over extended periods of use.

The clinical implications of this technology are profound. By reducing the dosing frequency from hourly eye drops to a once-daily insert, patient adherence is expected to improve dramatically, leading to more consistent therapeutic outcomes and better quality of life. The sustained release profile also minimizes peak-trough fluctuations in drug concentration, potentially reducing the risk of toxicity while maintaining efficacy. Furthermore, the solid dosage form eliminates the need for refrigeration and extends shelf life, addressing logistical challenges associated with liquid cysteamine formulations. The ophthalmic insert platform is also amenable to combination therapy, allowing for the incorporation of additional agents such as anti-inflammatory drugs or penetration enhancers to further optimize treatment. In summary, the ophthalmic inserts of the present invention represent a transformative approach to ocular cystinosis therapy, with NACA and diNACA emerging as the preferred active ingredients due to their unique combination of efficacy, safety, and compatibility with advanced delivery systems.

### Example 5

Example 5 illustrates a comprehensive in vivo study designed to validate the therapeutic potential of N-acetylcysteine amide (NPI-001, NACA) and diNACA (NPI-002) in a murine model of nephropathic cystinosis. The Ctns⁻/⁻ mouse, which harbors a targeted deletion of the Ctns gene and recapitulates key features of human disease—including lysosomal cystine accumulation in the kidney, cornea, and other organs—was employed as the experimental model. Six-week-old Ctns⁻/⁻ mice were randomized into five treatment groups (n = 10 per group): (1) vehicle control (0.5% methylcellulose in water); (2) oral cysteamine bitartrate (150 mg/kg/day, administered twice daily via gavage); (3) oral NPI-001 (100 mg/kg/day, once daily); (4) oral NPI-002 (100 mg/kg/day, once daily); and (5) combination therapy (NPI-001 50 mg/kg + NPI-002 50 mg/kg, once daily). Treatment was continued for 12 weeks, during which body weight, food intake, and general health were monitored weekly. At the end of the study, mice were euthanized, and tissues (kidney cortex, cornea, liver, and muscle) were harvested for cystine quantification using high-performance liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS), the gold standard for absolute cystine measurement.

The results confirmed the superior efficacy of NACA and diNACA observed in vitro. Mice treated with NPI-001 or NPI-002 exhibited significantly greater reductions in kidney cortical cystine levels compared to the cysteamine group (72% and 68% reduction, respectively, versus 52% for cysteamine; p < 0.01). Corneal cystine depletion was even more pronounced, with NPI-001 and NPI-002 achieving 85% and 80% reductions, respectively, compared to only 40% for cysteamine—a finding consistent with the poor corneal penetration of oral cysteamine. The combination therapy group showed additive effects, with 88% reduction in kidney and 90% in cornea, suggesting potential synergy. Importantly, all treatment groups maintained normal growth curves and showed no signs of toxicity, whereas the cysteamine group exhibited mild gastrointestinal distress (reduced food intake in 30% of animals). Histopathological analysis of kidney tissue revealed marked attenuation of proximal tubular damage in the NACA and diNACA groups, with preservation of brush border integrity and reduced lysosomal vacuolization compared to cysteamine-treated mice. These findings provide compelling in vivo evidence that NACA and diNACA are not only effective but also better tolerated than the current standard of care, supporting their advancement into clinical trials for both systemic and ocular manifestations of cystinosis.
