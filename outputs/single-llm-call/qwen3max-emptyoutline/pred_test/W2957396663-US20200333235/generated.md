# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to the field of biomedical diagnostics and, more specifically, to methods and systems for rapidly assessing the viability of biological cells in response to targeted therapeutic agents without the use of exogenous labels or dyes. The invention further pertains to microfluidic impedance cytometry integrated with machine learning algorithms for the classification and quantification of live and dead cancer cells based on their electrical properties across multiple frequencies. In particular, the invention provides a label-free, point-of-care compatible platform for evaluating the efficacy of antibody-conjugated antineoplastic drugs that target activated matriptase, a membrane-bound protease overexpressed in various epithelial cancers including breast carcinoma, B-cell lymphoma, and multiple myeloma. The system enables real-time, single-cell resolution analysis of cellular response to therapy by measuring multifrequency impedance signatures—specifically amplitude and phase changes—as cells traverse a microfluidic channel embedded with microfabricated electrodes. By leveraging supervised machine learning models, such as support vector machines with Gaussian kernels, the invention achieves high-accuracy classification of cell viability states, thereby facilitating personalized oncology treatment planning and drug sensitivity testing using minimal sample volumes and without compromising the integrity of cells for downstream molecular analyses.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Cancer remains a leading cause of global mortality, with therapeutic strategies often limited by systemic toxicity, variable patient response, and the lack of rapid, predictive diagnostic tools. Conventional chemotherapy, while effective in some contexts, is inherently non-selective, resulting in collateral damage to healthy tissues and significant adverse effects such as alopecia, gastrointestinal distress, and immunosuppression. In contrast, targeted cancer therapies aim to selectively eliminate malignant cells by exploiting molecular markers uniquely or overexpressed on tumor cell surfaces. One such promising target is activated matriptase, a type II transmembrane serine protease that is aberrantly expressed in a wide array of epithelial-derived malignancies, including luminal-type breast cancers, B-cell lymphomas, and multiple myeloma. Matriptase plays a critical role in tumor progression, invasion, and metastasis, making it an attractive candidate for antibody-directed drug delivery. When antineoplastic agents are conjugated to antibodies specific to activated matriptase, they can be selectively internalized by tumor cells, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. However, the success of such precision medicine approaches hinges on the ability to rapidly and accurately determine whether a patient’s tumor cells are responsive to the targeted therapy prior to or during treatment.

Current gold-standard methods for assessing cell viability, such as the trypan blue dye exclusion assay implemented in commercial instruments like the Vi-Cell analyzer from Beckman Coulter, rely on staining protocols that render cells non-viable for subsequent molecular or functional analyses. These optical methods require substantial sample volumes (typically 0.5–1 mL), bulky instrumentation, and manual or semi-automated processing, which limits their utility in resource-constrained or point-of-care settings. Moreover, dye-based assays provide only endpoint measurements and do not permit dynamic monitoring of cellular responses. Alternative approaches, including cytotoxicity assays based on metabolic activity (e.g., MTS assays) or caspase activation, also suffer from similar limitations, including the need for reagents, extended incubation times, and indirect inference of viability rather than direct physical assessment.

In recent years, microfluidic technologies have emerged as powerful platforms for high-throughput, low-volume biological analysis. Microfluidic channels enable precise manipulation of minute fluid volumes, reducing reagent consumption and enabling integration with sensing modalities. Among these, droplet microfluidics has been employed for drug screening in cancer cell lines, offering compartmentalization and scalability. However, such systems often still depend on fluorescent or colorimetric readouts, reintroducing the drawbacks of labeling. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has also been explored as a label-free method to track cell death in real time by detecting morphological changes associated with apoptosis. While OCT avoids staining, it requires complex and expensive optical setups, limiting its portability and clinical translation.

Electrical-based methods offer a compelling alternative for label-free, real-time cell analysis. Dielectrophoresis (DEP), which exploits differences in the dielectric properties of cells under non-uniform electric fields, has been widely used to assess cell viability, growth, and immune reactivity. However, conventional DEP typically operates at a single frequency, capturing only a narrow slice of a cell’s complex dielectric response. This limitation reduces the discriminatory power between subtly different cell states, particularly when distinguishing early apoptotic cells from healthy ones. In contrast, electrical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)—or impedance cytometry when applied at the single-cell level—measures the frequency-dependent opposition of a cell to alternating current, thereby providing a rich, multidimensional signature of cellular physiology, including membrane integrity, cytoplasmic conductivity, and nuclear morphology.

Impedance cytometry has a long history, dating back to early 20th-century studies of erythrocyte conductivity, and has since evolved into a sophisticated analytical tool. Modern microfluidic impedance cytometers have demonstrated success in differentiating leukocytes, platelets, and whole blood components, as well as in characterizing tumor cells and detecting protein biomarkers at picomolar concentrations. Notably, multifrequency impedance cytometry has been applied to assess the viability of bacterial cells such as Bacillus megaterium, though prior implementations often relied on a single frequency (e.g., 10 MHz) for classification, potentially overlooking critical information encoded at other frequencies. Given that cellular dielectric properties vary significantly across the frequency spectrum—with low frequencies (<1 MHz) primarily probing the plasma membrane and high frequencies (>10 MHz) penetrating into the cytoplasm and organelles—a multifrequency approach is essential for comprehensive cell characterization.

Despite these advances, existing impedance cytometry systems have not been effectively integrated with robust computational frameworks capable of interpreting complex, high-dimensional impedance data for clinical decision-making. Machine learning, particularly supervised learning algorithms, offers a powerful solution to this challenge. Support vector machines (SVMs), for instance, are well-suited for binary classification tasks such as live/dead cell discrimination. By mapping input features into high-dimensional spaces via kernel functions—such as the Gaussian (radial basis function) kernel—SVMs can identify nonlinear decision boundaries that separate classes with high accuracy, even when feature distributions overlap in the original space. In bioinformatics and biomedical engineering, machine learning has already proven invaluable for predicting protein interactions, classifying disease subtypes, and optimizing therapeutic regimens.

Nevertheless, no existing system combines multifrequency impedance cytometry with machine learning in a miniaturized, label-free, and clinically relevant platform specifically designed for assessing response to matriptase-targeted cancer therapy. Prior art lacks a device that simultaneously acquires amplitude and phase responses across multiple discrete frequencies during single-cell transit, extracts these as discriminative features, and employs them in a trained classifier to predict viability percentages with high fidelity. Furthermore, existing approaches do not address the practical constraints of point-of-care deployment, such as minimal sample volume, elimination of external noise, avoidance of syringe pumps (which introduce electrical interference), and compatibility with standard cell preparation protocols. The present invention bridges these gaps by providing a fully integrated, microfluidic impedance cytometer that operates under gravity-driven flow, uses gold microelectrodes to ensure electrochemical stability, and leverages both amplitude and phase changes across four or more frequencies as inputs to a machine learning model, thereby achieving unprecedented classification accuracy (>95%) in distinguishing live and dead cancer cells without any labeling or staining.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel, label-free method and system for rapidly assessing the viability of cancer cells in response to targeted therapeutic agents, particularly those conjugated to antibodies against activated matriptase. The invention overcomes the limitations of conventional dye-based and optical methods by integrating microfluidic impedance cytometry with machine learning to enable high-accuracy, single-cell resolution classification of live and dead cells based on their intrinsic electrical properties. The system comprises a microfluidic channel fabricated in polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) and bonded to a glass substrate embedded with microfabricated gold electrodes, where cells in suspension flow through the channel under gravity-driven pressure. An alternating current (AC) voltage is applied across the electrodes at multiple discrete frequencies simultaneously—typically including 500 kHz, 20 MHz, 25 MHz, and 30 MHz—and the resulting impedance response is recorded using a lock-in amplifier. As each cell passes through the sensing region, it induces transient changes in both the amplitude and phase of the measured signal, which are extracted as quantitative features.

These features are then processed by a supervised machine learning algorithm, specifically a support vector machine (SVM) with a Gaussian kernel, which has been trained on labeled datasets comprising impedance signatures from known populations of 100% live and 100% dead cancer cells (e.g., T47D breast cancer cells). The trained classifier outputs a binary prediction (live or dead) for each cell, and the overall viability percentage of a sample is computed as the proportion of cells classified as live. The invention demonstrates that using both amplitude and phase changes across multiple frequencies as combined features yields superior classification performance, with reported accuracy exceeding 95%, true positive rates of 95%, and true negative rates of 97%. This represents a significant improvement over systems relying on single-frequency or single-feature (amplitude-only or phase-only) analysis.

The device is designed for minimal sample consumption (on the order of microliters), eliminates the need for exogenous labels or dyes, preserves cell integrity for downstream molecular analysis, and operates without bulky optical components or syringe pumps, thereby enhancing portability and suitability for point-of-care applications. The microfluidic channel is rendered hydrophilic via oxygen plasma treatment to facilitate consistent gravity-driven flow, and measurements are conducted within a shielded enclosure to minimize electromagnetic interference. The system is particularly suited for personalized oncology, where dissociated tumor cells from a patient biopsy can be exposed to a matriptase-targeted drug conjugate, and their viability assessed within hours to predict therapeutic efficacy. In cases of inter-patient tumor heterogeneity, the classifier can be calibrated using untreated live cells and heat-killed cells from the same patient sample, ensuring robustness across diverse clinical specimens. The invention thus provides a rapid, cost-effective, and clinically actionable platform for drug sensitivity testing and therapeutic personalization in cancer care.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### A. Methods and Systems for Classifying Biological Particles

The present invention encompasses a comprehensive system and methodology for the label-free, high-accuracy classification of biological particles—specifically, cancer cells—based on their viability status following exposure to targeted therapeutic agents. Central to this invention is a microfluidic impedance cytometer that integrates microfabrication, multifrequency electrical interrogation, and machine learning to achieve real-time, single-cell resolution analysis without the need for staining, labeling, or optical detection. The system is engineered to address the critical clinical need for rapid assessment of patient-specific tumor cell response to matriptase-targeted antibody-drug conjugates, thereby enabling informed decisions in personalized cancer therapy.

The hardware architecture of the invention consists of three primary components: (1) a microfluidic chip containing a precisely dimensioned channel and embedded microelectrodes; (2) a multifrequency signal generation and acquisition unit, preferably a lock-in amplifier capable of simultaneous multi-tone excitation and demodulation; and (3) a computational module for data processing, feature extraction, and machine learning-based classification. The microfluidic chip is fabricated using a hybrid approach combining photolithography on glass substrates for electrode patterning and soft lithography in PDMS for channel formation. Specifically, a 3-inch fused silica wafer is cleaned and coated with a photoresist, which is then patterned using ultraviolet light exposure through a chromium mask defining the electrode geometry. Following development and hard bake, a 10-nanometer adhesion layer of chromium is deposited via electron beam evaporation, followed by a 100-nanometer layer of gold. The liftoff process removes excess metal, leaving behind gold electrodes with a width of 20 micrometers and a gap of 25 micrometers between opposing electrodes. Gold is selected for its excellent electrical conductivity, chemical inertness, and resistance to oxidation, ensuring stable electrode-electrolyte interfaces and minimal faradaic reactions during impedance measurements.

The microfluidic channel is independently fabricated in PDMS by casting a 10:1 mixture of prepolymer to curing agent onto a silicon master mold patterned with SU-8 photoresist. After curing at 80°C for two hours, the PDMS replica is peeled off, and inlet and outlet ports (5 mm and 1.5 mm in diameter, respectively) are punched. The PDMS slab is then aligned and bonded to the electrode-patterned glass wafer following oxygen plasma treatment of both surfaces, which renders them hydrophilic and promotes irreversible covalent bonding upon baking at 70°C for 30 minutes. The resulting channel has a cross-sectional dimension of 100 micrometers in width and 30 micrometers in height, optimized to ensure single-file cell transit and maximize signal-to-noise ratio during impedance sensing.

Operation of the device relies on gravity-driven flow to transport cell suspensions through the microchannel, eliminating the need for syringe pumps or external pressure controllers that can introduce mechanical vibrations and electrical noise. To ensure consistent capillary action, the PDMS channel is rendered hydrophilic immediately prior to each experiment by a brief 30-second oxygen plasma treatment, followed by priming with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cell samples, prepared in PBS at a concentration of approximately 400 cells per microliter, are introduced into the inlet reservoir, and the height difference between inlet and outlet generates a steady, low-shear flow that carries cells through the electrode region at a controlled rate. All measurements are conducted within a grounded metal enclosure to shield against 60 Hz line interference and other environmental electromagnetic noise.

During operation, the lock-in amplifier applies a composite AC voltage signal comprising four discrete frequency components—500 kHz, 20 MHz, 25 MHz, and 30 MHz—simultaneously across the electrode pair. The choice of frequencies is strategic: 500 kHz is sufficiently low to be sensitive to plasma membrane integrity and surface capacitance, while frequencies above 10 MHz (20–30 MHz) penetrate the cell interior, probing cytoplasmic conductivity and nuclear morphology. As each cell traverses the inter-electrode gap, it displaces conductive electrolyte, causing a transient perturbation in the local electric field. This perturbation manifests as a measurable change in both the amplitude (magnitude) and phase (angular shift) of the current or voltage at each frequency. The lock-in amplifier demodulates the response at each frequency in real time, outputting time-series data streams for both real and imaginary components, from which amplitude and phase changes are computed for every detected cell event.

Data preprocessing involves detrending and denoising the raw signals to remove baseline drift and high-frequency noise, ensuring that only genuine cell-induced transients are analyzed. For each cell passage, two key features are extracted per frequency: (1) the amplitude change, defined as the difference between the peak signal level during cell transit and the pre-event baseline; and (2) the phase change, calculated as the arctangent of the ratio of imaginary to real components, representing the angular deviation induced by the cell. Thus, for four frequencies, a total of eight features are generated per cell—four amplitude changes and four phase changes.

These features are then fed into a machine learning classifier, specifically a support vector machine (SVM) with a Gaussian (radial basis function) kernel. The SVM is trained on a labeled dataset comprising impedance signatures from control samples of 100% live T47D breast cancer cells and 100% dead T47D cells (induced by prolonged drug exposure or heat shock). Each training instance is labeled as “1” for live and “0” for dead. The training set includes over 1,000 cell events to prevent overfitting and ensure generalization. During training, the SVM learns a nonlinear decision boundary in the eight-dimensional feature space that optimally separates live and dead cells. Once trained, the classifier can predict the viability state of unknown cells with high confidence.

The system’s performance was validated using test samples with known viability percentages (90%, 82%, and 50% live cells), as determined independently by the Vi-Cell trypan blue exclusion method. When using amplitude-only features, the SVM achieved 89.7% accuracy; with phase-only features, accuracy improved to 90.6%; and with the combined eight-feature set, accuracy reached 95.9%, with true positive and true negative rates of 95% and 97%, respectively. These results demonstrate that the synergistic use of amplitude and phase information across multiple frequencies captures complementary aspects of cellular dielectric behavior, leading to superior classification.

The invention is particularly advantageous for clinical translation. It requires only microliters of sample, preserves cell viability for downstream genomic or proteomic analysis, and delivers results within minutes of sample loading. Moreover, the system can be adapted to patient-derived tumor cells by training the classifier on autologous live and dead controls, accommodating inter-patient heterogeneity. Future iterations may incorporate automated fluid handling, on-chip cell sorting, or integration with electronic health records to support real-time therapeutic decision support in oncology clinics.

### B. Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings ascribed below, unless the context clearly indicates otherwise:

“Activated matriptase” refers to the enzymatically active form of matriptase, a type II transmembrane serine protease that is overexpressed on the surface of various epithelial cancer cells, including but not limited to breast carcinoma, B-cell lymphoma, and multiple myeloma. Activation involves cleavage of the zymogen pro-form to yield a catalytically competent enzyme, which serves as a specific molecular target for antibody-conjugated therapeutic agents.

“Antibody-conjugated drug” or “antibody-drug conjugate (ADC)” denotes a chimeric molecule comprising a monoclonal antibody specific to a tumor-associated antigen (e.g., activated matriptase) linked covalently to a cytotoxic payload. Upon binding to the target antigen on the cancer cell surface, the ADC is internalized, leading to intracellular release of the drug and induction of apoptosis.

“Amplitude change” means the absolute difference between the baseline impedance signal level and the peak signal level observed when a single biological particle traverses the sensing region of the microfluidic impedance cytometer. It is measured in volts or arbitrary units and reflects the magnitude of perturbation in the electric field caused by the particle’s dielectric properties.

“Phase change” refers to the angular shift in the impedance signal, expressed in degrees or radians, resulting from the passage of a biological particle through the sensing zone. It is derived from the arctangent of the ratio of the imaginary to real components of the complex impedance and provides information about the capacitive and resistive balance of the particle.

“Microfluidic impedance cytometer” is a device that combines a microfluidic channel with integrated electrodes to perform single-cell electrical impedance measurements. Cells in suspension flow through the channel, and their passage modulates the impedance between the electrodes, allowing for label-free characterization based on dielectric properties.

“Multifrequency impedance spectroscopy” denotes the technique of applying alternating current signals at two or more discrete frequencies simultaneously across a sensing region and measuring the frequency-dependent impedance response of biological particles. This approach captures a broader spectrum of dielectric information compared to single-frequency methods.

“Support vector machine (SVM)” is a supervised machine learning algorithm used for classification and regression. In the context of this invention, it is employed to distinguish between live and dead cells based on extracted impedance features. The SVM constructs an optimal hyperplane in a high-dimensional feature space to separate classes, often using a kernel function (e.g., Gaussian) to handle nonlinear relationships.

“Viability” or “cell viability” refers to the physiological state of a cell indicating whether it is alive (metabolically active, intact membrane) or dead (loss of membrane integrity, cessation of metabolic activity). In this invention, viability is inferred from impedance signatures without the use of dyes or labels.

“Gravity-driven flow” describes a passive fluid transport mechanism in microfluidic systems where liquid movement is induced solely by the hydrostatic pressure difference between an elevated inlet reservoir and a lower outlet, eliminating the need for external pumps.

“Label-free” signifies that the analysis does not require the addition of exogenous markers, such as fluorescent dyes, enzymes, or radioactive tags, to the biological sample. The measurement relies entirely on intrinsic physical properties of the cells.

“Point-of-care” refers to medical diagnostic testing performed near or at the site of patient care, characterized by rapid turnaround, minimal infrastructure, and ease of use by non-specialists.

“T47D cells” are a human breast cancer cell line derived from a ductal carcinoma, classified as luminal type-A, estrogen receptor-positive (ER+), progesterone receptor-positive (PR+), and HER2-negative (HER2−), and used herein as a model system for epithelial cancers expressing activated matriptase.
