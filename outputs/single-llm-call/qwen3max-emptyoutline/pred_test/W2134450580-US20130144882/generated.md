# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of molecular diagnostics and computational biology has witnessed significant advances in the identification of disease biomarkers through high-throughput genomic technologies, particularly microarray-based gene expression profiling. These technologies enable the simultaneous measurement of expression levels for thousands of genes across biological samples, thereby offering a comprehensive snapshot of the transcriptional state associated with specific pathological conditions. In the context of complex neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), gene expression data derived from postmortem brain tissues or peripheral blood samples have been extensively analyzed to uncover molecular signatures that distinguish affected individuals from healthy controls. However, despite the wealth of data generated by such platforms, the translation of these findings into clinically actionable biomarkers remains challenging due to several inherent limitations.

One major limitation stems from the conventional analytical approaches employed in gene expression studies, which typically treat each gene as an independent entity during statistical analysis. This assumption neglects the well-established biological reality that genes do not function in isolation but rather operate within intricate networks of functional interactions, including protein-protein interactions, co-regulation, and shared involvement in biological pathways. Consequently, traditional differential expression analyses often fail to detect subtle yet coordinated changes in groups of functionally related genes whose individual expression alterations may fall below conventional thresholds of statistical significance. Such weakly differentially expressed genes are frequently dismissed as noise, even though their collective behavior might reflect critical pathophysiological processes underlying disease progression.

To address this gap, network-based gene expression analysis methods have emerged as a promising strategy for integrating prior biological knowledge—such as known disease-associated genes and interaction networks—with experimental expression data. These approaches aim to contextualize gene expression measurements within the framework of molecular interaction networks, thereby enhancing the sensitivity and specificity of biomarker discovery. By leveraging topological features of biological networks, such as node degree, betweenness centrality, or clustering coefficients, researchers can prioritize candidate genes that occupy central positions within disease-relevant subnetworks. Moreover, some methodologies attempt to identify modules or clusters of densely interconnected genes that exhibit coherent expression patterns across samples, under the hypothesis that such modules represent functional units perturbed in disease states.

Despite these advancements, existing network-based methods still largely rely on static representations of gene or protein interaction networks and apply standard clustering or ranking algorithms to organize network nodes. Commonly used techniques include hierarchical clustering, k-means clustering, spectral clustering, and various forms of graph-based ranking such as PageRank or random walk with restart. While these methods can reveal certain structural properties of the network, they often struggle to capture the nuanced interplay between local connectivity and global organization, especially in complex biological networks characterized by high inseparability and heterogeneous topologies. In particular, when applied to human disease networks, which tend to be scale-free and modular with overlapping communities, conventional clustering and ranking strategies may produce ambiguous or inconsistent results, failing to yield a clear ordering or grouping of genes that reflects both functional coherence and systemic relevance.

Furthermore, even when network-derived features are incorporated into machine learning models for classification tasks—such as distinguishing AD patients from controls—the resulting performance gains are often modest and highly dependent on the quality of the initial network construction and the choice of feature extraction methodology. A key unresolved issue is how to effectively transform raw gene expression profiles into a representation that preserves both the individual signal strength of each gene and the contextual information provided by its neighbors in the interaction network. Without a principled mechanism for integrating neighboring gene expressions in a biologically meaningful manner, much of the potential utility of network topology remains untapped.

In light of these challenges, there exists a pressing need for a novel computational framework that transcends the limitations of current network-based gene expression analysis methods. Such a framework should be capable of: (1) constructing disease-specific interaction networks that accurately reflect the molecular landscape of the condition under study; (2) reordering the nodes of these networks in a way that reveals latent structural and functional patterns not apparent through conventional means; and (3) transforming gene expression profiles into integrative representations that amplify the signal from weakly expressed but functionally relevant genes by incorporating the influence of their network neighbors. Importantly, this transformation must be grounded in a mathematically rigorous and biologically interpretable model that allows for seamless integration with standard classification algorithms while maintaining robustness across diverse datasets and experimental platforms.

The present invention addresses these needs by introducing a new paradigm termed Integrative eXpression Profiling (IXP), which leverages a bio-inspired optimization algorithm—ant colony optimization reordering (ACOR)—to reorder disease-specific protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks in a manner that enhances the detection of coordinated gene expression signals. Unlike traditional ranking or clustering approaches, ACOR simulates the collective foraging behavior of ant colonies to iteratively explore and refine the spatial arrangement of network nodes, ultimately producing a reordered adjacency matrix that exhibits fractal-like patterns indicative of hierarchical modularity and functional stratification. This reordered structure serves as the foundation for IXP, wherein gene expression values are integrated along the ordered gene list using a Gaussian influence function that accounts for the proximity of neighboring genes in the network. The resulting IXP profiles thus encapsulate both local topological information (via gene weights derived from node degree) and global organizational principles (via the ACOR-generated order), enabling more accurate and biologically informed classification of disease states.

This approach represents a significant departure from prior art in several respects. First, it moves beyond the binary notion of gene inclusion or exclusion based on differential expression cutoffs, instead embracing a continuous integration model that captures the cumulative effect of multiple weak signals. Second, it introduces a dynamic and adaptive reordering mechanism rooted in swarm intelligence, which is uniquely suited to navigating the complex topologies of human disease networks. Third, it provides a unified framework for feature transformation that is agnostic to the downstream classifier, thereby facilitating broad applicability across different diagnostic and prognostic settings. Finally, empirical validation using independent microarray datasets demonstrates that the ACOR-based IXP approach achieves superior classification accuracy compared to both conventional expression profiling and alternative network-based methods, underscoring its potential as a powerful tool for precision medicine and systems biology.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel method and system for improving the accuracy of disease classification through integrative expression profiling (IXP) based on ant colony optimization reordering (ACOR) of disease-specific protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks. This approach transforms conventional gene expression profiles into enhanced feature representations that incorporate both local and global topological information from biological networks, thereby amplifying subtle but coordinated expression signals that are often overlooked by traditional analytical methods.

In accordance with one aspect of the invention, a disease-specific PPI network is first constructed by selecting seed genes associated with the target disease from authoritative databases such as AlzGene and OMIM, followed by expansion using a nearest neighbor algorithm within a comprehensive human PPI database such as HAPPI. This ensures that the resulting network is tailored to the molecular pathology of the disease under investigation while retaining sufficient coverage of relevant interactors.

In accordance with another aspect of the invention, the nodes of the constructed PPI network are reordered using the ACOR algorithm, which simulates the collective behavior of ant colonies to iteratively optimize the spatial arrangement of proteins. This process yields a reordered adjacency matrix that reveals fractal-like patterns reflecting the hierarchical modularity and functional stratification of the network. Critically, the ACOR algorithm demonstrates superior capability in grouping functionally related genes together while simultaneously imposing a meaningful relative rank among them—a dual property that distinguishes it from conventional clustering or ranking techniques.

In accordance with a further aspect of the invention, gene expression profiles from individual samples are mapped onto the ACOR-reordered gene list and transformed into IXP profiles through integration using a Gaussian influence function. This function assigns greater weight to the expression values of neighboring genes based on their proximity in the reordered network, effectively smoothing the profile while preserving biologically relevant peaks corresponding to clusters of co-expressed, functionally related genes. As a result, genes that individually exhibit low differential expression but collectively form a coherent module within the network contribute meaningfully to the final IXP representation.

In accordance with yet another aspect of the invention, the resulting IXP profiles serve as input features for standard machine learning classifiers such as support vector machines (SVMs), enabling improved discrimination between disease and control samples. Empirical evaluation using independent microarray datasets for Alzheimer’s disease demonstrates that the ACOR-based IXP approach significantly outperforms both raw expression profiling and alternative network-based transformations, achieving classification accuracies that surpass those reported by recently published multi-gene signatures.

The invention further encompasses a computer-implemented system comprising one or more processors configured to execute instructions for performing the aforementioned steps, as well as non-transitory computer-readable media storing program code for implementing the method. Additionally, the invention includes kits and software tools designed to facilitate the application of ACOR-based IXP in clinical and research settings, supporting tasks such as biomarker discovery, patient stratification, and therapeutic response prediction.

By integrating bio-inspired network reordering with principled expression integration, the present invention offers a robust and scalable solution for enhancing the interpretability and predictive power of genomic data in the context of complex diseases. Its ability to uncover hidden patterns in gene expression through the lens of network topology makes it particularly well-suited for applications where subtle molecular shifts carry significant diagnostic or prognostic value.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENTS OF THE PRESENT INVENTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for generating integrative expression profiles (IXP) based on ant colony optimization reordering (ACOR) of disease-specific protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, which are then used to improve the accuracy of disease classification in genomic studies. The following detailed description sets forth specific embodiments of the invention, illustrating how the various components and steps interact to achieve the stated objectives. It will be understood by those skilled in the art that numerous modifications and variations may be made without departing from the spirit and scope of the invention as defined by the appended claims.

In one embodiment, the method begins with the selection of seed genes associated with a target disease of interest. For example, in the case of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), seed genes may be obtained from curated databases such as AlzGene (http://www.alzgene.org/) and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/omim). These databases compile genetic association studies and known disease-related mutations, providing a reliable source of candidate genes implicated in the etiology or progression of the disease. The selection of seed genes is a critical step, as it defines the starting point for network construction and influences the biological relevance of the resulting PPI network.

Following seed gene selection, a disease-specific PPI network is constructed using a nearest neighbor expansion algorithm applied to a comprehensive human PPI database such as the Human Annotated and Predicted Protein Interaction (HAPPI) database. The HAPPI database integrates experimentally validated interactions from sources like BioGRID and STRING with computationally predicted interactions, offering a rich resource for network modeling. The nearest neighbor expansion algorithm operates by iteratively adding direct interactors of the seed genes to the network, up to a predefined depth or until a desired network size is reached. This process ensures that the final network includes not only core disease-associated proteins but also their immediate functional partners, thereby capturing a broader spectrum of molecular players involved in the disease pathway.

Once the disease-specific PPI network is constructed, the next step involves reordering the network nodes using the ant colony optimization reordering (ACOR) algorithm. The ACOR algorithm is inspired by the foraging behavior of real ant colonies, wherein ants deposit pheromones along paths they traverse, leading other ants to follow trails with higher pheromone concentrations. In the computational implementation, simulated ants roam all possible paths in the network over multiple iterations, updating a probability distribution that guides the placement of nodes in a linear order. The objective is to maximize a fitness function that reflects the density and continuity of interactions in the reordered adjacency matrix. Through this iterative process, the algorithm converges on an optimal or near-optimal ordering that reveals underlying structural patterns in the network.

The output of the ACOR algorithm is a ranked list of genes/proteins, which serves as the basis for mapping gene expression data. In this embodiment, microarray gene expression profiles from individual biological samples are aligned with the ACOR-reordered gene list. Each gene’s expression value is then integrated with those of its neighbors using a Gaussian influence function defined as:

$$
\text{IXP}(i) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_j \cdot x_j \cdot \exp\left(-\frac{(r_i - r_j)^2}{2r^2}\right)
$$

where $ x_j $ denotes the original expression value of gene $ j $, $ w_j $ represents a weight assigned to gene $ j $ (e.g., based on its node degree in the PPI network), $ r_i $ and $ r_j $ are the ranks of genes $ i $ and $ j $ in the ACOR-ordered list, $ N $ is the total number of genes, and $ r $ is a horizontal influence coefficient that controls the spread of the Gaussian kernel. This formulation ensures that genes located close to each other in the reordered network exert a stronger influence on the integrated expression value at any given position, thereby emphasizing clusters of co-expressed, functionally related genes.

The resulting IXP profiles are then used as input features for a machine learning classifier, such as a support vector machine (SVM), to distinguish between disease and control samples. In practice, a training dataset—comprising labeled samples from known disease and control groups—is used to train the classifier on the IXP-transformed features. Once trained, the classifier can be applied to a separate testing dataset to evaluate its generalization performance. Notably, because the IXP transformation is performed independently of the classifier, the method is compatible with a wide range of supervised learning algorithms, including logistic regression, random forests, and neural networks.

Empirical validation of the invention was conducted using two publicly available microarray datasets related to Alzheimer’s disease: GSE1297 (training set, 31 samples: 22 AD, 9 controls) and GSE5281 (testing set, 151 samples: 84 AD, 67 controls). Using the ACOR-based IXP approach with an SVM classifier and a horizontal influence coefficient $ r = 0.9 $, the method achieved a classification accuracy of 82.78% on the blinded test set, compared to 74.83% using raw expression profiles ($ r = 0 $). In contrast, a recently published 1372-probe AD signature yielded only 61.59% accuracy under identical conditions. Furthermore, comparative analyses demonstrated that the ACOR-based ordering consistently outperformed alternative strategies, including network ranking (e.g., PageRank), graph clustering (e.g., 2D hierarchical clustering), and random node permutations, confirming the unique advantages of the ACOR algorithm in structuring the network for integrative expression profiling.

In another embodiment, the invention may be extended to multi-class classification scenarios, such as distinguishing between different stages of disease severity (e.g., incipient, moderate, and severe AD). In such cases, the IXP profiles can be used to train multi-class classifiers or to perform unsupervised clustering to identify sample subgroups with distinct molecular characteristics. Additionally, the method can be adapted for use with RNA-seq data or other high-throughput transcriptomic platforms by normalizing expression values appropriately before applying the IXP transformation.

The invention also contemplates the development of software tools and cloud-based platforms that implement the ACOR-based IXP pipeline, enabling researchers and clinicians to apply the method to their own datasets with minimal computational expertise. Such tools may include graphical user interfaces for uploading expression data, selecting disease contexts, visualizing reordered networks and IXP profiles, and exporting classification results. Moreover, the system may incorporate automated parameter tuning—for instance, optimizing the horizontal influence coefficient $ r $ via cross-validation—to further enhance performance.

From a practical standpoint, the ACOR-based IXP approach offers several key benefits over existing methods. First, it leverages both local (node degree) and global (ACOR ordering) network properties to enrich gene expression features, thereby increasing sensitivity to biologically relevant signals. Second, it is robust to variations in seed gene selection and network construction, as evidenced by consistent fractal-like patterns in reordered adjacency matrices across different experimental setups. Third, it provides interpretable results, as peaks in the IXP profiles correspond directly to clusters of functionally related genes, facilitating downstream biological validation and hypothesis generation.

In summary, the embodiments described herein illustrate a comprehensive and flexible framework for enhancing disease classification through integrative expression profiling grounded in bio-inspired network reordering. By bridging the gap between network topology and gene expression dynamics, the invention opens new avenues for biomarker discovery, patient stratification, and personalized medicine in the era of big data genomics.
