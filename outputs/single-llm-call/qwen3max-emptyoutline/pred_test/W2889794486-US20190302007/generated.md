## BACKGROUND

Optical spectroscopy, particularly ultraviolet-visible (UV-VIS) spectrophotometry, has served as a foundational analytical technique across scientific disciplines for over a century. Since its widespread adoption in the 1940s, UV-VIS spectrophotometry has enabled researchers to quantify molecular constituents in diverse media by measuring the attenuation of light as it passes through a sample. The fundamental assumption underlying this technique is that any reduction in transmitted light intensity relative to the incident beam results exclusively from molecular absorption—a process wherein photons are converted into internal energy within the sample’s constituent molecules. This principle, formalized in the Bouguer-Lambert-Beer law, provides a direct relationship between absorbance and the concentration of absorbing species, rendering UV-VIS spectrophotometry an indispensable tool in chemistry, biology, materials science, and related fields.

However, this foundational assumption breaks down when the sample under investigation exhibits significant optical scattering. In complex media—such as biological tissues, colloidal suspensions, nanoparticle dispersions, polymer films, and cellular environments—variations in refractive index at internal interfaces cause incident photons to be redirected from their original path without being absorbed. This scattering phenomenon redistributes light spatially but does not consume photon energy, thereby violating the core premise of conventional UV-VIS analysis. As a result, the measured extinction (often reported as absorbance) becomes a composite signal reflecting both true molecular absorption and extrinsic scattering losses. Because scattered photons often fail to reach the detector due to geometric constraints of the instrument, the spectrophotometer erroneously attributes all untransmitted light to absorption, leading to substantial overestimation of the true absorption coefficient.

The distinction between absorption and scattering is not merely academic; it carries profound implications for applications ranging from biomedical diagnostics to nanomaterial characterization. Absorption is an intrinsic molecular property, directly tied to electronic transitions and chemical identity, whereas scattering is an extrinsic physical phenomenon governed by particle size, morphology, concentration, and the surrounding medium’s optical properties. In simple, homogeneous liquids like pure water or dilute dye solutions, scattering is negligible, and UV-VIS spectra accurately reflect molecular composition. But in turbid or heterogeneous systems—such as blood, milk, tissue slices, or gold nanoparticle (GNP) suspensions—scattering can dominate the optical response, obscuring the true absorption signature and rendering spectral interpretation ambiguous or misleading.

Numerous attempts have been made to address this limitation. Integrating sphere-based methods, for instance, capture nearly all scattered light by enclosing the sample in a highly reflective cavity, thereby enabling more accurate determination of total attenuation. While effective in controlled settings, these systems are bulky, sensitive to sample positioning and chamber geometry, and require extensive wavelength-dependent calibration. Moreover, they remain incompatible with standard cuvette-based spectrophotometers used in most laboratories. Time-resolved techniques using ultrafast lasers can separate absorption from scattering by analyzing the temporal spread of transmitted pulses, but such approaches demand expensive, tunable femtosecond laser sources and sophisticated detection electronics, making them impractical for routine use.

Photothermal spectroscopy offers another route, exploiting the fact that only absorbed light generates heat, which can then be detected via probe beam deflection. Although capable of measuring pure absorption at the single-particle level, this method suffers from limited dynamic range and fails when absorbers are embedded in strongly scattering backgrounds, as scattering alters the local fluence and compromises quantification. Similarly, while photoacoustic (PA) imaging has long been used to visualize absorbers in scattering tissues by detecting ultrasound waves generated from thermoelastic expansion, conventional PA detection requires direct acoustic coupling—typically via piezoelectric transducers immersed in or pressed against the sample—hindering integration with non-contact optical platforms like standard UV-VIS spectrophotometers.

The problem is further exacerbated in the context of plasmonic nanoparticles, such as gold nanorods (GNRs), which simultaneously exhibit strong absorption and scattering due to localized surface plasmon resonance. Their optical response is exquisitely sensitive to size, shape, aggregation state, and local dielectric environment. In biomedical applications—ranging from photothermal therapy to molecular sensing—accurate knowledge of the *true* absorption spectrum is critical for predicting thermal dose, optimizing excitation wavelengths, and interpreting imaging contrast. Yet, conventional UV-VIS measurements conflate absorption and scattering, leading to erroneous conclusions about nanoparticle behavior, especially when particles are internalized by cells and undergo clustering or environmental changes that alter their plasmonic coupling.

For example, when GNRs are taken up by endothelial cells, they may aggregate within endosomes, inducing plasmon coupling that shifts and splits the extinction peak. UV-VIS spectrophotometry cannot disentangle whether these spectral changes arise from altered absorption, modified scattering, or a combination thereof. This ambiguity impedes the rational design of nanoparticle-based therapeutics and diagnostics, where precise control over light-matter interactions is essential. Existing theoretical models can predict absorption and scattering for idealized nanoparticles, but real-world samples exhibit polydispersity, surface coatings, and complex microenvironments that defy accurate simulation.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a practical, non-contact, and broadly applicable spectrophotometric method capable of measuring the true optical absorption spectrum of a sample—regardless of its scattering properties—without requiring prior knowledge of the scattering background or complex instrumental modifications. Such a method would enable accurate molecular fingerprinting in turbid media, facilitate reliable characterization of nanomaterials in biologically relevant conditions, and bridge the gap between optical spectroscopy and functional applications in medicine, materials science, and analytical chemistry.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel non-contact photoacoustic (PA) spectrophotometry system and method for accurately measuring the true optical absorption spectrum of a sample, independent of the presence, magnitude, or characteristics of optical scattering within the sample or its surrounding medium. Unlike conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry, which conflates absorption and scattering into a single extinction measurement, the disclosed invention isolates the absorption component by exploiting the photoacoustic effect—the generation of ultrasound waves resulting from the thermoelastic expansion of a medium upon absorption of modulated light energy. Because only absorbed photons contribute to heat deposition and subsequent acoustic wave generation, the amplitude of the photoacoustic signal is directly proportional to the optical absorption coefficient and inherently insensitive to scattering-induced photon redirection.

The invention comprises a tunable pulsed laser source configured to deliver nanosecond-duration optical pulses across a desired spectral range (e.g., 700–900 nm, extendable to UV-VIS and near-infrared regions), an optical focusing system to concentrate the laser beam into a small-diameter spot (e.g., ≤0.5 mm) within the sample, and a non-contact, all-optical ultrasound detection system based on a fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer. The sample is contained in a specialized measurement chamber formed between two transparent quartz plates with a thin diaphragm defining the sample volume, ensuring consistent optical path length and minimal interference from container walls. Critically, the small excitation beam diameter is selected to be less than the transport mean free path of photons in the medium, thereby minimizing the dependence of local laser fluence on scattering and ensuring that the photoacoustic signal remains linearly proportional to the absorption coefficient even in highly turbid samples.

The all-optical detection scheme employs a low-power continuous-wave probe laser beam reflected from a coated surface of the quartz chamber, with minute surface displacements induced by photoacoustic pressure waves modulating the phase of the reflected probe light. This phase modulation is detected with high sensitivity using a fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer, which converts mechanical vibrations into measurable optical interference signals without requiring physical contact or acoustic coupling fluid. This non-contact detection architecture enables seamless integration with standard spectrophotometric workflows and facilitates measurements in sealed or sterile environments.

To ensure quantitative accuracy, the system is calibrated using reference standards with known absorption coefficients, such as aqueous solutions of cupric sulfate. The method further incorporates spatial averaging by translating the sample chamber during data acquisition, mitigating artifacts from local heterogeneities or particle sedimentation. Spectral data are acquired by rapidly tuning the excitation laser wavelength and recording the corresponding photoacoustic signal amplitude at each wavelength, yielding a high-fidelity absorption spectrum that reflects only the intrinsic molecular or nanomaterial absorption properties.

The invention has been demonstrated to accurately measure the absorption spectrum of molecular absorbers (e.g., cupric sulfate) in the presence of polystyrene microspheres of varying sizes and concentrations—conditions that render conventional UV-VIS measurements grossly inaccurate. It further enables precise characterization of gold nanorods (GNRs), distinguishing their true absorption from scattering contributions that dominate their extinction spectra, particularly as particle size increases. Most significantly, the method successfully quantifies the absorption spectrum of GNRs after internalization by human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), revealing plasmon-coupling-induced spectral changes that are obscured in conventional UV-VIS measurements due to combined cellular and nanoparticle scattering.

By providing a scattering-insensitive, non-contact, and quantitatively accurate means of optical absorption spectroscopy, the invention overcomes a fundamental limitation of traditional spectrophotometry and enables reliable molecular and nanomaterial analysis in complex, turbid, and biologically relevant environments. Applications include, but are not limited to, optimization of photothermal and photodynamic therapies, development of nanoparticle-based biosensors, quality control of colloidal formulations, in situ characterization of cellular uptake dynamics, and enhancement of multi-spectral photoacoustic imaging through accurate spectral unmixing.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a non-contact photoacoustic spectrophotometry system and method for determining the true optical absorption spectrum of a sample, irrespective of the presence or magnitude of optical scattering within the sample or its surrounding medium. The system leverages the photoacoustic effect, wherein absorbed light energy is converted into heat, leading to transient thermoelastic expansion and the generation of ultrasonic pressure waves. These photoacoustic (PA) signals are detected using a non-contact, all-optical interferometric technique, ensuring that the measured signal amplitude is directly proportional to the optical absorption coefficient and independent of scattering effects.

In one embodiment, the system comprises a tunable nanosecond pulsed laser source, such as a diode-pumped solid-state laser (e.g., TiSon-B model from Laser-export Ltd.), capable of emitting optical pulses in a wavelength range of approximately 700 to 900 nanometers, with a pulse repetition rate of at least 1 kilohertz and a pulse duration on the order of nanoseconds. The laser is equipped with rapid wavelength tuning capability, allowing switching between wavelengths in less than one millisecond, thereby enabling efficient spectral scanning. The output of the laser is attenuated using calibrated neutral-density filters to control pulse energy, typically in the range of 1 to 2 microjoules per pulse, to prevent nonlinear thermal effects or damage to the sample, particularly when analyzing highly absorbing nanomaterials such as gold nanoparticles.

The laser beam is focused using a lens system to a spot diameter of approximately 0.5 millimeters or less within the sample volume. The sample is contained in a specialized measurement chamber consisting of two parallel, optically transparent quartz plates separated by a thin diaphragm, typically 0.1 millimeters thick with an inner diameter of 6 millimeters. This configuration ensures a consistent and well-defined optical path length while minimizing reflections and absorption from the container walls. The small excitation beam diameter is a critical feature of the invention, as it is selected to be smaller than the transport mean free path of photons in the medium under study. The transport mean free path is defined as the average distance a photon travels before its direction is randomized due to scattering, and is given by \( l_{\text{tr}} = 1 / [\mu_s (1 - g)] \), where \( \mu_s \) is the scattering coefficient and \( g \) is the anisotropy factor of the scattering medium. By ensuring that the beam diameter is less than \( l_{\text{tr}} \), the local laser fluence within the focal volume remains largely unaffected by multiple scattering events, thereby maintaining a linear relationship between the PA signal amplitude and the absorption coefficient \( \mu_a \).

The photoacoustic signals generated within the sample propagate as ultrasonic pressure waves and cause minute displacements of the front surface of the quartz chamber. To detect these displacements without physical contact, the system employs a fiber-optic Sagnac interferometer configured as a highly sensitive, all-optical ultrasound detector. A low-power continuous-wave probe laser, typically operating at a wavelength of 1550 nanometers to minimize interference with the excitation beam, is directed onto the front surface of the quartz plate, which is coated with a reflective layer optimized for the probe wavelength. The reflected probe beam is split and sent through counter-propagating paths in a fiber loop, and recombined to produce an interference pattern. Minute surface displacements induced by the PA pressure waves modulate the optical path length of the reflected beam, resulting in a measurable phase shift in the interference signal. This phase shift is converted into an electrical signal using a balanced photodetector and digitized for further processing.

The use of a Sagnac interferometer provides several advantages: it is inherently common-mode noise rejecting, highly stable, and capable of detecting sub-picometer surface displacements, making it suitable for weak PA signals. Moreover, because both excitation and detection are entirely optical, the system operates in a non-contact manner, eliminating the need for acoustic coupling gels or direct transducer contact, which is a significant limitation of conventional piezoelectric-based PA systems. This non-contact architecture also enables measurements in sealed, sterile, or hazardous environments and facilitates integration with standard laboratory spectrophotometers.

To enhance measurement accuracy and reproducibility, the sample chamber is mounted on a computer-controlled XY translation stage. During data acquisition, the stage moves the sample at a constant velocity (e.g., 80 mm/s) while synchronizing laser firing with position, such that each laser pulse interrogates a new location within the sample. This spatial averaging mitigates the effects of local heterogeneities, particle sedimentation, or clustering, and ensures that the measured PA signal represents the bulk absorption properties of the entire sample volume. For each wavelength, approximately 5,000 laser pulses are averaged over a 30-second acquisition period, resulting in a high signal-to-noise ratio. Spectral scans are performed with a step size of 10 nanometers, covering the full operational range of the laser in approximately 10.5 minutes.

Quantitative calibration of the system is achieved by measuring reference samples with known absorption coefficients. For instance, aqueous solutions of cupric sulfate (CuSO₄·5H₂O) are used due to their well-characterized absorption spectra in the visible and near-infrared regions. The PA signal amplitude from the reference solution is recorded and used to establish a conversion factor between the measured voltage output and the absolute absorption coefficient \( \mu_a \) in units of cm⁻¹. This calibration accounts for the efficiency of PA generation, denoted by the Grüneisen parameter \( \Gamma \), which represents the fraction of absorbed energy converted into acoustic energy. For aqueous solutions, \( \Gamma \) is assumed to be constant and equal to that of water, with an accuracy of approximately 10–15% for dilute biological samples. In the case of metal nanoparticles, although the particles themselves absorb light, the high thermal conductivity of gold ensures rapid heat transfer to the surrounding medium, so the PA signal is predominantly generated by the thermal expansion of the aqueous “coat” around the nanoparticle, justifying the use of the water-based \( \Gamma \) value.

The invention has been validated through a series of experiments demonstrating its ability to accurately measure absorption in scattering media. In a first demonstration, aqueous solutions of cupric sulfate were mixed with polystyrene microspheres of diameters ranging from 0.5 to 10 micrometers at various concentrations. Conventional UV-VIS spectrophotometry showed a strong increase in apparent absorbance with increasing scatterer concentration, even though the molecular absorber concentration remained constant. In contrast, the PA method yielded absorption spectra that matched the true cupric sulfate spectrum regardless of scatterer size or concentration, confirming its scattering insensitivity.

In a second demonstration, the absorption spectra of gold nanorods (GNRs) of different sizes (~40 nm and ~100 nm in length) were measured. Numerical simulations using finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) methods confirmed that larger GNRs exhibit a higher scattering-to-absorption ratio. Conventional UV-VIS extinction spectra failed to distinguish these components, whereas the PA method accurately recovered the absorption spectra, aligning closely with theoretical predictions. This capability is crucial for applications such as photothermal therapy, where the therapeutic efficacy depends on the actual absorbed energy, not the total extinction.

In a third and most challenging demonstration, GNRs were internalized by HUVEC cells, creating a complex environment with both intrinsic nanoparticle scattering and extrinsic cellular scattering. Conventional UV-VIS measurements showed a shifted and broadened extinction peak, but could not determine whether this resulted from altered absorption or scattering. The PA method, however, revealed a distinct absorption spectrum characterized by reduced and split peaks, consistent with non-parallel plasmonic coupling of clustered GNRs within endosomes. This result underscores the invention’s ability to provide true molecular and nanomaterial fingerprints in biologically relevant, highly scattering environments.

The system is not limited to the disclosed wavelength range or sample types. The laser source can be replaced or extended to cover the full UV-VIS-NIR spectrum (e.g., 200–2500 nm) using appropriate tunable laser technologies. The detection scheme can be adapted for different sample geometries, including thin films, tissue sections, or microfluidic channels. Furthermore, the method can be integrated into existing spectrophotometers as a complementary module, providing both conventional extinction and true absorption data in a single platform.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, non-contact, and scattering-insensitive method for optical absorption spectrophotometry, overcoming a century-old limitation of conventional techniques. By enabling accurate quantification of true absorption in complex media, it opens new avenues for research and development in nanomedicine, analytical chemistry, materials science, and biomedical imaging.
