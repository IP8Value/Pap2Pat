# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates to the field of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies, specifically to agonist monoclonal antibodies that bind to and activate the Ectodysplasin A receptor (EDAR). More particularly, the invention provides novel anti-EDAR monoclonal antibodies capable of cross-reacting with EDAR from multiple mammalian and avian species, including human, dog, mouse, rat, and chicken. These antibodies function as potent agonists of EDAR signaling and are effective in the treatment of X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (XLHED), a genetic disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the EDA gene encoding Ectodysplasin A. The invention further encompasses methods for producing these antibodies, pharmaceutical compositions comprising them, and their use in restoring the development and function of ectodermal derivatives—such as hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands, teeth, lacrimal glands, and mucous-secreting glands—in subjects suffering from EDA deficiency. The disclosed antibodies exhibit high affinity for EDAR, recognize conformational epitopes primarily within cysteine-rich domain 1 (CRD1) of the extracellular region of EDAR, and demonstrate robust in vivo efficacy even when administered as monomeric, divalent immunoglobulins or F(ab′)₂ fragments. The invention is particularly relevant to veterinary and human medicine, offering a durable, single-dose or limited-dose therapeutic strategy for correcting developmental defects associated with XLHED across species.

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (XLHED) is a rare congenital disorder resulting from mutations in the EDA gene located on the X chromosome. This gene encodes Ectodysplasin A (EDA), a member of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF) ligand superfamily, which plays a critical role in the embryonic development of ectoderm-derived structures. In the absence of functional EDA, individuals—primarily males due to the X-linked inheritance pattern—exhibit a constellation of clinical features including sparse scalp and body hair, absent or dysfunctional sweat glands leading to life-threatening hyperthermia, hypodontia or anodontia, dry eyes due to lacrimal gland dysfunction, and respiratory complications stemming from defective mucous gland development in the airways. Although the EDAR receptor itself remains genetically intact in XLHED patients, the lack of its natural ligand renders the signaling pathway inactive, thereby halting the morphogenesis of skin appendages during critical developmental windows.

Prior therapeutic approaches have focused on recombinant forms of EDA, such as Fc-EDA1, a fusion protein consisting of the trimeric TNF homology domain of EDA1 linked to the Fc portion of human immunoglobulin G. While Fc-EDA1 has demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models and early-phase human trials, its utility is constrained by several limitations. First, the protein must be administered during narrow perinatal windows to achieve meaningful phenotypic correction, particularly for complex structures like teeth and glands. Second, recombinant EDA proteins often require frequent dosing due to relatively short serum half-lives, although Fc fusion extends this somewhat. Third, manufacturing complexity and stability issues can hinder large-scale production and distribution, especially in resource-limited settings.

Monoclonal antibodies represent an alternative class of biologics with well-established pharmacokinetic advantages, including long serum half-lives, high specificity, and scalable production using standard mammalian cell culture platforms. However, the development of agonist antibodies targeting members of the TNF receptor superfamily has historically been challenging. Unlike antagonistic antibodies that merely block ligand-receptor interactions, agonist antibodies must not only bind the receptor but also induce conformational changes or receptor clustering sufficient to trigger downstream signaling cascades. For many TNF receptors, such as Fas (CD95), agonist activity has been shown to depend on antibody multivalency or artificial cross-linking, raising concerns about the feasibility of generating naturally active IgG-class agonists without inducing off-target toxicity or immune complex formation.

In the context of EDAR, previous attempts to generate functional agonist antibodies were limited by poor cross-species reactivity, low potency, or dependence on aggregated antibody forms. Moreover, conventional immunization strategies in wild-type animals often fail to yield high-affinity antibodies against self-antigens like EDAR due to immune tolerance mechanisms. The inventors recognized that using EDAR-deficient mice—specifically the OVE1B strain, which carries a complete deletion of the Edar gene—as immunization hosts would circumvent central and peripheral tolerance, thereby enabling the generation of high-affinity, non-tolerized monoclonal antibodies against conserved epitopes of EDAR.

Additionally, while sequence alignment reveals that the extracellular domain of EDAR is highly conserved across vertebrates—with only minor amino acid variations between human, dog, mouse, rat, and chicken—the structural implications of these differences were unknown. It was unclear whether an antibody raised against human EDAR would retain binding and functional activity against murine or canine EDAR, which is essential for translational research bridging rodent models and large-animal or human applications. The lack of a reliable in vitro assay that faithfully recapitulates native EDAR signaling further complicated the screening process, necessitating reliance on surrogate reporter systems or labor-intensive in vivo phenotypic readouts such as tail hair regrowth in Tabby mice.

Thus, there existed a significant unmet need for a new class of therapeutic agents that could safely, effectively, and durably activate EDAR signaling across species, correct multiple facets of the XLHED phenotype—including dentition and glandular function—with minimal dosing, and be produced under standard biopharmaceutical conditions. The present invention addresses these challenges by providing a panel of novel agonist anti-EDAR monoclonal antibodies with exceptional cross-reactivity, defined epitope specificity, and proven efficacy in both murine and canine models of EDA deficiency.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides isolated monoclonal antibodies that specifically bind to the Ectodysplasin A receptor (EDAR) and act as agonists of EDAR-mediated signaling. These antibodies are capable of inducing biological responses characteristic of EDA activation, including the development and functional restoration of ectodermal derivatives such as hair follicles, sweat glands, teeth, lacrimal glands, and mucous-secreting glands in subjects with EDA deficiency. The invention further includes antigen-binding fragments of these antibodies, such as F(ab′)₂ and Fab fragments, wherein the F(ab′)₂ fragments retain agonist activity while Fab fragments do not, demonstrating that divalency is necessary and sufficient for functional agonism.

In one aspect, the invention provides monoclonal antibodies that bind to conformational epitopes within the extracellular domain of EDAR, particularly involving cysteine-rich domain 1 (CRD1), either alone or in combination with CRD2 and/or CRD3. These epitopes are conserved across multiple species, enabling the antibodies to cross-react with EDAR from human, dog, mouse, rat, and chicken. The antibodies exhibit dissociation constants (K_D) in the nanomolar range, typically between 0.5 nM and 40 nM, as determined by surface plasmon resonance using monomeric Fab fragments.

In another aspect, the invention provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising one or more of the disclosed anti-EDAR agonist antibodies in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier. These compositions are suitable for systemic administration, including intravenous, intraperitoneal, or subcutaneous routes, and are particularly effective when administered during critical developmental periods—such as perinatally or in utero—to achieve comprehensive phenotypic correction.

The invention also encompasses methods of treating X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (XLHED) in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of an anti-EDAR agonist antibody as described herein. Treatment results in the induction of hair growth, restoration of sweat gland function, normalization of tooth morphology, improvement in tear production, and development of respiratory and digestive tract mucous glands. Notably, a single dose administered shortly after birth or during late gestation is sufficient to produce long-lasting, potentially lifelong correction of multiple ectodermal defects.

Furthermore, the invention includes methods for producing the anti-EDAR monoclonal antibodies, involving immunization of EDAR-deficient non-human mammals (e.g., OVE1B mice) with recombinant EDAR-Fc fusion proteins, hybridoma generation, and selection based on both in vitro surrogate reporter assays and in vivo phenotypic rescue in EDA-deficient animal models. The resulting hybridomas secrete IgG-class antibodies, predominantly of the IgG1 isotype, with variable heavy and light chain sequences derived from a limited but diverse repertoire of immunoglobulin genes.

The invention is distinguished by the unexpected finding that monomeric, divalent IgG antibodies—without requiring aggregation or artificial cross-linking—are fully capable of activating EDAR signaling in vivo. This contrasts with prior assumptions based on other TNF receptor family members, where agonist activity often depended on higher-order oligomerization. The disclosed antibodies thus represent a novel mechanistic class of receptor agonists with favorable drug-like properties, including long serum half-lives (approximately 10–11 days in mice), high specificity, and absence of observed toxicity in treated animals.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The present invention is directed to agonist monoclonal antibodies that bind to and activate the Ectodysplasin A receptor (EDAR), a type I transmembrane protein belonging to the tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) superfamily. EDAR is expressed on the surface of epithelial cells during embryonic development and is essential for the initiation and morphogenesis of ectodermal appendages, including hair follicles, sweat glands, sebaceous glands, teeth, mammary glands, and mucous-secreting glands of the respiratory and digestive tracts. Signaling through EDAR is normally triggered by its natural ligand, Ectodysplasin A1 (EDA1), a trimeric TNF-family cytokine. In X-linked hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (XLHED), mutations in the EDA gene result in the absence or dysfunction of EDA1, leading to failure of EDAR activation and consequent developmental arrest of these structures.

The anti-EDAR antibodies of the present invention overcome this signaling deficit by directly engaging EDAR and mimicking the biological effects of EDA1. These antibodies are generated through a specialized immunization protocol using EDAR-deficient mice (e.g., OVE1B strain), which lack any endogenous EDAR expression due to a complete genomic deletion of the Edar locus. This approach bypasses immune tolerance mechanisms that would otherwise suppress the generation of high-affinity antibodies against a self-antigen, thereby enabling the isolation of potent, non-tolerized clones.

The antibodies are produced by standard hybridoma technology. Female EDAR-deficient mice are immunized subcutaneously with recombinant EDAR-Fc fusion proteins—comprising the extracellular domain of human or mouse EDAR fused to the Fc region of human immunoglobulin G—in the presence of an adjuvant such as STIMUNE. Following booster immunizations, lymph node cells are harvested and fused with myeloma cells to generate hybridomas. Supernatants from these hybridomas are screened for EDAR binding by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and for functional agonist activity using two complementary assays: (1) a surrogate in vitro reporter system in which Jurkat T cells are engineered to express chimeric receptors consisting of the extracellular domain of human or mouse EDAR fused to the intracellular death domain of Fas (CD95); upon EDAR activation, these cells undergo apoptosis, providing a quantifiable readout of agonist potency; and (2) an in vivo rescue assay in newborn EDA-deficient Tabby mice (Eda^Ta/Y or Eda^Ta/Eda^Ta), which completely lack tail hairs; intraperitoneal administration of candidate antibodies within 24 hours of birth induces dose-dependent regrowth of tail hair, serving as a sensitive and physiologically relevant indicator of EDAR pathway activation.

Selected hybridomas are subcloned by limiting dilution to ensure monoclonality and adapted to serum-free culture conditions for scalable antibody production. Antibodies are purified from conditioned medium using protein G affinity chromatography, yielding preparations of high purity and integrity, as confirmed by SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions and native gel electrophoresis.

Structurally, the anti-EDAR antibodies are full-length immunoglobulins of the IgG class, predominantly IgG1, with distinct heavy and light chain variable regions. Sequencing of cDNA from hybridoma cells reveals usage of various immunoglobulin heavy chain variable (IGHV) and light chain variable (IGLV) gene segments, though certain combinations recur across independently derived clones, suggesting convergent evolution toward optimal EDAR recognition. The antibodies recognize conformational epitopes localized within the extracellular domain of EDAR, which comprises three cysteine-rich domains (CRD1, CRD2, CRD3) and a stalk region. Epitope mapping using truncated EDAR-Fc constructs demonstrates that all agonist antibodies require CRD1 for binding; some recognize CRD1 alone, others require CRD1 in combination with CRD2, and at least one binds an epitope spanning CRD1–CRD3, indicating the existence of at least three distinct epitope classes. Importantly, antibodies that bind CRD1 retain reactivity with denatured EDAR in Western blot under reducing conditions, whereas those requiring higher-order structure do not, confirming the conformational nature of most epitopes.

A key feature of the invention is the cross-species reactivity of the antibodies. Flow cytometry analysis using 293T cells transfected with glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored EDAR from human, dog, mouse, rat, and chicken demonstrates that the majority of the disclosed antibodies bind EDAR across all five species. This broad reactivity stems from the high degree of sequence conservation in the EDAR extracellular domain—particularly within CRD1—among vertebrates. Such cross-reactivity enables the use of the same therapeutic antibody in multiple preclinical models and facilitates translation to human therapy.

Functional characterization reveals that the antibodies exhibit nanomolar affinity for human EDAR, with equilibrium dissociation constants (K_D) ranging from 0.5 to 40 nM, as measured by surface plasmon resonance (SPR) using monomeric Fab fragments generated by ficin digestion and size-exclusion chromatography. Kinetic analysis shows variation in association (k_a) and dissociation (k_d) rates, with some antibodies displaying biphasic dissociation kinetics, though this does not correlate with enhanced agonist potency.

Critically, the invention demonstrates that divalency—not multivalency or aggregation—is both necessary and sufficient for in vivo agonist activity. Purified monomeric IgG (confirmed by gel filtration) retains full activity in the Tabby mouse tail hair assay, with EC₅₀ values comparable to recombinant Fc-EDA1 (approximately 0.1 mg/kg). Moreover, F(ab′)₂ fragments, which retain two antigen-binding sites but lack the Fc region, are equally effective when administered neonatally, whereas monovalent Fab fragments are inactive—even when co-administered at later time points where their shorter half-life cannot account for the lack of effect. This establishes that bivalent engagement of EDAR is the minimal requirement for signal activation, likely by inducing receptor dimerization or conformational rearrangement.

Therapeutic efficacy extends beyond hair follicle rescue. Prenatal administration of anti-EDAR antibodies to pregnant Tabby dams results in transplacental transfer and comprehensive correction of the XLHED phenotype in offspring, including restoration of molars to near-wild-type morphology, development of tracheal mucous glands, hair behind the ears, and normalized ocular appearance. These effects are durable, persisting for over two years in treated mice. Similarly, in EDA-deficient dogs—a large-animal model that closely mirrors human XLHED—single intravenous doses of anti-EDAR antibody (e.g., 10 mg/kg at postnatal day 2) correct dentition, improve lacrimation, and prevent life-threatening respiratory infections and dry eye disease, with no observed adverse effects.

Thus, the anti-EDAR agonist antibodies of the present invention represent a breakthrough in the treatment of ectodermal dysplasias, offering a potent, long-acting, cross-species therapeutic modality that addresses the root cause of XLHED by directly activating the intact EDAR receptor.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

Generation and initial screening of anti-EDAR monoclonal antibodies began with the immunization of female EDAR-deficient OVE1B mice. These mice, which harbor a complete deletion of the Edar gene, were selected to avoid immune tolerance to EDAR epitopes. Each mouse received a primary subcutaneous injection at the base of the tail containing 150 µg of either human EDAR-Fc (hEDAR-Fc) or mouse EDAR-Fc (mEDAR-Fc), formulated in a 1:1 mixture of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and STIMUNE adjuvant, in a total volume of 200 µl. Between days 10 and 14 post-immunization, mice were given a booster injection of 150 µg of the same antigen in 150 µl of PBS/STIMUNE. On day 30, serum samples were collected and tested by ELISA for anti-EDAR antibody titers. Mice exhibiting strong reactivity were given a final intravenous boost of 150 µg of antigen in PBS on day 40. Three days later, inguinal and axillary lymph nodes were harvested, and single-cell suspensions were prepared. These lymphocytes were fused with SP2/0 myeloma cells using polyethylene glycol according to standard protocols. The resulting hybridomas were cultured in 96-well plates containing complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, over a feeder layer of thioglycollate-elicited mouse peritoneal macrophages. After 24 hours, the medium was replaced with hypoxanthine/aminopterin/thymidine (HAT) selection medium to eliminate unfused myeloma cells. Hybridoma supernatants were screened by ELISA for binding to hEDAR-Fc coated at 1 µg/ml. Positive wells were expanded and subjected to a secondary screen for functional agonist activity using two assays: first, a surrogate in vitro cytotoxicity assay employing Jurkat cells stably expressing chimeric mEDAR:Fas or hEDAR:Fas receptors; second, an in vivo tail hair rescue assay in newborn Tabby (Eda^Ta/Y) pups. Hybridomas showing activity in either or both assays were subcloned twice by limiting dilution to ensure monoclonality. Subsequently, clones were gradually weaned off macrophage feeders and HAT medium and adapted to serum-free Opti-MEM for large-scale antibody production. Antibodies were purified from conditioned supernatants using protein G-Sepharose affinity chromatography, yielding preparations of >95% purity as assessed by Coomassie-stained SDS-PAGE under reducing conditions.

### Example 2

To characterize the molecular identity of the anti-EDAR antibodies, RNA was extracted from representative hybridoma cell lines using the RNeasy Mini Kit. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed using oligo(dT) primers and reverse transcriptase. The variable regions of the heavy and light immunoglobulin chains were amplified by PCR using degenerate primers specific for murine V_H and V_L gene families. Amplified products were purified and sequenced bidirectionally. Sequence analysis using IMGT/V-QUEST software revealed the usage of multiple IGHV and IGLV gene segments. For instance, antibody 1 utilized IGHV1-72*01 and IGLV1-11*01, while antibody 3 used IGHV1-72*01 and IGLV2-4*01, differing only in the light chain. Antibody 8, generated from a mouse immunized with mEDAR-Fc, shared identical V_H and V_L sequences with antibody 1 (over 90% nucleotide identity), despite originating from a different immunization cohort. This convergence suggests selective pressure for specific paratope configurations capable of EDAR agonism. Somatic hypermutation was evident in complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), particularly CDR-H3 and CDR-L3, contributing to affinity maturation. All identified antibodies were of the IgG1 isotype, as confirmed by isotype-specific ELISA using anti-mouse IgG subclass detection reagents.

### Example 3

Epitope mapping was performed to define the binding sites of the anti-EDAR antibodies on the EDAR extracellular domain. A panel of truncated EDAR-Fc fusion proteins was generated, including constructs containing: (i) full-length extracellular domain (amino acids 29–183), (ii) CRD1 only (aa 29–75), (iii) CRD1+CRD2 (aa 29–125), and (iv) CRD2+CRD3 (aa 76–183). These constructs, along with a control B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA)-Fc, were expressed in HEK 293T cells and captured from supernatants onto ELISA plates pre-coated with goat anti-human Fc antibody. Binding of each anti-EDAR antibody (at 1 µg/ml) was detected using peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse IgG. Results showed three distinct binding patterns: Group A antibodies (e.g., 2, 4, 6) bound only to constructs containing CRD1, including CRD1 alone; Group B antibodies (e.g., 1, 3, 8) required CRD1+CRD2 but did not bind CRD1 alone; and Group C (antibody 9) bound only the full-length construct, suggesting a conformational epitope dependent on the spatial arrangement of all three CRDs. None of the antibodies bound constructs lacking CRD1, confirming its essential role. To assess recognition of linear versus conformational epitopes, Western blot analysis was performed under reducing and non-reducing conditions. Only Group A antibodies (those binding CRD1 alone) recognized denatured hEDAR-Fc under reducing conditions, indicating that their epitopes are linear or locally folded, whereas Groups B and C required native disulfide-bonded structure.

### Example 4

Cross-reactivity of the anti-EDAR antibodies was evaluated by flow cytometry. HEK 293T cells were co-transfected with plasmids encoding enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) and GPI-anchored EDAR from human, dog, mouse, rat, or chicken. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were stained with 4 µg/ml of each anti-EDAR antibody, followed by phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody. GFP-positive cells were analyzed by FACScan flow cytometry. Thirteen of fifteen tested antibodies showed strong binding to EDAR from all five species. Antibody 5 exhibited weak reactivity with chicken EDAR, while antibody 15—selected for human specificity—bound only human and dog EDAR, consistent with sequence divergence in CRD1 between primates/carnivores and rodents/aves. This broad cross-reactivity aligns with the high conservation of CRD1: human and dog EDAR differ by only 4 amino acids in the extracellular domain, human and mouse by 10, and human and chicken by 13, with most variations located outside CRD1. The data confirm that the agonist antibodies target evolutionarily conserved functional domains of EDAR.

### Example 5

Affinity measurements were conducted using surface plasmon resonance (SPR) on a Biacore T100 instrument. Monomeric Fab fragments were prepared by digesting purified IgG with immobilized ficin for 72 hours at 37°C, followed by removal of Fc and undigested IgG via protein A chromatography. The Fab-containing flow-through was concentrated and fractionated by Superdex 200 size-exclusion chromatography to isolate monomeric species. Human EDAR-Fc was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip via anti-human Fc capture. Serial dilutions of Fab (ranging from 0.5 to 100 nM) were injected over the chip surface at 50 µl/min for 90 seconds, followed by buffer wash. Sensorgrams were analyzed using a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to derive association (k_a) and dissociation (k_d) rate constants and equilibrium dissociation constants (K_D). Affinities ranged from 0.5 nM (antibody 12) to 40 nM (antibody 11). Antibodies 7 and 14 displayed biphasic dissociation curves, suggesting heterogeneous binding populations or conformational changes post-binding, but these were not pursued further due to modest functional activity. The correlation between affinity and in vitro potency was weak, indicating that factors beyond simple binding strength—such as epitope location or induced receptor conformation—govern agonist efficacy.

### Example 6

Functional comparison of antibodies in vitro and in vivo revealed a dichotomy in activity profiles. In the mEDAR:Fas Jurkat cytotoxicity assay, only antibodies 1, 3, 8, 10, and 12 induced significant cell death, with EC₅₀ values between 10 and 100 ng/ml, whereas Fc-EDA1 exhibited an EC₅₀ of ~1 ng/ml. In the hEDAR:Fas assay, activity was restricted to antibodies 1, 3, 10, and 12. In stark contrast, all tested antibodies (1–14) rescued tail hair growth in newborn Tabby mice when administered intraperitoneally within 24 hours of birth. Dose-response curves yielded EC₅₀ values between 0.1 and 0.7 mg/kg, with antibody 11 being the least potent (EC₅₀ ~3 mg/kg). Sweat gland induction paralleled tail hair rescue. The discrepancy suggests that the Fas-based reporter system, while useful for detecting strong agonists, may not fully recapitulate native EDAR signaling dynamics in developing epithelia. Notably, antibodies active in vitro tended to have slower dissociation rates, implying that sustained receptor engagement may be critical for triggering the apoptotic readout in Jurkat cells but less so for developmental signaling in vivo.

### Example 7

To determine the valency requirements for agonist activity, purified antibody 1 was fractionated by size-exclusion chromatography to isolate the monomeric peak, confirmed by native PAGE. This monomeric IgG retained full activity in the Tabby tail hair assay, with an EC₅₀ of ~0.1 mg/kg, comparable to unfractionated antibody and superior to Fc-EDA1 (EC₅₀ ~0.05 mg/kg). In parallel, F(ab′)₂ and Fab fragments were generated from antibody 1. Newborn Tabby pups received a single dose of F(ab′)₂ (0.5 mg/kg) or Fab (1 mg/kg) on postnatal day 1. Only F(ab′)₂ induced tail hair growth. To rule out pharmacokinetic differences, a second experiment administered F(ab′)₂ on day 3—near the latest window for ventral tail hair induction—along with a 10-fold molar excess of Fab. The Fab fragment completely inhibited F(ab′)₂ activity, confirming that the lack of Fab agonism is due to monovalency, not rapid clearance. These results establish that bivalent binding is necessary and sufficient for EDAR activation in vivo.

### Example 8

Prenatal treatment was evaluated to assess correction of complex structures like teeth and glands. Pregnant Tabby (Eda^Ta/Eda^Ta) females were injected intravenously with 400 µg of antibody 3 on embryonic days 13 and 20 (E13/E20). Offspring were analyzed at 6 months of age. Treated mice exhibited full coats of body hair, hair behind the ears, functional sweat glands (confirmed by pilocarpine-induced sweating), and Alcian blue-positive mucous glands in the trachea—features absent in untreated Tabby controls. Dental morphology was strikingly normalized: micro-CT imaging revealed molars with proper cusp patterning and enamel thickness, nearly indistinguishable from wild-type littermates. Ocular abnormalities, such as reduced eyelid opening, were also corrected. One treated mouse remained phenotypically normal at 2.4 years of age, demonstrating lifelong efficacy from transient embryonic exposure. These findings indicate that EDAR activation during critical developmental windows can permanently restore ectodermal organogenesis.

### Example 9

Translation to a large-animal model was achieved using EDA-deficient dogs, which naturally develop XLHED with phenotypes closely mirroring human disease, including missing teeth, dry eye, and recurrent respiratory infections. Three affected male puppies received a single intravenous dose of antibody 3: two at postnatal day 2 (10 mg/kg) and one at day 14 (7 mg/kg). No acute adverse reactions were observed. At adulthood (~1 year), dental radiographs showed complete dentition in day-2-treated dogs, including premolars and molars with normal crown morphology—superior to historical Fc-EDA1-treated dogs, which often lacked premolars. The day-14-treated dog developed molars but remained premolar-deficient, indicating a narrower therapeutic window for tooth development. Schirmer tear tests revealed improved lacrimation in two of three dogs; the third (E237) showed no improvement but did not develop keratoconjunctivitis sicca, suggesting compensation by other ocular surface mechanisms. Histopathology at necropsy (1.6–2.4 years) confirmed the presence of mucous glands in trachea, bronchi, and esophagus, with extent correlating inversely with treatment age. Critically, none of the treated dogs suffered pneumonia or required ophthalmic intervention, unlike untreated XLHED dogs. Complete blood counts and serum biochemistry remained normal, confirming safety. This example validates the cross-species efficacy and clinical potential of anti-EDAR agonist antibodies for human XLHED therapy.
