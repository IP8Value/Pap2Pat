# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Symmetric key cryptography serves as a foundational pillar of modern secure communication, enabling two or more parties to exchange information confidentially and with assurance of authenticity over channels that may be subject to eavesdropping, tampering, or other adversarial interference. The dual objectives of confidentiality and integrity are typically achieved through authenticated encryption (AE), a cryptographic primitive that simultaneously provides both encryption and authentication in an integrated manner. Over the past several decades, numerous AE schemes have been proposed, standardized, and deployed across a wide range of applications—from consumer electronics and internet protocols to military and governmental systems. However, many existing constructions suffer from significant limitations that render them suboptimal for high-assurance environments where performance, security robustness, and ease of implementation must be carefully balanced.

One persistent challenge in the design of AE algorithms lies in the trade-off between computational efficiency and cryptographic strength. Traditional approaches often require multiple passes over the same data block—first to encrypt and then to authenticate—or rely on generic composition techniques that combine separate encryption and message authentication code (MAC) primitives. Such methods not only incur substantial performance overhead but also introduce additional complexity in key management, frequently necessitating the use of two independent secret keys: one for encryption and another for authentication. This dual-key requirement exacerbates the already difficult problem of secure key generation, distribution, storage, and lifecycle management, particularly in resource-constrained or embedded systems where cryptographic agility and minimal operational footprint are paramount.

Moreover, certain historically prominent AE schemes have been found vulnerable to sophisticated cryptanalytic attacks or exhibit usability pitfalls that can lead to incorrect deployment and consequent security failures. For instance, the Phelix cipher, once considered a promising candidate for efficient authenticated encryption, was later shown to be susceptible to differential attacks under specific conditions. Similarly, Counter with CBC-MAC (CCM), while standardized and widely used, imposes strict formatting constraints on input data and requires careful nonce management to avoid catastrophic security degradation. These shortcomings underscore the need for a new class of AE algorithms that are not only provably secure under well-established cryptographic assumptions but also inherently resistant to misuse and amenable to efficient hardware realization.

In parallel with these practical concerns, there exists a strategic imperative within defense and intelligence communities to employ cryptographic algorithms that are not publicly scrutinized to the same degree as open academic standards. While public peer review is generally beneficial for identifying weaknesses in cryptographic designs, it also enables adversaries—including nation-state actors—to invest significant resources into cryptanalysis with the goal of discovering exploitable vulnerabilities. Consequently, government and military applications often favor proprietary or customizable cryptographic primitives that can be uniquely instantiated per user, platform, or mission without requiring exhaustive revalidation of each variant’s security properties. This approach leverages the principle of algorithmic diversity to increase the cost and difficulty of large-scale cryptanalytic efforts, thereby enhancing overall system resilience.

The advent of the sponge and duplex constructions has provided a powerful framework for building versatile cryptographic primitives that unify hashing, encryption, and authentication within a single, mathematically coherent structure. Originally popularized by the KECCAK family of functions—which was selected as the basis for the SHA-3 hash standard—the sponge construction generalizes fixed-output hash functions to support arbitrary-length inputs and outputs through a two-phase process: absorption and squeezing. Its security is tightly linked to the capacity parameter, which determines the resistance against collision and preimage attacks, while the rate governs throughput performance. The duplex construction extends this model by maintaining internal state across successive operations, allowing interleaved absorption and emission of data in a single continuous stream. This property makes duplex-based designs particularly well-suited for online authenticated encryption, where headers (associated data) and payloads (plaintext) can be processed incrementally without buffering entire messages.

Despite these conceptual advances, practical instantiations of sponge- and duplex-based AE schemes have often relied on relatively small substitution boxes (S-boxes)—typically 4-bit or 8-bit mappings—as the primary source of nonlinearity. While such S-boxes are computationally lightweight and facilitate compact hardware implementations, they inherently limit the algebraic complexity and diffusion characteristics of the underlying permutation. As a result, achieving sufficient resistance against linear and differential cryptanalysis typically requires a larger number of rounds, which directly impacts latency and throughput. Furthermore, the design space for customizing these constructions remains constrained by the limited availability of efficiently implementable large S-boxes and the lack of systematic methodologies for modifying core components—such as bitwise permutations, mixing layers, and round constants—without compromising provable security guarantees.

Accordingly, there remains a compelling need in the art for an authenticated encryption algorithm that integrates the structural elegance and provable security of the duplex construction with novel architectural features that enhance both cryptographic strength and customization flexibility. Such an algorithm should leverage large bijective S-boxes to maximize nonlinearity and algebraic complexity while minimizing hardware area overhead, support seamless integration of key and initialization vector material through mute and blank duplexing calls, and provide clear guidelines for safe parameterization so that unique instantiations can be generated for different users or applications without necessitating full-scale cryptanalysis of each variant. The present invention addresses these unmet needs by introducing a highly customizable, hardware-friendly authenticated encryption scheme built upon a 512-bit duplex construction employing 16×16 S-boxes derived from finite field inversion and affine transformation, a high-order deranging bitwise permutation, a maximally diffusive mixer based on matrix multiplication in GF(2¹⁶), and programmable round constants—all operating within a rigorously analyzed security margin against known cryptanalytic techniques.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a novel authenticated encryption algorithm that combines provable security, high performance, and extensive customization capabilities within a unified duplex-based framework. At its core, the invention utilizes a 512-bit internal state divided into a 128-bit rate portion and a 384-bit capacity portion, enabling efficient processing of data blocks while maintaining a high security level against generic attacks. The algorithm supports two key lengths—128 bits and 256 bits—with corresponding round counts of 10 and 16, respectively, ensuring that the computational complexity of any successful cryptanalytic attack exceeds that of brute-force key search under established security models.

The underlying permutation function, which forms the cryptographic heart of the invention, is structured as an iterated round function comprising four distinct steps: a substitution layer using thirty-two identical 16×16 bijective S-boxes, a bitwise permutation layer designed to maximize long-range diffusion and eliminate fixed points, a mixing layer based on invertible 2×2 matrix multiplication over GF(2¹⁶) to achieve optimal differential and linear branch numbers, and a round constant addition step that disrupts symmetry and prevents slide attacks. Each of these components has been meticulously engineered to balance cryptographic strength with hardware efficiency, leveraging mathematical structures that admit compact circuit implementations without sacrificing security margins.

A key innovation of the present invention lies in its support for safe and effective customization. Unlike conventional cryptographic algorithms that are rigidly defined and offer little room for variation, the disclosed scheme permits authorized users to generate unique instantiations by modifying specific elements of the algorithm—such as the initial inner state value, the choice of S-box, the selection of bitwise permutation, the configuration of the mixing matrix, or the sequence of round constants—while remaining within analytically verified bounds that preserve resistance to differential, linear, and algebraic attacks. This capability is particularly valuable in high-assurance environments where algorithmic diversity is desired to mitigate the risk of systemic compromise due to cryptanalytic breakthroughs against a single standardized primitive.

The S-box employed in the preferred embodiment is derived from multiplicative inversion in the finite field GF(2¹⁶) modulo the irreducible polynomial p(x) = x¹⁶ + x⁵ + x³ + x + 1, followed by an affine transformation represented by a fixed 16×16 binary matrix and a constant vector. This construction ensures bijectivity, high nonlinearity, and low differential uniformity, while also enabling efficient hardware realization through arithmetic circuits rather than prohibitively large lookup tables. Notably, the maximum differential probability of the S-box is 2⁻¹⁴, and its worst-case linear bias is 2⁻⁸, both of which contribute to strong resistance against statistical cryptanalysis when combined with the high branch number of the mixing layer.

The bitwise permutation is specifically chosen to satisfy five critical properties: (a) all outputs from any single S-box are distributed to sixteen distinct mixers, ensuring maximal interaction between substitution and mixing layers; (b) the permutation is a derangement with no fixed points, preventing trivial invariant subspaces; (c) it has a high order (specifically 32), meaning it does not cycle within the number of rounds used; (d) every bit has the same order as the overall permutation, eliminating low-order bits that could be exploited in rotational or slide attacks; and (e) it is definable by an affine function, facilitating formal verification and efficient implementation. These constraints collectively ensure that differences or linear approximations propagate rapidly across the entire state, thwarting localized cryptanalytic strategies.

The mixing layer operates on pairs of 16-bit words using a symmetric, invertible 2×2 matrix over GF(2¹⁶) defined modulo the irreducible polynomial q(x) = x¹⁶ + x⁵ + x³ + x² + 1. This matrix is selected to achieve a differential and linear branch number of three—the theoretical maximum for a 2×2 transformation—thereby guaranteeing that any non-zero input difference affects at least two output words, and vice versa. Formal verification using SAT solvers confirms that the mixer satisfies this property, ensuring that the minimum number of active S-boxes over two rounds is three, which directly translates into exponential growth of attack complexity with each additional round.

Round constants are generated deterministically using the SHA3-512 hash function applied to the ASCII representation of the round index, yielding unique, pseudorandom 512-bit values that break symmetries and prevent slide attacks. However, the invention explicitly allows alternative constant generation methods, provided they produce distinct, asymmetric values for each round.

In operation, the algorithm initializes a duplex object with a zero state (or a user-defined inner state for customized variants), absorbs the secret key—and optionally an initialization vector—through one or more mute duplexing calls, processes associated data (headers) via additional mute calls, encrypts plaintext body blocks by XORing them with keystream generated during duplexing calls that absorb the plaintext, and finally produces an authentication tag through a blank call after all inputs have been processed. Domain separation, if required, may be implemented externally through frame bits or similar mechanisms, though the core algorithm assumes proper higher-layer handling of input boundaries.

The security of the invention is formally grounded in the generic security of the keyed duplex construction, which reduces to the security of the underlying permutation. Rigorous analysis demonstrates resistance to differential cryptanalysis beyond six rounds for 128-bit keys and beyond ten rounds for 256-bit keys, with the selected round counts providing a substantial safety margin. Similarly, linear cryptanalysis is rendered infeasible due to the combination of low S-box bias and high branch number, with attack complexity exceeding brute-force thresholds well before the specified round counts. Algebraic attacks are deemed impractical due to the high algebraic degree and complexity of the 16-bit S-box, which far exceeds that of smaller S-boxes used in prior art.

Thus, the present invention delivers a secure, efficient, and customizable authenticated encryption solution ideally suited for hardware implementation in applications demanding both high performance and algorithmic uniqueness, including but not limited to secure communications, trusted computing platforms, and classified information systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to an authenticated encryption algorithm based on a modified duplex construction, wherein the cryptographic strength, performance, and customization potential are significantly enhanced through the integration of large 16×16 bijective S-boxes, a carefully designed bitwise permutation, a maximally diffusive mixing layer, and programmable round constants. The following detailed description sets forth the complete specification of the algorithm, including its structural components, operational procedures, security parameters, and permissible customizations, all of which are essential to enable any person skilled in the art to make and use the invention without undue experimentation.

The algorithm operates on a 512-bit internal state, denoted as S, which is partitioned into two segments: a 128-bit outer state (rate r = 128) and a 384-bit inner state (capacity c = 384). This division aligns with the principles of the sponge and duplex constructions, where the rate determines the amount of data that can be absorbed or squeezed per invocation of the underlying permutation function f, and the capacity governs the security level against generic attacks. The total state size b = r + c = 512 bits is chosen to provide a balanced trade-off between throughput and security, supporting both 128-bit and 256-bit secret keys while maintaining a consistent interface across key sizes.

The secret key K, which may be either 128 or 256 bits in length, is absorbed into the duplex state during an initialization phase using one or more mute calls to the duplexing function. A mute call is defined as an invocation of the duplexing operation that accepts input data but produces no output. If the key length exceeds the rate (which it does for the 256-bit case), it is split into consecutive 128-bit blocks, each absorbed in a separate mute call. Optionally, an initialization vector (IV) may be concatenated with the key (i.e., K || IV) prior to absorption, provided that the combined length does not exceed practical limits imposed by the application context. The IV, if used, must be unique per encryption session to ensure semantic security.

Following key absorption, the algorithm processes associated data A = (A₀, A₁, …), which consists of arbitrary-length header information that must be authenticated but not encrypted. Each block of associated data is absorbed via mute calls in the same manner as the key. Subsequently, the plaintext body B = (B₀, B₁, …) is processed in blocks of up to 128 bits. For each body block Bⱼ, the algorithm performs a duplexing call that absorbs Bⱼ and simultaneously produces a keystream block Zⱼ of equal length. The ciphertext block Cⱼ is then computed as Cⱼ = Bⱼ ⊕ Zⱼ, where ⊕ denotes bitwise XOR. Finally, after all header and body inputs have been processed, an authentication tag T is generated by performing a blank call—a duplexing invocation with no input and a specified output length (typically 128 or 256 bits)—which squeezes the tag from the current state.

The underlying permutation function f, which is applied once per duplexing call (after input absorption and before output squeezing), constitutes the cryptographic core of the invention. It is defined as an iterated round function consisting of Nᵣ rounds, where Nᵣ = 10 for 128-bit keys and Nᵣ = 16 for 256-bit keys. Each round comprises four sequential operations: Substitution, Bitwise Permutation, Mix, and Add Round Constant. These operations are applied in-place to the 512-bit state S, which is logically represented as a union of a 512-element bit array S.bits and a 32-element array of 16-bit words S.words, facilitating both bit-level and word-level manipulations.

The Substitution step applies a bricklayer transformation using thirty-two identical 16×16 bijective S-boxes, one per 16-bit word of the state. The S-box is constructed as follows: given a 16-bit input x ∈ GF(2¹⁶), interpreted as a polynomial modulo the irreducible polynomial p(x) = x¹⁶ + x⁵ + x³ + x + 1, the output is computed as y = A · x⁻¹ + v if x ≠ 0, and y = A · 0 + v = v if x = 0, where x⁻¹ denotes multiplicative inversion in GF(2¹⁶), A is a fixed 16×16 binary matrix, and v is a 16-bit constant vector. The specific matrix A and vector v are chosen to minimize hardware gate count while maximizing cryptographic criteria such as nonlinearity and differential uniformity. In the preferred embodiment, this S-box requires only 1238 XOR gates and 144 AND gates for hardware implementation, making it significantly more area-efficient than random 16-bit lookup tables, which would require 131,072 bits of memory.

The Bitwise Permutation step rearranges all 512 bits of the state according to a fixed deranging permutation π: {0, 1, …, 511} → {0, 1, …, 511}. The permutation π is defined by the formula π(x) = (17x + 3) mod 512 for each bit position x. This affine mapping ensures that π is a single-cycle permutation of order 32 (since 17³² ≡ 1 mod 512 and no smaller exponent satisfies this congruence), has no fixed points (as 17x + 3 ≢ x mod 512 for any x), and distributes the output bits of each S-box uniformly across all 32 mixers in the subsequent layer. These properties collectively prevent the formation of invariant subspaces, ensure rapid diffusion of local changes, and maximize the number of active S-boxes in differential and linear trails.

The Mix step operates on adjacent pairs of 16-bit words (S.words[2i], S.words[2i+1]) for i = 0 to 15, applying a linear transformation defined by multiplication with a 2×2 matrix M over GF(2¹⁶)/q(x), where q(x) = x¹⁶ + x⁵ + x³ + x² + 1 is another irreducible polynomial. The matrix M is symmetric and invertible, specifically:

M = [[1, x],
     [x, 1]]

where x denotes the field element corresponding to the polynomial x (i.e., the bit pattern 0000000000000010). Multiplication by x in GF(2¹⁶) is implemented as a left rotation by one bit followed by conditional XOR with the reduction polynomial’s lower coefficients, requiring only three XOR gates per multiplication. The symmetry of M ensures that its differential and linear branch numbers are equal, and formal verification confirms that both are equal to three—the maximum possible for a 2×2 matrix—meaning that any non-zero input difference affects both output words, and any non-zero output mask depends on both input words.

The Add Round Constant step XORs the entire 512-bit state with a round-specific constant RCᵢ, where i ranges from 0 to Nᵣ−1. Each RCᵢ is computed as RCᵢ = SHA3-512(ASCII(i)), where ASCII(i) is the byte string representation of the decimal digits of i (e.g., ASCII(5) = "5", ASCII(12) = "12"). This method guarantees that all round constants are distinct, pseudorandom, and lack exploitable algebraic structure, thereby thwarting slide attacks and other symmetry-based cryptanalytic techniques.

Customization of the algorithm is permitted along five dimensions, each governed by explicit security constraints:

1. **State Initialization**: The inner state (bits 128 to 511) may be initialized to any 384-bit value instead of zero, provided that the value is kept secret or managed securely. This allows per-user or per-application instantiation without altering the core logic.

2. **S-box Selection**: Alternative 16×16 S-boxes may be used, provided they are bijective, derived from finite field inversion plus affine transformation, and exhibit differential uniformity ≤ 2⁻¹⁴ and nonlinearity ≥ 32768 − 2⁷. Any such S-box must undergo formal evaluation using tools like Eval16BitSbox to confirm compliance.

3. **Bitwise Permutation**: Any permutation satisfying the five properties listed above (derangement, high order, no low-order bits, full S-box output dispersion, affine definability) may be substituted. A catalog of such permutations is available in the referenced literature.

4. **Mixer Configuration**: Alternative 2×2 matrices over GF(2¹⁶)/q(x) may be employed, provided they are symmetric, invertible, and yield differential and linear branch numbers of three. Verification must be performed using SAT solvers as described.

5. **Round Constants**: Constants may be generated by any method that ensures uniqueness per round and absence of symmetry (e.g., cryptographic hash of a secret seed plus round index). Constants must not be reused across rounds or predictable from external observations.

Security analysis confirms that the base configuration resists differential cryptanalysis with a margin of four rounds beyond the theoretical minimum (six rounds for 128-bit keys), and similarly for linear cryptanalysis. Algebraic attacks are infeasible due to the high algebraic degree (≥ 15) of the S-box and the iterative application of nonlinear and linear layers. The generic security of the duplex construction ensures that the overall scheme achieves min(2¹²⁸, 2²⁵⁶) security for 128-bit and 256-bit keys, respectively, under the assumption that f is a pseudorandom permutation.

In summary, the invention provides a complete, secure, and customizable authenticated encryption algorithm suitable for high-performance hardware implementation, fulfilling a critical need in modern cryptographic systems where both provable security and operational flexibility are required.
