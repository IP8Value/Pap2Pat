# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The field of photovoltaic energy conversion has witnessed continuous advancements aimed at enhancing the efficiency and cost-effectiveness of solar cells, particularly those fabricated from multi-crystalline silicon (mc-Si). Among the various strategies employed to improve solar cell performance, surface texturing has emerged as a critical technique for minimizing optical losses due to reflection and maximizing light absorption within the semiconductor substrate. In conventional mc-Si solar cells, a significant portion of incident sunlight is reflected from the planar surface of the silicon wafer, thereby reducing the amount of photons available for conversion into electrical carriers. To mitigate this issue, surface texturing introduces micro- or nano-scale topographical features that promote multiple internal reflections, effectively increasing the optical path length of light within the absorber layer and enhancing the probability of photon absorption.

Historically, isotropic wet chemical etching using acidic solutions—typically mixtures of hydrofluoric acid (HF) and nitric acid (HNO₃)—has been widely adopted in industrial settings for texturing mc-Si wafers. This method produces relatively smooth, bowl-like surface morphologies that offer moderate anti-reflective properties with reflectance values typically ranging between 20% and 30% across the visible spectrum. While this approach is advantageous due to its simplicity, low cost, and compatibility with large-scale manufacturing processes, it falls short of achieving the ultra-low reflectance levels necessary for high-efficiency photovoltaic devices. Consequently, researchers have explored alternative texturing methodologies capable of generating more complex and optically superior surface structures.

Reactive ion etching (RIE), a plasma-based dry etching technique, has gained attention as a promising route to achieve sub-wavelength surface textures with exceptional light-trapping capabilities. Unlike isotropic wet etching, which relies solely on chemical reactions, RIE combines both chemical and physical etching mechanisms through the synergistic action of reactive plasma species and energetic ion bombardment. Under carefully controlled process parameters, RIE can produce highly irregular, nanoscale surface features—including sharp needles, deep pits, and dense protrusions—that collectively yield extremely low specular and diffuse reflectance, often below 10% across the entire solar spectrum and even below 1% in optimized configurations. Such textures significantly enhance light incoupling, particularly in the short-wavelength region (below 600 nm), where silicon exhibits high absorption coefficients but is also most susceptible to front-surface recombination losses.

Despite its optical advantages, the application of RIE for mc-Si texturing presents significant challenges related to material integrity and electronic quality. The energetic ion flux inherent to the RIE process can induce substantial lattice damage in the near-surface region of the silicon wafer, creating defect states that act as recombination centers for photogenerated charge carriers. These defects increase the surface recombination velocity (SRV), thereby degrading key electrical parameters of the solar cell, most notably the open-circuit voltage (V<sub>oc</sub>) and fill factor (FF). As a result, even though RIE-textured cells often exhibit a marked improvement in short-circuit current density (I<sub>sc</sub>) due to enhanced light absorption, the net gain in power conversion efficiency may be offset—or even negated—by the concurrent deterioration in V<sub>oc</sub> and FF. This trade-off between optical gain and electrical loss has historically limited the practical adoption of RIE texturing in high-performance mc-Si solar cells.

To address the issue of plasma-induced surface damage, post-RIE treatments known as damage removal etching (DRE) have been investigated. DRE typically involves a brief immersion of the RIE-textured wafer in a dilute acidic solution, such as a mixture of HF and HNO₃, which selectively removes the damaged surface layer without completely erasing the beneficial nanotexture. The goal of DRE is to strike an optimal balance: sufficient etching to eliminate recombination-active defects while preserving enough of the fine surface features to maintain low reflectance. However, the relationship between DRE duration, surface morphology, optical properties, and electrical performance is highly non-linear and sensitive to process conditions. Excessive DRE can over-smooth the texture, leading to increased reflectance and reduced I<sub>sc</sub>, whereas insufficient DRE fails to adequately passivate the surface, resulting in persistent recombination losses.

In parallel with advances in texturing, emitter engineering has also played a pivotal role in pushing mc-Si solar cell efficiencies toward theoretical limits. Conventional homogeneous emitters, formed by uniform phosphorus diffusion, present a compromise between series resistance under the metal contacts and surface recombination in the inter-finger regions. A lightly doped emitter minimizes Auger recombination and improves blue response but increases contact resistance, while a heavily doped emitter reduces contact resistance at the expense of higher surface recombination. The selective emitter architecture resolves this dilemma by creating a spatially varying doping profile: a heavily doped region directly beneath the front metal contacts to ensure low contact resistance, and a lightly doped region in the areas between contacts to suppress surface recombination and enhance carrier collection, especially for short-wavelength photons. Implementing selective emitters in conjunction with advanced texturing techniques offers a synergistic pathway to simultaneously optimize optical and electrical performance.

However, integrating RIE texturing with selective emitter formation in a manufacturable screen-printed solar cell process remains technically challenging. The fragility of RIE-generated nanostructures necessitates gentle handling during subsequent high-temperature diffusion and metallization steps. Moreover, the compatibility of DRE chemistry with downstream processing—such as diffusion barrier masking, phosphosilicate glass (PSG) removal, and silicon nitride (SiN<sub>x</sub>) anti-reflective coating deposition—must be ensured. Prior art lacks a comprehensive and industrially viable solution that harmonizes ultra-low-reflectance RIE texturing, effective damage removal via precisely timed DRE, and robust selective emitter implementation using standard screen-printing metallization.

Accordingly, there exists a long-felt but unresolved need in the photovoltaic industry for a high-efficiency mc-Si solar cell architecture that leverages the optical benefits of RIE texturing without incurring the electrical penalties associated with plasma-induced damage. Such a solution must be compatible with existing industrial fabrication lines, utilize cost-effective materials and processes, and deliver measurable improvements in all key performance metrics—V<sub>oc</sub>, I<sub>sc</sub>, FF, and overall conversion efficiency—relative to state-of-the-art acid-textured reference cells. The present invention addresses these needs by providing a novel and optimized process sequence that integrates RIE texturing, controlled DRE with a specific HNO₃/HF mixture for a defined duration, and a screen-print-compatible selective emitter structure, thereby achieving unprecedented performance in industrially fabricated mc-Si solar cells.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a high-efficiency multi-crystalline silicon (mc-Si) solar cell and a method for its fabrication that overcomes the limitations of prior art texturing and emitter technologies by synergistically combining reactive ion etching (RIE) surface texturing, precisely controlled damage removal etching (DRE), and a screen-print-compatible selective emitter architecture. The invention achieves a significant enhancement in short-circuit current density (I<sub>sc</sub>) through ultra-low reflectance enabled by RIE-generated nanotextures, while simultaneously preserving high open-circuit voltage (V<sub>oc</sub>) and fill factor (FF) by eliminating plasma-induced surface damage via an optimized DRE step. This dual optimization results in a net increase in power conversion efficiency that surpasses that of conventional acid-textured mc-Si solar cells fabricated using identical industrial processes.

In one aspect, the invention pertains to a method for fabricating a high-efficiency mc-Si solar cell, comprising the steps of: (a) providing a p-type boron-doped mc-Si wafer having a resistivity of approximately 1.9 ohm·cm and a thickness of about 200 micrometers; (b) performing an initial isotropic acid texturing of the wafer surface using a mixture of hydrofluoric acid (HF) and nitric acid (HNO₃) to form a base micro-texture; (c) subjecting the acid-textured wafer to reactive ion etching (RIE) in a plasma reactor to generate a secondary nanoscale texture superimposed upon the base micro-texture, thereby producing a hierarchical surface morphology characterized by deep, dense, and irregular features that yield a total hemispherical reflectance of less than 10% across the wavelength range of 300 to 1200 nanometers; (d) immersing the RIE-textured wafer in a damage removal etching (DRE) solution comprising a dilute mixture of HNO₃ and HF for a duration of between 20 and 30 seconds to selectively remove the plasma-damaged surface layer while substantially preserving the nanoscale texture responsible for low reflectance; (e) depositing a silicon dioxide (SiO₂) layer onto the DRE-treated surface to serve as a diffusion barrier mask; (f) patterning the SiO₂ layer using an etch paste applied via screen printing to define regions corresponding to future front-side metal contact fingers; (g) performing a first phosphorus diffusion step using a POCl₃ source in a tube furnace to create a shallow, lightly doped emitter region with a sheet resistance of approximately 80 ohms per square in the unmasked areas; (h) performing a second, localized phosphorus diffusion through the patterned openings in the SiO₂ mask to form heavily doped contact regions beneath the intended metal fingers with a sheet resistance of about 20 ohms per square, thereby establishing a selective emitter structure; (i) removing the residual SiO₂ mask and phosphosilicate glass (PSG) byproduct in a single-step etch using a 10% HF solution; (j) depositing a silicon nitride (SiN<sub>x</sub>) anti-reflective coating via plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD) with a refractive index of 2.06 and a thickness of 85 nanometers; (k) screen-printing silver (Ag) and aluminum (Al) pastes onto the front and rear surfaces, respectively; and (l) co-firing the metallization in a lamp-heated belt furnace to form ohmic contacts and complete the solar cell.

In another aspect, the invention provides a high-efficiency mc-Si solar cell fabricated according to the above method, wherein the front surface exhibits a hierarchical texture comprising microscale bowl-like features from the initial acid etch overlaid with preserved nanoscale protrusions and pits from the RIE process, the surface recombination velocity is reduced to levels comparable to or better than those of acid-textured reference cells due to the effective removal of plasma-induced defects by the 20–30 second DRE treatment, and the emitter comprises a selective doping profile with 20 ohm/sq contact regions and 80 ohm/sq inter-finger regions. The resulting solar cell demonstrates a short-circuit current density (I<sub>sc</sub>) that is at least 200 milliamperes higher in absolute terms than an otherwise identical acid-textured mc-Si solar cell processed without RIE and DRE, while maintaining open-circuit voltage and fill factor values that are equal to or greater than those of the reference cell, thereby achieving a certified power conversion efficiency of at least 17.6% under standard test conditions (AM1.5G, 1000 W/m², 25°C).

A key innovation of the present invention lies in the identification and precise control of the DRE duration window—specifically between 20 and 30 seconds using a dilute HNO₃/HF mixture—that optimally balances the competing demands of surface damage removal and texture preservation. Shorter DRE times fail to sufficiently eliminate recombination centers, leading to degraded V<sub>oc</sub> and FF, while longer DRE times excessively smooth the nanotexture, increasing reflectance and diminishing I<sub>sc</sub>. The inventors have discovered that within this narrow temporal window, the RIE-induced defects are removed without compromising the optical advantages of the nanotexture, enabling simultaneous maximization of both optical and electrical performance.

Furthermore, the invention demonstrates, for the first time, the successful integration of this optimized RIE-DRE texturing sequence with a screen-print-compatible selective emitter process. The use of a screen-printed etch paste to pattern the SiO₂ diffusion barrier enables precise alignment with subsequently printed metal grids using standard industrial equipment, eliminating the need for photolithography or laser ablation. This compatibility ensures that the performance gains achieved by the novel texturing approach can be realized within the constraints and economics of high-volume solar cell manufacturing.

The solar cells produced by the present invention exhibit markedly improved external quantum efficiency (EQE) in the wavelength range below 600 nanometers compared to acid-textured counterparts, directly attributable to the combined effects of reduced front-surface reflectance and suppressed surface recombination in the blue spectral region. This spectral response enhancement translates directly into the observed I<sub>sc</sub> gain, which constitutes a major contributor to the overall efficiency improvement.

In summary, the present invention represents a significant advancement in mc-Si photovoltaic technology by resolving the long-standing trade-off between optical absorption and electrical quality in plasma-textured solar cells. Through the strategic combination of RIE texturing, time-optimized DRE, and selective emitter engineering—all implemented using industrially scalable processes—the invention delivers a high-efficiency solar cell architecture that sets a new benchmark for performance in screen-printed mc-Si devices.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF EXEMPLARY EMBODIMENTS

The present invention will now be described in detail with reference to specific exemplary embodiments. It should be understood that these embodiments are provided for illustrative purposes only and are not intended to limit the scope of the invention, which is defined solely by the appended claims and their equivalents.

In accordance with the present invention, a high-efficiency multi-crystalline silicon (mc-Si) solar cell is fabricated through a sequence of process steps that integrate advanced surface texturing, defect mitigation, and emitter engineering. The starting material is a standard solar-grade mc-Si wafer, measuring 156 millimeters by 156 millimeters, with p-type conductivity imparted by boron doping. The wafer exhibits a bulk resistivity of approximately 1.9 ohm·centimeter and a thickness of about 200 micrometers, specifications commonly used in industrial photovoltaic production. The fabrication begins with a conventional isotropic acid texturing step, wherein the wafer is immersed in a mixture of hydrofluoric acid (HF) and nitric acid (HNO₃) under controlled temperature and agitation conditions. This initial etch serves to create a base micro-texture consisting of randomly distributed, bowl-shaped depressions with characteristic lateral dimensions on the order of several micrometers. While this acid texture alone provides only moderate anti-reflective properties, it establishes a foundation upon which a more sophisticated nanostructure can be built without compromising mechanical stability or introducing excessive surface area that could exacerbate recombination.

Following the acid texturing, the wafer undergoes reactive ion etching (RIE) in a plasma reactor. The RIE process employs a gas mixture—typically containing fluorine-based compounds such as CF₄ or SF₆, possibly with oxygen or argon diluents—and operates under conditions of moderate pressure (e.g., 10–100 mTorr) and radio-frequency power (e.g., 50–300 W). During RIE, the synergistic action of chemically reactive radicals and directionally accelerated ions etches the silicon surface anisotropically, preferentially along crystallographic orientations and defect sites prevalent in mc-Si. This results in the formation of a secondary, nanoscale texture superimposed upon the pre-existing micro-bowls. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis reveals that the RIE-textured surface is characterized by a high density of sharp needles, deep pits, and irregular protrusions with feature sizes ranging from tens to hundreds of nanometers. This hierarchical morphology dramatically increases the effective surface area and induces multiple scattering events for incident photons, leading to a total hemispherical reflectance that is consistently below 10% across the entire solar spectrum (300–1200 nm) and often dips below 5% in the visible range. Such ultra-low reflectance is instrumental in boosting the short-circuit current density (I<sub>sc</sub>) by enhancing light incoupling, particularly for short-wavelength photons that would otherwise be reflected or absorbed very near the surface.

However, the energetic ion bombardment inherent to RIE inevitably introduces lattice defects—such as dangling bonds, dislocations, and amorphous regions—in the top few nanometers of the silicon surface. These defects act as Shockley-Read-Hall recombination centers, significantly increasing the surface recombination velocity (SRV) and thereby degrading the open-circuit voltage (V<sub>oc</sub>) and fill factor (FF) of the resulting solar cell. To mitigate this detrimental effect, the invention incorporates a critical damage removal etching (DRE) step immediately following RIE. The DRE is performed by immersing the RIE-textured wafer in a dilute aqueous solution of HNO₃ and HF, with concentrations carefully selected to ensure isotropic etching at a controlled rate. The duration of this etch is the key parameter that determines the success of the overall process. Extensive experimental investigation has revealed that a DRE time between 20 and 30 seconds is optimal for the specified wafer and texturing conditions. Within this window, the etchant removes approximately 10 to 20 nanometers of the damaged surface layer, effectively eliminating the recombination-active defects without substantially altering the underlying nanotexture responsible for low reflectance. SEM imaging of samples subjected to 20-second and 30-second DRE shows that while the sharpest needle-like features are slightly blunted, the overall density and depth of the nanostructures remain sufficient to maintain reflectance well below that of acid-textured references. In contrast, DRE times shorter than 20 seconds leave residual damage, manifesting as reduced V<sub>oc</sub> and FF, whereas times exceeding 30 seconds progressively smooth the surface, causing reflectance to rise and I<sub>sc</sub> to decline.

After DRE, the wafer is prepared for selective emitter formation. A thin layer of silicon dioxide (SiO₂), typically 50 to 100 nanometers thick, is deposited uniformly across the front surface using a method such as thermal oxidation or plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD). This SiO₂ layer functions as a diffusion barrier during the subsequent phosphorus doping steps. To define the regions for heavy doping beneath the future metal contacts, a screen-printable etch paste—comprising fluoride-based compounds in a polymer binder—is applied through a stencil aligned to the intended grid pattern. Upon firing or chemical development, the etch paste locally removes the SiO₂ in the contact areas, exposing the underlying silicon. The wafer then undergoes a two-step phosphorus diffusion process in a conventional horizontal tube furnace using liquid POCl₃ as the dopant source. In the first step, a shallow, lightly doped emitter is formed across the entire front surface, including both masked and unmasked regions, yielding a sheet resistance of approximately 80 ohms per square. In the second step, phosphorus diffuses more rapidly through the exposed silicon windows, creating heavily doped regions directly beneath the contact fingers with a sheet resistance of about 20 ohms per square. This differential doping profile constitutes the selective emitter: the lightly doped inter-finger regions minimize Auger and surface recombination, enhancing blue response and V<sub>oc</sub>, while the heavily doped contact regions ensure low specific contact resistance with the screen-printed silver metallization.

Following diffusion, the residual SiO₂ mask and the phosphosilicate glass (PSG) byproduct are removed in a single cleaning step using a 10% hydrofluoric acid (HF) solution. This simultaneous etch simplifies the process flow and avoids additional handling that could damage the delicate nanostructure. Next, an anti-reflective and surface-passivating coating of silicon nitride (SiN<sub>x</sub>) is deposited by PECVD. The film is engineered to have a refractive index of 2.06 and a thickness of 85 nanometers, values chosen to provide destructive interference for reflected light at a wavelength near 600 nm while also supplying hydrogen for bulk and surface passivation during the subsequent firing step. Front and rear metallization is then applied using standard screen printing: a silver paste is printed on the front to form the grid fingers and busbars, and an aluminum paste is printed on the rear to form the back surface field (BSF) and contact. The cell is co-fired in a rapid thermal processing (RTP) system, such as a lamp-heated belt furnace, where the high temperature sinters the metal pastes, forms ohmic contacts, and activates the hydrogen passivation from the SiN<sub>x</sub> layer.

The resulting solar cell embodies the synergistic benefits of the invention’s integrated approach. Optical characterization confirms that the hierarchical RIE-DRE texture, capped with the optimized SiN<sub>x</sub> coating, achieves a weighted average reflectance below 3%, significantly outperforming acid-textured cells with similar ARC. Electrical testing under standard illumination (AM1.5G, 1000 W/m², 25°C) demonstrates that the cell exhibits a short-circuit current density (I<sub>sc</sub>) exceeding that of a comparative acid-textured cell by at least 200 milliamperes in absolute terms—corresponding to a relative gain of approximately 5–6%. Crucially, this I<sub>sc</sub> enhancement is achieved without any sacrifice in open-circuit voltage or fill factor; indeed, both parameters are equal to or slightly better than those of the reference cell, owing to the effective removal of RIE-induced damage by the 20–30 second DRE and the recombination-suppressing nature of the selective emitter. The net result is a power conversion efficiency of at least 17.6%, a figure that represents a substantial improvement over the typical 16.5–17.0% efficiency of state-of-the-art industrial mc-Si cells employing only acid texturing and homogeneous emitters.

External quantum efficiency (EQE) measurements further validate the invention’s efficacy. The RIE-DRE-selective emitter cell shows a pronounced increase in EQE across the entire spectrum, with the most significant gains occurring below 600 nanometers—precisely the region where front-surface recombination and reflection losses are most acute. This spectral response directly correlates with the observed I<sub>sc</sub> boost and confirms that the invention successfully decouples optical absorption from electrical recombination, a feat not achievable with prior art RIE-textured cells lacking optimized DRE.

It is emphasized that all process steps described herein are compatible with existing industrial solar cell production lines. The use of screen-printed etch paste for selective emitter definition eliminates the need for costly photolithography or laser tools. The RIE and DRE steps can be integrated into standard wet and dry benches with minimal modification. The co-firing of metallization follows conventional protocols. Thus, the invention provides not only a performance breakthrough but also a commercially viable pathway to higher efficiency in mass-produced mc-Si photovoltaics.

While the exemplary embodiment described utilizes a specific wafer resistivity, thickness, acid mixture, RIE chemistry, DRE duration, emitter sheet resistances, and ARC parameters, it will be appreciated by those skilled in the art that variations within reasonable ranges may be employed without departing from the spirit and scope of the invention. For instance, the DRE time window may shift slightly depending on the exact composition of the etchant or the severity of the RIE damage, but the principle of balancing defect removal against texture preservation remains central. Similarly, alternative diffusion schemes—such as ion implantation or in-situ doped layers—could be adapted to form the selective emitter, provided they maintain the spatial doping gradient. The core innovation resides in the recognition that RIE texturing, when coupled with a narrowly defined DRE treatment and selective emitter architecture, can overcome historical limitations and deliver simultaneous gains in optical and electrical performance in screen-printed mc-Si solar cells.
