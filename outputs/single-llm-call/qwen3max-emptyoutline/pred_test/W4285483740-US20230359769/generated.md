# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of data privacy and anonymization, particularly in the context of sharing binary datasets while preserving user privacy. More specifically, the invention provides a novel method and system for generating a privacy-preserving version of a binary dataset that maintains high utility for downstream applications while ensuring robust privacy guarantees. The invention is particularly applicable to scenarios involving sparse binary matrices representing user-feature relationships, such as bipartite graphs in social networks, user interest lists, location data, and other categorical data representations where each row corresponds to a user and each column represents a feature or attribute. The invention addresses the fundamental challenge of balancing privacy protection with data utility by introducing a new privacy framework called smooth-k-anonymity and providing efficient polynomial-time algorithms for its implementation.

## BACKGROUND

In the modern digital era, the collection, analysis, and sharing of user data have become ubiquitous across various domains including social media, healthcare, e-commerce, and scientific research. However, the widespread use of personal data raises significant privacy concerns, as the release of datasets containing sensitive user information can lead to re-identification attacks and potential harm to individuals. To address these concerns, researchers and practitioners have developed various formal privacy frameworks designed to protect individual privacy while enabling useful data analysis.

Two primary approaches to formal privacy have emerged in the literature: differential privacy and k-anonymity. Differential privacy, introduced by Dwork and colleagues, provides strong mathematical guarantees by ensuring that the output of a data processing algorithm remains approximately unchanged regardless of whether any individual's data is included or excluded from the input dataset. This property is achieved through the introduction of carefully calibrated randomness into the data processing mechanism. While differential privacy offers robust theoretical guarantees against arbitrary post-processing and side information attacks, it often requires the addition of substantial noise to achieve meaningful privacy levels, which can significantly degrade data utility, particularly for sparse datasets.

K-anonymity, originally proposed by Sweeney, takes a different approach by focusing on the structure of the released dataset itself rather than the algorithm used to generate it. In a k-anonymous dataset, each individual's record is indistinguishable from at least k-1 other records with respect to certain identifying attributes known as quasi-identifiers. This is typically achieved through generalization (replacing specific values with more general categories) or suppression (removing specific attribute values). While k-anonymity can preserve higher data utility compared to differential privacy in many scenarios, it has been shown to be vulnerable to certain types of attacks, particularly when adversaries possess additional background knowledge about the data subjects.

The choice between these privacy frameworks often involves a trade-off between the strength of privacy guarantees and the preservation of data utility. In the context of binary datasets, which are commonly used to represent user-feature relationships in various applications, this trade-off becomes particularly acute. Binary matrices naturally arise in numerous real-world scenarios, including bipartite graphs representing user-item interactions, user lists associated with specific features or interests, and points in binary space representing categorical attributes. When attempting to share such datasets while preserving privacy, existing approaches face significant limitations.

Differential privacy mechanisms applied to binary datasets, particularly sparse ones, often require prohibitively large privacy parameters to achieve acceptable utility levels. For instance, when using randomized response mechanisms to achieve edge differential privacy in bipartite graphs, the required privacy parameter ε grows logarithmically with the number of features, rendering the privacy guarantees practically meaningless for datasets with reasonable utility requirements. Similarly, node differential privacy, which provides stronger protection by considering the complete removal of a user's data, typically requires even larger privacy parameters or results in outputs that are nearly random, thus destroying the underlying data structure.

On the other hand, traditional k-anonymity approaches, while potentially preserving better utility, suffer from computational complexity issues and limited approximation guarantees. The problem of finding an optimal k-anonymous dataset that maximizes similarity to the original data is known to be NP-hard, and existing approximation algorithms provide meaningful guarantees only under restrictive assumptions about the original dataset's structure. Specifically, current algorithms guarantee good performance only when the optimal k-anonymous solution is already very similar to the original dataset, which may not hold in practical scenarios where significant anonymization is required.

Furthermore, the standard k-anonymity framework restricts modifications to the dataset to suppression operations only, meaning that entries can only be removed but not added. This limitation can be particularly problematic for sparse binary datasets, where the inability to add entries may force the removal of valuable information that could otherwise be preserved through strategic additions that maintain the k-anonymity property.

Recent research has attempted to bridge these gaps by exploring hybrid approaches and alternative privacy definitions. For example, some work has investigated combining differential privacy with k-anonymity guarantees, while others have proposed variants of k-anonymity tailored to specific data structures such as social networks. However, these approaches often fail to address the fundamental tension between privacy and utility in the context of general binary datasets, particularly when the goal is to release an entire dataset rather than summary statistics.

The limitations of existing approaches are particularly evident when considering real-world datasets, which are often sparse and exhibit complex structural patterns. Empirical studies have shown that many real-world binary matrices, such as those representing user-item interactions or social network connections, have constant average degree or degrees that grow sublinearly with the size of the dataset. In such scenarios, differential privacy mechanisms require unacceptably large privacy parameters to maintain reasonable utility, while traditional k-anonymity approaches may remove excessive amounts of data due to their restriction to suppression-only operations.

Moreover, the computational complexity of existing k-anonymity algorithms presents practical challenges for large-scale datasets. Many algorithms scale poorly with the number of features or fail to exploit the sparsity of real-world datasets, making them impractical for modern applications involving massive datasets with millions or billions of entries.

These limitations highlight the need for a new approach to privacy-preserving data sharing that can effectively balance privacy protection with data utility while remaining computationally feasible for large-scale binary datasets. Such an approach should provide meaningful privacy guarantees that are robust against realistic attack scenarios, preserve as much of the original data structure as possible, and be implementable using efficient algorithms that can handle the scale and sparsity characteristics of real-world datasets.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method and system for generating a privacy-preserving version of a binary dataset that overcomes the limitations of existing privacy frameworks by introducing a new concept called smooth-k-anonymity. This approach extends traditional k-anonymity by allowing both the addition and removal of entries in the binary dataset while maintaining the fundamental privacy guarantee that each user's record is indistinguishable from at least k-1 other records. The key innovation lies in the constraint that any additions to the dataset must respect a majority condition: for each group of users sharing the same pattern and each feature, if the majority of users in the group originally had the feature, then all users in the group are assigned the feature in the anonymized dataset; otherwise, none are assigned the feature.

The invention provides efficient polynomial-time algorithms for computing smooth-k-anonymized versions of binary datasets that achieve constant-factor approximation guarantees relative to the optimal solution, under the assumption that the optimal solution maintains at least 75% similarity to the original dataset as measured by Jaccard similarity. This represents a significant improvement over existing k-anonymity algorithms, which require much stronger assumptions about the similarity between the original and optimal datasets to achieve non-trivial approximation guarantees.

The method begins by representing each user as a point in a high-dimensional binary space, where each dimension corresponds to a feature and the coordinate value indicates whether the user possesses that feature. The algorithm then clusters users into groups of at least k members using techniques derived from lower-bounded facility location problems and metric facility location problems. For each cluster, the algorithm determines the final feature assignment by applying the majority rule: for each feature, if most users in the cluster originally had the feature, all users in the cluster are assigned the feature; otherwise, no users in the cluster are assigned the feature.

The invention's algorithms leverage advanced approximation techniques from combinatorial optimization, including an 82.6-approximation algorithm for the lower-bounded r-median problem and a 1.488-approximation algorithm for the metric facility location problem. These techniques enable the construction of clusters that balance the competing objectives of maintaining user privacy (through sufficiently large cluster sizes) and preserving data utility (through minimizing the total number of changes to the original dataset).

The smooth-k-anonymity framework provides several advantages over existing approaches. First, by allowing both additions and removals of entries, it can preserve more of the original data structure compared to traditional k-anonymity, which is restricted to suppression-only operations. Second, unlike differential privacy, it does not require the introduction of random noise, thereby avoiding the utility degradation associated with randomized mechanisms. Third, the privacy guarantees are intuitive and interpretable: each user is guaranteed to be indistinguishable from at least k-1 other users in the released dataset, providing protection against re-identification attacks based on quasi-identifiers.

The invention is particularly well-suited for sparse binary datasets, which are common in real-world applications such as social networks, user-item interaction matrices, and categorical data representations. Empirical evaluations demonstrate that the smooth-k-anonymity approach achieves significantly higher Jaccard similarity to the original dataset compared to traditional k-anonymity methods and differential privacy mechanisms, while maintaining the same level of privacy protection. Furthermore, the algorithms are designed to exploit the sparsity of real-world datasets, making them computationally feasible for large-scale applications involving billions of entries.

The method also demonstrates superior performance in downstream machine learning tasks, where models trained on smooth-k-anonymized datasets achieve better predictive accuracy compared to those trained on datasets anonymized using traditional methods. This indicates that the smooth-k-anonymity approach preserves not only the structural similarity to the original dataset but also the statistical properties that are important for machine learning applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for generating privacy-preserving versions of binary datasets through the introduction of smooth-k-anonymity and associated polynomial-time approximation algorithms. The detailed description begins with the formal definition of the problem setting and proceeds through the mathematical foundations, algorithmic implementations, and practical considerations necessary for implementing the invention.

The invention operates on binary datasets represented as bipartite graphs G = (U ∪ F, E), where U = {u₁, ..., uₙ} represents a set of n users, F = {f₁, ..., fₘ} represents a set of m features, and E ⊆ U × F represents the edge set indicating which users possess which features. This representation is equivalent to an n × m binary matrix where each row corresponds to a user and each column corresponds to a feature, with matrix entry (i, j) equal to 1 if user uᵢ possesses feature fⱼ and 0 otherwise. This formulation captures numerous real-world scenarios including social network connections, user-item interactions, location check-ins, and categorical attribute representations.

The core innovation of the invention is the smooth-k-anonymity framework, which extends traditional k-anonymity by allowing both additions and removals of edges while maintaining the fundamental privacy guarantee that each user's neighborhood is identical to at least k-1 other users' neighborhoods in the anonymized graph. Formally, a mechanism M: G → G that maps an input graph G = (U ∪ F, E) to an output graph G' = (U ∪ F, E') satisfies smooth-k-anonymity if two conditions are met: (1) for every user u ∈ U, there exist at least k-1 other users v ∈ U such that the neighborhood of u in G' is identical to the neighborhood of v in G'; and (2) for every equivalence class C of users sharing the same neighborhood in G' and every feature f ∈ F, if the majority of users in C had an edge to f in the original graph G, then all users in C have an edge to f in G', otherwise no users in C have an edge to f in G'.

This majority condition ensures that any additions to the dataset are justified by the original data distribution, preventing arbitrary modifications that could introduce misleading information while still allowing the algorithm to strategically add edges to create larger equivalence classes and preserve more of the original data structure. The smooth-k-anonymity framework thus strikes a balance between the restrictive nature of traditional k-anonymity (which allows only suppressions) and the potentially destructive nature of differential privacy mechanisms (which introduce random noise).

The invention provides two main algorithms for computing smooth-k-anonymized versions of input graphs, both of which operate by first embedding users into a high-dimensional binary space and then clustering them into groups of at least k members. In this embedding, each user u is represented as a point in ℝᵐ where the i-th coordinate is 1 if u possesses feature fᵢ and 0 otherwise. The distance between two users is measured using Hamming distance, which counts the number of features on which they differ.

The first algorithm, referred to as Alg₁, reduces the smooth-k-anonymization problem to the lower-bounded r-median problem. In this problem, given n points in a metric space, the goal is to select at most r centers and assign each point to exactly one center such that each center has at least k assigned points and the total distance from points to their assigned centers is minimized. The algorithm sets r = n/k, allowing as many centers as needed while ensuring each cluster contains at least k points. It employs an 82.6-approximation algorithm for the lower-bounded r-median problem, which represents the best-known approximation guarantee for this problem.

After obtaining the clustering from the lower-bounded r-median solution, Alg₁ processes each cluster by applying the majority rule: for each feature f, if most users in the cluster originally possessed feature f, all users in the cluster are assigned feature f in the output; otherwise, no users in the cluster are assigned feature f. This step ensures that the output satisfies both conditions of smooth-k-anonymity while minimizing the total number of changes to the original dataset.

The second algorithm, referred to as Alg₂, provides improved approximation guarantees under weaker assumptions about the similarity between the original dataset and the optimal smooth-k-anonymous solution. Alg₂ reduces the problem to the metric facility location problem, which involves selecting a subset of facilities to open and assigning each point to an open facility to minimize the sum of facility opening costs and assignment distances. The algorithm constructs a facility location instance where each user corresponds to a potential facility with an opening cost proportional to the distance to its k-th nearest neighbor.

Alg₂ proceeds through several steps: first, it solves the constructed facility location instance using a 1.488-approximation algorithm; second, it iteratively removes clusters with fewer than αk points (where α is a tunable parameter) and reassigns their points to the next closest facility; third, it merges small clusters to ensure all clusters contain at least k points while preventing any cluster from growing larger than 2k points; and finally, it applies the majority rule within each cluster to determine the final feature assignments.

Both algorithms provide theoretical guarantees on their performance relative to the optimal smooth-k-anonymous solution. Specifically, Alg₁ achieves a constant-factor approximation when the optimal solution maintains at least 99.4% similarity to the original dataset (as measured by Jaccard similarity), while Alg₂ achieves a constant-factor approximation under the weaker assumption that the optimal solution maintains at least 75% similarity. The Jaccard similarity between two edge sets E and E' is defined as |E ∩ E'| / |E ∪ E'|, providing a natural measure of structural similarity between the original and anonymized datasets.

The theoretical analysis of these algorithms relies on several key insights and lemmas. First, the total number of changes made by any algorithm can be related to the Jaccard similarity through the inequality Σᵤ Δᵤ ≤ 2φ/(1-φ) |E|, where Δᵤ represents the number of changes made to user u's feature vector, φ = 1 - J(E, E'), and |E| is the number of edges in the original graph. This relationship allows the analysis to translate between the objective of maximizing Jaccard similarity and the objective of minimizing the total number of changes.

Second, the existence of a good clustering solution can be established by considering the optimal smooth-k-anonymous solution and noting that it naturally induces a clustering of users into equivalence classes of size at least k. The total cost of this clustering (measured as the sum of Hamming distances from users to their cluster representatives) is exactly equal to the total number of changes made by the optimal solution, establishing a connection between the smooth-k-anonymization problem and clustering problems with lower bounds on cluster sizes.

Third, the approximation guarantees of the underlying clustering algorithms (lower-bounded r-median and metric facility location) can be translated into approximation guarantees for the smooth-k-anonymization problem through careful analysis of how the clustering costs relate to the final Jaccard similarity after applying the majority rule within clusters.

The invention also addresses practical implementation considerations for large-scale datasets. The algorithms are designed to exploit the sparsity of real-world binary datasets, avoiding operations that scale with the full n × m matrix dimensions and instead focusing on the actual non-zero entries. For extremely large datasets, the invention provides a parallelization strategy based on locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) techniques, which partition the dataset into chunks that can be processed independently while maintaining good overall performance.

Empirical evaluation of the invention demonstrates its superiority over existing approaches across multiple dimensions. When compared to traditional k-anonymity methods such as the Mondrian algorithm, the smooth-k-anonymity approach consistently achieves higher Jaccard similarity for the same privacy parameter k, often by factors of 1.5 to 2 times better. This improvement is particularly pronounced for sparse datasets, where the ability to add entries strategically allows the preservation of more original data structure.

When compared to differential privacy mechanisms, the smooth-k-anonymity approach provides dramatically better utility for comparable privacy levels. For instance, achieving 50% Jaccard similarity with edge differential privacy on sparse datasets requires privacy parameters ε ≥ 10, which provide essentially no meaningful privacy protection. In contrast, smooth-k-anonymity can achieve 50% or higher Jaccard similarity with reasonable values of k (e.g., k = 16) while providing clear and interpretable privacy guarantees.

The invention's performance in downstream machine learning tasks further validates its practical utility. Experiments training classifiers on anonymized versions of the Adult dataset show that models trained on smooth-k-anonymized data achieve better predictive accuracy compared to those trained on data anonymized using traditional k-anonymity methods, and significantly better accuracy compared to models trained on differentially private data. This indicates that the smooth-k-anonymity approach preserves not only structural similarity but also the statistical properties that are important for machine learning applications.

The invention's algorithms are robust across different types of datasets, including synthetic data generated from stochastic block models, real-world datasets from various domains (social networks, user-item interactions, web graphs), and proprietary datasets from major internet companies. The performance remains consistent across different values of the privacy parameter k, with the smooth-k-anonymity approach consistently outperforming baselines across the entire range of k values tested.

Implementation details of the invention include several practical optimizations that improve empirical performance without affecting the theoretical guarantees. These include modifications to the facility opening cost calculation in Alg₂, the use of Meyerson's algorithm for solving the facility location instances efficiently, and simplified procedures for handling small clusters during the merging phase. These optimizations make the algorithms practical for real-world deployment while maintaining their theoretical foundations.

The invention thus provides a complete solution for privacy-preserving data sharing that addresses the fundamental limitations of existing approaches. By introducing smooth-k-anonymity and providing efficient algorithms with strong theoretical guarantees, the invention enables the release of binary datasets that maintain high utility for downstream applications while providing robust and interpretable privacy protection. The framework is particularly well-suited for modern applications involving large-scale, sparse binary datasets, where the balance between privacy and utility is most critical.
