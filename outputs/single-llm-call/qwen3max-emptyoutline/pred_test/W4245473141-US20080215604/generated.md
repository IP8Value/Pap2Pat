# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of computational genetics and, more specifically, to methods and systems for accelerating the analysis of pedigree data through the use of reconfigurable hardware. The invention provides a novel architecture and implementation strategy for simulating genetic inheritance across multi-generational pedigrees by leveraging the parallel processing capabilities of Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs). This approach is particularly suited for applications involving the estimation of inbreeding coefficients, genotype probabilities, identity-by-descent (IBD) relationships, and haplotype reconstruction in diploid organisms. The technical domain encompasses digital electronics design, bioinformatics, statistical genetics, and high-performance computing, with specific relevance to human and livestock genomics where large-scale pedigree structures are routinely analyzed to infer genetic risk, carrier status, and trait associations. The invention exploits the regular, hierarchical structure inherent in pedigree data—where each non-founder individual has exactly two parents—and maps this structure onto a layered, pipelined hardware architecture that enables the concurrent simulation of meiosis events across entire generations. By representing individuals as modular processing units arranged in temporal layers corresponding to generational depth, and by synchronizing allele transmission through clock-driven logic, the invention achieves throughput rates that are orders of magnitude higher than conventional sequential software implementations running on general-purpose processors. The technical contribution lies not only in the algorithmic reformulation of genetic sampling procedures such as gene dropping into a form amenable to fine-grained parallelism, but also in the hardware-aware design of data pathways, memory elements, and control logic that collectively enable the generation of a complete, consistent pedigree-wide sample at every clock cycle of the FPGA. This represents a paradigm shift from iterative, loop-based computation to spatially distributed, synchronous execution, thereby addressing a critical bottleneck in modern genomic analysis workflows where the combinatorial complexity of inheritance patterns renders exact or even approximate inference computationally prohibitive for large pedigrees using traditional computing platforms.

## BACKGROUND ART

The analysis of pedigree data has long been a cornerstone of quantitative and population genetics, enabling researchers to disentangle the contributions of heredity and environment to phenotypic variation. In diploid species, each individual inherits one allele from each parent at every genetic locus, a process governed by the biological mechanism of meiosis. The stochastic nature of allelic segregation during meiosis introduces uncertainty in the unobserved genotypes of individuals within a pedigree, particularly when only partial genotype data is available for certain members. To address this, statistical methods have been developed to estimate probabilities of genotypes, IBD states, and other genetic parameters based on observed data and assumptions about founder allele frequencies and recombination rates. Among these, the gene dropping algorithm—first formalized by MacCluer et al. (1986)—has served as a foundational Monte Carlo technique wherein alleles are randomly assigned to founders according to a specified distribution, and then propagated down the pedigree through simulated meiosis events, with each offspring receiving one randomly selected allele from each parent. Repeated sampling yields empirical estimates of quantities such as inbreeding coefficients (defined as the probability that an individual’s two alleles at a locus are IBD) or posterior genotype probabilities conditional on observed marker data.

Despite its conceptual simplicity, the gene dropping algorithm becomes computationally intensive when applied to large or complex pedigrees, especially those containing inbreeding loops or marriage cycles that prevent decomposition into simple lineages. Exact maximum likelihood (ML) methods, which evaluate all possible combinations of meiosis indicators, are intractable beyond small pedigrees due to exponential growth in the state space. Consequently, approximate methods based on sampling—such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques including Metropolis-Hastings and Gibbs sampling—have been developed to explore high-probability regions of the inheritance space more efficiently. These algorithms, while more sophisticated, still rely on sequential iteration over individuals and loci, often requiring thousands or millions of iterations to achieve convergence. Implementations of these methods in software languages such as C++, Fortran, or Delphi typically employ nested loops to traverse the pedigree structure, with inner loops handling per-individual computations and outer loops managing the sampling process. Parallelization strategies have historically focused on coarse-grained approaches, such as distributing independent chains or batches of samples across multiple nodes in a computing cluster. While this yields linear speedup proportional to the number of processors, it does not reduce the per-sample computation time and incurs significant communication overhead.

Alternative hardware acceleration strategies have been explored in bioinformatics, notably the use of Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) for sequence alignment and molecular dynamics simulations. However, GPUs are optimized for data-parallel tasks with regular memory access patterns and are less suited to the irregular, pointer-chasing nature of pedigree traversal, where dependencies between individuals are defined by arbitrary graph structures rather than uniform grids. Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) offer high performance but lack flexibility, making them economically unviable for research applications where algorithms and data structures evolve rapidly. In contrast, FPGAs provide a middle ground: they consist of an array of programmable logic blocks and interconnects that can be configured post-manufacture to implement custom digital circuits tailored to a specific computational task. Prior work by Salwinski and Eisenberg demonstrated the utility of FPGAs for simulating biochemical reaction networks, exploiting their ability to model concurrent molecular interactions. However, no prior art has addressed the unique challenges of pedigree-based genetic simulation, where the key insight is that meiosis events for non-ancestral individuals are conditionally independent given parental genotypes, enabling a layered, pipeline-friendly decomposition of the inheritance process.

Existing FPGA applications in genomics have largely focused on sequence-level operations such as read mapping, variant calling, or k-mer counting, which benefit from bit-level parallelism and streaming data models. Pedigree analysis, by contrast, involves structured, hierarchical data with strong inter-individual dependencies that must be respected during simulation. Conventional wisdom held that such irregular dependency graphs were ill-suited for FPGA implementation due to the difficulty of mapping dynamic pointer structures onto static hardware. Moreover, the need to store intermediate results for potentially thousands of individuals and to handle conditional logic based on observed genotype constraints posed significant challenges in terms of memory bandwidth and logic resource utilization. As a result, the field has remained dominated by CPU-based software solutions, despite growing recognition that computational bottlenecks are limiting the scale and resolution of genetic analyses in both medical and agricultural contexts. The present invention overcomes these limitations by introducing a novel representation of pedigree data as a temporally layered, statically scheduled pipeline, wherein the generational hierarchy of the pedigree is directly mapped onto physical or logical layers of FPGA modules, each synchronized to a global clock. This architecture transforms the inherently sequential problem of gene dropping into a fully parallel, single-cycle-per-sample operation, thereby achieving unprecedented throughput without sacrificing correctness or flexibility.

## DISCLOSURE OF INVENTION

The present invention discloses a method and system for the high-throughput simulation of genetic inheritance in pedigrees using reconfigurable hardware, specifically Field Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs). The core innovation lies in the transformation of the pedigree data structure into a layered, pipelined hardware architecture that enables the concurrent simulation of meiosis events across all individuals in the pedigree, resulting in the generation of a complete, consistent sample of the entire pedigree at every clock cycle of the FPGA. This is achieved by representing each individual in the pedigree as a dedicated hardware module, with modules arranged in discrete layers corresponding to generational depth: Layer 0 contains founder individuals (those with no known parents), Layer 1 contains their immediate offspring, Layer 2 contains the next generation, and so on, up to Layer m for the most recent descendants. Each module is configured to perform the necessary computations for its role in the inheritance process, including allele reception from parent modules, meiosis simulation, genotype consistency checking, and result accumulation.

In operation, the system operates under a global clock signal. At each clock cycle, new founder alleles are generated—either randomly from a specified allele frequency distribution, systematically enumerated, or fixed according to experimental design—and injected into the Layer 0 modules. Simultaneously, meiosis indicators (binary values determining which parental allele is transmitted) are generated for all non-founder modules. Within a single clock cycle, each descendant module receives one allele from each of its two parent modules (or both alleles from a single parent in the case of "holder" modules, which are used to maintain temporal synchronization for individuals who reproduce across multiple generations). The received alleles are processed according to the module's function: for inbreeding coefficient estimation, a comparator checks if the two alleles are identical by descent (IBD), and a counter is incremented if they are; for genotype probability estimation, the simulated genotype is compared against any observed genotype data, and a validity signal is asserted only if the sample is consistent with all observations; for multi-locus analyses, haplotypes (sets of linked alleles) are tracked, and recombination events are simulated according to a specified genetic map distance.

A critical feature of the invention is the use of holder modules to manage asynchronous reproductive timelines. In real pedigrees, an individual may produce offspring in multiple successive generations, which would otherwise disrupt the strict layer-by-layer progression of the pipeline. Holder modules act as memory buffers that store an individual’s alleles across multiple clock cycles, effectively "holding" them until the appropriate descendant layer is ready to receive them. This ensures that all alleles propagating through Layer i at time t originated from Layer 0 at time t−i, preserving the causal relationship of inheritance while enabling full pipelining. As a result, at any given clock cycle, multiple independent samples are in flight through the pipeline—one per layer—yet each sample remains internally consistent because the modules within a layer operate synchronously on the same sample instance.

The output of the pipeline is a stream of complete pedigree samples, each generated per clock cycle. For estimation tasks, these samples are accumulated in terminal storage units located below the final layer (Layer m). In the case of inbreeding estimation, counters within each individual’s module directly accumulate IBD occurrences. For genotype probability estimation, however, consistency with observed data can only be determined after the entire sample has traversed the pedigree; thus, a master validity signal is generated by logically ANDing the validity signals from all individuals, and only if this master signal is active are the allele counts for all individuals incremented in the terminal storage. This decoupling of computation and accumulation ensures correctness while maintaining high throughput.

The invention further discloses specific optimizations for FPGA implementation. Founder and descendant modules are designed using integer arithmetic and bitwise logic to minimize resource usage, avoiding floating-point operations that consume excessive FPGA fabric. Pseudo-random number generation is performed using cellular automata-based generators, which are highly efficient in hardware and produce high-quality randomness suitable for Monte Carlo sampling. The entire pedigree structure, including module interconnections and holder chains, is pre-processed from input pedigree data and encoded directly into the FPGA configuration bitstream using a hardware description language such as VHDL. A soft-core microprocessor (e.g., Picoblaze) is embedded within the FPGA to provide a communication interface with a host computer, allowing parameter configuration, result retrieval, and control signaling.

The disclosed system achieves dramatic speed improvements over sequential software implementations. For example, on a modest Xilinx Spartan 3 FPGA operating at 50 MHz, the invention generates 50 million samples per second for a 32-individual pedigree, compared to approximately 300,000 samples per second on a 3 GHz Pentium 4 CPU—a speedup of over 160×. Extrapolating to high-performance FPGAs such as the Xilinx Virtex 4, which operate at 390 MHz and offer 25× more logic resources, the invention could process pedigrees of up to 1,500 individuals for inbreeding estimation or 500 individuals for genotype probability estimation, with potential speedups exceeding 17,000× relative to contemporary CPUs. Importantly, the sampling rate is independent of pedigree size (provided the FPGA has sufficient capacity to store all modules), as each sample is produced in a fixed number of clock cycles equal to the pedigree depth plus pipeline latency.

The invention is not limited to the gene dropping algorithm. It provides a general framework for implementing any sampling-based pedigree analysis that can be expressed as a forward simulation of inheritance. More sophisticated algorithms, such as Metropolis-Hastings MCMC, can be adapted to this architecture by incorporating acceptance/rejection logic within the modules and maintaining state across samples. The avoidance of floating-point arithmetic in favor of integer-based likelihood ratios or log-likelihood differences further enhances compatibility with FPGA constraints. Additionally, the invention contemplates hybrid computing architectures where the FPGA handles the high-throughput generation of candidate samples, while a coupled CPU performs floating-point-intensive tasks such as parameter optimization or convergence diagnostics, thereby combining the strengths of both platforms.

In summary, the invention discloses a transformative approach to pedigree analysis that leverages the spatial parallelism and deterministic timing of FPGA hardware to overcome the sequential bottlenecks of traditional software. By mapping the generational structure of pedigrees onto a pipelined hardware architecture, it enables real-time, high-fidelity genetic simulation at scales previously unattainable, opening new avenues for genomic research and applications in precision medicine and animal breeding.

## BEST MODE FOR CARRYING OUT THE INVENTION

The best mode for carrying out the present invention involves a systematic workflow that begins with the preparation of pedigree data, proceeds through hardware description synthesis and FPGA configuration, and culminates in the execution of high-throughput genetic simulation and result retrieval. This mode is exemplified using a Xilinx Spartan 3 XC3S400 FPGA operating at 50 MHz, though the principles are scalable to larger and faster FPGA devices such as the Xilinx Virtex series. The following detailed description outlines the step-by-step implementation, including data preprocessing, module design, interconnection strategy, random number generation, and integration with host computing systems.

First, the input pedigree data must be preprocessed to extract structural and genetic information necessary for hardware mapping. The pedigree is represented as a directed acyclic graph where nodes correspond to individuals and edges denote parent-offspring relationships. Individuals with no incoming edges are identified as founders and assigned to Layer 0. A breadth-first search is then performed to assign each non-founder individual to the lowest possible layer such that both parents reside in strictly lower layers. This ensures a topological ordering that respects generational precedence. Special handling is required for individuals who produce offspring in non-consecutive generations; for each such individual, a chain of holder modules is inserted to bridge the temporal gap. For example, if Individual A (in Layer 2) produces offspring in Layer 4 and Layer 6, then two holder modules are instantiated: one in Layer 3 to hold A’s alleles for the Layer 4 offspring, and another in Layer 5 to hold them for the Layer 6 offspring. The length of each holder chain is determined by the difference in layer indices between the individual and its latest offspring.

Next, the genetic parameters of the analysis are specified. For inbreeding coefficient estimation, each founder is assigned a unique allele identifier (e.g., integers 1 through N for N founders), ensuring that any IBD match in descendants must arise from shared ancestry rather than chance allele identity. For genotype probability estimation, founder alleles are sampled from a multinomial distribution reflecting population allele frequencies; this distribution is encoded into the founder module’s allele generator logic. In multi-locus scenarios, haplotypes are constructed by concatenating allele identifiers across loci, and recombination probabilities between adjacent loci are used to configure binomial samplers that determine whether a crossover event occurs during meiosis.

The hardware description is then authored in VHDL (VHSIC Hardware Description Language), a standard language for FPGA design. Each individual—whether founder, descendant, or holder—is instantiated as a VHDL entity with well-defined ports for allele input, meiosis control, validity signaling, and result output. Founder modules contain an allele generator that, on each clock cycle, outputs two alleles according to the specified scheme (unique, random, or fixed). Descendant modules include two allele input ports (one from each parent), a meiosis indicator generator (a 1-bit pseudo-random source), a multiplexer to select the transmitted allele from each parent, and application-specific logic: for inbreeding, a comparator and counter; for genotype probability, a genotype checker and validity flag; for multi-locus, a haplotype assembler and recombination simulator. Holder modules are simplified entities containing only dual-port memory to store and forward alleles without modification.

Inter-module connectivity is established by wiring the allele outputs of parent modules to the inputs of their offspring modules, with holder chains inserted as needed to align temporal layers. All modules share a common clock signal and reset line, ensuring synchronous operation. The terminal layer (below Layer m) contains accumulator modules that receive allele counts or validity signals from all individuals. A master validity unit performs a logical AND reduction across all individual validity signals; only when this master signal is high are the accumulators updated, ensuring that only consistent samples contribute to the final estimates.

Pseudo-random number generation is implemented using cellular automata (CA)-based generators, which are particularly well-suited for FPGA deployment due to their local connectivity and parallel update rules. A 32-bit CA generator is instantiated for each source of randomness (e.g., founder alleles, meiosis indicators), initialized with a seed value provided by the host computer. These generators produce high-quality pseudo-random sequences with minimal logic overhead, enabling independent random streams for each module without resource contention.

To facilitate interaction with a general-purpose computer, a Picoblaze 8-bit soft-core microprocessor is embedded within the FPGA fabric. The Picoblaze is programmed with a control routine written in Picoblaze assembly language that handles command parsing, memory-mapped I/O to the simulation modules, and data packetization for transmission over a serial or parallel interface (e.g., USB or PCIe). The host computer sends configuration packets specifying analysis type, allele frequencies, recombination rates, and run duration; in response, the FPGA executes the simulation and returns result packets containing accumulated counts or probabilities.

The complete VHDL design—including individual modules, interconnects, random generators, and Picoblaze interface—is synthesized using Xilinx ISE Design Suite or equivalent toolchain. During synthesis, the tool optimizes the logic for area and timing, mapping Boolean equations to lookup tables (LUTs) and flip-flops within the FPGA’s configurable logic blocks (CLBs). Placement and routing algorithms then assign physical locations to each logic element and establish wiring connections, ensuring that all timing paths meet the 50 MHz clock constraint. The resulting bitstream is loaded onto the FPGA via JTAG or configuration PROM, activating the custom pedigree processor.

During operation, the host computer initiates a simulation run by writing to a control register in the Picoblaze’s address space. The FPGA then begins generating samples at a rate of one per clock cycle (50 million per second at 50 MHz). After the specified number of cycles, the host reads back the accumulated results from the terminal layer’s memory. Probabilities are computed as ratios of accumulated counts (e.g., IBD matches divided by total samples for inbreeding coefficients; genotype occurrences divided by valid samples for genotype probabilities).

This best mode has been validated on multiple pedigree datasets. For a 32-individual pedigree with known structure, the FPGA implementation achieved a 166× speedup over a Delphi-coded sequential gene dropper on a 3 GHz Pentium 4. For a 20-individual pedigree with sparse genotype data, the speedup was 495×, demonstrating the scalability of the approach. The use of integer arithmetic, CA-based RNGs, and holder-based pipelining proved critical to maximizing throughput while minimizing resource usage. Future enhancements may include dynamic reconfiguration to support variable pedigree sizes without full re-synthesis, or integration with high-bandwidth memory interfaces to handle pedigrees exceeding on-chip storage capacity. Nonetheless, the described mode represents the optimal balance of performance, correctness, and practicality for current FPGA technology, enabling geneticists to perform analyses that were previously computationally infeasible.
