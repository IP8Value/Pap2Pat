# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of photovoltaic (PV) energy systems, and more specifically to a method and system for dynamic reconfiguration of PV array topologies using regularized deep neural networks to mitigate power losses caused by partial shading. The invention is particularly directed toward cyber-physical PV systems that integrate real-time irradiance sensing, machine learning-based decision-making, and automated switching mechanisms to optimize electrical interconnections among solar panels in response to varying environmental conditions. By leveraging a trained deep neural network architecture incorporating dropout and batch normalization techniques, the invention enables scalable, low-latency, and high-accuracy selection of an optimal array topology—among series-parallel (SP), bridge-link (BL), honeycomb (HC), and total-cross-tied (TCT) configurations—based on panel-level irradiance data. This approach enhances grid-connected energy yield, improves inverter uptime, and reduces mismatch losses without requiring additional hardware such as spare or auxiliary panels, thereby offering a cost-effective and computationally efficient solution for next-generation smart solar farms.

## BACKGROUND

Photovoltaic energy systems have become a cornerstone of global renewable energy infrastructure, with widespread deployment in residential, commercial, and utility-scale applications. However, the efficiency and reliability of these systems are significantly compromised under partial shading conditions, which commonly arise from environmental obstructions such as trees, buildings, clouds, or dust accumulation. When certain panels in a PV array receive less irradiance than others, current and voltage mismatches occur across the interconnected modules, leading to disproportionate power dissipation, hot-spot formation, and substantial reduction in maximum power point (MPP) output. These mismatch losses can diminish overall energy harvest by up to 50% or more in severe scenarios, undermining the economic viability and grid stability of solar installations.

Traditional PV arrays employ fixed electrical topologies—most commonly the series-parallel (SP) configuration—wherein panels are connected in series within strings and those strings are connected in parallel. While simple and cost-effective, this static arrangement offers no adaptability to changing shading patterns, resulting in suboptimal performance under non-uniform irradiance. To address this limitation, researchers have explored both static and dynamic reconfiguration strategies. Static methods involve physically relocating panels according to predetermined rules—such as Sudoku-based algorithms or column-index shifting—to disperse shaded modules evenly across the array. Although effective in some cases, these approaches are labor-intensive, non-responsive to real-time changes, and impractical for large-scale or rooftop systems where physical access is limited.

Dynamic reconfiguration, also known as electrical array reconfiguration (EAR), overcomes these drawbacks by enabling real-time alteration of electrical connections through embedded switching mechanisms. In such systems, each panel is equipped with smart monitoring devices (SMDs) that include sensors for measuring irradiance and temperature, as well as relay actuators capable of rerouting current paths. The array thus becomes a cyber-physical system: a network of physical components integrated with computational elements that enable remote monitoring, data analytics, and autonomous control. EAR strategies typically aim to select a topology that maximizes power output for a given shading pattern by evaluating candidate configurations such as SP, BL, HC, and TCT. The BL and HC topologies introduce cross-connections between adjacent strings to balance current flow, while the TCT configuration fully interconnects all rows to equalize voltage and enhance current delivery under mismatched conditions.

Early dynamic reconfiguration methods relied on exhaustive simulation or heuristic rules. For example, some approaches compute the maximum power for each candidate topology using circuit simulators and select the one yielding the highest output. While accurate, this method is computationally prohibitive for large arrays and unsuitable for real-time operation due to its high latency. Other strategies, such as irradiance equalization, attempt to balance the total irradiance per row to minimize mismatch, but they often fail to account for nonlinear I-V characteristics and wiring losses, leading to suboptimal decisions.

More recently, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising alternative for topology selection. Supervised learning models, including artificial neural networks (ANNs), support vector machines (SVMs), and ensemble methods, have been trained to map irradiance patterns to optimal topologies. A prior study by the inventors demonstrated feasibility using a shallow ANN on a small 3×4 array under idealized, lossless conditions. However, such models suffer from overfitting, poor generalization to unseen shading scenarios, and limited scalability to larger arrays with realistic parasitic effects such as wiring resistance. Moreover, existing ML approaches have not systematically evaluated performance across multiple topologies or incorporated modern regularization techniques essential for robustness in real-world deployments.

Furthermore, most prior work neglects the impact of wiring losses, which are inherent in physical implementations due to resistive elements in interconnecting cables. These losses vary with topology and current distribution, and their omission leads to overly optimistic performance estimates. Accurate modeling of such losses is critical for developing practical reconfiguration algorithms that translate effectively from simulation to field deployment.

In light of these limitations, there remains a pressing need for a scalable, generalizable, and computationally efficient method for dynamic PV topology reconfiguration that operates under realistic conditions—including wiring losses—and leverages advanced machine learning architectures to deliver reliable, high-accuracy decisions in real time. The present invention addresses this need by introducing a novel regularized deep neural network framework specifically designed for topology optimization in 5×5 PV arrays, validated against conventional ML baselines and shown to deliver an average power improvement of approximately 11% over standard SP configurations.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Approach

The present invention provides a comprehensive cyber-physical framework for dynamic reconfiguration of photovoltaic (PV) array topologies to mitigate power losses induced by partial shading. At the core of this framework is a supervised machine learning pipeline that transforms real-time panel-level irradiance measurements into actionable control signals for topology switching. The approach begins with the generation of a synthetic yet representative dataset that captures a wide spectrum of partial shading conditions across a 5×5 PV array. Each instance in this dataset consists of a 25-dimensional irradiance vector, where each element corresponds to the irradiance incident on a specific panel, encoded as either shaded (binary 1) or unshaded (binary 0). The actual irradiance values for shaded and unshaded panels are drawn from distinct uniform distributions—specifically, [0, 586) W/m² for shaded and [586, 1000] W/m² for unshaded—based on empirical thresholds derived from real-world solar irradiance data, such as that available from the National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB). This binary mapping scheme ensures diversity in shading patterns while maintaining statistical realism.

For each irradiance instance, the dataset is labeled by determining the optimal topology among four candidate configurations: series-parallel (SP), bridge-link (BL), honeycomb (HC), and total-cross-tied (TCT). This labeling is performed through high-fidelity circuit simulation using MATLAB-Simulink, where each topology is modeled with realistic electrical parameters, including panel-level bypass diodes and explicit wiring resistances. Specifically, a resistance of 0.01 Ω is introduced between panels across adjacent strings to model inter-string losses, and a resistance of 0.005 Ω is placed between panels within the same string to account for intra-string losses. These resistive elements are critical for capturing the true power dissipation characteristics of physical arrays, thereby ensuring that the learned model generalizes to real-world deployments. The maximum power point (MPP) for each topology is computed under a fixed temperature of 25°C, and the label assigned to the irradiance instance is the index of the topology that yields the highest MPP. This process results in a labeled dataset of 8,000 instances, each comprising a 25-dimensional input vector and a categorical output in {1, 2, 3, 4} corresponding to SP, BL, HC, and TCT, respectively.

The labeled dataset is then partitioned into training (90%) and testing (10%) subsets. Prior to training, the input features are standardized to have zero mean and unit variance, a preprocessing step that enhances the stability and convergence of gradient-based optimization. The central innovation of the approach lies in the design and training of a regularized deep neural network (DNN) that learns the complex, nonlinear mapping from irradiance patterns to optimal topologies. Unlike conventional ML models, this DNN incorporates two key regularization techniques—dropout and batch normalization—strategically applied across hidden layers to prevent overfitting and improve generalization. The network architecture consists of six fully connected layers with neuron counts of 64, 64, 128, 256, 64, and 64, respectively. Each layer applies an affine transformation followed by a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, dropout, and batch normalization (except the first and last layers, where regularization is omitted for architectural stability). The output layer employs a softmax activation to produce a probability distribution over the four topology classes, enabling probabilistic interpretation and confidence scoring.

During training, the DNN is optimized using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10⁻⁴ over 200 epochs, minimizing the categorical cross-entropy loss. The dropout rate is empirically set to 20% in layers 2 through 5, based on hyperparameter search, to balance regularization strength and model capacity. Batch normalization is applied in the same layers to mitigate internal covariate shift, accelerate convergence, and reduce sensitivity to initialization. Upon completion of training, the model is deployed in inference mode, where it receives real-time irradiance profiles from the PV array’s sensor network and outputs a predicted topology. This prediction is then translated into control signals for a switch bank—comprising solid-state relays or MOSFET-based actuators—integrated with each panel’s SMD. The switch bank reconfigures the electrical interconnections to realize the selected topology, thereby maximizing power extraction under the current shading conditions.

This approach offers several advantages over prior art. First, it is fully data-driven and does not rely on handcrafted heuristics or exhaustive simulation at runtime, enabling millisecond-scale decision latency suitable for real-time control. Second, by incorporating wiring losses in the training data, the model learns to account for parasitic effects that are often ignored in theoretical studies, enhancing practical relevance. Third, the use of regularization techniques ensures robustness to noise and variability in irradiance measurements, a critical requirement for field operation. Finally, the framework is modular and extensible: the same pipeline can be adapted to larger arrays, additional topologies, or different environmental conditions by retraining the model with appropriately generated data.

### Neural Network Model

The neural network model forming the basis of the present invention is a custom-designed, feed-forward deep neural network (DNN) engineered specifically for the task of photovoltaic (PV) array topology classification under partial shading. The architecture is meticulously constructed to balance representational capacity, computational efficiency, and generalization performance, with particular attention to mitigating the risks of overfitting that commonly plague neural networks trained on limited or structured datasets. The input to the network is a 25-dimensional vector **x** ∈ ℝ²⁵, where each component represents the irradiance level (in W/m²) incident on a corresponding panel in a 5×5 PV array. This input is preprocessed by standardization—subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation computed over the training set—to ensure that all features contribute equally to the learning process and to facilitate stable gradient propagation.

The network comprises six sequential hidden layers, with the number of neurons in each layer following the sequence: 64, 64, 128, 256, 64, and 64. This pyramidal structure, which expands to a bottleneck at the fourth layer before contracting, is designed to progressively extract higher-level abstractions from the raw irradiance data while preserving sufficient capacity to model the intricate spatial dependencies inherent in shading patterns. Each hidden layer performs three operations in sequence: (1) an affine transformation **z** = **W****a** + **b**, where **W** is the weight matrix, **b** is the bias vector, and **a** is the activation from the previous layer; (2) application of the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function, defined as ReLU(**z**) = max(0, **z**), which introduces nonlinearity and sparsity; and (3) regularization via dropout and batch normalization, applied in layers 2 through 5.

Dropout is implemented as a stochastic regularization technique wherein, during each training iteration, a fraction of neurons in the designated layers is randomly “dropped out” by setting their outputs to zero. The dropout rate is fixed at 20%, meaning that 20% of neurons are deactivated per forward pass. This prevents the network from becoming overly reliant on any specific subset of neurons, effectively training an ensemble of thinned subnetworks and averaging their predictions at test time. During inference, dropout is disabled, and the weights are scaled by the retention probability (0.8) to maintain expected activation magnitudes.

Batch normalization is applied immediately after the ReLU activation in the same layers. For each mini-batch of training examples, batch normalization computes the mean and variance of each feature dimension across the batch and uses these statistics to normalize the activations to zero mean and unit variance. Learnable scale (γ) and shift (β) parameters are then applied to restore representational flexibility. This mechanism stabilizes the distribution of layer inputs over the course of training, reducing internal covariate shift and enabling faster convergence with higher learning rates. It also acts as a mild regularizer by introducing noise through batch-dependent normalization statistics.

The final layer of the network consists of four neurons, each corresponding to one of the target topologies: SP (class 1), BL (class 2), HC (class 3), and TCT (class 4). These neurons apply no activation function beyond the affine transformation, producing raw logits **z** ∈ ℝ⁴. The logits are then passed through a softmax function to yield a probability distribution **ŷ** over the four classes:

\[
\hat{y}_j = \frac{\exp(z_j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{4} \exp(z_k)}, \quad j = 1, 2, 3, 4
\]

The predicted class is the one with the highest probability, i.e., \(\arg\max_j \hat{y}_j\). The network is trained end-to-end using backpropagation to minimize the categorical cross-entropy loss:

\[
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{4} y_{ij} \log(\hat{y}_{ij})
\]

where \(y_{ij}\) is the ground-truth label (one-hot encoded) for the \(i\)-th sample and \(N\) is the batch size. Optimization is performed using the Adam algorithm, which combines adaptive learning rates with momentum estimation, with a fixed learning rate of 10⁻⁴ and default hyperparameters (β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸). Training proceeds for 200 epochs with early stopping monitored on validation loss to prevent overfitting.

The resulting model is compact, with fewer than 50,000 trainable parameters, making it suitable for deployment on edge computing devices such as microcontrollers or FPGA-based controllers integrated within the PV array’s SMDs. Its inference latency is on the order of milliseconds, enabling real-time reconfiguration in response to rapidly changing cloud cover or moving shadows. Moreover, the probabilistic output allows for uncertainty quantification: if the maximum class probability falls below a confidence threshold, the system may defer reconfiguration or trigger a fallback strategy, enhancing operational safety.

### Results and Discussion

The efficacy of the proposed regularized deep neural network (DNN) for PV topology reconfiguration was rigorously evaluated through a series of experiments on a synthetically generated dataset of 8,000 irradiance instances, each labeled with the optimal topology among SP, BL, HC, and TCT based on simulated maximum power output under realistic wiring losses. The DNN achieved an average test accuracy of 81.1% and a macro-average F1 score of 0.74, significantly outperforming conventional machine learning baselines including K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests (RF), and XGBoost. The confusion matrix revealed strong diagonal dominance, indicating consistent correct classification across all four topology classes, with the highest accuracy observed for the TCT configuration and slightly lower performance for BL and HC—likely due to their structural similarity and overlapping power characteristics under certain shading patterns.

Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of regularization: removing dropout and batch normalization led to a 7–9% drop in test accuracy and pronounced overfitting, as evidenced by diverging training and validation loss curves. The inclusion of wiring losses in the simulation setup proved equally vital; models trained on lossless data exhibited a 12% degradation in real-world applicability when tested on loss-inclusive scenarios, underscoring the necessity of physically accurate modeling.

Beyond classification performance, the practical merit of topology reconfiguration was quantified by analyzing power gains relative to the standard SP configuration. Across the test set, switching from SP to the DNN-predicted optimal topology yielded a power improvement exceeding 50 W in 68% of cases, with an average gain of approximately 11%. This translates to meaningful energy yield enhancements in utility-scale deployments, where even single-digit percentage improvements can justify the cost of reconfiguration hardware. Illustrative P-V curves demonstrated that, under severe shading, TCT and HC configurations could recover up to 25% more power than SP by redistributing current paths and mitigating hotspot effects.

Compared to prior art, the present invention offers superior scalability and integration readiness. Unlike exhaustive simulation-based methods, which require seconds to minutes per decision, the DNN operates in milliseconds, enabling continuous adaptation to dynamic shading. Unlike static reconfiguration schemes, it requires no manual intervention or physical panel movement. And unlike earlier ML attempts, it generalizes reliably to unseen shading patterns thanks to its regularized architecture and realistic training regime.

One limitation is the restriction to four predefined topologies; future work may explore generative or reinforcement learning approaches to discover novel interconnection patterns. Nevertheless, the current system represents a significant advance toward autonomous, intelligent solar farms capable of self-optimization under real-world constraints.

## Computing Device

The present invention may be implemented on a computing device that serves as the central processing unit within a cyber-physical photovoltaic (PV) system. This computing device is communicatively coupled to a network of smart monitoring devices (SMDs) distributed across the PV array, each SMD being associated with one or more solar panels and equipped with irradiance sensors, temperature sensors, and relay actuators for topology reconfiguration. The computing device comprises a processor, a memory unit, and a communication interface configured to receive real-time sensor data from the SMDs, execute the trained deep neural network model, and transmit control signals back to the SMDs to effectuate the selected topology.

The processor is a multi-core central processing unit (CPU) or a low-power microcontroller (e.g., ARM Cortex-M series) capable of performing floating-point arithmetic and executing machine learning inference tasks. In alternative embodiments, the processor may be supplemented or replaced by a graphics processing unit (GPU), a neural processing unit (NPU), or a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) to accelerate neural network computations, particularly in large-scale arrays where low-latency response is critical. The memory unit includes both volatile memory (e.g., RAM) for storing the active neural network weights and intermediate activations during inference, and non-volatile memory (e.g., flash storage) for persisting the trained model, historical irradiance data, and system logs. The communication interface supports wireless protocols such as Wi-Fi, Zigbee, or LoRaWAN, or wired standards like RS-485 or Ethernet, enabling robust and secure data exchange with the SMDs over distances typical of solar farms.

The computing device executes a software stack comprising an operating system (e.g., Linux or a real-time OS), a data acquisition module, a preprocessing module, an inference engine, and a control module. The data acquisition module polls the SMDs at regular intervals (e.g., every 5–10 seconds) to collect panel-level irradiance readings. The preprocessing module standardizes these readings using the mean and standard deviation derived during training, ensuring compatibility with the neural network’s input expectations. The inference engine loads the trained DNN model—serialized in a format such as TensorFlow Lite or ONNX—and computes the predicted topology. The control module then translates this prediction into a bitmask or command sequence that specifies the state (open/closed) of each relay in the switch bank, which is transmitted to the SMDs via the communication interface.

The device is housed in a weatherproof enclosure with thermal management features (e.g., heat sinks or fans) to ensure reliable operation in outdoor environments. It may also include a backup power supply (e.g., a supercapacitor or battery) to maintain functionality during grid outages. In distributed architectures, multiple such devices may operate in a hierarchical manner, with local controllers managing subsections of the array and a central server coordinating global optimization. The computing device thus forms the intelligent core of the reconfiguration system, bridging the physical world of solar energy harvesting with the digital realm of data-driven decision-making.

## Methods

The present invention further provides a method for dynamically reconfiguring the electrical topology of a photovoltaic (PV) array to maximize power output under partial shading conditions. The method comprises the following steps: (a) receiving, at a computing device, a plurality of irradiance measurements from a corresponding plurality of solar panels arranged in an N×M array, wherein each irradiance measurement corresponds to the solar irradiance incident on a respective panel; (b) preprocessing the irradiance measurements to generate a standardized irradiance vector, wherein standardization comprises subtracting a predetermined mean value and dividing by a predetermined standard deviation, said mean and standard deviation having been computed from a training dataset used to train a deep neural network model; (c) inputting the standardized irradiance vector into the deep neural network model, wherein the deep neural network model is a feed-forward neural network comprising six hidden layers with neuron counts of 64, 64, 128, 256, 64, and 64, respectively, and wherein layers two through five incorporate dropout regularization with a dropout rate of 20% and batch normalization; (d) obtaining, from the deep neural network model, a predicted topology label indicating one of four candidate topologies: series-parallel (SP), bridge-link (BL), honeycomb (HC), or total-cross-tied (TCT); (e) generating a set of control signals based on the predicted topology label, wherein each control signal corresponds to a switching element in a switch bank associated with the PV array; and (f) transmitting the set of control signals to the switch bank to reconfigure the electrical interconnections among the solar panels according to the predicted topology.

In one embodiment, the deep neural network model is trained using a dataset comprising 8,000 irradiance instances, each instance labeled with the topology that yields the maximum power output under simulated conditions that include wiring resistances of 0.01 Ω between adjacent strings and 0.005 Ω within strings. The training is performed using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10⁻⁴ over 200 epochs, minimizing categorical cross-entropy loss. The method may further include the step of validating the predicted topology by comparing the predicted class probability to a confidence threshold, and abstaining from reconfiguration if the threshold is not met.

In another embodiment, the irradiance measurements are sampled at a frequency of at least once every 10 seconds to enable real-time adaptation to changing shading conditions. The method may be implemented in a cyber-physical system wherein the computing device and the switch bank are integrated within a distributed network of smart monitoring devices, each co-located with a solar panel. The method thereby enables autonomous, low-latency, and high-efficiency operation of PV systems in diverse and dynamic environmental conditions.
