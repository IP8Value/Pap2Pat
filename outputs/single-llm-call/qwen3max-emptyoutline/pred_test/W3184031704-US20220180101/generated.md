# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of machine learning and computer vision, and more specifically to systems and methods for self-supervised video representation learning through cooperative contrastive learning. The invention provides a novel framework for learning high-level semantic representations from unlabeled video data by leveraging multiple complementary views of the same visual content, such as RGB frames, optical flow, human pose keypoints, and segmentation masks. The disclosed technology enables the extraction of meaningful features that capture the underlying semantic structure of video content without requiring manual annotation or supervision, thereby facilitating downstream applications such as video classification, action recognition, and video retrieval. The invention is particularly relevant to the domains of artificial intelligence, deep learning, and multimedia analysis, where the ability to learn robust representations from large-scale unlabeled video datasets is crucial for advancing the state-of-the-art in automated video understanding.

The technical field encompasses several interrelated areas including but not limited to: self-supervised learning algorithms for visual representation learning; multi-view learning frameworks that exploit complementary information from different modalities or representations of the same data; contrastive learning methodologies that learn representations by distinguishing between similar and dissimilar instances; video analysis and understanding systems that process temporal sequences of visual data; and deep neural network architectures designed for processing spatio-temporal information. The invention addresses fundamental challenges in these areas, particularly the limitation of existing self-supervised approaches that rely solely on structural constraints such as temporal or spatial proximity to define positive and negative pairs, which often leads to representations that capture low-level discriminative features rather than high-level semantic content.

Furthermore, the invention operates within the broader context of representation learning, where the goal is to automatically discover the representations needed for feature detection or classification from raw data. In the specific case of video data, this involves capturing both spatial information within individual frames and temporal dynamics across sequences of frames. The disclosed cooperative contrastive learning framework represents a significant advancement over existing approaches by introducing a mechanism for cross-view consistency that encourages different views of the same video content to develop aligned similarity structures, thereby promoting the emergence of higher-order semantic relationships that are consistent across multiple representations of the same underlying visual phenomena.

## DESCRIPTION OF RELATED ART

Self-supervised learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for visual representation learning, offering the potential to leverage vast amounts of unlabeled data to learn meaningful features without requiring expensive manual annotation. In the domain of image representation learning, various approaches have been developed that exploit different forms of self-supervision, including color information manipulation, spatial relationship modeling, super-resolution tasks, and cross-domain learning frameworks. These methods typically create pretext tasks that can be solved using only the input data itself, thereby generating supervisory signals that guide the learning process toward useful representations.

In the video domain, self-supervised learning approaches have primarily focused on exploiting the inherent temporal structure of video sequences. One prominent class of methods involves predicting future frames or their representations based on past observations. These approaches can be broadly categorized into two types: those that attempt to reconstruct actual future frames and those that predict latent representations of future frames. The latter approach, inspired by developments in language modeling, has gained popularity because it allows the model to focus on higher-order semantic features rather than attempting to capture stochastic variations in frame appearance such as illumination changes, camera motion, or reflection effects. Recent work has utilized Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE) to perform prediction of latent representations rather than exact future frames, leading to improved performance in downstream tasks.

Another class of self-supervised video learning approaches is based on temporal ordering of frames, where the model learns to distinguish between correctly ordered sequences and randomly shuffled ones. Additional proxy tasks include temporal coherence analysis and 3D puzzle solving, which exploit spatio-temporal structures to create meaningful learning objectives. These methods typically rely on structural constraints such as temporal or spatial proximity to define positive and negative pairs for contrastive learning, where instances from the same video are considered positive pairs while instances from different videos are treated as negative pairs.

Multi-view learning has been recognized as a valuable approach for improving representation quality by leveraging complementary information from different perspectives or representations of the same data. The foundational idea is that useful higher-order semantics are consistently present across different views, while each view also provides unique complementary information that can aid learning in other views. In computer vision, this concept has been successfully applied through two-stream networks for action recognition, where separate streams process RGB frames and optical flow, demonstrating that even derivable views like optical flow can significantly improve performance compared to using RGB alone.

Recent approaches to multi-view self-supervised learning have extended these ideas by utilizing diverse views, sometimes derivable from one another, to learn better representations. Most of these methods employ core techniques such as contrastive learning and mutual information maximization to align representations across different views. However, despite the improvements achieved through view fusion, these approaches still suffer from the fundamental limitation of traditional contrastive learning: they enforce similarity constraints based solely on structural relationships, treating all instances from different videos as negatives even when they represent the same semantic content. This inherent drawback forces the learning of features with limited semantic knowledge and encourages low-level discrimination between different videos rather than capturing meaningful semantic relationships.

The key limitation of existing approaches is their inability to recognize implicit relationships between instances that share semantic content but originate from different videos. For example, two video clips showing the same action performed by different people in different environments would be treated as negative pairs in traditional contrastive learning frameworks, despite their semantic similarity. This restriction prevents the emergence of higher-level semantic representations that could generalize across different instances of the same concept. While some recent approaches have attempted to address multi-modal self-supervised learning with impressive results, they continue to suffer from the same fundamental drawback of discriminating between semantically similar instances due to their reliance on structural constraints for pair mining.

The present invention addresses these limitations by introducing a novel cooperative contrastive learning framework that leverages inter-view information to uncover implicit relationships between instances in a self-supervised multi-view setting. Unlike previous approaches that simply maximize mutual information between views or apply standard contrastive losses independently to each view, the disclosed method actively encourages consistency in similarity structures across different views, allowing each view to inform and guide the learning process in other views. This cooperative approach creates a positive feedback loop where better models generate better proposals for similar instances, which in turn improves the performance of all views simultaneously.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE DISCLOSURE

The present invention discloses a cooperative contrastive learning (CoCon) framework for self-supervised video representation learning that overcomes the limitations of existing approaches by leveraging multiple complementary views of video content to learn higher-level semantic representations. The core innovation lies in the introduction of a cooperative mechanism that encourages consistency in similarity structures across different views, thereby enabling the discovery of implicit relationships between instances that share semantic content but may originate from different videos.

The invention utilizes multiple views of the same video content, including both directly available views such as RGB frames and optical flow, as well as high-level inferred semantics generated as additional noisy views using off-the-shelf computer vision models, such as human pose keypoints and segmentation masks. These views, while potentially noisy and derived from the original input, provide complementary information that can be leveraged to improve representation learning across all views simultaneously.

The cooperative contrastive learning framework operates by computing view-specific distances between instances and then synchronizing these distances across all views through a consistency loss. This approach effectively encourages relationships between instances to be consistent across different views, meaning that pairs of instances that are similar in one view should also be similar in other views. This is achieved through two main components: a synchronization loss that minimizes the inconsistency between similarity matrices across views, and a hinge loss that promotes well-distributed distances by pushing representations of the same instance in different views closer together while pushing different instances apart.

The invention demonstrates that this cooperative approach leads to significant improvements in downstream video analysis tasks such as action classification, with experimental results showing that the proposed method outperforms existing state-of-the-art approaches even when trained on smaller datasets. Notably, the framework achieves better performance using multiple views of a small dataset (UCF101) compared to training on a much larger dataset (Kinetics400) with traditional methods, highlighting the effectiveness of the cooperative learning approach.

The extensible nature of the disclosed framework makes it applicable to any publicly available video dataset and compatible with other contrastive learning approaches, as it can be used in conjunction with existing self-supervised learning backbones. The invention represents a significant advancement in self-supervised video representation learning by addressing the fundamental limitation of existing methods that treat all instances from different videos as negatives, thereby enabling the emergence of higher-order semantic relationships that are consistent across multiple representations of the same underlying visual phenomena.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive framework for cooperative contrastive learning (CoCon) that enables self-supervised video representation learning through the strategic integration of multiple complementary views of video content. The detailed implementation of this framework involves several key components that work together to achieve superior representation learning capabilities compared to existing approaches.

The foundation of the CoCon framework begins with the processing of video data, where a video V is defined as a sequence of T frames with resolution H × W and C channels, represented as {i₁, i₂, ..., iₜ}, where each frame iₜ ∈ ℝ^(H×W×C). The video is partitioned into N disjoint blocks, where each block xⱼ ∈ ℝ^(K×H×W×C) contains K consecutive frames, assuming T = N × K. Each block is processed through a non-linear encoder f(·) that transforms the input block into its latent representation zⱼ = f(xⱼ). The encoder architecture is typically implemented using a 3D-ResNet structure, where convolutional kernels in the last two residual blocks are expanded to be three-dimensional to capture spatio-temporal information effectively.

Following the encoding step, an aggregation function g(·) takes the sequence of latent representations {z₁, z₂, ..., zⱼ} as input and generates a context representation cⱼ = g(z₁, z₂, ..., zⱼ). In the preferred implementation, the aggregation function is realized as a one-layer Convolutional Gated Recurrent Unit (ConvGRU) with kernel size (1, 1), where weights are shared across all spatial positions in the feature map. This design allows the aggregation function to propagate features along the temporal axis while maintaining spatial relationships. The resulting latent representations zⱼ ∈ ℝ^(H'×W'×D) preserve spatial layout information, where H' and W' represent down-sampled spatial dimensions and D represents the embedding size. These representations are then pooled to obtain zⱼ ∈ ℝ^D, and the composite function F(·) = g(f(·)) generates the final context representation c = F(V).

The framework creates a predictive task involving the prediction of future block representations based on past observations, following the general approach established in prior work but enhanced through the cooperative learning mechanism. Specifically, a predictive function φ(·) takes the context representation cₜ as input and predicts the latent state of future frames. This prediction task is designed to force the model to capture both contextual semantics in cₜ and frame-level semantics in zₜ, with the model typically configured to predict the next three blocks using the first five blocks as input.

For multi-view processing, the framework defines separate encoders φᵥ for each view v, where each view processes its corresponding input Vᵥ through its dedicated encoder to produce context features cᵥ = Fᵥ(Vᵥ). The views utilized in the preferred embodiment include RGB frames, optical flow (using either TVL1 flow for UCF101 and HMDB51 datasets or Farneback flow for Kinetics400), pose keypoint heatmaps generated using off-the-shelf keypoint detectors, and human segmentation masks produced by pre-trained semantic segmentation networks. These additional views, while potentially noisy due to the use of off-the-shelf models without post-processing, provide valuable complementary information that enhances the overall representation learning process.

The core innovation of the CoCon framework lies in its cooperative learning mechanism, which consists of two main loss components that work together to synchronize similarity structures across different views. The first component is the synchronization loss L_sync, which enforces consistency between distance matrices computed from different views. Specifically, for each view v, a graph similarity matrix Wᵛ of size K × K is constructed using cosine distance as the distance metric, where Wᵛ_ab = zᵃ · zᵇ represents the similarity between instances a and b in view v. The synchronization loss is then defined as the inconsistency between these similarity matrices across all pairs of views, effectively minimizing the differences between view-specific similarity structures.

The second component is the hinge loss L_sim, which promotes well-distributed distances by balancing positive and negative pair relationships. This loss function consists of two terms: the first term pushes representations of the same instance across different views closer together, while the second term pushes representations of different instances apart. To address the imbalance between the typically larger number of structural negative pairs compared to positive pairs, a balancing parameter μ is introduced to ensure equal contribution from both components of the loss. The value of μ is chosen such that the first and second components contribute equally to the overall loss, preventing the model from collapsing to trivial solutions.

The complete cooperative loss is formulated as L_coop = L_sync + α · L_sim, where α is a weighting parameter that balances the contributions of the synchronization and hinge loss components. In the preferred implementation, α is set to 1.0, although the framework demonstrates robust performance across different values of this parameter. The overall training objective combines the cooperative loss with the base contrastive predictive coding (CPC) loss L_cpc, resulting in the total loss L_total = Σᵥ L_cpcᵛ + L_coop, where the summation is over all views v.

The training procedure follows a two-stage approach. In the first stage, individual view-specific models are trained independently using only the L_cpc loss for a predetermined number of epochs (100 epochs for UCF101, 80 epochs for Kinetics400). This initial training phase ensures that each view develops reasonable baseline representations before engaging in cooperative learning. In the second stage, all view-specific models are trained simultaneously using the complete L_total loss for an additional period (60 epochs for UCF101, 40 epochs for Kinetics400). This cooperative training phase allows the models to benefit from cross-view information and develop synchronized similarity structures.

Data augmentation plays a crucial role in the training process, with different augmentation strategies applied depending on the view type. For RGB images, the framework employs random cropping, random horizontal flipping, random greying, and color jittering. Optical flow inputs receive only random cropping to preserve motion information. Pose keypoint heatmaps and segmentation confidence masks, treated as image-like inputs, undergo random cropping and horizontal flipping. Importantly, random cropping and flipping are applied consistently across entire blocks, while random greying and color jittering are applied frame-wise to prevent the network from learning low-level features such as optical flow patterns.

The implementation utilizes standard optimization techniques, employing the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10⁻³ and weight decay of 10⁻⁵. The learning rate is decayed to 10⁻⁴ when validation loss plateaus. Training is conducted using multiple GPUs (typically 4) with a batch size of 16 samples per GPU, ensuring sufficient negative examples despite the relatively small batch size through multiple spatio-temporal sampling strategies.

For downstream evaluation, the learned representations are assessed through their performance on action classification tasks. The evaluation protocol follows standard practice by using the self-supervised model weights as initialization for supervised fine-tuning. The complete architecture is then fine-tuned end-to-end using class label supervision, with the learned composite function F(·) generating context representations for video blocks. These context features pass through a spatial pooling layer followed by a fully-connected layer and a multi-way softmax for final classification. During inference, video clips from the validation set are densely sampled and processed with half-length overlapping blocks, with final predictions obtained by averaging softmax probabilities across all samples.

The framework's effectiveness is demonstrated through extensive experimental validation on standard benchmark datasets including UCF101 (13,320 videos across 101 action classes), HMDB51 (approximately 6,800 video clips across 51 action classes), and Kinetics400 (306,000 video clips across 400 human action classes). Results show consistent improvements across all views when increasing the number of views used during training, with particularly notable gains observed even when incorporating noisy views such as pose heatmaps and segmentation masks. The cooperative learning mechanism proves especially beneficial for views that are less expressive individually, such as optical flow, pose heatmaps, and segmentation masks, demonstrating the framework's ability to extract higher-order features even from low-dimensional inputs.

Qualitative analysis reveals that the CoCon framework successfully uncovers meaningful semantic relationships between action classes, with nearest neighbor relationships showing strong semantic coherence across different views. For instance, actions involving musical instruments cluster together, and sports-related activities form coherent groups, even when there is no direct visual commonality between the categories. This consistency across views, including those that lack background information such as optical flow and keypoint heatmaps, demonstrates that the framework cannot rely on superficial visual cues but must instead capture genuine semantic relationships.

An additional capability enabled by the improved representations is loose action alignment between videos, where the framework can identify corresponding segments in different instances of the same action despite variations in timing and execution. This is achieved by dividing videos into blocks, computing block-level features, and utilizing relative cosine similarities to infer associations between videos. The resulting alignment heatmaps reveal periodic patterns in repetitive actions such as pull-ups, demonstrating the framework's ability to capture temporal dynamics and structural regularities in human actions.

The extensible nature of the CoCon framework makes it particularly valuable for practical applications, as it can leverage "freely" available views that can be derived from standard video inputs without requiring specialized sensors or additional data collection. This enables the application of multi-view learning benefits even to datasets that are not traditionally considered multi-view, significantly expanding the scope of self-supervised learning applications in video analysis and understanding.
