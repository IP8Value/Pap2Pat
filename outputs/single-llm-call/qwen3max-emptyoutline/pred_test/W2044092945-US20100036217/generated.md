# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of medical imaging and diagnostic methods, particularly to a non-invasive, quantitative method for measuring tissue perfusion in peripheral tissues using near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging of indocyanine green (ICG) dynamics. The invention further encompasses systems and computational methods for generating spatially resolved perfusion maps and predictive tissue necrosis probability maps based on time-series analysis of ICG pharmacokinetics. The disclosed technology is applicable to preclinical research, therapeutic monitoring, and clinical diagnostics of conditions involving vascular insufficiency, such as peripheral arterial disease, diabetic complications, and post-ischemic tissue injury. The invention provides a novel framework for translating dynamic optical imaging data into quantifiable physiological parameters—specifically, regional perfusion rates—by integrating biophysical modeling with high-resolution spatiotemporal imaging. This approach enables not only the assessment of current tissue viability but also the prediction of future tissue outcomes, including the likelihood of necrosis, thereby offering a powerful tool for personalized medicine and intervention planning in vascular disorders.

## BACKGROUND

Peripheral vascular insufficiency represents a major clinical challenge, frequently arising from systemic conditions such as diabetes mellitus and atherosclerosis, which impair blood flow to distal extremities and increase the risk of tissue necrosis, ulceration, and amputation. Accurate assessment of tissue perfusion—the rate at which blood is delivered to capillary beds—is critical for both prognosis and therapeutic decision-making. However, existing clinical and preclinical tools for evaluating perfusion suffer from significant limitations in terms of quantification, sensitivity, cost, or invasiveness. Structural imaging modalities such as angiography, while useful for visualizing vessel anatomy, fail to capture functional perfusion status, which is more directly correlated with tissue viability. Conversely, functional imaging techniques like positron emission tomography (PET), single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide quantitative perfusion data but are prohibitively expensive, require specialized infrastructure, and are often unsuitable for longitudinal or high-throughput studies, particularly in animal models.

In preclinical research, the gold standard for quantitative perfusion measurement has been the microsphere technique, which involves injecting radiolabeled or fluorescent microspheres into the circulation and subsequently analyzing tissue samples ex vivo. While accurate, this method is terminal, precluding repeated measurements in the same subject and limiting its utility for monitoring dynamic processes such as therapeutic angiogenesis. Laser Doppler imaging (LDI) has been widely adopted as a non-invasive alternative for in vivo blood flow assessment; however, it provides only relative, qualitative measures of perfusion and lacks the sensitivity to distinguish subtle differences in perfusion that correlate with long-term tissue outcomes. Moreover, LDI is highly susceptible to motion artifacts and environmental noise, and its output is expressed in arbitrary perfusion units that cannot be standardized across subjects or time points.

Optical imaging using near-infrared (NIR) fluorophores, particularly indocyanine green (ICG), has emerged as a promising modality for vascular and perfusion imaging due to its deep tissue penetration, low autofluorescence background, and clinical safety profile. ICG, a tricarbocyanine dye approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for intravenous use, binds tightly to serum albumin upon injection, remaining intravascular under normal physiological conditions and enabling visualization of the vascular compartment. Its fluorescence emission in the NIR range (peak ~830 nm) allows imaging through several millimeters of tissue, making it suitable for superficial and peripheral tissue assessment. Despite these advantages, prior applications of ICG in perfusion imaging have largely been qualitative, relying on static or semi-quantitative metrics such as peak fluorescence intensity or time-to-peak without accounting for inter-subject variability in systemic pharmacokinetics, such as hepatic clearance rates, which significantly influence ICG dynamics.

Mathematical modeling of ICG kinetics has been attempted in cerebral and retinal perfusion studies, but these approaches have not yielded robust, generalizable quantitative perfusion metrics applicable to peripheral tissues. A key limitation has been the failure to decouple local tissue perfusion from systemic ICG clearance, leading to measurements that are confounded by individual differences in liver function, anesthesia depth, and hemodynamic status. Furthermore, existing methods do not provide spatially resolved perfusion maps at the pixel level, nor do they establish a predictive relationship between early perfusion measurements and subsequent tissue fate, such as necrosis.

The inability to quantitatively and predictively assess perfusion represents a critical gap in both research and clinical practice. In therapeutic angiogenesis studies, for example, the lack of a sensitive, quantitative endpoint impedes the evaluation of pro-angiogenic agents like vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and angiopoietin-1. Similarly, in the clinical setting, physicians often lack objective criteria to determine whether a limb with compromised perfusion will recover spontaneously or progress to necrosis, leading to delayed or inappropriate interventions. There is therefore a pressing need for a method that combines the non-invasiveness and accessibility of optical imaging with the rigor of quantitative physiology, enabling precise, reproducible, and predictive perfusion assessment in peripheral tissues.

## DISCLOSURE

### Technical Problem

The technical problem addressed by the present invention is the absence of a non-invasive, quantitative, and predictive method for assessing tissue perfusion in peripheral tissues that overcomes the limitations of existing imaging modalities. Specifically, conventional perfusion imaging techniques either lack quantitative accuracy (e.g., laser Doppler imaging), are too costly or invasive for routine or longitudinal use (e.g., PET, MRI, microsphere methods), or fail to account for inter-individual variability in systemic tracer kinetics when using contrast agents like indocyanine green (ICG). Prior attempts to use ICG dynamics for perfusion estimation have relied on static intensity measurements or simple temporal parameters that do not yield absolute perfusion rates and are confounded by differences in hepatic clearance, anesthesia effects, and tissue optical properties. Moreover, no existing method provides a spatially resolved perfusion map that can be directly correlated with future tissue outcomes, such as the probability of necrosis, thereby limiting its utility in prognostic decision-making and therapeutic monitoring. This problem is particularly acute in the context of peripheral vascular disease, where early and accurate prediction of tissue viability is essential for preventing irreversible damage and guiding interventions.

### Technical Solution

The present invention provides a novel solution to this technical problem through a method for quantitative measurement of tissue perfusion based on time-series near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging of indocyanine green (ICG) dynamics, coupled with a biophysically grounded mathematical model that accounts for systemic ICG clearance and translates pixel-level temporal fluorescence profiles into absolute perfusion rates. The core innovation lies in the derivation and application of a perfusion rate equation that uses two measurable parameters—the time-to-peak (Tmax) of ICG fluorescence in each pixel and the systemic half-life of ICG derived from a reference region (e.g., the trunk)—to compute a quantitative perfusion rate (%/min) for every pixel in the region of interest. This approach effectively normalizes for inter-subject variability in ICG pharmacokinetics, enabling direct comparison of perfusion across different subjects, time points, and experimental conditions.

The method begins with the intravenous administration of ICG, followed by rapid sequential acquisition of NIR fluorescence images over a period sufficient to capture the full ICG bolus passage and clearance (e.g., 12 minutes). Using a high-sensitivity imaging system capable of sub-second temporal resolution, the spatiotemporal dynamics of ICG fluorescence are recorded for both ischemic and control regions. A silhouette image is then acquired to define anatomical regions of interest (ROIs), such as the ischemic and normal hindlimbs. From a reference ROI in the trunk, the systemic ICG half-life (τ) is determined by fitting the fluorescence decay curve to a monoexponential function. Simultaneously, for each pixel in the tissue ROI, the time-to-peak (Tmax) is identified as the time point at which the first derivative of the fluorescence intensity curve equals zero.

These two parameters—Tmax and τ—are then input into a perfusion rate equation derived from Fick’s principle of mass conservation and a compartmental model of ICG kinetics, which assumes steady-state blood volume and first-order hepatic clearance. The resulting perfusion rate map is generated by assigning the calculated perfusion value to each corresponding pixel, producing a pseudocolor-coded image that visually represents the spatial distribution of perfusion rates with high resolution. This map not only distinguishes between macrovascular and microvascular compartments—evident as bimodal versus unimodal perfusion histograms—but also enables quantitative tracking of perfusion changes over time in response to therapeutic interventions.

Furthermore, the invention extends beyond mere perfusion measurement to include a predictive analytics component. By correlating early post-operative perfusion rates with actual tissue necrosis outcomes observed days later, an inverse sigmoidal relationship is established between regional perfusion rate and the probability of necrosis. This empirical relationship is then used to construct a tissue necrosis probability map, wherein each pixel’s perfusion rate is converted into a predicted likelihood of future necrosis, displayed as a color-coded overlay. This predictive capability represents a significant advancement over existing methods, which can only assess current perfusion status without forecasting tissue fate.

The invention thus solves the technical problem by providing a complete, integrated platform for quantitative, spatially resolved, and predictive perfusion imaging that is non-invasive, cost-effective, and suitable for both preclinical and potential clinical applications.

## BEST MODE

### MODE FOR INVENTION

The best mode for carrying out the invention involves a systematic protocol encompassing animal preparation, imaging acquisition, computational analysis, and predictive mapping, as detailed below. The method is exemplified using a murine hindlimb ischemia model, but the principles are generalizable to other peripheral tissues and species, including humans.

First, a subject (e.g., a mouse) is prepared under appropriate anesthesia, typically with a ketamine–xylazine mixture, to ensure immobility and physiological stability during imaging. Hindlimb ischemia is induced surgically by ligation and excision of the femoral artery and vein in one limb, while the contralateral limb serves as an internal control. For therapeutic studies, pro-angiogenic agents such as VEGF, COMP-angiopoietin-1 (cAng1), or combinations thereof may be administered via intramuscular injection into the ischemic limb at defined time points.

Immediately following surgery or at designated follow-up intervals (e.g., postoperative day 3 or 7), the subject undergoes NIR fluorescence imaging. A bolus of ICG (e.g., 0.1 mL of 400 µmol/L) is injected intravenously via the tail vein. A high-sensitivity NIR imaging system is employed, preferably one capable of rapid frame acquisition (e.g., 1-second intervals) to accurately capture the time-to-peak of ICG fluorescence, especially in well-perfused tissues. The imaging system consists of a cooled CCD camera equipped with a narrow-bandpass filter centered at 830 nm and illuminated by 760-nm LED arrays to excite ICG fluorescence. Sequential images are acquired for a duration of approximately 12 minutes to encompass the full ICG transit and clearance phase.

Following the time-series acquisition, a white light silhouette image is captured to delineate anatomical boundaries. Regions of interest (ROIs) are then manually or semi-automatically defined on this silhouette, including the ischemic hindlimb, the normal hindlimb, and a reference region in the trunk (e.g., a square mask over the abdominal area). These ROIs are applied to the time-series fluorescence images to extract pixel-level intensity data over time.

Computational analysis is performed using custom software implemented in environments such as MATLAB or Visual C++. For the trunk ROI, the average fluorescence intensity over time is fitted to a monoexponential decay function to determine the systemic ICG half-life (τ), which accounts for inter-subject variability in hepatic clearance. For each pixel within the limb ROIs, the time-to-peak (Tmax) is identified as the time point where the first derivative of the fluorescence intensity curve crosses zero from positive to negative, indicating the peak of the ICG bolus.

Using the derived values of Tmax and τ, the perfusion rate (P) for each pixel is calculated according to the following equation:

P = (1 / Tmax) * ln(2) / ln(2 + (ln(2) * Tmax) / τ)

This equation, derived from a compartmental model based on Fick’s law and assuming constant blood volume and first-order systemic clearance, yields perfusion rate in units of %/min, representing the fraction of blood exchanged per minute in the vascular volume of that pixel. The resulting perfusion values are compiled into a spatial map, where each pixel is color-coded according to its perfusion rate, generating a high-resolution perfusion map.

To enable predictive assessment, a calibration curve is established by correlating early perfusion measurements (e.g., 4 hours post-surgery) with necrosis outcomes observed at a later time point (e.g., postoperative day 7). In a cohort of subjects, regions that ultimately develop necrosis are compared to their initial perfusion rates, revealing an inverse sigmoidal relationship: regions with perfusion rates below a critical threshold (e.g., <20%/min) exhibit high necrosis probability, while those above a higher threshold (e.g., >100%/min) rarely necrose. This empirical relationship is mathematically modeled and stored in the analysis software.

During routine application, once a perfusion map is generated, the software automatically applies this necrosis probability function to each pixel’s perfusion rate, producing a tissue necrosis probability map. This map is displayed as a pseudocolor overlay, where colors indicate the likelihood of future necrosis (e.g., red for high probability, blue for low). This predictive map can be used to guide clinical decisions or evaluate therapeutic efficacy in real time.

The entire process—from imaging to predictive mapping—is designed to be non-invasive, rapid, and reproducible, representing the best mode for implementing the invention in both research and clinical settings.

## Comparative Example 1

### Prediction for Tissue Necrosis through Doppler Imaging

Laser Doppler imaging (LDI) is currently regarded as the clinical and preclinical “gold standard” for non-invasive assessment of cutaneous and peripheral blood flow. It operates by detecting the Doppler shift in laser light scattered by moving red blood cells, thereby providing a measure of microvascular perfusion. However, despite its widespread use, LDI suffers from fundamental limitations that render it inadequate for the quantitative prediction of tissue necrosis, as demonstrated in comparative studies against the present invention.

In a representative experiment, mice subjected to identical hindlimb ischemia surgery were imaged using both LDI and the ICG-based perfusion imaging method of the present invention immediately after surgery. LDI scans were performed using a commercial system (e.g., MLDI5063, Moor Instruments), with three consecutive scans acquired to ensure stability. The resulting LDI images displayed a generalized reduction in perfusion in the ischemic limb compared to the contralateral control, consistent with expected outcomes. However, when animals were stratified post hoc based on their necrosis severity on postoperative day 7—into no necrosis, moderate necrosis, and severe necrosis groups—the LDI-derived blood flow ratios (ischemic/normal) showed no statistically significant differences among the groups. The mean ratios were 0.32 ± 0.05, 0.29 ± 0.04, and 0.27 ± 0.03, respectively, with overlapping confidence intervals and p > 0.05 by ANOVA. This lack of discrimination indicates that LDI cannot resolve the subtle but critical differences in baseline perfusion that determine long-term tissue fate.

In contrast, the ICG-based method of the present invention revealed marked differences in absolute perfusion rates among the same groups: 47.2 ± 5.8%/min, 19.4 ± 2.1%/min, and 4.11 ± 0.7%/min, respectively, with p < 0.001. More importantly, spatial analysis showed that LDI provided only a homogeneous, low-resolution representation of the ischemic limb, failing to capture regional heterogeneity in perfusion. For instance, in a mouse that developed focal toe necrosis, LDI displayed uniformly reduced perfusion across the entire foot, offering no indication of which specific regions would necrose. Conversely, the ICG perfusion map exhibited clear spatial variation, with some pixels showing perfusion rates above 30%/min (predicted to survive) and others below 10%/min (predicted to necrose). The subsequent necrosis probability map, generated by applying the inverse sigmoidal function, precisely overlaid the actual necrotic regions observed seven days later.

This comparative example underscores the critical deficiency of LDI: it provides only relative, qualitative, and spatially averaged perfusion data that lack the sensitivity and quantification necessary for predictive necrosis assessment. The inability to assign absolute perfusion values or to resolve sub-regional differences means that LDI cannot inform targeted interventions or accurately stratify patients by risk. The present invention, by contrast, overcomes these limitations through quantitative, pixel-level perfusion mapping and empirically derived predictive modeling, thereby offering a superior diagnostic and prognostic tool.

## Example 1

### Establishment of Method of Measuring Perfusion Using ICG

The foundational step in the present invention was the establishment of a robust, reproducible method for measuring absolute tissue perfusion using indocyanine green (ICG) dynamics. This involved optimizing imaging parameters, validating the mathematical model, and demonstrating the correlation between ICG-derived perfusion rates and physiological reality.

Initially, time-series NIR fluorescence imaging was performed on mice with unilateral hindlimb ischemia using two imaging systems: a commercial Kodak Image Station 4000MM (20-second intervals) and a custom-built system with 1-second temporal resolution. Both systems used 760-nm excitation and 830-nm emission filtering. ICG (400 µmol/L, 0.1 mL) was injected intravenously, and fluorescence was recorded for 12 minutes. The custom system was essential for capturing the rapid time-to-peak (~20 seconds) in normal hindlimbs, which the Kodak system undersampled, though both adequately captured the slower dynamics in ischemic limbs.

Silhouette images were used to define ROIs for the ischemic limb, normal limb, and trunk. From the trunk ROI, the systemic ICG half-life (τ) was determined by fitting the fluorescence decay to F(t) = F₀·e^(−t/τ), yielding τ values ranging from 3 to 5 minutes, consistent with literature reports on hepatic clearance variability. For each pixel in the limb ROIs, the time-to-peak (Tmax) was identified algorithmically by computing the first derivative of the fluorescence-time curve and locating the zero-crossing point.

A compartmental model was then developed based on Fick’s principle: the rate of change of ICG concentration in a tissue voxel equals the difference between inflow and outflow, with outflow proportional to both concentration and perfusion rate. Assuming the vascular input function (VIF) follows the same exponential decay as the trunk, the model yielded the differential equation dF/dt = (F_VIF − F)·P − F/τ, where F is tissue fluorescence, F_VIF is the input function, P is perfusion rate, and τ is the systemic clearance time constant. Solving this equation and applying the condition dF/dt = 0 at Tmax led to the perfusion rate formula: P = (1/Tmax)·ln(2) / ln(2 + (ln(2)·Tmax)/τ).

To validate this model, in silico simulations were conducted with varying P values (10–500%/min). The simulated ICG curves matched empirical data closely, confirming the model’s biological plausibility. Experimentally, normal hindlimbs exhibited a bimodal perfusion histogram, with peaks at ~150%/min (microvasculature) and ~450%/min (macrovasculature). To confirm this assignment, skin was removed to expose the femoral artery, and ICG imaging revealed that the exposed vessel corresponded precisely to the high-perfusion peak, validating the model’s ability to distinguish vascular compartments.

Absolute perfusion rates were then quantified: normal limbs averaged 475 ± 19%/min, while ischemic limbs averaged 56 ± 6%/min, representing a 12.2% residual perfusion attributed to collateral circulation. This quantitative measurement established the method’s sensitivity and laid the groundwork for predictive and therapeutic applications.

## Example 2

### Measurement of Perfusion Using Indocyanine Green and the Construction of Perfusion Map and Tissue Necrosis Probability Map Based on Correlation Coefficient

Building upon the established ICG perfusion measurement method, the invention was extended to generate spatially resolved perfusion maps and, critically, predictive tissue necrosis probability maps. This was achieved by correlating early perfusion measurements with late-stage necrosis outcomes and applying a statistical transformation to convert perfusion rates into necrosis probabilities.

In a cohort of 20 mice subjected to hindlimb ischemia, ICG perfusion imaging was performed 4 hours post-surgery. Perfusion maps were constructed by calculating the perfusion rate for each of approximately 1,200–1,500 pixels in the ischemic limb using the Tmax and τ-derived equation. On postoperative day 7, the same limbs were examined for necrosis, and regions were classified as necrotic or non-necrotic based on visual inspection. For each 2×2 pixel block (representing a small tissue region), the average perfusion rate was computed and paired with its necrosis outcome.

Statistical analysis revealed a strong inverse relationship between perfusion rate and necrosis probability, best described by an inverse sigmoidal function: Necrosis Probability = 1 / (1 + e^(a·(P − b))), where P is perfusion rate, and a and b are fitted parameters. The correlation coefficient (R²) for this fit was 0.93, indicating excellent predictive power. Regions with P < 10%/min had >95% necrosis probability, while those with P > 60%/min had <5% probability.

This empirical function was then embedded into the analysis software. During routine imaging, once a perfusion map is generated, the software automatically applies this function to each pixel’s perfusion value, producing a necrosis probability map. This map is displayed as a pseudocolor image, where color intensity reflects the likelihood of future necrosis.

In validation experiments, the predicted necrosis maps showed remarkable concordance with actual necrosis patterns observed on day 7. For example, in a mouse with patchy toe necrosis, the probability map highlighted specific distal regions in red (high probability), which precisely matched the necrotic areas. In another case with no necrosis, the entire limb was coded in blue (low probability), consistent with the outcome.

Furthermore, the method was used to evaluate therapeutic angiogenesis. Mice treated with VEGF and cAng1 showed increased perfusion rates in initially low-perfusion regions, and the corresponding probability maps shifted from red to green/blue, accurately predicting improved tissue survival. This demonstrates the invention’s utility not only in diagnosis but also in monitoring treatment response.

Thus, Example 2 establishes the complete workflow of the invention: from ICG imaging to perfusion mapping to predictive necrosis probability mapping, all based on a robust, empirically derived correlation between quantitative perfusion and tissue fate.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

The present invention possesses broad industrial applicability across multiple sectors, including pharmaceutical research, medical device development, clinical diagnostics, and personalized medicine. In the pharmaceutical industry, the method provides a highly sensitive, quantitative, and non-invasive endpoint for evaluating the efficacy of pro-angiogenic, anti-angiogenic, or vasoprotective therapeutics in preclinical models of peripheral vascular disease, wound healing, and ischemic injury. By enabling precise measurement of perfusion changes at the pixel level, the invention reduces the number of animals required for statistically significant results, aligning with 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) in animal research. It also allows for longitudinal monitoring of the same subject, improving data quality and reducing inter-subject variability.

In the medical device sector, the invention can be integrated into commercial NIR imaging systems as a software module, transforming existing hardware into advanced diagnostic platforms capable of quantitative perfusion mapping and predictive analytics. This adds significant value to devices used in operating rooms, wound care centers, and vascular clinics, where real-time assessment of tissue viability is critical—for example, during flap surgery, burn debridement, or diabetic foot ulcer management.

Clinically, the invention has direct applicability in the diagnosis and management of peripheral arterial disease (PAD), critical limb ischemia (CLI), and diabetic complications. By providing an objective, quantitative measure of perfusion and a predictive map of necrosis risk, clinicians can make more informed decisions about revascularization, amputation levels, or conservative management. The method’s non-invasiveness, speed, and low cost make it suitable for point-of-care use, even in resource-limited settings.

Moreover, the underlying principles of the invention—dynamic tracer imaging coupled with biophysical modeling—are extensible to other contrast agents and imaging modalities, suggesting potential adaptations for cerebral, myocardial, or tumor perfusion assessment. The predictive modeling framework can also be trained on human data to develop AI-driven diagnostic tools for precision medicine.

In summary, the invention bridges a critical gap between qualitative imaging and quantitative physiology, offering a scalable, cost-effective, and clinically translatable solution with wide-ranging industrial applications in healthcare innovation.
