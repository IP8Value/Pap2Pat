# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The fabrication of bulk nanocrystalline and ultrafine-grained materials via powder consolidation has long been recognized as a promising route to achieve superior mechanical, thermal, and functional properties unattainable in conventional coarse-grained counterparts. However, the practical realization of this approach has been persistently hindered by two fundamental challenges: the thermodynamic instability of nanoscale grains during high-temperature processing, which leads to rapid grain coarsening, and the kinetic limitations of solid-state sintering that often result in incomplete densification and residual porosity. Traditional sintering of refractory metals such as tungsten, for example, typically requires temperatures exceeding 2,000 °C to achieve full density, conditions under which even micron-scale grains are prone to significant growth, let alone nanoscale microstructures. Consequently, the production of fully dense, bulk nanocrystalline components without the application of external pressure or electric fields has remained an elusive goal in materials science and engineering.

To address these limitations, various strategies have been developed to enhance sintering kinetics while simultaneously stabilizing fine grain structures. Among these, liquid phase sintering has been widely employed, wherein a low-melting-point additive forms a transient liquid that facilitates mass transport and pore elimination. However, this approach is inherently restricted to systems with suitable phase diagrams and often results in undesirable secondary phases or compositional inhomogeneities in the final product. Solid-state activated sintering, another common technique, relies on the segregation of dopants to grain boundaries to form disordered, nanometer-thick interfacial films that enhance diffusion. While effective in certain systems like tungsten doped with nickel or iron, this method typically requires precise control over dopant chemistry and is limited by the thermodynamic stability of the grain boundary films, which may not persist at lower temperatures or in refractory alloy systems.

More recently, advanced consolidation techniques such as spark plasma sintering (SPS) and hot isostatic pressing (HIP) have demonstrated success in producing dense nanocrystalline materials by applying high pressures and/or pulsed electric currents to accelerate densification at reduced temperatures. Nevertheless, these methods are capital-intensive, impose geometric constraints on component size and shape, and are generally unsuitable for large-scale or cost-sensitive industrial applications. Moreover, the reliance on external fields or mechanical loading complicates the integration of these processes into conventional manufacturing workflows.

In parallel, the concept of “seed-assisted sintering” has emerged, wherein second-phase particles are introduced to pin grain boundaries and suppress coarsening during sintering. While this approach effectively mitigates grain growth, it does not inherently accelerate densification; in fact, excessive second-phase content can impede pore removal and reduce final density. Thus, despite decades of research, there remains a critical need for a sintering methodology that simultaneously enables rapid, pressureless densification and preserves nanoscale microstructures—particularly in high-melting-point alloy systems where conventional approaches fall short.

The present invention arises from a novel understanding of the interplay between nanocrystallinity, supersaturation, and phase separation in mechanically alloyed powders. It has been discovered that when a supersaturated solid solution is engineered with nanoscale grain structure within micron-sized powder particles, controlled thermal decomposition during heating can lead to the precipitation of a second phase not only within the particle interiors but, crucially, at particle surfaces and interparticle necks. This surface-localized second phase acts as a highly efficient diffusional transport pathway, dramatically accelerating densification at temperatures far below those required for conventional sintering of the base material. Importantly, this mechanism operates entirely in the solid state, without the formation of a liquid phase, and does not rely on externally applied pressure or electric fields. The resulting process—termed herein “nanophase separation sintering”—represents a paradigm shift in powder consolidation technology, offering a generalizable route to fully dense, bulk nanocrystalline alloys with unprecedented control over microstructure and composition.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel method for the pressureless sintering of bulk nanocrystalline and ultrafine-grained alloys, based on the principle of nanophase separation sintering. This method enables the production of fully dense components from mechanically alloyed powders at significantly reduced temperatures and without the application of external pressure, overcoming longstanding limitations in the field of powder metallurgy. The invention specifically relates to the preparation and thermal processing of supersaturated, nanocrystalline alloy powders wherein each powder particle is micron-sized and internally composed of nanoscale grains of a single-phase solid solution that is metastable with respect to phase separation.

In accordance with the invention, a powder is first prepared by high-energy mechanical alloying of a base metal with one or more solute elements that exhibit negligible equilibrium solubility in the base metal at ambient temperature but can be forced into supersaturated solid solution through severe plastic deformation. The resulting powder consists of micron-sized particles, each of which is polycrystalline with an average grain size of less than 100 nanometers, preferably less than 50 nanometers, and most preferably less than 20 nanometers. Within these nanograins, the solute atoms are homogeneously distributed in a body-centered cubic (or other appropriate crystal structure) lattice of the base metal, forming a metastable supersaturated solid solution.

This powder is then compacted into a green body using conventional uniaxial or cold isostatic pressing, and subsequently sintered in a protective or reducing atmosphere without the application of external pressure. During heating, the metastable solid solution undergoes spinodal decomposition or nucleation and growth-driven phase separation at a temperature well below the conventional sintering onset of the base metal. Critically, due to the nanoscale grain size and the associated high density of grain boundaries and free surfaces, the solute-rich phase preferentially precipitates not only within the particle interiors but also at the external surfaces of the powder particles and, most importantly, at the interparticle necks formed during initial compaction. This surface-localized solute-rich phase exhibits a significantly higher diffusivity for the base metal atoms than the base metal itself, thereby establishing a rapid transport pathway for mass flow during sintering.

The invention further encompasses the resulting sintered articles, which are characterized by near-theoretical density (greater than 98% of theoretical density), fine grain sizes in the ultrafine to nanocrystalline regime (typically less than 500 nanometers, and in optimized cases less than 100 nanometers), and a homogeneous microstructure free of macroscopic porosity or deleterious secondary phases. The method is particularly advantageous for refractory metal systems such as tungsten-based alloys, where conventional sintering requires extreme temperatures that induce grain coarsening, but is also applicable to other alloy systems meeting the criteria of supersaturation, nanocrystallinity, and favorable phase separation kinetics.

In one embodiment, the invention provides a tungsten-based alloy comprising 10 to 20 atomic percent chromium, wherein the alloy is sintered to full density at temperatures between 950 °C and 1,500 °C without applied pressure, yielding a microstructure with average grain size of approximately 13 to 100 nanometers. In another embodiment, the alloy further includes titanium in amounts of 20 to 40 atomic percent to enhance thermal stability of the nanocrystalline structure during sintering. In yet another embodiment, the invention is applied to chromium-based alloys containing 5 to 20 atomic percent nickel, enabling accelerated sintering of chromium at temperatures below 1,000 °C.

The key innovation of the present invention lies in the synergistic combination of nanocrystallinity and supersaturation, which together enable the in situ formation of a fast-diffusion layer at critical sintering interfaces during thermal processing. Unlike prior art methods that introduce second phases exogenously or rely on liquid formation, the present method generates the transport-enhancing phase endogenously through controlled decomposition of a metastable precursor, ensuring intimate contact and optimal distribution at particle junctions. This results in a self-accelerating sintering mechanism that is both energetically efficient and microstructurally refined, opening new avenues for the scalable production of advanced nanocrystalline materials.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method for the pressureless sintering of nanocrystalline and ultrafine-grained alloys, based on a newly discovered mechanism termed “nanophase separation sintering.” This mechanism exploits the unique microstructural and thermodynamic characteristics of mechanically alloyed, supersaturated solid solutions with nanoscale grain structure to achieve rapid densification at temperatures far below those conventionally required for the base metal. The method involves the preparation of a specific class of precursor powders, their compaction into green bodies, and their thermal processing under controlled atmospheres to induce phase separation and concurrent densification.

The precursor powders used in the present invention are produced by high-energy mechanical alloying, a process that subjects elemental or pre-alloyed powders to intense ball milling in a high-energy mill such as a SPEX 8000 shaker mill. The milling parameters—including ball-to-powder ratio, milling duration, use of process control agents, and milling atmosphere—are carefully selected to achieve complete dissolution of solute elements into the base metal matrix, resulting in a homogeneous supersaturated solid solution. For instance, in the case of tungsten-chromium alloys, elemental tungsten powder (average particle size 1–5 μm, purity ≥99.9%) and chromium powder (average particle size <10 μm, purity ≥99.2%) are mixed in stoichiometric proportions to achieve a target composition of 15 atomic percent chromium. The mixture is then milled in a tungsten carbide vial with tungsten carbide balls at a ball-to-powder ratio of 5:1, using 1 weight percent stearic acid as a process control agent to prevent excessive cold welding and agglomeration. Milling is carried out for 20 hours under an inert atmosphere to minimize oxidation and contamination.

The resulting powder consists of micron-sized particles, typically with diameters ranging from 0.5 to 5 micrometers, as confirmed by laser diffraction particle size analysis. Each particle is polycrystalline, with an internal grain structure composed of nanoscale crystallites. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) reveals an average grain size of approximately 13 nanometers for the W–15 at% Cr alloy, and X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirms the formation of a single-phase body-centered cubic (BCC) solid solution, with no detectable peaks corresponding to elemental chromium. The absence of chromium diffraction peaks after 20 hours of milling indicates complete dissolution of chromium into the tungsten lattice, despite the fact that chromium has negligible equilibrium solubility in tungsten at room temperature. This supersaturated state is metastable and is maintained at ambient conditions due to the kinetic barriers imposed by the nanoscale grain structure and the low diffusivity of solute atoms at low temperatures.

The powder is then compacted into a green body using conventional powder metallurgy techniques. Uniaxial pressing at a pressure of 360 MPa is sufficient to produce cylindrical compacts of 6 mm diameter and 3–4 mm height with a relative green density of approximately 62–67%. No binders or lubricants are required, as the fine particle size and irregular morphology promote adequate green strength. The compact is then placed in a thermomechanical analyzer (TMA) or a conventional high-temperature furnace equipped with a controlled atmosphere system. Sintering is conducted under a flowing gas mixture of argon containing 3% hydrogen, which serves both as an inert carrier and a reducing agent to remove any surface oxides that may have formed during handling.

During heating, the compact undergoes a series of microstructural transformations that drive rapid densification. When heated at a constant rate of 10 °C per minute, densification begins at approximately 950 °C, as detected by in situ length change measurements in the TMA. This onset temperature is remarkably lower than the typical sintering initiation temperature for pure tungsten (1,100–1,200 °C) or even for pure chromium of comparable particle size. By the time the temperature reaches 1,500 °C, the compact achieves a final density greater than 98% of theoretical density, despite the absence of any externally applied pressure.

This accelerated sintering behavior is attributed to the concurrent phase separation of the supersaturated solid solution and the nanocrystalline nature of the powder particles. As the temperature increases beyond 950 °C, the metastable W(Cr) solid solution becomes thermodynamically unstable and begins to decompose into chromium-rich and tungsten-rich phases. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and scanning transmission electron microscopy with energy dispersive spectroscopy (STEM-EDS) reveal that the chromium-rich phase precipitates not only within the particle interiors but also preferentially at the external surfaces of the powder particles and at the interparticle necks. This surface localization is facilitated by the high density of grain boundaries and free surfaces in the nanocrystalline powder, which provide short-circuit diffusion pathways for chromium atoms to migrate from the particle cores to the surfaces.

The chromium-rich phase that forms at the necks serves as a highly efficient diffusional transport medium. Although chromium has a high melting point (1,863 °C) and remains solid throughout the sintering cycle, it exhibits a significantly higher diffusivity for tungsten atoms than tungsten itself. Literature data indicate that the activation energy for tungsten diffusion in chromium is approximately 386 kJ/mol, compared to 550–670 kJ/mol for self-diffusion in tungsten. Kinetic analysis of the sintering process using the master sintering curve method yields an apparent activation energy of 373 kJ/mol for the W–15 at% Cr system, which closely matches the value for W diffusion in Cr and is distinct from other possible diffusion mechanisms. This confirms that the rate-limiting step in densification is the transport of tungsten atoms through the chromium-rich neck regions, where they can be redistributed to fill pores and shrink interparticle gaps.

The method is not limited to binary W–Cr alloys. In a preferred embodiment, ternary alloys such as W–35Ti–10Cr (atomic percent) are employed to further enhance microstructural stability. Titanium is added because it forms stable carbides and intermetallic compounds that pin grain boundaries and suppress coarsening during sintering. Using the same mechanical alloying and sintering protocol, this ternary alloy achieves nearly full density with an average grain size of 100 nanometers, demonstrating the tunability of the process for microstructure control. Moreover, the method has been successfully extended to other systems, such as chromium-nickel alloys. In the Cr–15 at% Ni system, nanophase separation sintering leads to the precipitation of nickel-rich phases at chromium particle necks, with a measured sintering activation energy of 258 kJ/mol—close to the value for Cr diffusion in Ni (272 kJ/mol)—thereby validating the generality of the mechanism.

Critical to the success of the invention is the requirement that the precursor powder possess both nanocrystallinity and supersaturation. A series of control experiments demonstrates that neither feature alone is sufficient to trigger accelerated sintering. Pure nanocrystalline tungsten, nanocrystalline tungsten physically mixed with chromium powder, supersaturated tungsten-chromium powder with coarse grains (produced by quenching from high temperature), and other combinations lacking either nanoscale grains or homogeneous supersaturation all fail to densify significantly below 1,100 °C. Only when both conditions are met—nanograins providing rapid diffusion paths and supersaturation enabling phase separation—does the self-generating fast-transport layer form at the necks, enabling pressureless full densification.

The resulting sintered articles are fully dense, free of macroscopic porosity, and retain fine grain sizes due to the relatively low sintering temperatures and the presence of second-phase particles that inhibit grain growth. The process is scalable, as demonstrated by the successful sintering of a 15 mm diameter, 11 mm height specimen in a conventional furnace, achieving full density under the same thermal profile. This scalability, combined with the elimination of external pressure or electric fields, makes the invention highly suitable for industrial adoption in the production of advanced structural and functional components from refractory and other high-performance alloys.

### Non-Limiting Working Examples

The following examples illustrate the practice of the present invention and are not intended to limit the scope of the claims. All percentages are atomic percent unless otherwise specified.

**Example 1: Preparation and Sintering of W–15 at% Cr Alloy**

Elemental tungsten powder (99.9% purity, average particle size 1–5 μm) and chromium powder (99.2% purity, average particle size <10 μm) were weighed in a molar ratio corresponding to W–15 at% Cr. The powders were loaded into a tungsten carbide vial along with tungsten carbide balls at a ball-to-powder ratio of 5:1 and 1 wt% stearic acid as a process control agent. The vial was sealed under argon and milled in a SPEX 8000 high-energy mill for 20 hours. The resulting powder was analyzed by XRD, which showed a single BCC phase with no detectable chromium peaks, confirming complete dissolution. TEM revealed an average grain size of 13 nm.

The powder was uniaxially pressed at 360 MPa into cylindrical compacts (6 mm diameter, 3.5 mm height) with a green density of 62%. The compacts were sintered in a Netzsch TMA under flowing Ar+3% H₂ at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Densification began at 950 °C, and the sample reached 98.5% theoretical density at 1,500 °C. SEM and STEM-EDS confirmed the presence of Cr-rich necks between W-rich particles. The sintering activation energy, determined by the master sintering curve method using heating rates of 5, 10, 15, and 20 °C/min, was 373 kJ/mol.

**Example 2: Control Experiment – Nanocrystalline W Without Cr**

Pure tungsten powder was milled under identical conditions as in Example 1 for 20 hours to produce nanocrystalline W with a grain size of 10 nm. Compacts were prepared and sintered as above. No significant densification occurred below 1,200 °C, and the final density at 1,500 °C was only 85%, demonstrating that nanocrystallinity alone is insufficient for accelerated sintering.

**Example 3: Control Experiment – Supersaturated W(Cr) with Coarse Grains**

W–15 at% Cr powder was milled for only 30 minutes to achieve partial alloying, then sealed in a quartz tube under argon and annealed at 1,400 °C for 20 hours followed by quenching. This produced a supersaturated solid solution with grain size >1 μm. Sintering under the same conditions as Example 1 resulted in minimal densification below 1,100 °C, confirming that supersaturation without nanocrystallinity does not enable the invention.

**Example 4: Ternary Alloy – W–35Ti–10Cr**

W, Ti (−150 mesh, 99.9% purity), and Cr powders were milled for 30 hours under the same conditions. The resulting powder had a grain size of 15 nm. Compacts were sintered at 1,350 °C for 1 hour under Ar+3% H₂ in a conventional furnace. The final product exhibited 99% density and an average grain size of 100 nm, as confirmed by SEM.

**Example 5: Cr–15 at% Ni System**

Chromium and nickel (99.9% purity, 2–3 μm) powders were milled for 15 hours to produce nanocrystalline Cr–15 at% Ni. Compacts were sintered at heating rates of 3–20 °C/min. Densification onset occurred at 850 °C, and full density was achieved at 1,100 °C. The sintering activation energy was 258 kJ/mol, consistent with Cr diffusion in Ni. EDS mapping confirmed Ni-rich necks.

These examples demonstrate the robustness and versatility of the nanophase separation sintering method across multiple alloy systems, enabling the production of fully dense, nanocrystalline bulk materials through a simple, pressureless, and scalable process.
