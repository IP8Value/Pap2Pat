# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of electrochemical energy conversion and storage devices, and more particularly to protonic ceramic electrochemical cells (PCECs) that operate reversibly in both electrolysis and fuel cell modes at intermediate temperatures. Specifically, the invention concerns a novel triple-conducting oxide (TCO) material based on a perovskite structure, which serves as a highly active and durable oxygen electrode for PCECs. The TCO material exhibits simultaneous conduction of protons (H⁺), oxygen ions (O²⁻), and electrons (e⁻), thereby enabling extended electrochemical reaction zones beyond conventional triple-phase boundaries. This innovation facilitates high-efficiency, low-temperature operation of PCECs for applications in renewable energy integration, grid-scale energy storage, distributed power generation, and self-sustaining hydrogen-based energy systems. The invention further encompasses methods of synthesizing the TCO material, fabricating PCEC devices incorporating the material, and operating such devices in reversible, self-sustainable cycles without external hydrogen supply.

## BACKGROUND

The global transition toward renewable energy sources such as solar and wind power has intensified the need for efficient, scalable, and cost-effective energy storage technologies. Unlike conventional fossil fuel-based power plants, renewable sources are inherently intermittent and variable, posing significant challenges to grid stability and reliability. Electrochemical energy storage systems, particularly those based on hydrogen, offer a promising solution by converting surplus electrical energy into chemical energy in the form of hydrogen via water electrolysis, which can later be reconverted into electricity through fuel cell operation. Among various electrolysis and fuel cell technologies, solid oxide electrochemical cells (SOECs/SOFCs) have attracted considerable attention due to their high theoretical efficiency, fuel flexibility, and potential for reversible operation. However, traditional oxygen-ion conducting SOECs typically require high operating temperatures (700–1000 °C), leading to material degradation, thermal stress, and high system costs.

To address these limitations, proton-conducting solid oxide cells—commonly referred to as protonic ceramic electrochemical cells (PCECs)—have emerged as a compelling alternative. PCECs utilize proton-conducting ceramic electrolytes, such as doped barium cerates or zirconates, which enable operation at reduced temperatures (400–600 °C). At these intermediate temperatures, material compatibility improves, thermal cycling becomes feasible, and system durability is enhanced. Moreover, because protons migrate through the electrolyte rather than oxygen ions, the oxygen evolution reaction occurs at the steam electrode (anode during electrolysis), eliminating the need for oxygen separation and simplifying system design. Despite these advantages, the widespread deployment of PCECs has been hindered by the lack of high-performance, stable oxygen electrodes that exhibit rapid kinetics for both the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) in fuel cell mode and the water oxidation reaction (WOR) in electrolysis mode.

In conventional mixed ionic-electronic conducting (MIEC) electrodes used in PCECs, electrochemical reactions are confined to the triple-phase boundaries (TPBs)—regions where the electrolyte (proton conductor), electrode (electronic conductor), and gas phase intersect. This spatial limitation restricts the active reaction zone to a narrow interface, resulting in high polarization resistance and poor performance, especially at lower temperatures where reaction kinetics slow significantly. To overcome this bottleneck, researchers have sought to develop triple-conducting oxides (TCOs) that simultaneously conduct protons, oxygen ions, and electrons. In such materials, the entire bulk of the electrode becomes electrochemically active, dramatically expanding the reaction zone and enhancing catalytic activity. However, the design of effective TCOs is scientifically challenging. Proton conduction in oxides requires the presence of oxygen vacancies that can be hydrated to form protonic defects (OH•), but many conventional MIEC materials either lack sufficient oxygen vacancies or exhibit poor hydration thermodynamics. Furthermore, introducing proton conductivity without compromising electronic or oxygen-ion conductivity—and while maintaining structural and chemical stability under high steam partial pressures and thermal cycling—has proven exceptionally difficult.

Early attempts to create TCOs involved doping proton-conducting perovskites (e.g., BaZrO₃ or BaCeO₃) with transition metals to induce electronic conductivity. Examples include BaCe₀.₅Bi₀.₅O₃₋δ, BaZr₀.₅Pr₀.₃Y₀.₂O₃₋δ, and BaZr₀.₆Co₀.₄O₃₋δ. However, these materials suffer from low total conductivity due to insufficient electronic pathways and limited proton mobility. More recently, layered perovskites such as PrBa₀.₅Sr₀.₅Co₂O₅₊δ and NdBa₀.₅Sr₀.₅Co₁.₅Fe₀.₅O₅₊δ have shown promise as TCO candidates, but experimental validation of their proton conductivity remains incomplete, and their long-term stability under operational conditions is uncertain. Additionally, many high-performance oxygen electrodes rely on alkaline earth metals like strontium or barium, which are prone to surface segregation under polarization, leading to performance degradation over time.

Another critical challenge lies in achieving self-sustainable reversible operation—where a single PCEC device alternates between electrolysis (storing electricity as hydrogen) and fuel cell (converting hydrogen back to electricity) modes without external hydrogen input. This functionality demands not only high catalytic activity but also exceptional interfacial stability, thermal resilience, and tolerance to dynamic changes in gas composition and temperature. Existing PCEC systems often fail under such rigorous cycling due to electrode delamination, chemical decomposition, or kinetic sluggishness at reduced temperatures. Consequently, there remains a pressing need for a new class of oxygen electrode materials that combine triple conduction, robust stability, and superior electrocatalytic activity across a wide temperature range (400–600 °C), enabling practical, reversible, and self-sustaining energy storage systems.

## BRIEF SUMMARY

The present invention provides a protonic ceramic electrochemical cell (PCEC) comprising a novel triple-conducting oxide (TCO) oxygen electrode material with the chemical formula PrNi₀.₅Co₀.₅O₃₋δ (hereinafter “PNC”), which enables high-performance, reversible, and self-sustainable operation at intermediate temperatures ranging from 400 °C to 600 °C. The PNC material is a perovskite-type oxide that simultaneously conducts protons (H⁺), oxygen ions (O²⁻), and electrons (e⁻), thereby extending the electrochemical reaction zone from the conventional triple-phase boundary into the entire bulk of the electrode. This triple conduction mechanism significantly enhances the kinetics of both the water oxidation reaction (WOR) during electrolysis and the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) during fuel cell operation, resulting in unprecedented current densities and power outputs at reduced temperatures.

The invention further includes a method for synthesizing the PNC material via a modified glycine-citrate combustion process, yielding a single-phase orthorhombic perovskite structure (space group Pbnm) with high crystallinity and homogeneous elemental distribution. Through strategic substitution of 50% of cobalt sites in PrCoO₃ with nickel, the invention achieves a dramatic reduction in oxygen vacancy formation energy—as confirmed by density functional theory (DFT) calculations—from approximately 3.4 eV in PrCoO₃ to as low as 1.6 eV in PNC. This facilitates abundant oxygen vacancy creation, which in turn promotes extensive hydration in humid atmospheres, leading to high proton concentration. DFT studies further reveal that nickel doping lowers the proton migration energy barrier along both inter-octahedral and intra-octahedral pathways, accelerating bulk proton transport. Experimental validation via thermogravimetric analysis, in-situ high-temperature X-ray diffraction, Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy, and hydrogen permeation measurements confirms the presence of significant proton conductivity, with hydrogen fluxes exceeding those of state-of-the-art proton-conducting ceramics and cermets.

In electrochemical testing, PCECs incorporating the PNC oxygen electrode and a BaCe₀.₄Zr₀.₄Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃ (BCZYYb4411) electrolyte demonstrate peak power densities of up to 528 mW cm⁻² at 600 °C in fuel cell mode and electrolysis current densities of 1.31 A cm⁻² at 1.4 V and 600 °C—among the highest reported for intermediate-temperature PCECs. Remarkably, the cell maintains substantial performance even at 400 °C, achieving 0.15 A cm⁻² under the same voltage, demonstrating viability for low-temperature operation. The PNC electrode exhibits exceptional stability under high steam concentrations (up to 30% H₂O), prolonged electrolysis (480 hours), and repeated thermal cycling between 400 °C and 600 °C without degradation. Faradaic efficiencies exceed 86% at 500 °C, indicating minimal electronic leakage and high hydrogen production fidelity.

The invention also encompasses a microstructurally optimized PNC electrode in the form of a self-architectured, mesh-like network of hollow nanofibers composed of 20–50 nm nanoparticles. This three-dimensional porous architecture enhances gas diffusion and exposes a high density of active sites, further boosting performance to 1.72 A cm⁻² at 1.4 V and 600 °C in electrolysis mode and 611 mW cm⁻² in fuel cell mode. Most significantly, the invention demonstrates, for the first time, self-sustainable reversible operation: the PCEC alternates between electrolysis and fuel cell modes using only internally generated hydrogen, without any external fuel supply. Over multiple cycles, the cell stably produces hydrogen and generates electricity, confirming its capability as an integrated energy storage and conversion device. Thus, the invention provides a complete technological solution for efficient, durable, and reversible hydrogen-based energy systems suitable for renewable energy integration and decentralized power applications.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a protonic ceramic electrochemical cell (PCEC) that incorporates a novel triple-conducting oxide (TCO) oxygen electrode material, PrNi₀.₅Co₀.₅O₃₋δ (PNC), which enables high-efficiency, reversible, and self-sustaining operation at intermediate temperatures (400–600 °C). The PNC material is engineered to overcome the fundamental limitations of conventional mixed ionic-electronic conducting (MIEC) electrodes by introducing proton conductivity into a perovskite lattice that already supports oxygen-ion and electronic conduction. This triple conduction mechanism transforms the entire electrode volume into an active reaction zone, drastically improving electrocatalytic activity for both water splitting and oxygen reduction.

The PNC material is synthesized through a wet-chemical route, specifically a modified glycine-citrate combustion method. Stoichiometric amounts of praseodymium nitrate hexahydrate (Pr(NO₃)₃·6H₂O), nickel nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O), and cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co(NO₃)₃·6H₂O) are dissolved in an aqueous solution containing citric acid and glycine as complexing agents. The mixture is heated under continuous stirring to form a viscous gel, which undergoes auto-ignition upon further heating, yielding a fine ash. This precursor is then calcined in air at 1000 °C for 5 hours to crystallize the perovskite phase. The resulting powder exhibits a single-phase orthorhombic perovskite structure with space group Pbnm, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Rietveld refinement, with lattice parameters a = 5.405 Å, b = 5.380 Å, and c = 7.617 Å. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) reveals a highly crystalline morphology with uniform distribution of Pr, Ni, and Co elements, indicating successful incorporation of nickel into the B-site of the perovskite lattice.

A key innovation of the PNC material lies in its defect chemistry, which is tailored to promote both oxygen vacancy formation and proton incorporation. Iodometric titration determines an oxygen non-stoichiometry (δ) of 0.085 at 600 °C, reflecting a significant concentration of oxygen vacancies. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations, performed using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with GGA+U corrections for Co and Ni 3d electrons, demonstrate that substituting 50% of Co³⁺ with Ni²⁺ reduces the oxygen vacancy formation energy at symmetrically distinct oxygen sites (O1 and O2) from approximately 3.4 eV in undoped PrCoO₃ to 1.6–1.7 eV in PNC. This reduction arises because Ni²⁺ introduces charge imbalance that is compensated by oxygen vacancy creation. The presence of these vacancies is essential for hydration: when exposed to humid atmospheres, water molecules dissociatively incorporate into the lattice, filling oxygen vacancies and forming protonic defects (OH•). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) quantifies this hydration, showing a weight gain of 0.055% ± 0.003% for PNC after 15 hours at 500 °C in wet air—significantly higher than benchmark materials such as PrBa₀.₅Sr₀.₅Co₁.₅Fe₀.₅O₅₊δ (0.035%), La₀.₆Sr₀.₄Co₀.₂Fe₀.₈O₃₋δ (0.031%), and PrCoO₃ (0.028%). In-situ high-temperature XRD further corroborates full hydration, revealing measurable chemical expansion of the PNC lattice upon exposure to steam at 60 t°C, consistent with proton defect formation.

Crucially, the PNC material does not merely host protons—it facilitates their rapid migration through the bulk. DFT-based climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) calculations map the minimum energy paths for proton hopping between adjacent oxygen ions. Two representative pathways are analyzed: inter-octahedral hopping (between oxygen ions in neighboring Co/NiO₆ octahedra) and intra-octahedral hopping (within the same octahedron). In both cases, the proton migration energy barrier in PNC is substantially lower than in conventional materials, attributed to the electronic and structural modifications induced by Ni doping. For intra-octahedral hopping, the pathway involves a three-stage process: (i) proton transfer from O2 to O3, (ii) rotation of the O–H bond at the O3 site due to hydrogen bonding interactions, and (iii) transfer from O3 back to O2. The lowered barriers enable efficient proton conduction even at 500 °C. This is experimentally verified by hydrogen permeation tests on dense PNC membranes. At 500 °C, the hydrogen flux reaches 1.5 × 10⁻⁷ mol cm⁻² s⁻¹ in wet 3% H₂—surpassing the performance of Ni-BaCe₀.₇Zr₀.₁Y₀.₁Yb₀.₁O₃ cermet at 700 °C by 25% and SrCe₀.₉₅Yb₀.₀₅O₃₋δ ceramic at 900 °C by a factor of 17. Such high flux confirms the coexistence of proton and electronic conductivity, fulfilling the criteria for a true triple conductor.

When integrated into a PCEC with a BCZYYb4411 electrolyte and a Ni-BCZYYb4411 hydrogen electrode, the PNC oxygen electrode delivers exceptional electrochemical performance. In fuel cell mode with pure H₂ fuel, the cell achieves open-circuit voltages (OCVs) of 1.06 V at 600 °C—close to the theoretical Nernst potential—indicating a gas-tight electrolyte and robust sealing. Peak power densities reach 528 mW cm⁻² at 600 °C, 354 mW cm⁻² at 550 °C, and 230 mW cm⁻² at 500 °C, outperforming prior art by factors of two to three. In electrolysis mode with 10% H₂/90% Ar on the hydrogen side and ~10% H₂O in air on the oxygen side, current densities of 1.31 A cm⁻² (600 °C), 0.82 A cm⁻² (550 °C), and 0.62 A cm⁻² (500 °C) are achieved at 1.4 V. Even at 400 °C, a current density of 0.15 A cm⁻² is sustained, demonstrating unprecedented low-temperature activity. Impedance spectroscopy reveals that interfacial polarization resistance—the dominant loss at lower temperatures—is remarkably low: 0.025 Ω cm² at 600 °C and 0.6 Ω cm² at 400 °C. Stability tests confirm robustness: under constant voltage (1.4 V or 1.6 V) at 500 °C for 120 hours, current density increases by 8–10%, likely due to interface activation. Performance remains stable in 20–30% steam, and thermal cycling between 400 °C and 600 °C over five cycles induces no degradation. Faradaic efficiencies of 79–86% confirm high hydrogen production fidelity.

Performance is further enhanced by engineering the PNC electrode microstructure into a self-architectured, mesh-like network of hollow nanofibers. Fabricated via a templated synthesis method followed by calcination at 800 °C, this 3D electrode features bundles of fibers (~3 µm diameter) with through-holes, each fiber composed of 20–50 nm nanoparticles. This architecture maximizes porosity (>60%) and active surface area, facilitating rapid gas diffusion to reaction sites throughout the electrode bulk. When incorporated into a PCEC, this nanostructured electrode boosts electrolysis current density to 1.72 A cm⁻² at 1.4 V and 600 °C—a 31% improvement over the standard PNC electrode—and increases peak power density to 611 mW cm⁻² in fuel cell mode. Electrode polarization resistance drops to 0.055 Ω cm² at 500 °C, reflecting improved reaction kinetics and mass transport. Long-term testing shows no degradation over 220 hours at 500 °C and 1.4 V, though interfacial adhesion remains an area for optimization.

Most significantly, the invention demonstrates self-sustainable reversible operation. In a closed-loop test, the PCEC first operates in electrolysis mode at 0.6 A cm⁻² for three minutes to produce hydrogen, which is immediately consumed in fuel cell mode at 0.2 A cm⁻² for two minutes—without any external hydrogen supply. Voltage profiles remain stable over ten cycles, confirming the feasibility of transient hydrogen storage and utilization within a single device. At 500 °C, the system yields an average hydrogen production rate of 44.7 mL cm⁻² h⁻¹ and electricity generation of 70 mWh cm⁻² h⁻¹; at 550 °C, these increase to 89.3 mL cm⁻² h⁻¹ and 200 mWh cm⁻² h⁻¹. Daily projections suggest 643–1069 mL cm⁻² of hydrogen and 671–2400 mWh cm⁻² of electricity, establishing the PCEC as a viable prototype for integrated renewable energy storage. Post-test characterization shows intact electrode/electrolyte interfaces, underscoring the material’s durability under dynamic cycling.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Synthesis and Characterization of PNC Material**

PrNi₀.₅Co₀.₅O₃₋δ (PNC) powder was synthesized via a modified glycine-citrate combustion method. Pr(NO₃)₃·6H₂O (99.9%, Alfa Aesar), Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O (99+%, Alfa Aesar), and Co(NO₃)₃·6H₂O (99%, STREM Chemicals) were dissolved in deionized water at a molar ratio of 1:0.5:0.5. Citric acid and glycine were added as complexing agents at a total metal-to-complexant ratio of 1:1.5. The solution was heated at 80 °C under stirring until a viscous gel formed, which was then transferred to a preheated muffle furnace at 300 °C for auto-ignition. The resulting ash was ground and calcined at 1000 °C in air for 5 hours. XRD analysis (Rigaku SmartLab, Cu Kα radiation) confirmed a single-phase orthorhombic perovskite structure (space group Pbnm), with no secondary phases detected. Rietveld refinement yielded lattice parameters a = 5.405 Å, b = 5.380 Å, c = 7.617 Å. TEM (JEOL 4000 EX) images showed particles of 100–200 nm with uniform elemental distribution by EDX mapping. Oxygen non-stoichiometry was determined by iodometric titration to be δ = 0.085 at 600 °C. XPS analysis indicated Ni predominantly in the +2 oxidation state, consistent with charge compensation via oxygen vacancy formation.

**Example 2: Hydration and Proton Conductivity Assessment**

Hydration behavior was evaluated by TGA (TA Instruments Q500). Approximately 50 mg of PNC powder was heated to 950 °C in dry air (1 °C min⁻¹), held for 1 hour, cooled to 500 °C, and exposed to wet air (~3% H₂O) for 15 hours. A weight gain of 0.055% ± 0.003% was recorded, compared to 0.035% for PBSCF, 0.031% for LSCF, and 0.028% for PCO under identical conditions. In-situ high-temperature XRD (600 °C) showed a 0.3% expansion in the c-axis upon switching from dry to wet air, confirming lattice hydration. FTIR (Nicolet iS50) of hydrated PNC revealed a broad OH stretching band (3000–3500 cm⁻¹) that intensified with heating to 600 °C, indicating bulk proton defects. TPD-mass spectrometry detected H₂O desorption peaks between 400–600 °C, corresponding to dehydroxylation of lattice protons. Hydrogen permeation was measured on a dense PNC membrane (12 mm diameter, 0.3 mm thick, sintered at 1250 °C). With wet 3% H₂ on the feed side and Ar sweep gas, a flux of 1.5 × 10⁻⁷ mol cm⁻² s⁻¹ was achieved at 500 °C—exceeding literature values for SrCe₀.₉₅Yb₀.₀₅O₃₋δ and Ni-BCZY.

**Example 3: PCEC Fabrication and Electrochemical Testing**

NiO-BCZYYb4411/BCZYYb4411 half-cells (~1 inch diameter) were fabricated by tape-casting and co-sintering at 1450 °C. PNC ink (50 wt% PNC powder, 45 wt% terpineol, 5 wt% ethyl cellulose) was screen-printed onto the electrolyte and fired at 950 °C for 5 hours, yielding an active area of 3.1 cm². Electrochemical tests were conducted in a custom reactor with humidified air (80 mL min⁻¹, ~10% H₂O) on the oxygen side and 10% H₂/90% Ar (30 mL min⁻¹) on the hydrogen side. In fuel cell mode, peak power densities of 528 mW cm⁻² (600 °C), 354 mW cm⁻² (550 °C), and 230 mW cm⁻² (500 °C) were achieved. In electrolysis mode at 1.4 V, current densities reached 1.31 A cm⁻² (600 °C), 0.82 A cm⁻² (550 °C), 0.62 A cm⁻² (500 °C), 0.27 A cm⁻² (450 °C), and 0.15 A cm⁻² (400 °C). Stability tests at 500 °C showed <2% variation in current density over 480 hours in 20% H₂O. Thermal cycling between 400 °C and 600 °C (5 cycles) induced no performance loss. Faradaic efficiency, measured by gas chromatography, was 86.2% at 500 °C and 1.3 V.

**Example 4: Nanostructured PNC Electrode and Enhanced Performance**

A mesh-like PNC electrode was synthesized by electrospinning a PNC-polymer precursor, followed by calcination at 800 °C. SEM revealed a 3D network of hollow fibers (~3 µm diameter) with nanoparticle walls (20–50 nm). This electrode was adhered to the BCZYYb4411 electrolyte using minimal PNC slurry. The resulting PCEC achieved 1.72 A cm⁻² at 1.4 V and 600 °C in electrolysis mode—31% higher than the standard electrode—and 611 mW cm⁻² in fuel cell mode. EIS at 500 °C showed polarization resistance of 0.055 Ω cm², down from 0.13 Ω cm². Durability testing at 1.4 V and 500 °C for 220 hours showed no degradation.

**Example 5: Self-Sustainable Reversible Operation**

A PCEC with standard PNC electrode was operated in a closed hydrogen loop. Electrolysis at 0.6 A cm⁻² for 3 minutes produced hydrogen, which was immediately consumed in fuel cell mode at 0.2 A cm⁻² for 2 minutes. Ten consecutive cycles showed stable voltage profiles (1.35 ± 0.02 V in electrolysis, 0.75 ± 0.03 V in fuel cell). At 500 °C, average hydrogen production was 44.7 mL cm⁻² h⁻¹; electricity generation was 70 mWh cm⁻² h⁻¹. Daily yields projected to 643 mL cm⁻² H₂ and 671 mWh cm⁻² electricity. Post-test SEM confirmed intact interfaces, validating self-sustainability.
