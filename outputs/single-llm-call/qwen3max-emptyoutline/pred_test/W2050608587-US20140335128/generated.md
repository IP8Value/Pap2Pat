# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Malaria remains one of the most devastating infectious diseases affecting human populations worldwide, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. According to global health estimates, the disease caused approximately 216 million clinical cases and over 650,000 deaths in a single recent year, with the majority of fatalities occurring among children under five years of age in sub-Saharan Africa. The causative agent, *Plasmodium falciparum*, is transmitted through the bite of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes and initiates a complex life cycle involving both hepatic and erythrocytic stages within the human host. Despite decades of research and public health interventions, including vector control and antimalarial drug therapies, the development of a highly effective vaccine has remained elusive. This persistent gap in preventive medicine underscores the urgent need for novel immunological strategies capable of inducing robust and durable protection against malaria infection.

Historically, evidence from experimental human challenge studies has demonstrated that sterile immunity can be achieved through exposure to radiation-attenuated sporozoites or live sporozoites administered in conjunction with chemoprophylactic agents such as chloroquine. In these paradigms, the parasite is allowed to invade hepatocytes but is prevented from progressing to the pathogenic blood stage, thereby eliciting a potent immune response without causing clinical disease. Animal models and human trials alike have shown that this form of whole-parasite vaccination confers high-level protection, primarily mediated by cell-mediated immunity (CMI), particularly cytotoxic CD8+ T lymphocytes. These effector cells recognize parasite-derived peptides presented on the surface of infected hepatocytes via major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules and eliminate the intracellular reservoir through mechanisms such as perforin/granzyme-mediated lysis or interferon-gamma (IFN-γ)-induced nitric oxide production. While conceptually compelling, the logistical and regulatory challenges associated with manufacturing, storing, and delivering live or attenuated whole parasites have hindered the widespread deployment of such approaches.

In parallel, subunit vaccine strategies have been pursued to recapitulate the protective efficacy of whole-parasite immunization using defined molecular components. The most advanced candidate to date, RTS,S/AS01, is a recombinant protein vaccine based on the circumsporozoite protein (CSP)—the dominant surface antigen expressed by *P. falciparum* sporozoites. RTS,S fuses a portion of CSP to the hepatitis B surface antigen to form virus-like particles, which are formulated with an adjuvant system to enhance immunogenicity. Clinical trials have shown that RTS,S provides partial protection (~50%) against controlled human malaria infection, primarily through the induction of high-titer anti-CSP antibodies that neutralize sporozoites before they reach the liver. However, this vaccine fails to elicit significant CD8+ T cell responses due to its reliance on exogenous antigen delivery, which predominantly activates the MHC class II pathway and humoral immunity. Consequently, RTS,S offers limited efficacy against established hepatic infections and wanes rapidly over time, highlighting the need for alternative platforms capable of engaging cellular immune mechanisms.

Gene-based vaccines represent a promising avenue for overcoming these limitations. By delivering genetic material encoding malaria antigens directly into host cells, these platforms enable endogenous expression of the target proteins, facilitating their processing and presentation via the MHC class I pathway—a prerequisite for activating CD8+ T cells. Two primary modalities have emerged: plasmid DNA vaccines and viral vector-based vaccines. DNA vaccines consist of bacterial plasmids engineered to carry eukaryotic expression cassettes, typically driven by strong promoters such as the human cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate-early enhancer/promoter. Upon intramuscular injection, the plasmid enters host cells, where the encoded antigen is transcribed and translated in the cytoplasm, leading to MHC-I presentation and T cell priming. Although safe and stable, early-generation DNA vaccines often exhibited suboptimal immunogenicity in humans, prompting the development of heterologous prime-boost regimens that combine DNA priming with viral vector boosting to amplify cellular responses.

Viral vectors, particularly replication-deficient adenoviruses, have proven highly effective at transducing antigen-presenting cells and driving robust transgene expression. Human adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5) has been widely used due to its high transduction efficiency, ease of production, and capacity to accommodate large transgenes. When used as a boost following DNA priming, Ad5 vectors can dramatically enhance the magnitude and quality of T cell responses, including polyfunctional CD8+ T cells capable of producing multiple cytokines such as IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and interleukin-2 (IL-2). This heterologous DNA/Ad5 prime-boost strategy has demonstrated superior efficacy over homologous regimens in preclinical models of malaria, HIV, and tuberculosis, leading to its evaluation in human clinical trials.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain. Pre-existing immunity to common adenovirus serotypes, particularly Ad5, is prevalent in many populations due to natural infections. Neutralizing antibodies (NAb) against the viral capsid can significantly impair vector uptake and transgene expression, thereby reducing vaccine immunogenicity and efficacy. Additionally, the selection of appropriate target antigens is critical; while CSP is a logical choice due to its surface exposure on sporozoites and role in hepatocyte invasion, it is not expressed during the blood stage, limiting its utility as a multi-stage vaccine component. Apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1), another key sporozoite and liver-stage antigen involved in host cell invasion, is also expressed during the blood stage and has been associated with naturally acquired immunity in endemic areas. Combining CSP and AMA1 in a single vaccine regimen thus offers the potential to target multiple stages of the parasite’s life cycle and engage complementary immune mechanisms.

Prior to the present invention, no gene-based malaria vaccine had demonstrated sterile protection in humans linked specifically to CD8+ T cell responses against defined antigens. While earlier trials using DNA or viral vectors alone showed modest immunogenicity, they failed to achieve consistent or high-level protection. Moreover, the contribution of individual antigens within multi-antigen constructs had not been clearly delineated, nor had the impact of pre-existing adenovirus immunity on protective outcomes been fully characterized in the context of a prime-boost regimen. The absence of a licensed human vaccine relying solely on CMI further underscores the novelty and significance of demonstrating such a mechanism in the context of malaria prevention.

Thus, there exists a critical unmet need for a safe, well-tolerated, and efficacious malaria vaccine that harnesses the power of cell-mediated immunity—particularly CD8+ T cells—to eliminate infected hepatocytes and prevent progression to blood-stage infection. The present invention addresses this need by providing a heterologous prime-boost vaccine platform comprising codon-optimized DNA plasmids and non-replicating adenovirus vectors, both encoding *P. falciparum* CSP and AMA1 antigens derived from the 3D7 strain. This regimen is designed to induce high-magnitude, antigen-specific T cell responses capable of mediating sterile protection, as demonstrated in a controlled human malaria infection trial. The invention further encompasses methods of administration, formulations, and dosing schedules optimized to maximize immunogenicity while minimizing reactogenicity, as well as strategies to mitigate the impact of pre-existing adenovirus immunity through antigen selection, vector engineering, or alternative delivery technologies.

## BRIEF SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a heterologous prime-boost vaccine regimen for the prevention of *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria, comprising a priming component consisting of a mixture of two codon-optimized DNA plasmids encoding the circumsporozoite protein (CSP) and apical membrane antigen-1 (AMA1) from the *P. falciparum* 3D7 strain, and a boosting component consisting of a mixture of two non-replicating recombinant human adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5) vectors encoding the same CSP and AMA1 antigens. The DNA plasmids are constructed within the VR1020 backbone and include modifications to enhance mammalian expression, including a human tissue plasminogen activator (TPA) signal sequence fused to the N-terminus of each antigen and a bovine growth hormone transcriptional terminator segment at the C-terminus of CSP. The adenovirus vectors are derived from the GV11D strain, are deleted in the E1, E4, and part of the E3 regions to render them replication-deficient, and utilize a modified human cytomegalovirus immediate-early (CMV-IE) promoter to drive transgene expression. The CSP construct in the adenovirus lacks the TPA signal sequence but is otherwise identical to the DNA-encoded version, while the AMA1 construct includes the full-length protein with its native signal sequence and transmembrane/cytoplasmic domains.

The vaccine regimen is administered to a human subject in a specific sequence: three intramuscular priming doses of the DNA plasmid mixture are given at weeks 0, 4, and 8, each dose containing 1 mg of each plasmid (total 2 mg) delivered via needle-free jet injection into the deltoid muscles; this is followed by a single intramuscular boosting dose of the adenovirus vector mixture at week 24, containing 1×10¹⁰ particle units (pu) of each vector (total 2×10¹⁰ pu) administered by conventional needle injection into the deltoid muscle. This regimen has been shown to induce sterile protection against controlled human malaria infection in 27% of vaccinated volunteers, with protection significantly associated with antigen-specific interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) responses measured by enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISpot) assay and flow cytometry with intracellular cytokine staining (ICS). Specifically, protection correlates strongly with CD8+ T cell responses directed against AMA1, and to a lesser extent CSP, while antibody responses show no statistically significant association with protection. Depletion studies confirm that both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell subsets contribute to the observed IFN-γ production, with protected individuals exhibiting either CD8+ T cell-dominant or CD4+ T cell-dependent responses depending on the antigen and individual.

The invention further provides that the vaccine is safe and well-tolerated, with the majority of adverse events being mild and transient, including local reactions such as erythema, induration, and pain at the injection site, as well as systemic symptoms like fatigue and headache. No serious adverse events were deemed related to the vaccine. The regimen is particularly effective in individuals with low or absent pre-existing neutralizing antibodies to adenovirus serotype 5 (NAb titer <500), as high pre-existing NAb titers are associated with reduced immunogenicity, particularly for antibody responses to AMA1 and ELISpot responses to both antigens. The invention thus enables the induction of protective cell-mediated immunity against malaria through a subunit vaccine platform, representing a significant advancement over prior art vaccines that rely primarily on humoral immunity. Additionally, the invention contemplates future improvements, including the incorporation of additional malaria antigens to broaden HLA coverage, the use of rare-serotype adenovirus vectors to circumvent pre-existing immunity, electroporation-enhanced DNA delivery, and combination with protein-based boosts to elicit both cellular and humoral responses.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

### Example 1

The present invention is exemplified by a clinical trial conducted under Investigational New Drug (IND) application BB-IND 13977, which evaluated the safety, immunogenicity, and efficacy of the NMRC-M3V-D/Ad-PfCA vaccine regimen in healthy, malaria-naïve adult volunteers. The priming component, designated NMRC-M3V-D-PfCA, consists of two separate DNA plasmids—pDNA-CSP and pDNA-AMA1—each constructed in the VR1020 plasmid backbone (Vical, Inc., San Diego, CA). The CSP gene corresponds to the 3D7 strain sequence (GenBank no. X15363) and was modified by deleting 16 of the central NANP repeat units (64 amino acids) to reduce potential immunodominance of repetitive epitopes, while retaining the N-terminal and C-terminal regions critical for T cell recognition. To enhance secretion and expression in mammalian cells, the native CSP signal sequence was retained and supplemented with an additional human tissue plasminogen activator (TPA) leader sequence at the N-terminus. Furthermore, a 23-amino acid segment derived from the bovine growth hormone (BGH) transcriptional terminator was appended to the C-terminus to stabilize mRNA and increase protein yield. The AMA1 gene encodes the ectodomain of the 3D7 strain AMA1 (GenBank no. XM1347979) and was modified by replacing its native signal sequence with the TPA leader sequence to improve translocation into the endoplasmic reticulum and subsequent processing. Both genes were codon-optimized for human expression to maximize translational efficiency. Each plasmid contains a kanamycin resistance gene for bacterial selection and is produced under current Good Manufacturing Practice (cGMP) conditions in kanamycin-containing media. For administration, equal amounts of pDNA-CSP and pDNA-AMA1 are mixed to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL of each plasmid, and 1 mL of the mixture is injected into each deltoid muscle using a Biojector 2000® needle-free jet injector, resulting in a total DNA dose of 2 mg per immunization.

The boosting component, NMRC-M3V-Ad-PfCA, comprises two replication-deficient adenovirus serotype 5 (Ad5) vectors—Ad5-CSP and Ad5-AMA1—each carrying the corresponding codon-optimized 3D7 antigen gene inserted into the E1-deleted region of the adenoviral genome. The vectors are derived from the GV11D parental strain (GenVec, Inc., Gaithersburg, MD) and are additionally deleted in the E4 region and part of the E3 region to enhance safety and reduce immunogenicity of viral proteins. Transgene expression is controlled by a modified human cytomegalovirus immediate-early (CMV-IE) promoter known to drive high-level expression in a broad range of cell types. The CSP insert in the adenovirus vector is identical to that in the DNA plasmid except that it lacks the TPA signal sequence, relying instead on the native CSP signal peptide for proper localization. The AMA1 insert in the adenovirus vector encodes the full-length protein, including the ectodomain, transmembrane domain, and cytoplasmic tail, and retains its native signal sequence rather than the TPA leader. This design allows for more authentic post-translational processing and potential presentation of conformational epitopes relevant to natural infection. The two adenovirus vectors are manufactured separately under cGMP, stored as lyophilized powders, reconstituted immediately prior to use, and mixed in equal volumes to achieve a final dose of 1×10¹⁰ particle units (pu) of each vector in a total volume of 1 mL, which is administered intramuscularly into the deltoid muscle using a standard hypodermic needle.

In the clinical trial, twenty healthy adult volunteers aged 18–50 years, who were malaria-naïve and met all eligibility criteria including cardiac risk screening and absence of contraindications, received the first DNA immunization. One volunteer was excluded after the first dose due to benign ethnic leukopenia, and three others were withdrawn after the third DNA dose for non-vaccine-related reasons (relocation and deep venous thrombosis unrelated to immunization). One additional volunteer was excluded after the Ad5 boost due to a migraine episode consistent with pre-existing history. Thus, fifteen fully immunized volunteers completed the regimen and underwent controlled human malaria infection (CHMI) by exposure to five bites from *P. falciparum* 3D7-infected mosquitoes at week 28. Six unimmunized infectivity controls were included to confirm mosquito infectivity. All controls developed patent parasitemia by quantitative PCR (qPCR) between days 7 and 8 post-challenge, validating the challenge model. Remarkably, four of the fifteen immunized volunteers (27%) remained completely free of detectable parasitemia by both qPCR and microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained blood smears through day 28, demonstrating sterile protection. The remaining eleven immunized volunteers became parasitemic at kinetics similar to controls, with no evidence of delayed patency, suggesting an all-or-nothing protective effect rather than partial suppression.

Immunological analyses revealed that protection was strongly correlated with cell-mediated immunity rather than humoral responses. Antibody titers against CSP and AMA1, measured by ELISA and immunofluorescence assay (IFA), were low to moderate and showed no statistically significant association with protection after Bonferroni correction. In contrast, IFN-γ ELISpot responses to AMA1 peptide pools at the time of challenge (post-Ad) were significantly higher in protected volunteers (p = 0.019, Bonferroni corrected), with the three highest responders (v10, v11, v18) all being protected. CSP-specific ELISpot responses showed a trend but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.23). Flow cytometry with intracellular cytokine staining confirmed that CD8+ T cells were the primary source of IFN-γ in protected individuals, with frequencies of AMA1-specific CD8+ T cells reaching up to 0.38% of total CD8+ T cells in one protected volunteer. Depletion studies using anti-CD4+ and anti-CD8+ magnetic beads demonstrated that both T cell subsets contributed to the IFN-γ response, with some protected volunteers showing CD8+ dependence (v18), others CD4+ dependence (v6), and others dual dependence (v10, v11). These findings establish that the DNA/Ad5 prime-boost regimen induces functional, antigen-specific T cells capable of mediating sterile protection against malaria, fulfilling a long-standing goal in vaccinology.

### Example 2

Further characterization of the immune responses induced by the NMRC-M3V-D/Ad-PfCA vaccine regimen reveals important insights into the quality and functionality of the elicited T cells. Using multiparametric flow cytometry with intracellular cytokine staining (ICS), the cytokine profiles of CSP- and AMA1-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were analyzed at multiple time points: pre-immunization, post-third DNA, pre-Ad5 boost, post-Ad5 boost (pre-challenge), and post-challenge. The analysis focused on the production of IFN-γ, TNF, and IL-2, allowing classification of T cells into polyfunctional subsets (e.g., IFN-γ⁺TNF⁺IL-2⁺) or single-cytokine producers. In protected volunteers, the dominant CD8+ T cell response to both CSP and AMA1 consisted of single IFN-γ-secreting cells (IFN-γ⁺IL-2⁻TNF⁻), which accounted for 96–100% of the total IFN-γ response in the case of CSP and 66–95% for AMA1. This phenotype is consistent with effector or effector-memory T cells capable of rapid cytotoxic activity upon re-encounter with antigen. Notably, protected volunteer v11 exhibited the highest frequencies of both CSP- and AMA1-specific CD8+ T cells (0.10% and 0.32%, respectively), while v6—the only protected volunteer without elevated CD8+ T cell responses at post-Ad—showed the highest CD4+ T cell response to AMA1 at the pre-Ad time point (0.14% IFN-γ⁺, 0.23% IL-2⁺), suggesting that CD4+ T cells may also play a protective role, possibly through helper functions or direct cytotoxicity.

The impact of pre-existing immunity to adenovirus serotype 5 was rigorously evaluated by measuring neutralizing antibody (NAb) titers at screening and prior to Ad5 boost. Volunteers were stratified into three groups: NAb-negative (<1:12), low-titer (1:12–1:500), and high-titer (>1:500). All four protected volunteers had NAb titers ≤1:169, and none of the five volunteers with high pre-existing NAb titers were protected. Statistical analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between pre-existing NAb titers and AMA1-specific ELISA responses (p = 0.038), with trends toward reduced ELISpot and IFA responses as well. However, CD8+ T cell responses measured by flow cytometry were not significantly impaired by pre-existing NAb, suggesting that the threshold for T cell priming may be lower than that for antibody induction. This finding has important implications for vaccine deployment in regions with high Ad5 seroprevalence, indicating that while humoral responses may be blunted, cellular immunity—and thus protection—can still be achieved in individuals with low-to-moderate pre-existing immunity. For populations with high Ad5 seroprevalence, the invention contemplates the use of alternative adenovirus vectors derived from rare human serotypes (e.g., Ad26, Ad35) or non-human adenoviruses (e.g., chimpanzee-derived) to circumvent neutralizing antibody interference.

Safety monitoring throughout the trial confirmed the excellent tolerability profile of the regimen. A total of 58 DNA immunizations and 16 Ad5 immunizations were administered across 20 volunteers. Solicited adverse events within seven days of immunization were predominantly mild (Grade 1), with local reactions (erythema, induration, pain) accounting for 85% of all events. Only one severe (Grade 3) event was reported—a transient episode of diarrhea following the first DNA dose—which resolved within 48 hours and was deemed possibly related to immunization. Laboratory abnormalities were infrequent and asymptomatic, resolving without intervention. The only serious adverse event was a saphenous vein thrombosis in one volunteer six days after the third DNA dose, which occurred in the context of prolonged travel and was judged unlikely to be vaccine-related. These data support the conclusion that the DNA/Ad5 prime-boost regimen is safe for use in healthy adults and suitable for further clinical development.

### Example 3

Building upon the foundational results of the initial clinical trial, the present invention envisions several strategic enhancements to improve the breadth, potency, and applicability of the malaria vaccine platform. First, the inclusion of additional *P. falciparum* antigens is proposed to broaden HLA coverage and target multiple stages of the parasite life cycle. Candidate antigens include liver-stage antigen-1 (LSA-1), exported protein-1 (EXP-1), and merozoite surface protein-1 (MSP-1), each of which has been implicated in naturally acquired immunity or shown to confer protection in animal models. By incorporating three or more antigens into the DNA and adenovirus constructs, the likelihood of generating T cell responses restricted by diverse HLA alleles is increased, thereby enhancing population-level efficacy. Second, to overcome the limitations imposed by pre-existing Ad5 immunity, the invention proposes the development of next-generation adenovirus vectors based on rare human serotypes (e.g., Ad28, Ad48) or simian adenoviruses (e.g., ChAd3, ChAd63), which have demonstrated low seroprevalence in human populations and high immunogenicity in clinical trials for other diseases. These vectors can be substituted for Ad5 in the boosting component without altering the DNA priming strategy, preserving the benefits of heterologous prime-boost while evading neutralizing antibodies.

Third, the physical delivery of the DNA component can be enhanced through the use of in vivo electroporation, a technique that applies brief electrical pulses to the injection site to transiently permeabilize cell membranes and increase plasmid uptake. Preclinical studies have shown that electroporation can boost DNA vaccine immunogenicity by 10- to 100-fold, potentially reducing the number of priming doses required or enabling lower DNA doses to achieve equivalent responses. Fourth, the formulation of the DNA and/or adenovirus components with molecular adjuvants is contemplated to further shape the immune response. For example, co-delivery of plasmids encoding immunomodulatory cytokines such as IL-12, GM-CSF, or type I interferons can promote Th1 polarization and enhance CD8+ T cell expansion and memory formation. Similarly, adenovirus vectors can be engineered to express these cytokines in trans, creating a self-adjuvanting vaccine platform.

Finally, the invention considers hybrid prime-boost regimens that combine the DNA/Ad5 platform with protein-based vaccines to elicit both cellular and humoral immunity. For instance, following the DNA/Ad5 prime-boost series, a final boost with recombinant CSP and AMA1 proteins formulated in a potent adjuvant (e.g., AS01, CpG/alum) could amplify antibody titers and induce long-lived plasma cells, while preserving the established T cell memory. This approach would aim to replicate the dual mechanisms of protection seen in whole-sporozoite vaccines—antibodies to block sporozoite invasion and T cells to eliminate infected hepatocytes—within a scalable, subunit-based framework. Collectively, these innovations represent a comprehensive strategy to advance the NMRC-M3V-D/Ad-PfCA platform toward a highly effective, deployable malaria vaccine capable of significantly reducing the global burden of this deadly disease.
