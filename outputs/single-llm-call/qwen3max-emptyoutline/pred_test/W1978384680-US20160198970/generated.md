# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD

The present invention relates generally to the field of medical imaging, and more particularly to methods and systems for determining optimal cardiac gating windows in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) procedures. Specifically, the invention provides a novel magnetic resonance (MR)-based motion monitoring technique—referred to herein as the “Septal Scout”—that enables high-temporal-resolution assessment of ventricular septal motion to accurately identify the diastasis period of the cardiac cycle. This technique is especially advantageous in applications requiring precise synchronization of image data acquisition with periods of minimal cardiac motion, such as in magnetic resonance coronary angiography (MRCA). The invention further encompasses integrated imaging protocols, signal processing methodologies, and system architectures that leverage this Septal Scout technique to improve the spatial fidelity, diagnostic clarity, and clinical utility of cardiac MR images, particularly those of the coronary arteries.

## BACKGROUND

Magnetic resonance coronary angiography (MRCA) has long held promise as a non-invasive alternative to x-ray coronary angiography—the current clinical gold standard—for diagnosing coronary artery disease (CAD). Unlike x-ray angiography, MRCA does not expose patients to ionizing radiation or require invasive catheterization, thereby significantly reducing procedural risks and enabling safer longitudinal monitoring of coronary health. Moreover, MRCA offers the unique capability of generating three-dimensional volumetric reconstructions of the coronary vessel lumens, which can provide comprehensive anatomical context beyond the planar projections typical of conventional angiography. Despite these compelling advantages, the widespread clinical adoption of MRCA has been hindered by persistent challenges related to motion artifacts arising from the complex and rapid dynamics of the beating heart.

The coronary arteries, particularly the right coronary artery (RCA) and its branches, exhibit substantial motion throughout the cardiac cycle due to myocardial contraction, torsion, and translation. During systole, velocities can exceed 100 mm/s, while even during diastole—traditionally considered a quiescent phase—residual motion persists at levels on the order of 10 mm/s. Given that the diagnostic evaluation of CAD often requires sub-millimeter spatial resolution to detect stenoses or plaque buildup, even minor motion during image acquisition can result in significant blurring, ghosting, or misregistration artifacts that compromise image interpretability. To mitigate these effects, MRCA protocols universally employ cardiac gating, a technique that restricts data acquisition to specific phases of the cardiac cycle when coronary motion is minimized.

Prospective cardiac gating, the most effective approach for motion suppression in MRCA, synchronizes the imaging window with the diastasis period—a brief interval within mid-to-late diastole characterized by relative mechanical quiescence of the ventricles. Empirical studies have estimated that diastasis durations sufficient to limit motion blur to within sub-millimeter voxels range from approximately 65 to 330 milliseconds, depending on the coronary segment and individual physiology. Accurate identification of this narrow temporal window is therefore critical to achieving high-quality MRCA images.

Conventionally, the timing of diastasis is determined using a two-step process involving electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring and a preliminary cine cardiovascular magnetic resonance (cine-CMR) acquisition. The ECG provides a fiducial marker—the R-wave peak—that denotes the onset of ventricular systole, serving as a temporal reference point for the cardiac cycle. A breath-held, retrospectively gated cine-CMR scan is then performed in a standard cardiac view (e.g., four-chamber long-axis) to capture dynamic images of the heart over multiple phases of the cycle. From these cine images, the diastasis window is inferred by analyzing frame-to-frame motion patterns, often through metrics such as correlation coefficients between successive frames. A plateau in these correlations—indicating minimal structural change—is interpreted as diastasis, and its boundaries are typically defined by inflection points in the correlation curve between the E- and A-waves of diastolic filling.

However, this conventional cine-CMR-based approach suffers from several fundamental limitations that undermine its reliability and precision. First, the temporal resolution of cine-CMR is inherently constrained by the physics of k-space sampling. In segmented cine acquisitions, the true temporal resolution is determined by the product of the repetition time (TR)—the duration required to acquire a single line of k-space—and the number of k-space lines acquired per heartbeat (commonly referred to as lines per segment, or LPS). For example, with a TR of 4 ms and LPS of 16, the effective temporal resolution is 64 ms. While adequate for gross assessment of ventricular function, this resolution is insufficient to resolve the rapid transitions into and out of diastasis, particularly in segments like the mid-RCA where motion deceleration occurs over tens of milliseconds. As a result, the inferred diastasis window may be temporally misaligned, leading to residual motion during the actual MRCA acquisition.

Second, cine-CMR is highly susceptible to temporal data mixing in the presence of heart rate variability (HRV). Because cine acquisitions typically span multiple heartbeats to achieve full k-space coverage, any fluctuation in heart rate during the breath-hold—such as the common 5–10 bpm increase observed during prolonged apnea—causes inconsistent binning of k-space data across cardiac phases. This results in blurred or aliased temporal representations of cardiac motion, further degrading the accuracy of diastasis window estimation. Unlike real-time techniques, conventional cine cannot adapt on a beat-to-beat basis to HRV, rendering it ill-suited for dynamic physiological conditions.

Third, the reliance on global ventricular motion as a surrogate for coronary-specific quiescence introduces an additional layer of uncertainty. While the four-chamber cine view captures overall chamber dynamics, it does not directly monitor the motion of the coronary arteries themselves or their immediate myocardial substrate. Regional heterogeneities in diastolic timing—well-documented in the literature—mean that global diastasis may not coincide precisely with local coronary quiescence, particularly in distal or branching segments.

Alternative modalities such as tissue Doppler echocardiography (TDE) have demonstrated superior temporal resolution and direct measurement of myocardial tissue velocities, including at the interventricular septum (IVS). TDE has been shown to accurately identify diastasis windows correlated with coronary motion minima. However, TDE is operator-dependent, requires specialized ultrasound equipment and expertise, and is not natively integrated into the MR workflow, making it impractical for routine use in MRCA protocols.

In light of these shortcomings, there exists a clear and unmet need for an MR-compatible, high-temporal-resolution method to directly and robustly determine the diastasis window without reliance on low-resolution cine imaging or external modalities. Such a method must be capable of capturing rapid cardiac motion dynamics, adapting to physiological variations like HRV, and providing a reliable surrogate for coronary quiescence—all within the constraints of a clinical MR environment. The present invention addresses this need through the development and implementation of the Septal Scout technique, a novel motion monitoring strategy that leverages one-dimensional projection imaging of the basal ventricular septum to achieve unprecedented temporal fidelity in diastasis detection.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive solution to the problem of inaccurate cardiac gating in magnetic resonance coronary angiography (MRCA) through the introduction of a novel motion monitoring technique termed the “Septal Scout.” This technique fundamentally reconfigures the trade-off between spatial and temporal resolution in cardiac MR by sacrificing one dimension of spatial encoding to achieve ultra-high temporal sampling of a targeted region of interest—the basal interventricular septum (IVS). The core insight underlying the invention is that the long-axis motion of the basal septum serves as a highly reliable surrogate for global ventricular quiescence during diastasis, and that direct monitoring of this motion with millisecond-level temporal resolution enables more precise identification of the optimal imaging window than conventional cine-based methods.

In accordance with the invention, a Septal Scout acquisition is performed as a pre-scan calibration step prior to the main MRCA sequence. This acquisition utilizes a modified steady-state free precession (SSFP) pulse sequence in which phase encoding gradients are disabled, resulting in the collection of one-dimensional (1D) projection profiles along a prescribed scout plane that intersects the basal IVS. By eliminating the time-consuming phase encoding steps, the repetition time (TR) of the sequence can be reduced to as low as 3–10 milliseconds, enabling the acquisition of a new projection every few milliseconds throughout the cardiac cycle. Over a 5-second breath-hold, this yields hundreds of temporal samples, effectively creating an M-mode-like representation of septal motion analogous to that used in echocardiography.

The resulting 1D projection data is processed to extract a pseudo-displacement signal representative of septal tissue motion. Specifically, a region of interest (ROI) is defined along the depth dimension of the scout plane to encompass the basal septum, and signals within this ROI are averaged to enhance signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). The temporal evolution of this averaged signal is then analyzed using optical flow methods or similar motion estimation algorithms to derive a velocity-like index that reveals the characteristic E-wave (early diastolic filling) and A-wave (atrial contraction) peaks of diastole. The diastasis period is identified as the interval between these two waves, with its start and end times precisely demarcated by the inflection points (i.e., zero-crossings of the second derivative) of the velocity index curve.

Critically, because the Septal Scout acquires data continuously at high temporal resolution over multiple heartbeats, it can account for heart rate variability (HRV) that commonly occurs during breath-holds. Rather than assuming a fixed cardiac cycle length—as cine-CMR does—the invention computes a diastasis window for each individual heartbeat and then determines a consensus or intersection window that remains valid across the observed range of heart rates. This adaptive capability ensures that the final gating window used for MRCA is robust to physiological fluctuations, thereby minimizing motion artifacts even under non-ideal scanning conditions.

The invention further includes an integrated imaging protocol that seamlessly incorporates the Septal Scout calibration into a standard MRCA workflow. This protocol comprises: (1) initial anatomical localization using real-time imaging; (2) prescription of a four-chamber cine view for anatomical reference; (3) performance of the Septal Scout acquisition aligned with the basal septum; (4) automated or semi-automated processing of the scout data to determine the optimal diastasis window; and (5) execution of the high-resolution 3D MRCA sequence using the derived gating parameters. Optionally, the protocol may include a second Septal Scout at the end of the breath-hold to confirm stability or adjust for drift.

In comparative validation studies involving healthy volunteers, MRCA images acquired using Septal Scout-derived gating windows demonstrated significantly improved vessel sharpness compared to those using conventional cine-CMR-derived windows. Quantitative analysis via full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) measurements revealed narrower vessel profiles—indicative of reduced motion blur—for both large and small coronary segments when imaged with the Septal Scout method (p = 0.03 for both categories). Subjective image quality assessments by blinded observers also consistently favored the Septal Scout approach, with higher Likert-scale scores for edge definition across all evaluated vessel segments. Notably, these improvements were achieved without compromising signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), confirming that the benefits stem from enhanced motion control rather than signal enhancement.

Furthermore, the invention establishes strong concordance between the Septal Scout and tissue Doppler echocardiography (TDE)—a high-fidelity reference standard—for diastasis timing estimation. In a subset of subjects, correlation coefficients exceeded 0.98 (p < 0.001) for both start and end times of the diastasis window, with Bland-Altman analysis showing no systematic bias. This validates the physiological relevance of septal motion as a surrogate for ventricular quiescence and confirms the technical accuracy of the MR-based approach.

The invention also contemplates several advanced implementations and extensions. These include the use of two-dimensional (2D) radiofrequency (RF) excitation pulses—such as spiral or parallel-transmit designs—to selectively excite only the septal tissue, thereby reducing contamination from static background structures and minimizing potential interference with subsequent coronary imaging. Additionally, the Septal Scout concept may be evolved into a real-time “Septal Navigator” that operates interleaved with the imaging sequence, providing continuous motion feedback for prospective gating without requiring a separate calibration scan. Such a navigator could be combined with respiratory navigators to enable fully motion-compensated whole-heart MRCA.

While the current embodiment focuses on diastasis detection for MRCA, the underlying principle of targeted, high-temporal-resolution motion monitoring is broadly applicable to other cardiac MR applications requiring precise motion synchronization, such as myocardial tagging, strain imaging, or valve assessment. The invention thus represents a paradigm shift in cardiac gating methodology, moving from indirect, low-resolution inference to direct, high-fidelity motion observation.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a method and system for determining an optimal cardiac gating window for use in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), particularly in the context of magnetic resonance coronary angiography (MRCA). The core innovation lies in the development and application of a novel MR-based motion monitoring technique—herein designated the “Septal Scout”—which enables high-temporal-resolution tracking of ventricular septal motion to accurately identify the diastasis period of the cardiac cycle. The following detailed description sets forth the structural, functional, and operational aspects of the invention, including hardware configurations, pulse sequence design, data processing algorithms, integration into clinical workflows, and performance characteristics.

The invention is implemented on a standard clinical MRI system equipped with appropriate hardware and software capabilities. In the exemplary embodiment, imaging is performed on a 1.5 Tesla MR scanner (e.g., GE Optima 450w) using a multi-channel phased-array cardiac coil (e.g., 32-channel) to ensure adequate signal reception from the heart and surrounding vasculature. The system must support advanced pulse sequence programming, real-time triggering from physiological signals (e.g., ECG), and the ability to disable phase encoding gradients for projection imaging. While the examples herein utilize SSFP-based sequences, the principles of the invention are equally applicable to gradient-echo, spin-echo, or other contrast mechanisms, provided that sufficient tissue contrast exists between the myocardium and surrounding structures in the scout plane.

The imaging protocol according to the invention comprises a series of coordinated steps designed to localize anatomy, calibrate gating parameters, and acquire high-resolution angiographic data. Initially, standard real-time imaging sequences (e.g., MR-Echo or similar) are employed to obtain orthogonal views of the heart, including short-axis and long-axis orientations. From these, a four-chamber long-axis view is prescribed to visualize both ventricles and the interventricular septum. This view serves as the anatomical roadmap for subsequent steps.

Following localization, the Septal Scout acquisition is performed. A thin imaging slab (typically 5–10 mm thick) is prescribed perpendicular to the long-axis of the heart, intersecting the basal portion of the interventricular septum. This “Scout Plane” is carefully aligned to remain within the septal tissue throughout the cardiac cycle, avoiding inclusion of adjacent blood pools or epicardial fat that could confound motion analysis. The alignment is facilitated by visual inspection of the four-chamber cine or real-time images, ensuring that the scout plane traverses the thickest part of the basal septum near the atrioventricular groove.

The Septal Scout pulse sequence is a modified SSFP sequence in which all phase encoding gradients are turned off. Consequently, instead of acquiring a two-dimensional image, the sequence collects a one-dimensional projection profile along the frequency-encoding direction of the scout plane with each repetition. The repetition time (TR) is minimized—typically set to 10 ms or less—to maximize temporal sampling density. Other sequence parameters are optimized for tissue contrast and SNR: for instance, a flip angle of 55°, echo time (TE) of approximately 1.7–1.9 ms, and field of view (FOV) of 31 cm may be used. The acquisition is prospectively triggered from the R-peak of the simultaneously recorded ECG signal and continues for a predetermined duration (e.g., 5 seconds), yielding a time series of 1D projections that collectively form a spatiotemporal map of septal motion.

Each 1D projection consists of a vector of signal intensities sampled along the spatial axis of the scout plane. Over time, these vectors are stacked to create a two-dimensional dataset where one axis represents spatial position (depth along the septum) and the other represents time (cardiac phase). This dataset resembles an M-mode ultrasound image, with bright and dark bands corresponding to tissue interfaces and motion trajectories.

To extract meaningful motion information, a region of interest (ROI) is defined within this spatiotemporal map. The ROI spans a narrow depth range (e.g., ±2.5 mm) centered on the expected location of the basal septum. This height is chosen to balance two competing considerations: it must be small enough to approximate a rigid body (minimizing internal deformation effects) yet large enough to allow spatial averaging for noise reduction. Within this ROI, signal intensities are averaged across the spatial dimension at each time point, producing a single time-series signal that reflects the aggregate intensity variation of the septal tissue over the cardiac cycle.

This time-series signal is not a direct measure of displacement but serves as a proxy due to the inherent texture and contrast of myocardial tissue. As the septum moves through the fixed scout plane during the cardiac cycle, different tissue elements enter and exit the imaging slice, causing temporal fluctuations in the averaged signal. These fluctuations correlate with tissue velocity and can be converted into a motion index using computer vision techniques adapted for medical imaging.

In the preferred embodiment, the gradient optical flow method is applied to the spatiotemporal map within the ROI. Optical flow algorithms estimate the apparent motion of brightness patterns between consecutive frames under the assumption of brightness constancy. In this 1D+time context, the method computes a pseudo-velocity field by solving the optical flow constraint equation: ∂I/∂t + v·∇I = 0, where I is the image intensity, t is time, v is velocity, and ∇I is the spatial gradient. Because absolute velocity calibration is unnecessary for diastasis detection—only relative timing matters—the output is treated as a qualitative velocity index.

The resulting velocity index exhibits a characteristic waveform with two prominent peaks corresponding to the E-wave (rapid early diastolic filling) and A-wave (late diastolic atrial contraction). Between these peaks lies a trough or plateau representing diastasis—the period of minimal motion. To objectively define the boundaries of this interval, the velocity index curve is subjected to mathematical analysis. Specifically, the second derivative of the curve is computed, and its zero-crossings (inflection points) between the E- and A-waves are identified. The earlier inflection point marks the end of rapid deceleration from the E-wave and the onset of diastasis; the later inflection point marks the beginning of acceleration toward the A-wave and the end of diastasis. These two time points, referenced to the R-peak of the ECG, constitute the diastasis window (Wsep).

A key advantage of the Septal Scout over conventional cine is its ability to handle heart rate variability (HRV). Because the scout acquires data continuously at high temporal resolution over many heartbeats, it can compute a separate diastasis window for each cardiac cycle. In practice, the acquisition is performed at both the beginning and end of a simulated 20-second breath-hold to capture the expected range of heart rates. The final gating window is then defined as the intersection of all individual diastasis windows across the observed heartbeats. This ensures that the selected window remains within the quiescent period regardless of minor HRV, a feature impossible with cine-CMR, which assumes a fixed cycle length.

Once Wsep is determined, it is used to configure the prospective gating parameters for the subsequent 3D MRCA acquisition. The MRCA sequence—typically a fat-suppressed 3D SSFP with isotropic or near-isotropic resolution (e.g., 1.5 × 1.5 × 2.0 mm)—is programmed to acquire k-space data only during the validated diastasis window. The number of TRs per heartbeat, and thus total scan time, is automatically adjusted based on the window duration.

In alternative embodiments, the Septal Scout may be enhanced through advanced RF pulse design. Standard slice-selective RF pulses excite a rectangular slab that includes not only the septum but also static tissues in the chest wall and spine, contributing background signal that can reduce motion contrast. To mitigate this, 2D RF excitation pulses—such as spiral or k-space trajectory-based designs—can be employed to selectively excite only a cylindrical or curved volume conforming to the septal geometry. Although such pulses may slightly increase TR due to longer duration, maintaining TR ≤ 10 ms is feasible with modern hardware and preserves sufficient temporal resolution.

Furthermore, the invention contemplates the evolution of the Septal Scout into a real-time “Septal Navigator.” In this mode, the 1D projection acquisition is interleaved with the imaging sequence itself, providing continuous motion feedback. Each heartbeat, the navigator data is processed in real-time to verify that the current cardiac phase falls within the pre-defined diastasis window before allowing image data acquisition to proceed. This closed-loop system offers maximum robustness against unexpected arrhythmias or respiratory motion. Integration with existing respiratory navigators (e.g., diaphragm tracking) enables dual-gating for both cardiac and respiratory motion compensation.

The invention has been validated in a cohort of healthy volunteers, demonstrating superior performance over cine-CMR. Quantitative FWHM analysis showed significantly sharper vessel profiles with Septal Scout gating (large vessels: 3.6 vs. 4.1 mm; small vessels: 2.1 vs. 2.4 mm; p = 0.03). Subjective scoring by blinded radiologists confirmed these findings, with consistent preference for Septal Scout images across all vessel segments. Concordance with TDE was excellent (r > 0.98), validating the physiological basis of the method.

While the current focus is on MRCA, the Septal Scout principle is extensible to other applications requiring precise motion synchronization, such as myocardial perfusion imaging, fibrosis characterization, or electrophysiological mapping. Future work will explore performance in patient populations with cardiac pathology, where septal motion abnormalities (e.g., septal bounce in constrictive cardiomyopathy) may necessitate algorithmic adaptations or alternative motion surrogates.

In summary, the invention provides a robust, MR-native, high-temporal-resolution method for cardiac gating calibration that overcomes the limitations of conventional cine-CMR. By directly monitoring septal motion with millisecond fidelity and adapting to physiological variability, it enables sharper, more diagnostic coronary images and represents a significant advance in the field of cardiovascular magnetic resonance.
