## BACKGROUND

The innate immune system serves as the body’s first line of defense against invading pathogens, particularly viruses, through a sophisticated network of cellular sensors known as pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). These receptors detect conserved molecular signatures associated with microbial infection or cellular stress—collectively termed pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)—and initiate signaling cascades that culminate in the production of cytokines, chemokines, and effector molecules designed to limit pathogen replication and orchestrate adaptive immunity. Among the most critical outcomes of PRR activation is the induction of type I and type III interferons (IFNs), which establish an antiviral state in both infected and neighboring uninfected cells. This IFN response is mediated through the Janus kinase–signal transducer and activator of transcription (Jak-STAT) signaling pathway, leading to the transcription of hundreds of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) that collectively inhibit viral replication at multiple stages of the life cycle.

A central transcription factor in this process is interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), which, upon activation via phosphorylation by the kinases TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and IκB kinase ε (IKKε), dimerizes, translocates to the nucleus, and drives the expression of IFNβ and a subset of ISGs. IRF3 activation is typically initiated through adaptor proteins that integrate signals from upstream PRRs. For example, Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) and TLR4 signal through the adaptor TRIF (TIR-domain-containing adaptor-inducing IFNβ), while cytosolic RNA sensors RIG-I and MDA5 rely on the mitochondrial adaptor IPS-1 (also known as MAVS, VISA, or Cardif). More recently, the adaptor protein STING (stimulator of interferon genes; also designated TMEM173, MITA, MPYS, or ERIS) has emerged as a pivotal hub for innate immune responses to cytosolic DNA and cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs). STING functions both as a direct receptor for bacterial CDNs such as c-di-GMP and 3′3′-cGAMP, and as an essential signaling node downstream of multiple DNA-sensing PRRs, including cGAS, IFI16, DDX41, and ZBP1/DAI. Upon ligand binding or activation by upstream sensors, STING traffics from the endoplasmic reticulum to perinuclear vesicles, recruiting TBK1 and facilitating IRF3 phosphorylation.

Given the broad antiviral potency of the IFN system, pharmacologic stimulation of IRF3-dependent pathways has long been pursued as a strategy for developing broad-spectrum antivirals. Indeed, agonists of TLRs and RIG-I have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models against diverse viral pathogens. Similarly, synthetic small molecules that activate the STING pathway hold immense therapeutic promise—not only as antivirals but also as vaccine adjuvants, anti-angiogenic agents, and immunotherapeutics for cancer. Two such compounds, 5,6-dimethylxanthenone-4-acetic acid (DMXAA) and 10-carboxymethyl-9-acridanone (CMA), were shown to potently activate STING in murine systems, inducing robust IFN responses and exhibiting antiviral and antitumor activity in mouse models. However, both compounds failed in human clinical trials due to their inability to activate human STING, a consequence of species-specific differences in the STING ligand-binding domain. Despite extensive efforts, no synthetic small molecule capable of selectively and effectively activating human STING had been reported prior to the present invention.

Alphaviruses represent a significant and growing public health threat. Members of this genus include Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), which has caused explosive outbreaks across the Caribbean, Central, and South America since its introduction to the Americas in 2013, affecting over one million individuals and causing debilitating, often chronic arthralgia. Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), a New World alphavirus, is even more virulent, with case fatality rates reaching 35% in children. Critically, both CHIKV and VEEV are exquisitely sensitive to type I IFN, and their replication is potently suppressed by IRF3 activation. Moreover, as RNA viruses that replicate in the cytoplasm without a DNA intermediate, they are unlikely to have evolved specific countermeasures against the cytosolic DNA-sensing STING pathway. This renders STING an attractive and potentially vulnerable target for pharmacologic intervention against alphaviral infections, for which no FDA-approved antivirals or vaccines currently exist.

In light of these considerations, there exists a pressing need for novel pharmacologic agents that can safely and effectively stimulate the human STING pathway to induce a potent, IRF3-driven antiviral state. The present invention addresses this unmet need by providing the first synthetic small molecule, designated G10, that has been definitively shown to activate human STING-dependent signaling, leading to IRF3 phosphorylation, IFN production, and robust inhibition of clinically relevant alphaviruses in human cells. Unlike previously described murine-specific STING agonists, G10 functions exclusively in human and primate systems, offering a unique tool for therapeutic development and mechanistic studies of human innate immunity.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel synthetic small molecule, 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, referred to herein as G10, and its use in the treatment of viral infections, particularly those caused by alphaviruses such as Chikungunya virus (CHIKV) and Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV). The invention further encompasses pharmaceutical compositions comprising G10, alone or in combination with other therapeutically active agents, and methods of using such compositions to induce an antiviral state in a subject by activating the stimulator of interferon genes (STING)-dependent signaling pathway.

G10 has been identified through high-throughput screening as a potent inducer of interferon-stimulated response element (ISRE)-dependent transcription in human fibroblasts. Subsequent characterization demonstrates that G10 selectively activates the IRF3 branch of innate immune signaling without concomitant activation of the NF-κB pathway in stromal cells, thereby promoting a focused antiviral response with potentially reduced pro-inflammatory side effects. The antiviral activity of G10 is shown to be highly effective against both Old World (e.g., CHIKV, Sindbis virus) and New World (e.g., VEEV) alphaviruses, reducing viral replication by several orders of magnitude in vitro.

Through a comprehensive series of reverse genetic experiments employing CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout, the invention establishes that the biological activity of G10 is strictly dependent on the presence of STING and IRF3. Cells genetically deficient in either STING or IRF3 fail to mount any transcriptional or antiviral response to G10, confirming that G10 acts upstream of these essential signaling components. In contrast, G10-mediated activity is independent of the IPS-1/MAVS adaptor protein, indicating that it does not engage the canonical RIG-I/MDA5 RNA-sensing pathway. Furthermore, the invention demonstrates that G10 induces the synthesis and secretion of bioactive type I and type III interferons in a STING-dependent manner, and that the full antiviral effect requires downstream STAT1 signaling, highlighting the contribution of both the initial IRF3-driven response and the subsequent IFN amplification loop.

Although G10 does not appear to bind directly to the C-terminal ligand-binding domain of human STING—as evidenced by thermal shift assays—the invention confirms that its activity is nonetheless channeled exclusively through the STING pathway. The invention also shows that G10 is active in primary human cells, including peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and endothelial cells, underscoring its physiological relevance. While G10 is specific to human and non-human primate systems and inactive in murine cells, the invention leverages the murine-specific STING agonist DMXAA to demonstrate in a mouse model of CHIKV infection that pharmacologic activation of the STING pathway can completely abrogate viremia when administered prophylactically or early post-infection, thereby validating STING agonism as a viable in vivo antiviral strategy.

In summary, the present invention provides the first definitive evidence of a synthetic small molecule capable of activating the human STING pathway to elicit a potent and therapeutically relevant antiviral state. G10 represents a novel chemical scaffold for the development of human-specific STING agonists with applications in the treatment and prevention of alphaviral and other viral diseases.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Terms

As used herein, the term “G10” refers to the compound 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, and pharmaceutically acceptable salts, solvates, hydrates, stereoisomers, and prodrugs thereof. The chemical structure of G10 is characterized by a benzo[b][1,4]thiazine core substituted at the 4-position with a 2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl group and at the 6-position with a carboxamide linked to a furan-2-ylmethyl moiety.

The term “STING” or “stimulator of interferon genes” refers to the human protein encoded by the TMEM173 gene (UniProt ID Q86WV6), including all naturally occurring isoforms and variants that retain the ability to mediate IRF3 activation in response to cytosolic nucleic acids or cyclic dinucleotides. The term encompasses the full-length transmembrane protein as well as functional fragments, such as the C-terminal domain (CTD), provided they participate in the signaling cascade leading to interferon production.

The term “IRF3” or “interferon regulatory factor 3” refers to the human transcription factor (UniProt ID Q14653) that, upon phosphorylation at serine residues in its C-terminal region (notably S386), forms homodimers or heterodimers, translocates to the nucleus, and binds to interferon-stimulated response elements (ISREs) in the promoters of target genes, including IFNβ and numerous interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs).

The term “alphavirus” refers to any member of the Alphavirus genus within the Togaviridae family, including but not limited to Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), Eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV), Western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV), Sindbis virus (SINV), and Ross River virus (RRV). These are positive-sense, single-stranded RNA viruses transmitted primarily by mosquitoes.

The term “antiviral activity” or “anti-alphaviral activity” refers to the ability of a compound or composition to reduce the replication, infectivity, or yield of a virus in a cell culture system or in a subject, as measured by standard virological assays such as plaque assays, TCID50, or quantitative RT-PCR for viral RNA.

The term “pharmaceutically acceptable” refers to those salts, carriers, excipients, and formulations that are suitable for administration to a human or animal subject without undue toxicity, irritation, or allergic response, and that are commensurate with a reasonable benefit/risk ratio.

The term “treatment” includes both prophylactic and therapeutic administration, meaning the prevention of disease onset in a susceptible individual or the amelioration of symptoms and reduction of viral load in an already infected individual.

The compound may be synthesized according to standard organic chemistry procedures known in the art, starting from commercially available precursors such as 4-hydroxybenzo[b][1,4]thiazine-3(2H)-one, which can be alkylated with 2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl bromide followed by carboxylation and amidation with furan-2-ylmethylamine. Alternative synthetic routes may involve the construction of the thiazine ring from appropriately substituted anilines and α-halo carbonyl compounds.

Pharmaceutically acceptable salts of G10 include those formed with inorganic acids (e.g., hydrochloric, hydrobromic, sulfuric, phosphoric) or organic acids (e.g., acetic, citric, maleic, succinic, tartaric). Prodrugs of G10 are also encompassed, wherein the compound is modified to enhance bioavailability, solubility, or stability, and which are converted in vivo to the active parent compound by enzymatic or chemical processes. Such prodrugs may include ester, amide, or phosphate derivatives that are cleaved under physiological conditions.

The invention further contemplates analogs and derivatives of G10 that retain the core structural features necessary for STING-dependent activity. Structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies, as partially disclosed in the accompanying research (see S7 Fig), indicate that the 2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl group at the 4-position and the furan-2-ylmethyl carboxamide at the 6-position are critical for activity, while modifications to the thiazine ring or the linker regions may be tolerated to varying degrees. Compounds that share at least 70% structural similarity with G10 and exhibit comparable or superior STING-dependent IRF3 activation and antiviral potency are considered within the scope of the invention.

### Pharmaceutical Compositions

The invention provides pharmaceutical compositions comprising an effective amount of G10, or a pharmaceutically acceptable salt or prodrug thereof, in combination with one or more pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, diluents, or excipients. The compositions may be formulated for any suitable route of administration, including oral, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, intraperitoneal, intranasal, topical, or inhalation, depending on the clinical indication and desired pharmacokinetic profile.

For oral administration, the composition may be in the form of tablets, capsules, powders, or solutions, containing conventional excipients such as lactose, starch, magnesium stearate, and cellulose derivatives. For parenteral administration, the composition may be a sterile solution or suspension in isotonic saline, buffered saline, or other pharmaceutically acceptable vehicles, optionally containing solubilizing agents, stabilizers, or preservatives. The concentration of G10 in the composition will vary depending on the intended dose, but typically ranges from 0.1 mg/mL to 100 mg/mL for injectable formulations and from 1 mg to 500 mg per unit dose for oral formulations.

The compositions may also include additional therapeutic agents to enhance efficacy or broaden the spectrum of antiviral activity. For example, G10 may be combined with direct-acting antivirals that target viral enzymes (e.g., protease or polymerase inhibitors), or with immunomodulators that act on complementary pathways (e.g., TLR agonists, RIG-I agonists). Such combinations may produce synergistic effects, allowing for lower doses of each agent and reducing the risk of toxicity or resistance development.

The pharmaceutical compositions are prepared under aseptic conditions where appropriate and are packaged in unit-dose or multi-dose containers, such as sealed ampoules or vials, or in blister packs for solid dosage forms. The compositions are stable under standard storage conditions (e.g., 2–8°C for liquids, room temperature for solids) and have a shelf life of at least 12 months.

### Treatment

The present invention provides methods for treating or preventing viral infections in a subject, comprising administering to the subject a therapeutically effective amount of G10 or a pharmaceutical composition thereof. The viral infections targeted by the invention are primarily those caused by alphaviruses, including Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV), Eastern equine encephalitis virus (EEEV), Western equine encephalitis virus (WEEV), Sindbis virus (SINV), and Ross River virus (RRV). Given the broad mechanism of action—induction of a STING-dependent antiviral state—the invention may also be effective against other viruses that are sensitive to type I interferon, such as flaviviruses (e.g., West Nile virus, dengue virus), orthomyxoviruses (e.g., influenza virus), and certain DNA viruses.

The method is applicable to both human and non-human primate subjects. Administration may be prophylactic, for example, in individuals traveling to or residing in regions endemic for alphaviruses, or therapeutic, in patients presenting with acute alphaviral disease. The dosing regimen will depend on the severity of infection, the route of administration, and the pharmacokinetic properties of the formulation, but typical doses range from 0.1 mg/kg to 50 mg/kg of body weight, administered once or multiple times daily for a duration of 3 to 14 days.

The efficacy of treatment can be monitored by measuring reductions in viral load in serum or tissues, alleviation of clinical symptoms (e.g., fever, joint pain, encephalitis), or induction of interferon-stimulated gene expression in peripheral blood cells. The invention is particularly advantageous because G10 induces a focused IRF3-driven response with minimal NF-κB activation in key cell types like fibroblasts, potentially avoiding the systemic inflammation associated with broader innate immune activators.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1 Identification of a Novel IFN/IRF3-Inducing Molecule by High Throughput In Vitro Screening

A high-throughput screen was conducted to identify novel small molecules capable of stimulating innate immune signaling in human cells. Telomerized human foreskin fibroblasts (THF) stably expressing a luciferase reporter gene under the control of an interferon-stimulated response element (ISRE)—designated THF-ISRE cells—were used as the screening platform. These cells respond to both direct IRF3 activation and indirect IFN/Jak-STAT signaling by producing luminescence proportional to pathway activity. A library of 51,632 chemically diverse compounds was screened in duplicate in a 384-well plate format. Each plate included 16 positive controls (1000 U/mL recombinant human IFNβ) and 16 negative controls (1% DMSO vehicle). A compound was considered a hit if its luminescence reading (R) exceeded the threshold defined by R > (μP - μN) * 0.5, where μP and μN are the mean luminescence values of the positive and negative controls, respectively.

Among the hits, the compound 4-(2-chloro-6-fluorobenzyl)-N-(furan-2-ylmethyl)-3-oxo-3,4-dihydro-2H-benzo[b][1,4]thiazine-6-carboxamide, designated G10, exhibited the third highest luminescence signal. G10 was selected for further study due to its potent activity and low cytotoxicity, as assessed by ATP-based cell viability assays (CellTiter-Glo), which showed no significant reduction in cell viability even at concentrations up to 100 μM. The chemical structure of G10 was confirmed by mass spectrometry and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. This example demonstrates the successful identification of G10 as a novel inducer of IFN/IRF3-dependent transcription in human cells.

### Example 2—G10 Induces IFN/IRF3- but not NF-κB-Dependent Transcription in Human Fibroblasts

To validate and characterize the signaling specificity of G10, dose-response experiments were performed in THF-ISRE cells. G10 induced luciferase expression in a concentration-dependent manner, with activity detectable at 1 μM and maximal at 50 μM. To confirm that this reporter activity reflected genuine activation of endogenous innate immune genes, quantitative RT-PCR (qPCR) was used to measure mRNA levels of established IRF3- and IFN-dependent genes, including ISG54, ISG56, ISG15, and Viperin. Treatment of THF cells with G10 (50 μM, 7 hours) induced robust transcription of these genes, comparable to the response elicited by UV-inactivated human cytomegalovirus (UV-CMV), a known STING-dependent stimulus.

To assess whether G10 also activated the NF-κB pathway—a common co-activator of inflammatory responses—THF cells stably expressing an NF-κB-dependent luciferase reporter were treated with G10. Even at high concentrations (up to 100 μM), G10 failed to induce any significant NF-κB-dependent luminescence, in stark contrast to positive controls such as TNFα, LPS, or Sendai virus (SeV). Consistent with this, qPCR analysis showed no induction of classic NF-κB-dependent genes such as IL8, IL1β, or MIP1α in G10-treated THF cells. These results demonstrate that G1,0 selectively activates the IRF3 arm of innate immunity without triggering canonical NF-κB signaling in human fibroblasts, suggesting a favorable safety profile with reduced risk of pro-inflammatory pathology.

### Example 3—G10 Elicits Antiviral Activity Against New and Old World Alphaviruses

The functional consequence of G10-induced innate immune activation was evaluated by testing its ability to confer an antiviral state against a panel of viruses. THF cells were pre-treated with G10 (1–50 μM) for 6 hours, then infected with West Nile virus (WNV), Vaccinia virus (VACV), Chikungunya virus (CHIKV), or Venezuelan equine encephalitis virus (VEEV). Viral titers were quantified by plaque assay 24 hours post-infection. G10 treatment resulted in only modest reductions in WNV (<1 log) and VACV (~1 log) replication. In striking contrast, G10 potently inhibited CHIKV replication by over 3 logs, with an IC90 (concentration inhibiting 90% of viral replication) of 8.01 μM. Similarly, VEEV replication was suppressed by more than 3 logs, with an IC90 of 24.57 μM. These results establish that G10 induces a cellular state highly refractory to alphavirus replication, with efficacy spanning both Old World (CHIKV) and New World (VEEV) clades, despite their significant genetic divergence.

### Example 4—IRF3 is Essential to G10-Induced Transcription and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

To determine the transcription factors required for G10 activity, CRISPR/Cas9 was used to generate THF-ISRE cell lines lacking either IRF3 or STAT1. Immunoblotting confirmed complete knockout of the respective proteins. Functional validation showed that STAT1-deficient cells failed to respond to IFNβ, while IRF3-deficient cells did not respond to UV-CMV, as expected. When treated with G10, STAT1-deficient cells still exhibited strong luciferase induction, whereas IRF3-deficient cells showed no response, demonstrating that G10 acts upstream of IRF3 and does not require STAT1 for initial reporter gene activation.

Direct evidence of IRF3 activation was obtained by immunoblotting with an antibody specific for phosphorylated IRF3 (S386). G10 treatment induced IRF3 phosphorylation with kinetics similar to UV-CMV and SeV. To test the role of IRF3 in antiviral activity, wild-type and IRF3-knockout THF cells were pre-treated with G10 or IFNβ and then infected with CHIKV, VEEV, or Sindbis virus (SINV). G10 completely lost its ability to inhibit alphavirus replication in IRF3-deficient cells, whereas IFNβ remained fully active, confirming that G10’s antiviral effect is entirely dependent on IRF3-mediated signaling.

### Example 5—G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation and Anti-Alphaviral Activity Occur Independently of IPS-1/MAVS-Dependent Signaling

To investigate whether G10 engages the RIG-I/MDA5 RNA-sensing pathway, which signals through the adaptor IPS-1 (MAVS), CRISPR/Cas9 was used to generate IPS-1-knockout THF cells. Immunoblotting confirmed loss of IPS-1 protein. As expected, these cells failed to phosphorylate IRF3 in response to poly(I:C), ppp-dsRNA, or SeV (all IPS-1-dependent stimuli), but responded normally to UV-CMV and 2’3’-cGAMP (STING-dependent stimuli). G10-induced IRF3 phosphorylation was fully intact in IPS-1-knockout cells, indicating that G10 does not require IPS-1 for activity.

Consistent with this, G10 retained its full anti-alphaviral potency in IPS-1-deficient cells, reducing CHIKV and VEEV replication by multiple logs, just as in wild-type cells. These findings exclude the involvement of RIG-I, MDA5, or any other upstream PRRs that signal through IPS-1, and instead point to a distinct, IPS-1-independent pathway—later identified as STING-dependent—for G10’s mechanism of action.

### Example 6—STING is Required for G10-Mediated IRF3 Activation, Gene Expression, and Anti-Alphaviral Activity

CRISPR/Cas9 was employed to generate STING-knockout THF-ISRE cells. Loss of STING protein was confirmed by immunoblotting, and functional deficiency was demonstrated by the absence of IRF3 phosphorylation in response to UV-CMV or 2’3’-cGAMP, while responses to SeV (IPS-1-dependent) remained intact. Critically, G10 failed to induce IRF3 phosphorylation in STING-knockout cells.

Reporter gene assays showed that G10-induced luciferase expression was abolished in STING-deficient cells, but preserved in IPS-1-deficient cells. Similarly, qPCR revealed that G10-induced transcription of ISG54, ISG15, and Viperin was absent in STING-knockout cells but robust in IPS-1-knockout cells. Most importantly, the anti-alphaviral activity of G10 was completely lost in STING-deficient cells, whereas IFNβ remained effective. These data provide conclusive evidence that STING is an essential and non-redundant component of the signaling pathway mediating all observed effects of G10.

### Example 7—G10 Induces STING-Dependent Synthesis and Secretion of Bioactive Interferon

To determine whether G10 triggers IFN production, qPCR was performed on THF cells treated with G10. Both IFNβ and IFNλ1 mRNA were induced, though IFNλ1 levels were lower than those induced by UV-CMV or SeV. To assess functional IFN secretion, a bioassay was developed using THF-ISRE-ΔIRF3 cells, which cannot produce IFN but respond to exogenous IFN via their intact Jak-STAT pathway. Conditioned media from G10-treated wild-type THF cells induced strong luciferase activity in these reporter cells, confirming the presence of secreted, bioactive IFN.

This IFN secretion was absent in media from STING-knockout cells treated with G10, but intact in IPS-1-knockout cells, demonstrating that G10-induced IFN production is STING-dependent but IPS-1-independent. Interestingly, IFN secretion was partially reduced in IPS-1-knockout cells, suggesting potential crosstalk between the two pathways, though this is not required for the core antiviral effect. These results establish that G10 stimulates a complete innate immune cascade: STING-dependent IRF3 activation leads to IFN synthesis and secretion, which then acts in an autocrine/paracrine manner to amplify ISG expression via Jak-STAT signaling.

### Example 8—STAT1 is Required for G10-Mediated Anti-Alphaviral Activity and ISG Expression

Although G10’s initial signaling is IRF3-dependent and STAT1-independent, the contribution of the secondary IFN/Jak-STAT loop to antiviral activity was examined. STAT1-knockout THF cells were treated with G10 and infected with CHIKV, VEEV, or SINV. In the absence of STAT1, G10 completely lost its ability to inhibit viral replication, whereas IFNβ retained partial activity (especially against SINV), consistent with known STAT1-independent IFN signaling mechanisms.

At the molecular level, G10 failed to induce protein expression of classic IFN-dependent ISGs like Mx2 and ISG56 in STAT1-knockout cells, whereas other stimuli (SeV, UV-CMV, IFNβ) could still induce these proteins, likely via STAT2 homodimers. This unique dependence on STAT1 for G10’s effect suggests that the IFN subtype profile or magnitude induced by G10 may be insufficient to engage alternative STAT complexes effectively. Thus, the full antiviral potency of G10 requires both the initial IRF3-driven response and the subsequent STAT1-dependent amplification loop.

### Example 9—G10 Triggers IRF3-Dependent Activity with Less Potency than 2′3′-cGAMP

To benchmark G10 against a canonical STING ligand, its activity was compared to 2’3’-cGAMP. Kinetic analysis showed that G10 induced IRF3 phosphorylation as early as 30 minutes post-treatment, slightly faster than transfected 2’3’-cGAMP (detectable at 1 hour), possibly due to better cell permeability. However, dose-response qPCR revealed that 2’3’-cGAMP induced higher levels of ISG mRNAs (IFIT1, IFIT2, ISG15, Viperin, OAS, Mx2) at lower concentrations than G10. IFNβ induction was more comparable between the two stimuli.

These results indicate that while G10 accesses the STING pathway efficiently, it is a less potent agonist than the natural ligand 2’3’-cGAMP. This may reflect differences in binding affinity, mechanism of STING activation, or intracellular metabolism. Nonetheless, G10 achieves therapeutically relevant levels of antiviral activity, as demonstrated by its low micromolar IC90 values against alphaviruses.
