# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

### 1. Field

The present invention relates generally to optical imaging systems and, more particularly, to methods and apparatuses for high-speed axial re-focusing in microscopy without mechanical movement of the objective lens or the sample. The invention enables rapid volumetric imaging by converting lateral scanning motion into axial displacement of the focal plane while maintaining diffraction-limited optical performance across the entire scan range. This technology is especially suited for applications requiring high temporal resolution and high spatial fidelity, such as neuroscience, intravital imaging, light-sheet microscopy, and dynamic cellular processes monitoring.

### 2. Discussion of Related Art

In modern optical microscopy, the ability to rapidly shift the focal plane along the optical axis (Z-direction) is critical for capturing three-dimensional dynamics of biological specimens. Traditional approaches to axial scanning involve physically translating either the microscope objective or the sample stage. While effective in static or slow-moving scenarios, these mechanical methods suffer from inherent inertia due to the mass of the moving components, which severely limits achievable scan speeds—typically below tens of hertz. This limitation becomes prohibitive when studying fast physiological phenomena such as neuronal action potentials, calcium transients, or cardiac contractions, which occur on millisecond or sub-millisecond timescales.

To overcome this bottleneck, several remote focusing techniques have been developed that modulate the wavefront of the illumination beam rather than moving physical components. Among these, electrically tunable lenses (ETLs) and tuneable acoustic gradient index (TAG) lenses are widely used. ETLs adjust their focal length via an applied voltage, thereby introducing a quadratic phase shift that mimics defocus. Similarly, TAG lenses utilize resonant acoustic waves to create a time-varying refractive index profile, enabling axial scanning at frequencies up to several hundred kilohertz. However, both ETLs and TAG lenses approximate only a parabolic phase function and do not correct for higher-order optical aberrations that arise when imaging away from the native focal plane of the objective. Consequently, image quality degrades significantly with increasing axial displacement, rendering these technologies unsuitable for high-resolution volumetric imaging over extended ranges.

Deformable mirrors (DMs) offer greater flexibility by allowing arbitrary wavefront shaping, including compensation for spherical and other higher-order aberrations. Nevertheless, DMs face a fundamental trade-off between actuator stroke and response speed: high-speed DMs typically provide limited surface deformation (on the order of one wavelength), restricting their axial scan range, whereas large-stroke DMs operate too slowly for real-time volumetric imaging. Thus, while DMs can achieve aberration-free focusing, they fail to meet the dual demands of speed and range required for many advanced imaging applications.

An alternative strategy involves conjugate-plane remote focusing, wherein a secondary objective is aligned such that its pupil matches that of the primary imaging objective. In this configuration, axial translation of a mirror placed at the focal plane of the secondary objective induces a corresponding axial shift in the primary focal spot while preserving optical quality. Although this method maintains diffraction-limited performance, it remains constrained by the same mechanical limitations as traditional Z-stages—namely, the finite bandwidth of available actuators, which rarely exceeds 1 kHz even with optimized piezoelectric or voice-coil drives.

More recently, ultrafast axial scanning has been demonstrated using pulse multiplexing or reverberation loops, where multiple temporally separated laser pulses are directed through different optical paths to generate distinct focal planes. While capable of nanosecond-scale switching between planes, these techniques are fundamentally limited by the fluorescence lifetime of common dyes and the repetition rate of pulsed lasers. Moreover, they produce exponentially decaying intensities across focal planes and introduce only quadratic phase shifts, leading to significant spherical aberrations and non-uniform illumination. As a result, reverberation-based methods are restricted to low-numerical-aperture imaging and sparse samples, making them impractical for high-resolution, dense biological tissues.

Given these shortcomings, some researchers have abandoned axial scanning altogether in favor of projection imaging or spatial encoding of depth information. Projection methods collapse the third dimension into a two-dimensional image, sacrificing valuable volumetric data. Spatial encoding techniques, such as those based on point spread function (PSF) engineering, require sparsely labeled samples to avoid ambiguity in depth assignment and are generally incompatible with dense or highly scattering specimens.

Despite decades of innovation, no existing technology simultaneously achieves multi-kilohertz axial scan rates, diffraction-limited resolution across the full scan range, and flexible control over focal plane positioning. There remains a pressing need for a remote focusing system that leverages the maturity and speed of lateral scanning technologies—such as galvanometric mirrors, acousto-optic deflectors, and electro-optic scanners—to enable truly high-speed, high-fidelity volumetric microscopy.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel optical system and method for high-speed axial re-focusing in microscopy by transforming lateral scanning motion into pure axial displacement of the focal plane, without moving the primary objective or the sample. This is achieved through a dual-arm optical architecture comprising a remote-focusing arm and an illumination arm, each containing a 4F telescope and an objective lens. The pupils of the two objectives are precisely matched to ensure aberration-free imaging across the axial scan range.

In the remote-focusing arm, a collimated laser beam is directed onto a high-speed lateral scanner—such as a galvanometric mirror—which laterally displaces the focus formed by a first objective (OBJ1). A reflective element positioned at or near the focal plane of OBJ1 then reflects the beam back through the same optical path. Critically, the returning beam passes again through the lateral scanner, which descanes the lateral motion, leaving only a controlled axial phase shift in the wavefront. This modified wavefront is relayed to the illumination arm, where a second objective (OBJ2), pupil-matched to OBJ1, forms an aberration-free focus at a correspondingly shifted axial position in the sample space.

Two distinct implementations of the reflective element are disclosed: (1) a **step mirror** with discrete height levels, enabling rapid switching between a finite number of predefined axial planes; and (2) a **tilted planar mirror**, which, when combined with a slightly offset alignment of OBJ1, allows continuous axial scanning over a defined range. In the step mirror configuration, each lateral position of the scanner corresponds to a specific step, and thus a specific axial focus. In the tilted mirror configuration, lateral scanning across the inclined surface produces a linear relationship between scan angle and axial displacement, enabling smooth, continuous Z-scanning.

The invention exploits the high bandwidth of commercially available lateral scanners—including resonant galvanometric mirrors operating at 12 kHz or higher—to achieve axial re-focusing rates far exceeding those of conventional mechanical or wavefront-modulation approaches. Importantly, because the system operates in a conjugate-plane remote focusing geometry with matched pupils, it inherently compensates for spherical and other monochromatic aberrations, maintaining diffraction-limited performance throughout the scan range.

Experimental validation demonstrates axial scanning at 12 kHz with near-diffraction-limited resolution (lateral FWHM ≈ 370 nm, axial FWHM ≈ 2 µm at 920 nm excitation) over a 55 µm range in two-photon microscopy. In light-sheet microscopy, the invention accelerates axially swept light-sheet microscopy (ASLM) by an order of magnitude, achieving volumetric imaging at 3.5 Hz with isotropic ~400 nm resolution. Biological applications include tracking of rapidly diffusing nanoparticles in live cells and high-frame-rate imaging of the beating zebrafish heart in cross-sectional (XZ) and volumetric modes.

The system is fully compatible with existing scanning technologies and can be integrated into both widefield and point-scanning microscopes. Furthermore, because the axial scan waveform is dictated solely by the drive signal to the lateral scanner, the invention supports arbitrary scan patterns—including resonant sinusoidal, triangular, or user-defined trajectories—enabling flexible trade-offs between speed, range, and duty cycle. Intensity uniformity across focal planes can be passively optimized by adjusting scan amplitude or using spatial masks, without requiring synchronized laser modulation.

Thus, the invention overcomes the long-standing trade-offs between speed, resolution, and scan range in axial focusing, providing a scalable platform for high-speed volumetric imaging in neuroscience, developmental biology, cardiology, and beyond.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Experimental Setup

The optical system of the present invention comprises two orthogonally arranged arms connected via a shared beam path, forming a dual-objective remote focusing architecture. The first arm, referred to as the **remote-focusing arm**, contains a lateral scanning device (e.g., a galvanometric mirror or resonant scanner), a first objective lens (OBJ1), and a reflective element (either a step mirror or a tilted planar mirror) positioned at or near the focal plane of OBJ1. The second arm, the **illumination arm**, houses a second objective lens (OBJ2) that is pupil-matched to OBJ1 and is used to form the final focus in the sample space. Both arms are linked by a 4F relay system consisting of two telecentric tube lenses, ensuring that the back focal planes of OBJ1 and OBJ2 are conjugate and that the lateral scanner is imaged onto both back apertures.

In operation, a collimated laser beam—continuous-wave or pulsed, depending on the application—is introduced into the system via a polarizing beam splitter. The beam is directed into the remote-focusing arm, where it is incident on the lateral scanner. Deflection of the scanner translates the beam angle, which, after passage through the first tube lens and OBJ1, results in a lateral displacement of the focal spot in the front focal plane of OBJ1. This focal spot is then reflected by the reflective element back through OBJ1 and the first tube lens, returning as a converging, diverging, or collimated beam depending on the axial position of the reflective surface relative to the nominal focus.

Crucially, the returning beam retraces its path through the lateral scanner, which now acts to descan the lateral motion. Because the scanner imparts equal but opposite angular deviations on the forward and return paths, the net lateral displacement at the output is canceled, leaving only a net axial phase shift encoded in the wavefront curvature. This phase-modulated beam is then relayed through the second tube lens into the illumination arm, where it is focused by OBJ2 into the sample. Due to the precise pupil matching between OBJ1 and OBJ2—achieved by selecting appropriate magnifications and numerical apertures—the system ensures that any defocus introduced in the remote arm is faithfully reproduced in the sample arm without introducing spherical aberrations.

For discrete axial stepping, the reflective element is a **microfabricated step mirror** with precisely defined height levels. Each step corresponds to a specific axial offset relative to the nominal focal plane of OBJ1. When the lateral scanner positions the focus on a given step, the reflected wavefront acquires a curvature proportional to the step height, resulting in a corresponding axial shift of the focus formed by OBJ2. The number of steps is limited by the field of view of OBJ1 and the minimum resolvable lateral feature size of the scanner, but in practice, three to ten steps can be readily implemented. Step heights are chosen based on the desired axial spacing and the optical parameters of the system, with typical values ranging from 5 to 10 µm for high-NA objectives.

For continuous axial scanning, the step mirror is replaced by a **planar mirror tilted at a small angle** (e.g., 5°–10°) relative to the optical axis. To ensure normal incidence and optimal wavefront return, OBJ1 is slightly translated perpendicular to the optical axis, creating a tilted focal line that is orthogonal to the mirror surface. Lateral scanning across this inclined plane causes the focus to move off the mirror surface, introducing a continuously variable defocus. The axial scan range scales approximately with the product of the lateral field of view and the tangent of the tilt angle. For example, with a 7.5° tilt and a 1 mm lateral scan range, an axial displacement of ~130 µm is theoretically possible, though practical limits due to field curvature and vignetting typically restrict usable ranges to 40–60 µm in current implementations.

The system is designed to be modular and adaptable to various imaging modalities. In light-sheet microscopy, a cylindrical lens is inserted into the input beam path to generate a thin sheet of light, which is then scanned axially using the tilted mirror configuration. In two-photon raster-scanning microscopy, the system is integrated into the excitation path, with the lateral scanner in the remote arm synchronized with the primary XY scanners to enable volumetric imaging. All optical components are selected for high transmission at the relevant wavelengths (e.g., 488 nm for confocal/ASLM, 920 nm for two-photon), and anti-reflection coatings are used throughout to minimize losses.

Alignment is critical to performance. The pupils of OBJ1 and OBJ2 must be matched within a few percent to avoid residual aberrations. This is accomplished by choosing objectives with compatible numerical apertures and using relay lenses with appropriate focal lengths to achieve the required demagnification (typically ~1.33 for air-to-water immersion transitions). The reflective element must be precisely positioned at the focal plane of OBJ1, and for the tilted mirror, the lateral offset of OBJ1 must be calibrated to ensure retroreflection. These adjustments are performed using iterative optimization based on point spread function measurements.

The invention has been experimentally validated using both standard galvanometric scanners (non-resonant, <1 kHz) and resonant scanners (12 kHz). In all cases, the axial focus remained diffraction-limited across the scan range, with lateral resolutions of ~370 nm and axial resolutions of ~2 µm in two-photon mode. Volumetric imaging of live zebrafish hearts and intracellular nanoparticles demonstrates the system’s capability to capture rapid biological dynamics with high spatiotemporal fidelity. The technology is extensible to even faster scanners, including acousto-optic deflectors, suggesting potential for MHz-rate axial focusing in future implementations.
