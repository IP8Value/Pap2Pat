# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Synthetic lethality represents a critical concept in molecular biology and therapeutic development, wherein the simultaneous disruption of two nonessential genes results in cellular inviability, while disruption of either gene alone remains compatible with cellular survival. Initially characterized in yeast model systems, synthetic lethality has emerged as a powerful paradigm for understanding genetic interactions and developing targeted therapeutic strategies, particularly in oncology. The fundamental principle underlying synthetic lethality offers a compelling approach to cancer treatment by exploiting the unique genetic vulnerabilities present in malignant cells while sparing healthy tissues. In cancer cells harboring specific mutations or genetic alterations, targeting a complementary gene through pharmacological intervention can selectively induce cell death, thereby providing a mechanism for tumor-specific cytotoxicity without affecting normal cells that maintain functional copies of both genes.

Despite its therapeutic potential, comprehensive mapping of synthetic lethal interactions in humans presents formidable challenges. The sheer scale of possible gene pair combinations—exceeding 200 million potential pairs in a single cellular context—renders systematic experimental evaluation practically impossible. This experimental burden is compounded by ethical considerations that preclude direct interrogation of synthetic lethality in human subjects, as well as the inherent difficulty in distinguishing context-specific synthetic lethal interactions from global ones using standard experimental cell lines. Furthermore, synthetic lethal relationships exhibit significant variability across different cellular contexts, with gene pairs demonstrating synthetic lethality in one cell type potentially remaining viable in another. This context-dependency, while offering opportunities for tissue-specific therapeutic targeting, also complicates efforts to establish comprehensive synthetic lethal maps applicable across diverse biological scenarios.

Previous approaches to predicting synthetic lethality have primarily relied on genetic homology between model organisms and humans, operating under the assumption that synthetic lethal relationships are conserved between orthologous gene pairs. However, this methodology suffers from significant limitations. First, the number of genes with clear homologous relationships between commonly studied model organisms like Saccharomyces cerevisiae and humans is relatively small, accounting for only approximately 1% of all possible human gene pairs. This leaves the vast majority of potential synthetic lethal interactions without predictive data based on homology alone. Second, evolutionary processes including gene duplication events and the development of species-specific genetic redundancies can fundamentally alter synthetic lethal relationships between orthologous genes. Over hundreds of millions of years of divergent evolution, these genetic changes may introduce functional redundancies that either create new synthetic lethal relationships or eliminate existing ones, rendering simple homology-based predictions insufficiently accurate.

Alternative approaches have explored structural similarity using protein structure families, domain similarity through protein domain analysis, and functional similarity employing gene ontology annotations. While these methods provide valuable insights into potential genetic relationships, they remain limited in their ability to comprehensively predict synthetic lethality across diverse biological contexts. Additionally, network-based models utilizing information centrality have been proposed, but these univariate approaches fail to capture the complex multivariate relationships that characterize synthetic lethal interactions. The fundamental limitation of these existing methodologies lies in their reliance on direct sequence, structure, or function comparisons, which cannot adequately account for the emergent properties of biological networks and the complex interdependencies that govern cellular viability.

The development of more sophisticated computational approaches capable of leveraging the rich information contained within biological networks represents a critical need in the field. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks, in particular, offer a promising framework for understanding genetic relationships beyond simple homology or functional similarity. These networks capture the complex web of physical interactions between proteins, reflecting the functional organization of cellular processes and potentially revealing patterns indicative of synthetic lethal relationships. However, effectively translating knowledge from well-characterized model organisms to less-studied species, including humans, requires novel computational methodologies that can bridge the gap between different biological systems while accounting for species-specific network characteristics and evolutionary divergence.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel computational methodology designated as Species-INdependent TRAnslation (SINaTRA) for predicting synthetic lethal relationships across diverse species, including humans, using only experimentally derived protein-protein interaction (PPI) network data. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on genetic homology, structural similarity, or functional annotations, SINaTRA introduces the concept of connectivity homology—a measure of relatedness between genes based on their network connectivity profiles rather than their sequence, structure, or annotated function. This innovative approach enables the prediction of synthetic lethal interactions in any species for which PPI network data is available, without requiring prior knowledge of experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs in the target species.

The core innovation of SINaTRA lies in its ability to identify and quantify connectivity patterns that are indicative of synthetic lethal relationships through supervised machine learning trained on well-characterized model organisms, particularly Saccharomyces cerevisiae. The methodology represents each gene as a vector of network parameters derived from graph-theoretic properties such as degree centrality, betweenness centrality, and shortest path length, among others. Gene pairs are similarly represented by vectors incorporating both individual gene connectivity profiles and pairwise network parameters. Through rank normalization of these network parameters, SINaTRA enables meaningful comparison of connectivity patterns across species with vastly different network topologies and scales, effectively translating knowledge from source species to target species.

The invention demonstrates superior performance compared to existing methodologies, achieving significantly higher area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values and positive predictive values (PPV) when predicting synthetic lethal interactions in Schizosaccharomyces pombe using models trained on S. cerevisiae data. Specifically, SINaTRA achieves an AUC of 0.86 compared to 0.60 for homology-based methods and substantially outperforms structural similarity (AUC=0.62), domain similarity (AUC=0.56), and functional similarity (AUC=0.81) approaches. At a SINaTRA score threshold of 0.85, the methodology achieves a PPV of approximately 7%, representing a 70-fold improvement over the expected baseline rate of 0.1% for synthetic lethal interactions in diploid organisms.

The robustness of SINaTRA extends to species with incomplete PPI networks, maintaining high predictive performance even when network completeness is reduced to levels comparable to those of human and mouse PPI networks. The methodology successfully predicts synthetic lethal interactions in Mus musculus despite the scarcity of experimentally validated mouse synthetic lethal pairs, achieving an AUC of 0.937. When applied to human PPI networks, SINaTRA generates probabilistic scores between 0 and 1 for all possible human gene pairs, providing a comprehensive resource for identifying potential synthetic lethal relationships relevant to human health and disease.

The invention further incorporates post-processing steps to enhance prediction accuracy by filtering out likely false positives using population genetic variation data. By analyzing severe, tolerated, homozygous, deleterious co-mutations from large-scale genomic databases, the methodology identifies gene pairs that are unlikely to be synthetic lethal due to their co-occurrence in healthy human populations. This filtering process reduces the false discovery rate to 0.36% at a SINaTRA score threshold of 0.85, significantly enhancing the reliability of predictions for experimental validation.

The practical applications of SINaTRA extend to the identification of novel cancer combination therapies through the discovery of "hot zones" of high-scoring synthetic lethal pairs involving established drug targets. The methodology reveals significant enrichment of high SINaTRA scores among gene pairs targeted by cancer drugs, with median scores increasing progressively from single-drug targets to combination therapy targets. This capability enables the rational design of polypharmacological interventions that exploit synthetic lethal relationships specific to cancer cells, potentially leading to more effective and selective therapeutic strategies with reduced off-target effects.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### EXAMPLE 1

The implementation of the SINaTRA methodology begins with the construction of comprehensive protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks for both source and target species using experimentally derived interaction data from established databases such as BioGRID. For the source species, typically Saccharomyces cerevisiae due to its extensive characterization of synthetic lethal interactions, the PPI network is constructed by representing each gene as a node and each experimentally verified physical interaction between protein products as an edge. The network is pruned to contain a single connected component to ensure meaningful connectivity analysis across all nodes. Similarly, PPI networks are constructed for target species, including Schizosaccharomyces pombe, Mus musculus, and Homo sapiens, using available experimental interaction data.

Once the PPI networks are established, a comprehensive set of network parameters is calculated for each gene and gene pair within each species. Each gene is represented by a vector of eight single-node network parameters, including degree centrality (the number of direct connections a node has), betweenness centrality (the extent to which a node lies on paths between other nodes), closeness centrality (the average shortest path length to all other nodes), eigenvector centrality (the influence of a node based on the influence of its neighbors), clustering coefficient (the degree to which neighbors of a node are connected to each other), eccentricity (the maximum shortest path length to any other node), PageRank (a measure of node importance based on link structure), and k-core number (the maximum subgraph where each node has at least k connections). Additionally, each gene pair is represented by a vector of four node-pair parameters: inverse shortest path length (the reciprocal of the shortest path between two nodes), communicability (a measure of the total number of walks between two nodes), shared neighbors (the number of common neighbors between two nodes), and shared non-neighbors (the number of nodes not connected to either of the two nodes).

The calculation of these network parameters is performed using established graph theory libraries such as NetworkX in Python, ensuring computational efficiency and accuracy. For parameters requiring adjacency matrix operations, such as shared neighbors and shared non-neighbors, specialized matrix computations are implemented to handle the large-scale nature of PPI networks efficiently. The resulting parameter vectors for individual genes and gene pairs form the foundation for subsequent connectivity homology analysis and machine learning model training.

To address the significant differences in network topology and scale between species, a rank normalization procedure is applied to all network parameters. This normalization method involves ranking all calculated values for each parameter from smallest to largest within each species, resolving ties randomly, and then dividing each rank by the total number of genes (for single-node parameters) or gene pairs (for node-pair parameters). This transformation ensures that all parameter values fall within the range [0,1], making them directly comparable across species with vastly different network characteristics. The rank normalization approach is particularly effective because it preserves the relative ordering of nodes and gene pairs within each species while enabling meaningful cross-species comparisons.

The normalized parameter vectors are then used to train supervised machine learning models, specifically random forest classifiers, using experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs from the source species as positive training examples and randomly selected non-synthetic lethal pairs as negative examples. The random forest algorithm is chosen for its robustness to overfitting, ability to handle high-dimensional data, and capacity to capture complex nonlinear relationships between network parameters and synthetic lethal status. The training process involves five-fold cross-validation to ensure model generalizability and prevent overfitting to the training data. During training, the algorithm learns to identify patterns in connectivity profiles that are indicative of synthetic lethal relationships, effectively encoding the "connectivity signature" of synthetic lethality.

Once trained, the model is applied to the normalized parameter vectors of the target species to generate SINaTRA scores for all possible gene pairs. These scores represent the probability that a given gene pair exhibits a synthetic lethal relationship, with values ranging from 0 (predicted non-synthetic lethal) to 1 (predicted synthetic lethal). The application of the trained model to target species requires no prior knowledge of synthetic lethal interactions in the target species, making SINaTRA truly species-independent in its translation capability.

The methodology's performance is rigorously evaluated using multiple metrics, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), positive predictive value (PPV), and odds ratios. Cross-validation procedures ensure that performance estimates are robust and representative of the model's true predictive capability. Additionally, the contribution of individual network parameters to model performance is assessed through feature importance analysis, providing insights into which connectivity patterns are most strongly associated with synthetic lethal relationships.

### EXAMPLE 2

The validation of SINaTRA's predictive capabilities is demonstrated through comprehensive testing in Schizosaccharomyces pombe, a species with sufficient experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs to enable rigorous performance assessment. The methodology is trained on S. cerevisiae data, which contains over 13,000 experimentally validated synthetic lethal pairs, and then applied to predict synthetic lethal interactions in S. pombe, which has approximately 628 known synthetic lethal pairs. This cross-species validation provides a stringent test of the methodology's ability to translate knowledge between evolutionarily divergent species while maintaining high predictive accuracy.

The performance comparison between SINaTRA and existing methodologies reveals significant advantages of the connectivity homology approach. When using untranslated network parameters (raw values without normalization), the predictive performance is poor, achieving an AUC of only 0.67. However, after applying rank normalization to enable cross-species comparison, the AUC improves dramatically to 0.86, representing a substantial enhancement in predictive capability. This improvement is accompanied by a significant increase in precision, rising from 50% to 98% at a recall rate of 30% in the testing data. The reciprocal validation, where the model is trained on S. pombe data and applied to S. cerevisiae, similarly demonstrates improved performance with rank normalization (AUC=0.74) compared to untranslated parameters (AUC=0.67).

Direct comparison with alternative methodologies further highlights SINaTRA's superior performance. Genetic homology-based approaches achieve an AUC of 0.60 when restricted to gene pairs with known homologous relationships between S. cerevisiae and S. pombe, but this performance degrades to an AUC of 0.52 when considering all possible gene pairs due to the limited coverage of homologous genes. Structural similarity based on SCOP protein classifications achieves an AUC of 0.62, while domain similarity using PFam data reaches an AUC of 0.56. Functional similarity based on Gene Ontology annotations performs better with an AUC of 0.81, but still falls short of SINaTRA's 0.86 AUC. Pairwise information centrality, a univariate network-based approach, shows no significant predictive performance with an AUC of 0.46.

The practical implications of these performance differences become particularly evident when examining positive predictive values (PPVs) at various score thresholds. At a SINaTRA score threshold of 0.85, the PPV reaches approximately 7%, representing a 70-fold improvement over the expected baseline rate of 0.1% for synthetic lethal interactions in diploid organisms. This increases to 17% at a cutoff of 0.95, corresponding to a 170-fold improvement. In contrast, the untranslated method achieves a PPV of 17% only at a much lower cutoff of 0.65, with performance deteriorating sharply at higher thresholds. Other methodologies, including genetic homology, structural similarity, and functional similarity, fail to produce any gene pairs with scores exceeding 0.05, severely limiting their utility for high-confidence predictions.

The methodology's ability to identify previously unknown synthetic lethal pairs is demonstrated through estimation of missing interactions in S. pombe. At a SINaTRA score threshold of 0.85, the model predicts 177 synthetic lethal pairs, of which only 65 have been experimentally identified, suggesting the existence of 112 previously unknown interactions. With 1,759 gene pairs scoring above this threshold, the expected hit rate is 1 in 15, providing a highly enriched candidate list for experimental validation. This enrichment is further enhanced when considering gene pairs within the same protein complex, where the PPV reaches 0.27 at a cutoff of 0.95, corresponding to an odds ratio of 148.4.

The robustness of SINaTRA to variations in network completeness is tested by systematically ablating the S. pombe network to densities ranging from 90% to 50% of the original network. This ablation simulates the incomplete nature of PPI networks in less-studied species, including humans and mice. The results show that while untranslated parameters exhibit poor performance across all ablation levels (AUCs between 0.43 and 0.60), rank-normalized SINaTRA maintains high predictive performance even at 50% network density, with AUC decreasing only from 0.86 to 0.79. This robustness ensures that the methodology remains effective even when applied to species with incomplete PPI data.

Additional validation analyses confirm that SINaTRA's predictions are not driven by node popularity bias, where highly connected or frequently studied genes might artificially inflate prediction scores. While SINaTRA scores correlate with node degree and popularity (frequency of appearance in databases), they show no correlation with normalized popularity (degree divided by popularity). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) confirms that predictive performance is independent of degree, popularity, and normalized popularity measures, ensuring that predictions reflect genuine connectivity patterns rather than research bias.

### EXAMPLE 3

The application of SINaTRA to human synthetic lethality prediction represents the culmination of the methodology's development, providing a comprehensive resource for biomedical research and therapeutic development. The human PPI network, constructed from experimentally derived interaction data, contains 14,820 proteins and enables the generation of SINaTRA scores for 109.8 million possible gene pairs. Each gene pair receives a probabilistic score between 0 and 1, indicating the likelihood of a synthetic lethal relationship based on connectivity patterns learned from S. cerevisiae data.

To enhance the reliability of human predictions, a post-processing step filters out likely false positives using population genetic variation data from large-scale genomic databases. Specifically, the methodology identifies gene pairs where both genes harbor severe, tolerated, homozygous, deleterious co-mutations in healthy human populations. These co-mutations, defined as homozygous deleterious mutations (nonsense mutations, early stop codons, or loss of start codons) occurring simultaneously in at least one patient but no more than 5% of patients in either the 1000 Genomes project or the Sweden-Schizophrenia Population-Based Case-Control Exome Sequencing study, indicate that the gene pair is unlikely to be synthetic lethal since individuals carrying both mutations remain viable. This filtering process identifies 450,010 gene pairs (0.4% of all possible pairs) that are confidently classified as non-synthetic lethal.

The effectiveness of this filtering approach is demonstrated by the significantly lower SINaTRA scores of filtered gene pairs compared to the general population of gene pairs (median score of 0.116 versus 0.122, p ≤ 2.2e-16). At a SINaTRA score threshold of 0.85, the false discovery rate from this filtering is only 0.36%, with 61 false positives among 16,886 predicted true positives. This high-confidence filtering enables the provision of a curated list of 1,309 predicted human synthetic lethal pairs with SINaTRA scores greater than 0.95, along with the complete database of 109,358,780 gene pairs and their corresponding SINaTRA scores.

The biological relevance of SINaTRA predictions is validated through multiple lines of evidence. First, predicted synthetic lethal pairs show significant enrichment for intra-pathway interactions according to KEGG pathway annotations. Gene pairs with SINaTRA scores ≥0.95, 0.90, and 0.80 all demonstrate significant enrichment for sharing the same pathway compared to randomly selected pairs (p<2.2e-16 for all cutoffs). This finding aligns with established biological understanding that synthetic lethal interactions often occur between genes functioning in the same biological pathway or process.

Second, protein complexes are significantly enriched for high-scoring synthetic lethal pairs. Analysis of mammalian protein complexes from the CORUM database reveals that genes whose products participate in the same protein complex have significantly higher SINaTRA scores than expected by chance (p<2.2e-16). This enrichment holds true for complexes of various sizes, with the proportion of within-complex gene pairs having high SINaTRA scores being significantly higher than expected across all score cutoffs (p<0.0001). This observation supports the biological hypothesis that protein complexes may tolerate single deleterious mutations but become non-functional when multiple components are simultaneously disrupted.

The methodology's ability to identify context-specific synthetic lethal interactions is demonstrated through integration with tissue-specific gene expression data from the Human Protein Atlas. By filtering out gene pairs where both genes are not expressed in specific tissues or cell lines, the approach generates context-specific synthetic lethal predictions. This analysis reveals significant deviations from expected filtering rates in certain tissues, with rectal tissue showing fewer filtered pairs than expected (OR=0.477, p=1.6e-5) and small intestine tissue showing more filtered pairs than expected (OR=2.11, p<2.2e-16). These findings suggest tissue-specific differences in susceptibility to synthetic lethal interactions, potentially reflecting varying levels of functional redundancy or alternative pathway activation.

Comparison with previously published human synthetic lethal predictions further validates SINaTRA's performance. Gene pairs from the Syn-Lethality database and DAISY method show significantly higher SINaTRA scores than randomly selected pairs (p<2.2e-16 for both datasets). The methodology achieves AUCs of 0.93 and 0.73 for predicting membership in the Syn-Lethality and DAISY datasets, respectively, substantially outperforming homology-based approaches (AUC=0.50 for both) and matching or exceeding functional similarity approaches.

The therapeutic implications of SINaTRA predictions are particularly evident in the identification of novel cancer combination therapy opportunities. Analysis of high-scoring gene pairs (SINaTRA≥0.85) involving established drug targets reveals 58 unique genes targeted by 68 unique cancer drugs. These gene pairs show significantly higher SINaTRA scores than non-targeted pairs, with median scores increasing progressively from single-drug targets (0.156) to combination therapy targets (0.283). Visual clustering of these interactions reveals "hot zones" of high SINaTRA scores that correspond to active areas of combination therapy development, with Area 1 enriched for genes involved in gene expression, transcription initiation, and steroid hormone receptor activity, and Area 2 associated with protein autophosphorylation.

The comprehensive landscape of human synthetic lethality, mapped using Reactome pathway annotations, reveals that 73% of high-confidence predicted interactions occur within the same biological pathway, while 27% represent between-pathway interactions. Within-pathway interactions are particularly prominent in apoptosis, immune system, and gene expression pathways, with the immune system showing the highest number of associated synthetic lethal pairs (101). Between-pathway interactions, while less frequent, may represent novel biological insights or previously unidentified functional relationships between seemingly distinct biological processes.

Function-specific analysis of synthetic lethal mechanisms reveals distinct patterns across different biological categories. Immune system and signal transduction pathways show significant enrichment for "parallel" synthetic lethal relationships, where genes function in the same pathway without direct interaction. In contrast, gene expression, meiosis, chromatin organization, and DNA repair pathways are enriched for "complex" relationships, where gene products participate in the same protein complex. These findings provide mechanistic insights into how synthetic lethality operates across different biological contexts and support the development of function-specific therapeutic strategies.
