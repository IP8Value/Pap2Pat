# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The intestinal epithelium serves as a critical interface between the external environment and the internal milieu of the host, functioning not only as a conduit for nutrient absorption but also as a robust barrier against luminal pathogens, toxins, and microbial antigens. This barrier is maintained through a multi-layered defense system that includes a mucus layer secreted by goblet cells, tight junctions that seal the paracellular space between adjacent epithelial cells, and a dense network of resident immune cells that surveil and respond to potential threats. Disruption of this barrier—commonly referred to as “intestinal barrier dysfunction” or “leaky gut”—has been increasingly implicated in a wide array of pathological conditions, including inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic syndrome, neurodegenerative disorders, autoimmune diseases, and even the aging process itself. The integrity of the intestinal epithelium is therefore not merely a local concern but a systemic determinant of health and longevity.

A growing body of evidence suggests that oxidative stress plays a central role in the pathogenesis of intestinal barrier dysfunction. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly superoxide and its derivative hydrogen peroxide, are natural byproducts of mitochondrial respiration. Under physiological conditions, these molecules are tightly regulated by endogenous antioxidant systems such as superoxide dismutases (SOD1 in the cytosol, SOD2 in the mitochondria) and catalase. However, under conditions of metabolic overload—such as those induced by high-nutrient diets—mitochondrial electron transport can become dysregulated, leading to excessive ROS production. Among the various sites of mitochondrial ROS generation, complex III of the electron transport chain, specifically at the outer ubiquinone-binding site (designated site IIIQo), has been identified as a major source of superoxide with high capacity for both matrix and cytosolic release. This site is uniquely positioned to influence redox signaling across cellular compartments, thereby modulating pathways that affect cell survival, inflammation, and barrier integrity.

Despite the recognized importance of oxidative stress in intestinal pathology, conventional approaches to mitigating ROS—such as the use of broad-spectrum antioxidants—have yielded inconsistent or even detrimental outcomes in clinical trials. This is largely attributed to the non-specific nature of such compounds, which indiscriminately scavenge ROS regardless of their subcellular origin or signaling function. Consequently, there is a pressing need for targeted interventions that selectively inhibit pathological ROS production without disrupting essential redox signaling. In this context, the development of site-specific suppressors of mitochondrial electron leak—namely, the S3QELs (Suppressors of site IIIQo Electron Leak)—represents a significant advance. These small molecules selectively inhibit superoxide production at site IIIQo of complex III without impairing normal electron flow or oxidative phosphorylation, thereby offering a precise tool to dissect the causal role of this specific ROS source in disease mechanisms.

Prior studies have demonstrated that dietary composition profoundly influences intestinal barrier integrity and organismal lifespan. In model organisms such as *Drosophila melanogaster*, increasing dietary yeast extract (YE)—a proxy for protein and nutrient density—accelerates age-related intestinal permeability, increases enterocyte apoptosis, stimulates compensatory stem cell proliferation, and shortens median lifespan. Conversely, dietary restriction ameliorates these phenotypes. These observations suggest a direct link between nutrient intake, intestinal homeostasis, and longevity. However, the molecular mechanisms connecting high-nutrient diets to barrier failure remain incompletely understood. While elevated oxidative stress has been observed in the intestines of animals fed rich diets, the specific mitochondrial sources of ROS and their downstream effectors have not been definitively established.

Furthermore, although mouse models of high-fat diet-induced metabolic dysfunction recapitulate many features of human metabolic syndrome—including obesity, insulin resistance, and intestinal permeability—the extent to which mitochondrial ROS from site IIIQo contributes to these phenotypes in mammals has not been systematically investigated. The conservation of mitochondrial structure and function across species raises the possibility that findings in *Drosophila* may be translatable to mammals, but this requires empirical validation. Additionally, potential downstream mediators of ROS-induced barrier dysfunction, such as hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (Hif-1α), have been implicated in intestinal pathology, but their causal relationship to mitochondrial ROS remains speculative.

In light of these gaps, there exists a compelling need for a therapeutic strategy that targets the root cause of diet-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction by selectively inhibiting pathological ROS production at its source. The present invention addresses this unmet need by demonstrating that S3QELs—small-molecule inhibitors of superoxide production at site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III—effectively prevent and reverse intestinal barrier disruption in both invertebrate and mammalian models of overnutrition. Moreover, the invention establishes a mechanistic link between mitochondrial ROS, Hif-1α stabilization, and barrier failure, providing a rational basis for the development of novel pharmaceutical compositions and treatment regimens for a spectrum of conditions associated with intestinal hyperpermeability.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides methods and compositions for preventing, treating, or ameliorating intestinal barrier dysfunction by administering a therapeutically effective amount of a compound that selectively inhibits superoxide production at site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III. Such compounds, herein referred to as S3QELs, include but are not limited to S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3, which are structurally distinct small molecules that share the common mechanism of suppressing electron leak at the outer ubiquinone-binding site of complex III without inhibiting normal mitochondrial respiration or ATP synthesis. The invention further encompasses the use of these compounds in the treatment of conditions characterized by increased intestinal permeability, including but not limited to celiac disease, ulcerative colitis, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and other disorders wherein barrier dysfunction contributes to pathogenesis or progression.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method of reducing intestinal permeability in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering a pharmaceutical composition comprising an S3QEL. The method is effective in subjects suffering from or at risk of developing conditions associated with diet-induced metabolic stress, aging, or chronic inflammation. In exemplary embodiments, the subject is a human, and the S3QEL is administered orally, allowing for direct exposure to intestinal epithelial cells with minimal systemic absorption, thereby maximizing local efficacy while minimizing off-target effects.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method of extending healthspan or lifespan in a subject by preserving intestinal barrier integrity through the administration of an S3QEL. This method is particularly applicable in contexts of overnutrition, where high-calorie or high-protein diets accelerate intestinal aging and permeability. The invention demonstrates that inhibition of site IIIQo-derived superoxide not only reduces epithelial damage and apoptosis but also attenuates the expression of inflammatory markers and restores the expression of tight junction and mucin genes, thereby reinforcing the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal barrier.

In a further aspect, the invention identifies Hif-1α as a key downstream mediator of site IIIQo-derived superoxide in the pathogenesis of intestinal barrier dysfunction. Genetic knockdown of Hif-1α in intestinal enterocytes mimics the protective effects of S3QELs, while overexpression exacerbates permeability and shortens lifespan. Critically, the efficacy of S3QELs is diminished when Hif-1α is knocked down and enhanced when Hif-1α is overexpressed, indicating that Hif-1α stabilization is a primary mechanism by which mitochondrial ROS from site IIIQo drives barrier failure. This mechanistic insight enables the stratification of patients based on Hif-1α activity and supports the development of combination therapies targeting both ROS production and Hif-1α signaling.

The invention also provides pharmaceutical formulations comprising S3QELs in combination with pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, or diluents, optimized for oral delivery to the gastrointestinal tract. These formulations may be designed for immediate release, delayed release, or targeted delivery to specific intestinal regions (e.g., colon for ulcerative colitis). Additionally, the invention encompasses kits comprising an S3QEL and instructions for use in the treatment of intestinal barrier dysfunction or associated disorders, facilitating clinical implementation and patient compliance.

In summary, the present invention establishes, for the first time, that superoxide generated specifically at site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III is a causative driver of diet-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction in both invertebrate and mammalian models. By selectively inhibiting this source of ROS, S3QELs offer a novel, mechanism-based therapeutic approach to preserve intestinal homeostasis, mitigate systemic inflammation, and potentially extend healthspan in conditions of metabolic excess and aging.

## Definitions

For the purposes of this patent application, the following terms shall have the meanings set forth below:

“Intestinal barrier dysfunction” refers to a pathological state characterized by increased permeability of the intestinal epithelium, resulting in the translocation of luminal contents—including bacteria, bacterial products, toxins, and undigested food antigens—into the underlying lamina propria and systemic circulation. This condition is also commonly referred to as “leaky gut” and is assessed by methods such as the smurf assay in *Drosophila*, FITC-dextran uptake in mice, or fecal albumin detection.

“S3QEL” or “Suppressor of site IIIQo Electron Leak” denotes a class of small-molecule compounds that selectively inhibit the production of superoxide at the outer ubiquinone-binding site (site IIIQo) of mitochondrial complex III without impairing electron transport or oxidative phosphorylation. Exemplary S3QELs include S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3, as described in Orr et al. (2015) and Brand et al. (2016).

“Site IIIQo” refers to the outer ubiquinone-binding pocket of mitochondrial complex III (ubiquinol:cytochrome c oxidoreductase), which is a major site of superoxide production during mitochondrial respiration, particularly under conditions of high membrane potential or substrate overload.

“Therapeutically effective amount” means an amount of a compound sufficient to produce a desired therapeutic effect, such as a reduction in intestinal permeability, attenuation of inflammatory markers, restoration of tight junction protein expression, or extension of healthspan, without causing unacceptable toxicity.

“Pharmaceutical composition” refers to a formulation comprising an active agent (e.g., an S3QEL) together with one or more pharmaceutically acceptable carriers, excipients, diluents, or stabilizers, suitable for administration to a subject.

“Subject” refers to any animal, including humans, non-human primates, rodents, and other mammals, that may benefit from the methods or compositions described herein.

“Healthspan” denotes the period of life spent in good health, free from chronic disease and disability, as opposed to lifespan, which refers solely to the duration of life.

“Tight junction genes” encompass genes encoding proteins that form the paracellular seals between adjacent epithelial cells, including but not limited to claudins, occludin, and zonula occludens (ZO) proteins. In *Drosophila*, functional equivalents are referred to as septate junction genes.

“Mucin genes” refer to genes encoding gel-forming glycoproteins secreted by goblet cells to form the protective mucus layer overlying the intestinal epithelium, such as Muc2 in mammals.

“Hif-1α” or “hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha” is a transcription factor that is stabilized under low oxygen conditions or in response to mitochondrial ROS, and regulates the expression of genes involved in angiogenesis, metabolism, and inflammation. In the context of this invention, Hif-1α is identified as a key downstream effector of site IIIQo-derived superoxide in driving intestinal barrier dysfunction.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Celiac Disease and Ulcerative Colitis (UC)

Celiac disease and ulcerative colitis (UC) are chronic inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract that share a common pathophysiological feature: compromised intestinal barrier integrity. In celiac disease, ingestion of gluten triggers an autoimmune response in genetically predisposed individuals, leading to villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and increased epithelial permeability. Similarly, UC is a form of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by diffuse mucosal inflammation of the colon, epithelial ulceration, and loss of barrier function. In both conditions, the breach of the intestinal barrier permits the entry of luminal antigens and microbes, perpetuating chronic inflammation and tissue damage.

Current therapeutic strategies for these disorders focus primarily on immunosuppression (e.g., corticosteroids, biologics) or dietary modification (gluten-free diet in celiac disease). However, these approaches do not directly address the underlying epithelial defect and often fail to induce complete mucosal healing. The present invention offers a novel therapeutic paradigm by targeting the root cause of barrier dysfunction—mitochondrial oxidative stress originating from site IIIQo of complex III.

Evidence from the research paper demonstrates that high-nutrient diets induce intestinal permeability through a mechanism involving mitochondrial superoxide production, which is effectively blocked by S3QELs. Although celiac disease and UC are not solely driven by diet, they are exacerbated by metabolic stress and oxidative damage. In celiac disease, gluten exposure has been shown to increase mitochondrial ROS production in enterocytes, leading to tight junction disassembly. In UC, inflamed colonic tissue exhibits elevated markers of oxidative stress and reduced expression of mucins and tight junction proteins. The administration of S3QELs, as disclosed herein, would mitigate this oxidative insult at its source, thereby preserving epithelial integrity and reducing antigenic load.

Moreover, the invention’s demonstration that S3QELs restore the expression of tight junction and mucin genes in mice fed a high-fat diet directly supports their applicability in UC, where goblet cell depletion and mucin deficiency are hallmarks of disease. By enhancing Klf4 expression—a transcription factor essential for goblet cell differentiation—S3QELs promote mucus layer regeneration, a critical step in mucosal healing. In celiac disease, where rapid epithelial turnover and apoptosis contribute to barrier loss, the anti-apoptotic effects of S3QELs observed in *Drosophila* and inferred in mammals would help maintain epithelial continuity during gluten exposure.

Thus, the invention provides a targeted, mechanism-based therapy for celiac disease and UC that complements existing immunomodulatory approaches by directly fortifying the intestinal barrier against oxidative damage.

### Parkinson's Disease and Alzheimer's Disease.

Emerging evidence links intestinal barrier dysfunction to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD) and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). The “gut-brain axis” hypothesis posits that increased intestinal permeability allows bacterial endotoxins (e.g., lipopolysaccharide) and pro-inflammatory cytokines to enter systemic circulation, triggering neuroinflammation and accelerating neuronal degeneration. In PD, alpha-synuclein pathology is thought to originate in the gut and propagate to the brain via the vagus nerve. In AD, systemic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation contribute to amyloid-beta deposition and cognitive decline.

The research paper establishes that diet-induced intestinal permeability correlates with reduced lifespan and systemic inflammation, processes that are also central to neurodegeneration. Critically, the invention demonstrates that S3QELs not only reduce intestinal permeability but also lower the expression of inflammatory markers such as antimicrobial peptides and Upd3 (a cytokine homologous to mammalian IL-6). By preventing the leakage of inflammatory mediators from the gut, S3QELs could attenuate the chronic low-grade inflammation that drives neurodegeneration.

Furthermore, mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress are well-documented features of both PD and AD. Neurons are highly dependent on mitochondrial energy production, and ROS-induced damage to mitochondrial DNA and proteins is a key contributor to neuronal death. The invention’s identification of site IIIQo as a major source of pathological ROS suggests that S3QELs may have dual benefits: protecting the intestinal barrier and reducing systemic oxidative stress, thereby indirectly preserving neuronal function. Although the current data focus on intestinal epithelial cells, the conservation of mitochondrial biology implies that S3QELs could also mitigate neuronal ROS if delivered systemically, though the invention emphasizes localized gut action to minimize off-target effects.

In summary, the invention provides a preventive or adjunctive strategy for PD and AD by targeting intestinal barrier dysfunction—a modifiable risk factor that precedes clinical neurodegeneration. By maintaining gut integrity, S3QELs may delay disease onset or slow progression in at-risk individuals.

### Active Agents.

The active agents of the present invention are S3QELs—small-molecule compounds that selectively suppress superoxide production at site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III. These include S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3, which are structurally distinct but share the same mechanism of action. Each compound has been validated in *Drosophila* and murine models to reduce intestinal permeability, decrease enterocyte apoptosis, and extend lifespan without affecting food intake or caloric restriction pathways.

S3QELs are distinguished from general antioxidants and other mitochondrial inhibitors by their specificity: they do not scavenge ROS after formation, nor do they inhibit electron transport or ATP synthesis. Instead, they bind to complex III and prevent the electron leak that generates superoxide at site IIIQo. This precision avoids the disruption of redox signaling necessary for normal physiology, a limitation of conventional antioxidants.

The active agents may be used alone or in combination with other therapeutic agents, such as anti-inflammatory drugs, probiotics, or Hif-1α inhibitors, to enhance efficacy. The invention also contemplates the use of derivatives or analogs of S3QELs that retain the core mechanism of site IIIQo suppression.

### Permeability Assays

The efficacy of the invention is demonstrated using validated assays of intestinal permeability. In *Drosophila*, the smurf assay involves feeding flies a non-absorbable blue dye; permeable intestines allow dye leakage into the hemolymph, turning the fly blue. In mice, permeability is quantified by oral gavage of FITC-dextran followed by measurement of plasma fluorescence, or by detection of fecal albumin, which reflects back-leakage of serum proteins due to barrier failure. These assays provide objective, quantitative endpoints for evaluating therapeutic interventions.

The invention discloses that S3QEL treatment significantly reduces the incidence of smurf flies and lowers plasma FITC-dextran and fecal albumin levels, confirming barrier restoration. These assays are integral to the development and quality control of pharmaceutical formulations described herein.

### Pharmaceutical Formulations.

The invention provides pharmaceutical formulations comprising S3QELs in a form suitable for oral administration. Given the localized action in the gut and minimal systemic absorption, formulations are designed to maximize intestinal exposure. These may include tablets, capsules, powders, or liquid suspensions containing S3QELs in combination with excipients such as microcrystalline cellulose, lactose, or hydroxypropyl methylcellulose. Enteric coatings may be employed to target release in the distal small intestine or colon, particularly for UC treatment.

Dosage ranges are determined based on preclinical data, with effective concentrations in the low micromolar range (e.g., 0.08–8 μM in *Drosophila*, equivalent doses in mammals). The formulations are stable, non-toxic, and compatible with chronic administration.

### Kits.

The invention further encompasses kits comprising one or more S3QELs, packaged with instructions for use in the treatment of intestinal barrier dysfunction or associated disorders. The kits may include multiple dosage forms, patient diaries, and educational materials on diet and gut health. Such kits facilitate clinical adoption and ensure proper usage.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

*Drosophila melanogaster* strains w1118 and Canton S were maintained on standard cornmeal-agar medium. For experiments, flies were switched to defined diets containing 0.5%, 2.5%, or 5% yeast extract (YE) at day 3 post-eclosion. S3QELs (S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, S3QEL3) were dissolved in DMSO and added to the diet at final concentrations of 0.08, 0.8, or 8 μM. Control diets contained equivalent DMSO volumes.

Intestinal permeability was assessed using the smurf assay: flies were fed 2.5% blue dye (FD&C Blue No. 1) for 24 hours, then scored for whole-body blue coloration under a dissecting microscope. Apoptotic cells were quantified by acridine orange staining of dissected intestines, and proliferating cells by phospho-histone H3 (PH3) immunofluorescence. Lifespan was monitored daily, with deaths recorded and flies transferred to fresh food every 2–3 days.

Gene expression was analyzed by qRT-PCR from dissected intestines. RNA was extracted using TRIzol, reverse-transcribed, and amplified with gene-specific primers for *Dpt*, *Drs*, *Def*, *upd3*, septate junction genes, *Sod1*, *Sod2*, and *Cat*.

Results showed that 5% YE significantly increased smurf incidence, apoptotic and PH3-positive cells, and decreased median lifespan compared to 0.5% YE. Co-administration of any S3QEL at ≥0.08 μM reduced smurf incidence by ~50%, halved apoptotic cell numbers, and extended lifespan by 10–20%, without affecting food consumption. S3QELs also suppressed the upregulation of inflammatory and antioxidant genes, while enhancing septate junction gene expression.

In genetic experiments, NP1-GAL4-driven RNAi knockdown of *Sod1* or *Sod2* in enterocytes increased permeability and apoptosis and shortened lifespan; these effects were rescued by S3QELs. Similarly, 5966(GS)-GAL4-mediated *Hif-1α* knockdown in enterocytes protected against 5% YE-induced damage, while overexpression worsened it. S3QEL efficacy was enhanced in *Hif-1α*-overexpressing flies and blunted in knockdown flies, confirming Hif-1α as a key mediator.

In parallel, male C57BL/6J mice were fed a 60% high-fat diet (HFD) with or without S3QEL1.2 or S3QEL2.2 (10 mg/kg/day in drinking water). After 12 weeks, intestinal permeability was assessed by FITC-dextran gavage (4 kDa, 600 mg/kg) and plasma fluorescence measurement, and by ELISA for fecal albumin. Tissues were harvested for qRT-PCR of tight junction (*Ocln*, *Cldn1*), mucin (*Muc2*), and *Klf4* genes.

HFD significantly increased plasma FITC-dextran and fecal albumin, and decreased colonic *Muc2* and *Klf4* expression. S3QEL treatment normalized permeability markers and restored gene expression, without altering body weight, food intake, or glucose tolerance. Plasma S3QEL levels were >1000-fold below IC50, confirming local gut action.

These data demonstrate that S3QELs prevent diet-induced intestinal barrier dysfunction across species by inhibiting site IIIQo-derived superoxide and its downstream effector Hif-1α, supporting their therapeutic potential in human diseases.
