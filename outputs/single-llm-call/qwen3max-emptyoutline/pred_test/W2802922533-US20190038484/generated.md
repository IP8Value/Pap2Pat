# DESCRIPTION

## FIELD OF THE INVENTION

The present invention relates generally to systems and methods for enhancing postural stability in individuals with impaired neuromuscular control, and more particularly to a real-time event detection and responsive neuromuscular stimulation system designed for manual wheelchair users with spinal cord injury (SCI). The invention provides an integrated apparatus that utilizes inertial measurement data from a wheelchair-mounted sensor to detect destabilizing events such as collisions and sharp turns, and automatically triggers functional neuromuscular stimulation (FNS) of paralyzed trunk and hip musculature to mitigate postural displacement and facilitate rapid return to an erect seated posture. This technology is especially applicable to individuals with thoracic or cervical SCI who retain sufficient residual neural pathways for electrical activation of lower motor neurons but lack voluntary control over core stabilizing muscles. By coupling threshold-based inertial event classification with precisely timed FNS delivery through either implanted pulse generators (IPGs) or surface electrodes, the invention offers a non-restrictive, user-responsive solution to prevent injurious falls and improve mobility confidence without compromising upper-body function or requiring external mechanical restraints.

## BACKGROUND

Spinal cord injury represents a significant cause of long-term disability worldwide, with approximately 285,000 individuals in the United States alone living with some degree of paralysis resulting from traumatic or non-traumatic damage to the spinal cord. Of these, roughly two-thirds rely on manual wheelchairs or scooters for daily mobility, creating a substantial population vulnerable to secondary complications arising from impaired postural control. Among the most pressing concerns voiced by individuals with SCI is the desire to restore or improve trunk stability, which plays a critical role in maintaining upright sitting posture during both static positioning and dynamic wheelchair maneuvers. Loss of voluntary control over trunk and hip musculature leads to excessive postural sway even during quiet sitting, rendering individuals highly susceptible to destabilization from minor environmental perturbations. Such instability frequently results in injurious falls during wheelchair use, which constitute the leading cause of emergency department visits among this population—accounting for over two-thirds of the more than 100,000 annual wheelchair-related injuries requiring medical intervention. These incidents can result in fractures, lacerations, contusions, abrasions, and in severe cases, death. The rehabilitation burden following a fracture in an individual with SCI typically involves four to eight weeks of hospitalization, during which time muscle atrophy accelerates and the risk of life-threatening complications such as deep vein thrombosis increases significantly. Moreover, the economic impact is considerable, with treatment costs for fall-related injuries commonly ranging between $25,000 and $75,000 per incident.

Current strategies aimed at improving trunk stability for manual wheelchair users with SCI are largely mechanical in nature and suffer from significant limitations that reduce their clinical utility and user acceptance. Common interventions include the use of seat belts to prevent forward trunk collapse, specialized seating cushions designed to counteract posterior pelvic tilt caused by hip and trunk paralysis, increased seat dump angles to leverage gravitational forces in a backward-leaning posture, and lateral trunk supports to restrict side-to-side motion. While these approaches may offer marginal improvements in static stability, they impose substantial functional trade-offs that severely limit user independence and quality of life. Specifically, external restraints and rigid supports dramatically reduce the user’s work volume—the spatial envelope within which they can reach and manipulate objects—thereby impairing performance of essential activities of daily living. Additionally, prolonged contact with restrictive hardware increases the risk of pressure ulcers, skin tears, and other integumentary complications, while also contributing to psychological distress including lowered self-esteem and feelings of infantilization. In extreme cases, improperly fitted restraints have been associated with respiratory compromise and even asphyxiation. Perhaps most critically, compliance with these mechanical stabilization strategies remains persistently low due to their interference with desired voluntary movements and functional tasks, particularly when the wheelchair is stationary and the user wishes to engage in unimpeded upper-body activity.

Power wheelchairs offer somewhat greater flexibility in seating system design, with many models incorporating powered tilt-in-space and recline functions that allow users to independently adjust their posture in anticipation of destabilizing events or to relieve pressure during prolonged sitting. However, these systems still fundamentally rely on external mechanical constraints that restrict natural trunk motion and do not address the underlying neuromuscular deficit. Furthermore, many individuals with SCI express strong reluctance to transition from manual to powered mobility, often perceiving such a change as a symbolic acknowledgment of increased disability severity rather than a pragmatic safety measure. This psychological barrier significantly limits the adoption of power wheelchair solutions even among those who might benefit clinically from their enhanced postural support capabilities.

In contrast to purely mechanical approaches, functional neuromuscular stimulation (FNS) represents a biologically grounded strategy for restoring trunk stability by directly activating otherwise paralyzed musculature through controlled electrical excitation of motor nerves. Prior research has demonstrated that FNS can significantly increase trunk extension moment and multidirectional trunk stiffness in individuals with SCI, thereby enhancing resistance to unexpected destabilizing forces. Electromyographic studies of able-bodied individuals have confirmed that the erector spinae muscles exhibit robust activation in response to anteriorly directed trunk perturbations, suggesting that replicating this natural protective reflex through FNS could provide meaningful stability benefits for paralyzed users. Indeed, continuous activation of hip and trunk extensor muscles via FNS has been shown to effectively maintain upright posture during anteriorly directed force application in individuals with SCI, while also enabling automatic restoration of erect sitting from forward-flexed or laterally deviated positions based on angular thresholds detected by body-worn tilt sensors. Additional investigations involving able-bodied participants subjected to anterior-posterior perturbations while seated in powered wheelchairs have demonstrated that FNS of trunk flexors and extensors can significantly reduce both trunk displacement and velocity during destabilizing events. Moreover, continuous electrical stimulation of trunk and hip extensors has been found to improve manual wheelchair propulsion mechanics by decreasing peak pushrim forces and increasing overall propulsion efficiency, suggesting that FNS may confer dual benefits of enhanced stability and improved mobility performance.

Despite these promising findings, existing FNS applications for trunk stability have largely relied on continuous or posture-threshold-triggered stimulation paradigms that do not specifically address the acute, high-magnitude perturbations characteristic of real-world wheelchair use. Crucially, no prior system has demonstrated the ability to automatically detect and respond to potentially injurious destabilizing events—such as collisions with obstacles or negotiation of sharp turns—through precisely timed FNS delivery calibrated to the specific biomechanical demands of each event type. The development of such an event-responsive system requires not only effective stimulation protocols but also reliable, real-time detection methods capable of distinguishing destabilizing events from normal wheelchair operation with sufficient speed to enable timely muscular response. Inertial measurement units (IMUs) have emerged as promising tools for monitoring wheelchair dynamics, having been successfully employed to track trunk angle in static conditions, classify phases of the manual propulsion cycle, and categorize activities of daily living with clinically useful accuracy rates exceeding 90%. Previous attempts to classify wheelchair instability have combined IMU data with machine learning algorithms to differentiate destabilizing conditions, though these approaches often require extensive training data and computational resources that may be impractical for real-time embedded implementation.

Separate lines of research have focused on modeling the static and dynamic tipping stability of wheelchair-user systems to establish fundamental mechanical limits of safe operation. Mathematical models have been developed to calculate critical velocities for unrecoverable forward tipping during collisions and to predict rollover thresholds during sharp turning maneuvers based on system parameters such as center of gravity location, mass distribution, and geometric configuration. While these modeling studies provide valuable insights into wheelchair design principles and inherent stability boundaries, they remain purely predictive and do not incorporate active intervention mechanisms to assist users in maintaining or regaining stable postures when destabilizing events occur. The critical gap addressed by the present invention lies in bridging these theoretical stability models with practical, real-time intervention capabilities through an integrated detection-stimulation system that operates within the narrow temporal window available for effective postural correction.

The physiological reality that FNS requires approximately 100 milliseconds to generate measurable torque in stimulated muscles imposes strict timing constraints on any event-responsive system, necessitating detection and classification algorithms that can identify destabilizing events with minimal latency. Given that many injurious wheelchair incidents unfold over timescales of several hundred milliseconds or less, the detection system must operate with sufficient speed to allow stimulation onset well before instability reaches an irreversible state. This requirement favors simple, computationally efficient detection strategies over complex pattern recognition algorithms that may introduce unacceptable processing delays. The present invention addresses this challenge through a novel threshold-based approach that leverages distinctive inertial signatures associated with specific destabilizing events—namely, peak anterior-posterior acceleration for collision detection and superior-inferior angular velocity for turn classification—thereby achieving both high detection accuracy and minimal processing latency suitable for real-time implementation.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel system and method for enhancing trunk stability in manual wheelchair users with spinal cord injury through real-time detection of destabilizing events and automatic delivery of functional neuromuscular stimulation to paralyzed trunk and hip musculature. The system comprises a wheelchair-mounted inertial measurement unit configured to continuously monitor tri-axial acceleration and angular velocity of the wheelchair frame, a real-time event detection module implementing threshold-based classification algorithms to identify collision and turning events based on characteristic inertial signatures, and a neuromuscular stimulation subsystem capable of delivering precisely timed electrical stimulation to selected muscle groups through either implanted or surface electrodes. Upon detection of a destabilizing event, the system automatically activates stimulation of appropriate muscle groups—specifically trunk and hip extensors for collision events, and asymmetric activation of quadratus lumborum and hip extensors for turning events—to resist postural displacement and facilitate rapid return to an erect seated posture.

In one aspect, the invention provides a method for detecting wheelchair collisions comprising the steps of continuously measuring anterior-posterior acceleration of the wheelchair using an inertial measurement unit mounted on the wheelchair frame, comparing the measured acceleration against a subject-specific threshold value derived from calibration trials, and classifying an event as a collision when the measured acceleration exceeds the threshold. The subject-specific threshold is preferably calculated as the mean peak absolute anterior-posterior acceleration observed during multiple calibration collision trials minus two standard deviations, thereby ensuring high detection sensitivity while minimizing false positive classifications.

In another aspect, the invention provides a method for detecting sharp wheelchair turns comprising the steps of continuously measuring superior-inferior angular velocity of the wheelchair using the same inertial measurement unit, comparing the measured angular velocity against subject-specific threshold values for left and right turns, and classifying an event as a left or right turn based on both magnitude and sign of the angular velocity relative to the respective thresholds. The turn thresholds are similarly calculated from calibration trials as mean peak absolute superior-inferior angular velocity minus two standard deviations, with positive values indicating left turns and negative values indicating right turns.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a stimulation protocol for collision events wherein maximal stimulation levels are delivered simultaneously to trunk extensor muscles (including lumbar erector spinae and paraspinal muscles) and hip extensor muscles (including gluteus maximus, posterior adductor, and hamstrings) to generate a coordinated extension moment that resists forward trunk flexion and assists in returning to upright sitting. For turning events, the stimulation protocol employs asymmetric muscle activation patterns wherein the quadratus lumborum on the inside of the turn and the hip extensor on the outside of the turn are selectively activated to counteract lateral trunk displacement and maintain pelvic alignment during centrifugal loading.

The invention further provides a real-time implementation architecture wherein the detection algorithms and stimulation control logic are executed on an embedded computing platform that receives inertial data from the wheelchair-mounted sensor, performs threshold comparisons with minimal processing latency, and communicates stimulation commands to an implanted pulse generator or surface stimulator with precise timing coordination. The system is designed to activate stimulation immediately upon threshold crossing and maintain stimulation until the user manually terminates it upon achieving stable upright posture, thereby providing just-in-time assistance without imposing continuous constraints on voluntary movement.

Experimental validation of the invention demonstrates detection accuracy exceeding 90% for both collision and turning events, with average detection delays of 88 milliseconds for collisions and 342 milliseconds for turns. Biomechanical testing with individuals with SCI shows that event-triggered FNS significantly reduces maximum anterior-posterior trunk angle during collision events (from 24.78° to 18.88° for one subject and from 16.10° to 9.92° for another) and significantly decreases return time to erect posture (from 3.94 seconds to 0.73 seconds for one subject). User perception ratings indicate improved feelings of stability during stimulated trials, though the effectiveness of turn-related stimulation was limited by detection latency, suggesting opportunities for algorithm refinement through alternative sensor modalities or predictive event detection strategies.

The invention thus represents a significant advancement over existing approaches by providing an integrated, event-responsive stability enhancement system that operates without external mechanical restraints, preserves upper-body function and work volume, and leverages the user's own musculature to generate protective postural responses. By addressing the specific biomechanical challenges of real-world wheelchair use through targeted, automatically triggered neuromuscular activation, the invention offers a practical pathway to reducing injurious falls, improving mobility confidence, and enhancing quality of life for individuals with spinal cord injury.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention provides a comprehensive system and method for enhancing trunk stability in manual wheelchair users with spinal cord injury through real-time detection of destabilizing events and automatic delivery of functional neuromuscular stimulation. The detailed description that follows outlines the specific components, operational principles, implementation details, and experimental validation of this innovative approach to postural stability enhancement.

The core architecture of the invention consists of three primary subsystems: a sensing subsystem comprising a wheelchair-mounted inertial measurement unit (IMU), a real-time event detection and classification subsystem implementing threshold-based algorithms, and a neuromuscular stimulation subsystem capable of delivering precisely timed electrical stimulation through either implanted or surface electrodes. These subsystems work in concert to create a closed-loop system that monitors wheelchair dynamics, identifies potentially injurious destabilizing events, and automatically triggers appropriate muscular responses to maintain or restore stable upright sitting posture.

The sensing subsystem employs a wireless IMU mounted at the center of the rear cross bar of a standard manual wheelchair. This strategic placement ensures that the sensor captures the fundamental inertial dynamics of the wheelchair-user system while minimizing interference from localized vibrations or component-specific movements. The IMU incorporates a tri-axial accelerometer capable of measuring linear acceleration along anterior-posterior (AP), medial-lateral (ML), and superior-inferior (SI) axes, as well as a tri-axial gyroscope for measuring angular velocity about the same three orthogonal axes. In the preferred embodiment, the accelerometer has a measurement range of ±4g and the gyroscope measures angular velocity up to ±2000 degrees per second, though alternative specifications may be employed depending on the specific application requirements and expected perturbation magnitudes. Both sensors sample data at a rate of 100 Hz, providing sufficient temporal resolution to capture the rapid dynamics characteristic of collision and turning events while maintaining reasonable computational and power requirements for real-time implementation.

The event detection and classification subsystem implements two distinct threshold-based algorithms—one for collision detection and another for turn classification—each designed to identify specific destabilizing events based on their characteristic inertial signatures. The collision detection algorithm focuses exclusively on the AP acceleration channel, recognizing that forward-directed collisions produce a distinctive spike in anterior-posterior deceleration that serves as a reliable indicator of impact events. The turn classification algorithm, by contrast, monitors the SI angular velocity channel, as sharp turning maneuvers generate substantial rotational acceleration about the vertical axis that manifests as high-magnitude angular velocity in the superior-inferior direction. This channel-specific approach simplifies the detection logic while maintaining high classification accuracy, as preliminary testing confirmed that these particular signal features provide unique and repeatable signatures for their respective event types.

Subject-specific threshold calibration represents a critical aspect of the invention's implementation methodology. Rather than employing fixed, population-average thresholds that may not account for individual differences in wheelchair configuration, user mass distribution, or typical operating conditions, the invention utilizes a personalized calibration procedure wherein each user completes multiple baseline trials of each event type under controlled conditions. For collision threshold determination, the user performs twenty controlled collision trials at a standardized velocity (preferably 1.5 m/s as determined through preliminary safety modeling), and the system records the peak absolute AP acceleration value observed during each trial. The collision detection threshold is then calculated as the mean of these twenty peak values minus two standard deviations, establishing a conservative threshold that reliably captures genuine collision events while minimizing false positives from normal wheelchair operation or minor disturbances. Similarly, for turn threshold determination, the user completes twenty controlled left and right turn trials through a standardized turning radius (preferably 63.5 cm corresponding to a 25-inch radius), and the system records the peak absolute SI angular velocity values for each direction. Separate thresholds are established for left and right turns, with the left turn threshold being positive (reflecting counterclockwise rotation when viewed from above) and the right turn threshold being negative (reflecting clockwise rotation), enabling directional discrimination based on signal polarity in addition to magnitude.

The real-time implementation of these detection algorithms occurs within a Simulink-based control environment running on an embedded computing platform with sufficient processing capability to handle the 100 Hz data stream with minimal latency. The control software continuously compares incoming IMU data against the pre-calibrated thresholds, triggering appropriate responses within milliseconds of threshold crossing. Upon detection of a collision event (defined as AP acceleration exceeding the positive collision threshold), the system immediately activates stimulation of trunk and hip extensor muscles according to a predetermined protocol optimized for resisting forward trunk flexion. Upon detection of a left turn event (defined as SI angular velocity exceeding the positive turn threshold), the system activates a different stimulation pattern targeting the left quadratus lumborum and right hip extensor muscles to counteract centrifugal forces pulling the trunk outward during the turn. Conversely, detection of a right turn event (defined as SI angular velocity falling below the negative turn threshold) triggers activation of the right quadratus lumborum and left hip extensor muscles. This directional specificity ensures that the stimulation response is biomechanically appropriate for the particular destabilizing force vector encountered.

The neuromuscular stimulation subsystem can be implemented using either implanted pulse generators (IPGs) with intramuscular or nerve cuff electrodes, or surface stimulation electrodes placed over relevant motor points, depending on the user's clinical status and available infrastructure. In the implanted configuration, which represents the preferred embodiment for long-term clinical deployment, the system utilizes commercially available or custom-designed IPGs capable of delivering asymmetrical charge-balanced current-controlled stimulus waveforms with independent control over pulse amplitude (0-20 mA), pulse duration (0-250 μsec), and stimulation frequency (0-20 Hz) for each stimulation channel. Electrode placement targets key trunk and hip musculature including paraspinal muscles at T12-L2 spinal levels (activated via intramuscular electrodes inserted near the T12-L2 spinal nerves), gluteus maximus and posterior adductor muscles (activated via intramuscular electrodes at motor points), hamstring muscles (similarly activated via intramuscular electrodes), and quadratus lumborum muscles (activated via appropriately positioned intramuscular electrodes). Spiral nerve cuff electrodes may also be employed on femoral nerves to activate quadriceps muscles when needed for additional postural support, though the primary focus remains on trunk and hip extensors for collision response and lateral trunk stabilizers for turning events.

In the surface stimulation configuration, which provides greater accessibility for users without implanted systems, self-adhesive electrode pads are positioned over the motor points of the same target muscles, with stimulation parameters adjusted to achieve comparable muscle activation levels while accounting for the higher impedance and reduced selectivity inherent in transcutaneous stimulation. Both configurations enable the delivery of maximal stimulation intensities immediately upon event detection, with stimulation maintained until the user manually terminates it through a simple switch interface once stable upright posture has been restored. This user-controlled termination mechanism ensures that stimulation is applied only when needed and for the minimum duration required, preventing unnecessary muscle fatigue and preserving battery life while maintaining user autonomy over the assistance provided.

The temporal coordination between event detection and stimulation delivery represents another critical aspect of the invention's design. Given that FNS requires approximately 100 milliseconds to generate measurable torque in stimulated muscles, the detection system must operate with sufficient speed to allow stimulation onset well before postural displacement reaches irreversible levels. Experimental validation demonstrates that the collision detection algorithm achieves an average detection delay of 88 ± 48 milliseconds from actual event onset (as determined by high-speed motion capture), which, when combined with the 100-millisecond neuromuscular response time, still allows for meaningful intervention before maximum trunk displacement occurs. However, the turn detection algorithm exhibits longer average delays of 342 ± 73 milliseconds, which partially explains the reduced effectiveness of turn-related stimulation observed in experimental testing. This performance difference arises from the fundamental distinction between collision events (which produce nearly instantaneous acceleration spikes) and turning events (which involve more gradual angular velocity buildup), highlighting the importance of event-specific algorithm optimization.

The invention's stimulation protocols are carefully designed to match the biomechanical demands of each event type. For collision events, which primarily threaten forward trunk stability through rapid deceleration forces, the protocol emphasizes simultaneous activation of all available trunk and hip extensor muscles to generate a powerful extension moment that resists forward flexion and actively pulls the trunk back toward upright alignment. This includes bilateral activation of erector spinae, paraspinal muscles, gluteus maximus, posterior adductors, and hamstrings, with stimulation intensities set to achieve maximal comfortable contraction levels as determined during individual user calibration sessions. For turning events, which create lateral destabilizing forces through centrifugal acceleration, the protocol employs asymmetric activation patterns that create a corrective torque opposing the direction of trunk lean. During left turns, the left quadratus lumborum (which laterally flexes the trunk to the left) and right hip extensor (which stabilizes the right pelvis against upward rotation) are activated to pull the trunk inward toward the center of the turn. During right turns, the opposite pattern is employed with right quadratus lumborum and left hip extensor activation. This directional specificity ensures that the generated muscular forces directly counteract the specific destabilizing moments created by each turning direction.

Experimental validation of the invention was conducted through a series of controlled laboratory tests involving both crash dummy trials and human subjects with spinal cord injury. Preliminary crash dummy testing established safe experimental parameters by confirming that a collision velocity of 1.5 m/s produces sufficient deceleration (approximately 4g peak AP acceleration) to induce meaningful trunk displacement without causing wheelchair tipping or user injury. This velocity was verified through both mathematical modeling using established wheelchair stability equations and computer simulation in Simwise 4D software, ensuring that human testing would occur within safe operational boundaries. The same testing protocol confirmed that 63.5 cm radius turns at 1.5 m/s produce substantial but non-rollover lateral forces, with peak SI angular velocities consistently exceeding 100 degrees per second.

Human testing involved four participants with chronic spinal cord injuries ranging from C7 to T4 neurological levels, three of whom utilized implanted stimulation systems while one employed surface stimulation due to technical constraints. Each participant completed a comprehensive calibration phase to establish individualized detection thresholds, followed by randomized experimental trials comparing performance with and without event-triggered stimulation. Primary outcome measures included detection accuracy and latency, maximum trunk displacement angles (AP for collisions, ML for turns), return time to erect posture, and user-perceived stability ratings using a modified Usability Rating Scale.

Results demonstrated that the collision detection algorithm achieved 93% accuracy across all tested subjects, with an average detection delay of 88 milliseconds that enabled timely stimulation delivery. Two of the three collision-tested subjects showed statistically significant reductions in maximum AP trunk angle with stimulation (from 24.78° to 18.88° and from 16.10° to 9.92°, respectively), while all three showed trends toward faster return to upright posture. The turn detection algorithm also achieved 93% accuracy for right turns and 100% accuracy for left turns, though the longer detection delays (averaging 342 milliseconds) limited the effectiveness of turn-related stimulation, with some subjects actually showing increased ML trunk angles during stimulated trials—likely because stimulation activated too late in the turn sequence to prevent initial displacement, though it may have assisted in subsequent recovery. User perception ratings generally favored stimulated trials, though the delayed turn response reduced the perceived benefit for turning events specifically.

Several important design considerations emerge from this experimental validation. First, the choice of inertial signal features (AP acceleration for collisions, SI angular velocity for turns) proves highly effective for event discrimination, with clear separation between event types and normal operation. Second, the threshold-based approach provides excellent detection accuracy with minimal computational overhead, making it suitable for real-time embedded implementation. Third, the directional specificity of turn-related stimulation protocols is biomechanically sound, though timing optimization remains critical for maximum effectiveness. Fourth, the system demonstrates compatibility with both implanted and surface stimulation approaches, suggesting broad applicability across different user populations and clinical settings.

Future refinements of the invention may address the turn detection latency issue through several potential strategies. One approach involves monitoring angular acceleration rather than angular velocity, as post-hoc analysis suggests this could reduce detection delays to approximately 17 milliseconds. Another strategy involves predictive event detection using alternative sensor modalities such as LIDAR, computer vision, or sonar to identify potential collision or turn scenarios before they occur, enabling preemptive stimulation activation. Additionally, the system could be expanded to address other common destabilizing events such as curb negotiation, rough terrain traversal, and ramp descent through development of additional event-specific detection algorithms and stimulation protocols.

The invention's clinical significance extends beyond immediate stability enhancement to broader quality-of-life improvements for manual wheelchair users with SCI. By reducing the risk of injurious falls without imposing restrictive mechanical constraints, the system preserves upper-body function and work volume while enhancing mobility confidence. This may encourage users to engage more actively in community participation and activities of daily living, potentially reducing secondary complications associated with sedentary behavior and social isolation. Furthermore, the system's compatibility with existing wheelchair designs and stimulation technologies facilitates relatively straightforward clinical translation, with minimal modification required to standard equipment.

In summary, the present invention represents a significant advancement in postural stability enhancement for manual wheelchair users with spinal cord injury through its integration of real-time inertial event detection with precisely timed functional neuromuscular stimulation. By automatically responding to specific destabilizing events with biomechanically appropriate muscular activation patterns, the system provides targeted assistance exactly when and where it is needed, without compromising user autonomy or upper-body function. Experimental validation confirms the system's technical feasibility, detection accuracy, and biomechanical effectiveness, while also identifying opportunities for further refinement and expansion to address additional real-world mobility challenges.
