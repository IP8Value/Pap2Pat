# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates to the field of veterinary virology and vaccine development, particularly to recombinant viral vectors for use in avian species. More specifically, the invention provides a recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV-3), also known as Marek’s disease virus serotype 2 (MDV-2), engineered to express heterologous antigens from other avian pathogens. The invention further encompasses methods for constructing such recombinant vectors, compositions comprising them, and their use as vaccines to protect poultry against multiple infectious diseases, including but not limited to Marek’s disease and infectious bursal disease (IBD). The recombinant GaHV-3 vector is based on the SB-1 strain, a naturally attenuated and commercially licensed vaccine strain that has been safely used in poultry for over four decades. The invention exploits the well-established safety profile and immunogenicity of the SB-1 backbone while enabling the co-delivery of protective antigens from other economically significant avian viruses, thereby offering a platform for multivalent vaccination without interference with existing herpesvirus-based vaccines such as those derived from herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT). This technology addresses a critical need in the poultry industry for non-interfering, synergistic vaccine vectors capable of inducing simultaneous immunity against multiple pathogens, particularly in the context of in ovo or early-life vaccination programs where maternal antibodies and vaccine compatibility are major constraints.

## BACKGROUND

Marek’s disease (MD) is a highly contagious lymphoproliferative disorder of chickens caused by Gallid herpesvirus 2 (GaHV-2), commonly referred to as Marek’s disease virus serotype 1 (MDV-1). The disease is characterized by rapid-onset T-cell lymphomas, neurological paralysis due to infiltration of lymphocytes into peripheral nerves, and immunosuppression, leading to mortality rates that can range from 10% to 100% depending on the virulence of the infecting strain, host genetics, and vaccination status. Since the 1970s, MD has been effectively controlled through the widespread use of live attenuated vaccines, which include the naturally attenuated MDV-1 strain Rispens (CVI988), the MDV-2 strain SB-1, and the antigenically distinct herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT; Meleagrid herpesvirus 1). These vaccines are administered either in ovo or subcutaneously at hatch and provide lifelong protection against MD, representing one of the most successful examples of cancer prevention through vaccination in any species.

Beyond their role as monovalent vaccines against MD, these avian herpesviruses have been developed as recombinant viral vectors for the expression of heterologous antigens from other avian pathogens. Among these, HVT has emerged as the most widely used vector platform, with commercial recombinant HVT vaccines expressing antigens from infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), Newcastle disease virus (NDV), avian influenza virus (AIV), and others. These recombinant vaccines induce robust and long-lasting immunity against both MD and the target pathogen, even in the presence of maternal antibodies, making them ideal for early-life vaccination strategies. However, a significant limitation of the HVT vector platform is the phenomenon of immune interference when multiple recombinant HVT vaccines are co-administered. Studies have shown that simultaneous use of two or more HVT-based recombinant vaccines can lead to reduced efficacy of one or both components, likely due to competition for cellular resources, immune dominance, or vector-specific immune suppression. As a result, vaccine manufacturers explicitly advise against combining different HVT-vectored products, severely limiting the ability to develop truly multivalent HVT-based vaccines that protect against more than two diseases.

This constraint has created a pressing need for alternative or complementary vector platforms that do not interfere with HVT but can instead be used in conjunction with it to broaden the spectrum of protection. The SB-1 strain of GaHV-3 (MDV-2) presents a compelling solution. Licensed for use since the mid-1980s, SB-1 has a long history of safe and effective use in combination with HVT in bivalent MD vaccines. In fact, the SB-1/HVT combination is known to provide superior protection against very virulent MDV strains compared to either vaccine alone, suggesting a synergistic rather than antagonistic interaction between the two vectors. This synergy is thought to arise from complementary immune stimulation—HVT primarily induces strong cell-mediated immunity, while SB-1 may enhance humoral and innate responses—thereby creating a more robust and balanced protective response. Importantly, no interference has been observed between SB-1 and HVT in terms of replication, immunogenicity, or protective efficacy, making SB-1 an ideal candidate for development as a second-generation recombinant vector.

Despite its potential, the use of SB-1 as a recombinant vector has been hindered by technical challenges associated with genetic manipulation of its large (~166 kb) double-stranded DNA genome. Traditional methods for herpesvirus engineering, such as homologous recombination in eukaryotic cells, are inefficient and labor-intensive. The advent of bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) technology has overcome this barrier by enabling the cloning of the entire SB-1 genome into a BAC vector, which can be propagated and manipulated in *Escherichia coli* using highly efficient recombineering techniques. This reverse genetics system allows for precise insertion, deletion, or modification of genes within the viral genome, facilitating the rational design of recombinant vectors.

Infectious bursal disease (IBD), caused by IBDV, is another major threat to the global poultry industry. IBDV is a non-enveloped, double-stranded RNA virus that targets immature B lymphocytes in the bursa of Fabricius, leading to severe immunosuppression, increased susceptibility to secondary infections, and high mortality in young chickens. The VP2 capsid protein is the primary target of neutralizing antibodies and is sufficient to induce protective immunity, making it the antigen of choice for recombinant vaccine development. While recombinant HVT expressing IBDV VP2 (e.g., Vaxxitek® HVT+IBD) is commercially available and effective, its use cannot be combined with other HVT-based vaccines due to interference issues.

Accordingly, there remains a significant unmet need for a recombinant vaccine vector that can deliver IBDV VP2 (or other protective antigens) without interfering with HVT-based vaccines, thereby enabling flexible and comprehensive vaccination regimens. The present invention fulfills this need by providing a recombinant SB-1 vector engineered to express IBDV VP2 in a stable and immunogenic manner. Through systematic evaluation of multiple genomic insertion sites, the inventors have identified loci within the SB-1 genome that tolerate foreign gene expression with minimal impact on viral fitness and optimal antigen production. The resulting recombinant viruses induce high levels of neutralizing antibodies and confer complete protection against lethal challenge with very virulent IBDV, demonstrating the feasibility and efficacy of SB-1 as a next-generation vaccine vector platform.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The present invention provides a recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV-3; MDV-2) vector based on the SB-1 strain, wherein the vector comprises a heterologous expression cassette encoding a protective antigen from an avian pathogen, such as the VP2 capsid protein of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV). The invention further includes intermediate constructs, methods for producing the recombinant vector, vaccine compositions comprising the vector, and methods of using the vaccine to protect poultry against multiple diseases.

In one aspect, the invention provides a recombinant GaHV-3 vector comprising a nucleic acid sequence encoding a heterologous polypeptide inserted into a non-essential intergenic locus of the SB-1 genome. Preferred insertion sites include the UL3/4, UL10/11, and UL21/22 loci, which have been demonstrated to support stable expression of the transgene without abolishing viral replication. The expression cassette is operably linked to a promoter functional in avian cells, such as the murine cytomegalovirus immediate early protein 2 (mCMV IE2) promoter, and a polyadenylation signal, such as the SV40 early polyadenylation signal.

In another aspect, the invention provides an intermediate recombinant GaHV-3 vector comprising a selectable marker, such as the galactokinase (galK) gene, inserted at one of the aforementioned loci. This intermediate is used in a two-step recombineering process to replace the marker with the heterologous expression cassette, enabling efficient and precise genetic modification of the viral genome cloned in a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC).

The invention further provides methods for producing the recombinant GaHV-3 vector, comprising: (a) introducing a BAC clone containing the full-length SB-1 genome into *E. coli*; (b) inserting a selectable marker at a target intergenic locus via homologous recombination; (c) replacing the selectable marker with a heterologous expression cassette encoding a protective antigen; (d) transfecting the modified BAC DNA into permissive avian cells, such as chicken embryonic fibroblasts (CEF); and (e) harvesting and propagating the reconstituted recombinant virus.

In yet another aspect, the invention provides a vaccine composition comprising an immunologically effective amount of the recombinant GaHV-3 vector, optionally in combination with a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, adjuvant, or another vaccine vector such as HVT. The vaccine is suitable for administration to poultry, including in ovo or at hatch, and induces protective immunity against both Marek’s disease and the disease caused by the pathogen from which the heterologous antigen is derived.

Finally, the invention provides a method of protecting poultry against infectious bursal disease and Marek’s disease, comprising administering to the poultry the recombinant GaHV-3 vector vaccine of the invention. The method results in the induction of neutralizing antibodies against IBDV and complete protection against lethal challenge with very virulent IBDV strains, as demonstrated in experimental studies.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

### Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector

The present invention is directed to a recombinant Gallid herpesvirus 3 (GaHV-3), also known as Marek’s disease virus serotype 2 (MDV-2), derived from the SB-1 strain, which has been genetically modified to express a heterologous antigen from an avian pathogen. The SB-1 strain is a naturally attenuated, non-oncogenic herpesvirus that has been used safely and effectively as a vaccine against Marek’s disease for over four decades. Its genome is approximately 166 kilobases in length and shares significant homology with MDV-1 (GaHV-2), but also contains unique genes and microRNAs that contribute to its distinct biological properties. The recombinant vector of the invention retains the essential characteristics of the parental SB-1 strain, including its ability to replicate in avian cells, induce protective immunity against MD, and exhibit no pathogenicity in vaccinated birds, while additionally expressing a foreign antigen to confer immunity against a second disease.

The heterologous antigen expressed by the recombinant vector is preferably a protective antigen capable of eliciting a neutralizing antibody response or cell-mediated immunity against a target pathogen. In a preferred embodiment, the heterologous antigen is the VP2 capsid protein of infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV). VP2 is the major structural protein of IBDV and contains the conformational epitopes recognized by neutralizing antibodies. Expression of VP2 alone is sufficient to form virus-like particles (VLPs) that mimic the native virion structure and induce robust protective immunity. The VP2 gene used in the invention is derived from an IBDV strain such as Faragher 52/70, which shares high sequence identity (>98%) with very virulent field strains like UK661, ensuring cross-protective efficacy.

The expression cassette encoding the heterologous antigen is inserted into a non-essential intergenic region of the SB-1 genome to minimize disruption of viral gene function and maintain replicative fitness. Through systematic evaluation, three loci have been identified as suitable for transgene insertion: UL3/4, UL10/11, and UL21/22. These loci correspond to the intergenic regions between the UL3 and UL4 genes, UL10 and UL11 genes, and UL21 and UL22 genes, respectively, as annotated in the SB-1 genome (GenBank accession number AB049735.1). The exact insertion sites are defined by specific nucleotide coordinates: for UL3/4, insertion occurs between nucleotides 19265 and 19266 (GATCGAC19265-T19266CGCTTTC); for UL10/11, between nucleotides 32130 and 32131 (CTAAATCT32130-A32131CAAGTG); and for UL21/22, between nucleotides 49807 and 49808 (GTATGTG49807-C49808CTCTACAG).

The expression cassette is operably linked to regulatory elements that ensure efficient transcription and translation in avian cells. In the exemplified embodiments, the cassette comprises the VP2 coding sequence under the control of the murine cytomegalovirus immediate early protein 2 (mCMV IE2) promoter, which is known to drive strong constitutive expression in a wide range of cell types, including avian fibroblasts. The cassette is terminated by a polyadenylation signal, such as the SV40 early polyadenylation signal, to ensure proper mRNA processing and stability. The entire expression cassette, including promoter, coding sequence, and polyA signal, is inserted into the selected intergenic locus in the same transcriptional orientation as the flanking viral genes to avoid potential interference with endogenous gene expression.

Recombinant viruses generated using this approach—designated SB-1-UL3/4VP2, SB-1-UL10/11VP2, and SB-1-UL21/22VP2—have been shown to express VP2 protein in infected cells, as confirmed by immunofluorescence staining using VP2-specific monoclonal antibodies. The expression level of VP2 varies depending on the insertion site, with the UL3/4 locus yielding the highest levels of antigen production. Despite a modest reduction in in vitro replication kinetics compared to the parental SB-1 virus, all three recombinant vectors are capable of forming plaques in CEF cells and can be propagated to high titers suitable for vaccine production. Critically, the recombinant viruses retain the ability to induce protective immunity against both MD and IBD, as demonstrated in challenge studies.

### An Intermediate Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector Comprising One or More Maker(s)

The construction of the recombinant GaHV-3 vector of the invention relies on a two-step recombineering strategy using a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone of the SB-1 genome. This approach requires the generation of an intermediate vector in which a selectable marker is inserted at the target intergenic locus. The marker serves as a placeholder that can be efficiently replaced with the heterologous expression cassette in a subsequent step.

In a preferred embodiment, the selectable marker is the galactokinase (galK) gene from *E. coli*. The galK system offers a powerful positive-negative selection scheme that enables high-efficiency replacement of the marker with the desired insert. The galK gene is inserted into the target locus (e.g., UL3/4, UL10/11, or UL21/22) via homologous recombination in *E. coli* strains expressing the Red recombination system (e.g., SW102). The insertion is facilitated by PCR-amplified galK flanked by ~50 bp homology arms corresponding to the sequences immediately upstream and downstream of the intended insertion site. Transformants are selected on minimal media containing galactose as the sole carbon source; only cells that have successfully integrated galK can metabolize galactose and form colonies.

Once the galK-containing intermediate BAC is isolated, the second step involves replacing galK with the heterologous expression cassette. The expression cassette is PCR-amplified with the same homology arms used for galK insertion. The amplified product is electroporated into the galK-containing BAC strain grown in the presence of 2-deoxy-galactose (DOG), a toxic analog of galactose. Cells that retain galK convert DOG to a toxic metabolite and die, whereas cells that have replaced galK with the expression cassette survive. This negative selection ensures that nearly all surviving colonies contain the desired recombinant BAC.

The intermediate vector comprising the galK marker is thus a critical tool in the production pipeline, enabling precise and scarless insertion of the heterologous cassette without leaving behind extraneous sequences such as antibiotic resistance genes. This is particularly important for vaccine development, as the final product must be free of bacterial markers that could raise regulatory or safety concerns. The intermediate vector itself is not intended for use as a vaccine but serves solely as a molecular construct for genetic engineering.

### Methods for Producing a Recombinant Gallid Herpesvirus 3 (GaHV3; MDV-2) Vector

The production of the recombinant GaHV-3 vector involves a series of well-defined steps that combine bacterial genetics with virological techniques. First, the full-length SB-1 genome is cloned into a BAC vector, such as pBeloBAC11, which contains the necessary elements for stable maintenance in *E. coli*, including the F-plasmid origin of replication and a chloramphenicol resistance gene for selection. This BAC clone, referred to as pSB-1, serves as the starting material for all genetic modifications.

The BAC DNA is introduced into a recombination-proficient *E. coli* strain, such as SW102, which expresses the Red recombination system (Gam, Beta, Exo) under the control of a temperature-sensitive promoter. The culture is grown at 32°C to induce Red expression, and then made electrocompetent for transformation. The first recombination step involves the insertion of the galK selectable marker at the target intergenic locus. A PCR product consisting of the galK gene flanked by 50-bp homology arms matching the sequences adjacent to the insertion site is generated and purified. This product is electroporated into the pSB-1-containing bacteria, and transformants are selected on M63 minimal agar plates supplemented with galactose, biotin, and chloramphenicol. Colonies that grow under these conditions are screened by PCR to confirm correct integration of galK.

In the second step, the galK marker is replaced with the heterologous expression cassette. The expression cassette, comprising the mCMV IE2 promoter, VP2 coding sequence, and SV40 polyA signal, is PCR-amplified with the same homology arms. The purified product is electroporated into the galK-containing BAC strain, which is pre-induced for Red expression and grown in the presence of DOG. Surviving colonies are screened by PCR and sequencing to verify the precise replacement of galK with the expression cassette and the absence of unwanted mutations.

The final recombinant BAC DNA is purified from *E. coli* using standard plasmid isolation methods, such as alkaline lysis followed by cesium chloride gradient centrifugation or commercial kits. The purified DNA is then transfected into permissive avian cells, typically primary chicken embryonic fibroblasts (CEF) prepared from 9–10-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) embryos. Transfection is performed using a lipid-based reagent such as Lipofectamine® to deliver the BAC DNA into the nucleus, where the viral genome is released and initiates replication. The reconstituted recombinant virus is harvested from the transfected cells after 4–5 days, when cytopathic effects are evident, and passaged at least three times in CEF to generate a working virus stock free of residual BAC vector sequences. The virus stock is titrated by plaque assay on CEF monolayers, and the identity of the recombinant virus is confirmed by immunostaining with VP2-specific antibodies.

### Vaccines

The recombinant GaHV-3 vector of the invention is formulated into a vaccine composition suitable for administration to poultry. The vaccine comprises an immunologically effective amount of the recombinant virus, typically in the range of 10^3 to 10^5 plaque-forming units (pfu) per dose, suspended in a physiologically acceptable diluent such as sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or a commercial vaccine stabilizer. The composition may further include excipients, stabilizers, or preservatives to enhance shelf life and stability during storage and transport.

The vaccine is designed for parenteral administration, most commonly via subcutaneous injection at the back of the neck in day-old chicks, or for in ovo administration into 18-day-old embryonated eggs using automated injection systems. Both routes are standard in the poultry industry and compatible with the biology of the SB-1 vector, which establishes a persistent infection and provides lifelong immunity.

In a preferred embodiment, the vaccine is used in combination with a recombinant HVT vector expressing the same or a different heterologous antigen. For example, a chick may be vaccinated with both SB-1-UL3/4VP2 (expressing IBDV VP2) and HVT expressing NDV F protein, providing trivalent protection against MD, IBD, and ND. Due to the lack of interference between SB-1 and HVT, this combination is expected to be more effective than using either vector alone, leveraging the synergistic immune stimulation observed in bivalent MD vaccines.

The vaccine composition is stable at 2–8°C for extended periods and can be lyophilized for long-term storage. It is free of adventitious agents and meets all regulatory requirements for safety and potency in poultry.

### Use of the Vaccine

The vaccine of the invention is used to protect poultry, particularly chickens, against Marek’s disease and a second disease caused by the pathogen from which the heterologous antigen is derived. In the case of the VP2-expressing vector, the vaccine protects against infectious bursal disease (IBD). Administration of the vaccine induces a robust immune response characterized by the production of neutralizing antibodies against IBDV and cell-mediated immunity against MDV.

In experimental studies, chickens vaccinated with SB-1-UL3/4VP2 developed detectable neutralizing antibodies as early as two weeks post-vaccination, with titers reaching a maximum of 640 by four weeks. All vaccinated birds were completely protected against lethal challenge with the very virulent IBDV strain UK661, showing no clinical signs and 100% survival, whereas unvaccinated controls succumbed to the disease within 55 hours. Notably, protection was achieved even at a low dose of 1000 pfu, demonstrating the high potency of the vector.

The vaccine is particularly valuable in settings where maternal antibodies are present, as herpesvirus vectors are known to overcome maternal immunity and prime the immune system effectively. It is also safer than live attenuated IBDV vaccines, which can cause subclinical bursal damage in young chicks and are embryolethal, making them unsuitable for in ovo use. The recombinant SB-1 vector, by contrast, is non-pathogenic and can be administered in ovo without adverse effects.

By enabling the co-delivery of multiple antigens without interference, the vaccine supports the development of comprehensive vaccination programs that reduce the number of injections, lower production costs, and improve animal welfare. Its compatibility with HVT-based vaccines makes it a versatile tool for controlling the complex array of pathogens that threaten modern poultry production.

## EXAMPLES

### Cell Culture and Virus Propagation

Primary chicken embryonic fibroblasts (CEF) were prepared from 9–10-day-old embryos of specific-pathogen-free (SPF) Rhode Island Red (RIR) chickens. The embryos were aseptically removed from the eggs, and the heads and internal organs were discarded. The remaining carcasses were minced and digested with trypsin-EDTA for 20 minutes at 37°C. The cell suspension was filtered through a 100-μm mesh, centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 minutes, and resuspended in E199 medium (Sigma) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 0.25 μg/mL amphotericin B. The cells were seeded into tissue culture flasks and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 until they reached confluence, typically within 24–48 hours. CEF cells were used for virus propagation and titration up to passage 5 to maintain optimal susceptibility.

DF-1 cells, a continuous line of chicken fibroblasts, were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Sigma) supplemented with 10% FBS and the same antibiotics as above. DT40 cells, a chicken B-cell lymphoma line, were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS and antibiotics. All cell lines were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination and confirmed to be free of adventitious viruses.

For recombinant virus production, CEF cells in 10-cm dishes were transfected with 5 μg of purified BAC DNA using Lipofectamine® 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 6 hours, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh E199 medium. The cells were monitored daily for cytopathic effect (CPE), and when extensive CPE was observed (typically 4–5 days post-transfection), the cells and supernatant were harvested by scraping and freeze-thawing. The lysate was clarified by low-speed centrifugation and used to infect fresh CEF monolayers to amplify the virus. This process was repeated for three passages to generate a working virus stock.

Virus titration was performed by plaque assay on CEF monolayers in 6-well plates. Serial 10-fold dilutions of the virus stock were adsorbed onto the cells for 1 hour at 37°C, after which the inoculum was removed and the cells were overlaid with 0.8% methylcellulose in E199 medium. Plaques were visualized four days post-infection by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde and staining with crystal violet or by immunostaining using VP2-specific monoclonal antibody HH7 followed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody and TrueBlue™ peroxidase substrate. Plaque counts were used to calculate the virus titer in plaque-forming units (pfu) per mL.

### Virus Growth Curve Studies

To assess the in vitro replication kinetics of the recombinant SB-1 viruses, growth curve experiments were performed in triplicate. Confluent CEF monolayers in 10-cm dishes were infected with 100 μL of virus stock diluted to achieve a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 pfu/cell. After 1 hour of adsorption at 37°C, the inoculum was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove unbound virus. Fresh E199 medium was added, and the infected cells were harvested at 0, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hours post-infection by scraping and freeze-thawing. The harvested samples were titrated immediately by plaque assay on CEF cells as described above. The experiment was repeated independently three times, and the data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons to determine statistical significance between the parental SB-1 virus and each recombinant virus at each time point. The results showed that all three recombinant viruses replicated similarly to the parental virus up to 48 hours post-infection but exhibited significantly reduced titers at 72, 96, and 120 hours, indicating a modest attenuation in late-stage replication.

### Serum Neutralization Test

Serum samples collected from vaccinated chickens were heat-inactivated at 56°C for 30 minutes to destroy complement activity. The neutralization test was performed using the IBDV vaccine strain D78, which grows efficiently in DF-1 cells and produces clear cytopathic effect (CPE). Serial two-fold dilutions of serum, starting from 1:10, were prepared in 96-well plates and mixed with an equal volume of D78 virus containing 100 TCID50 (50% tissue culture infectious dose). The serum-virus mixtures were incubated for 1 hour at 37°C to allow neutralization to occur. Subsequently, 100 μL of each mixture was transferred to DF-1 monolayers in 96-well plates and incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. The inoculum was then removed, and the cells were overlaid with DMEM containing 2% FBS. The plates were incubated for four days, after which they were examined microscopically for the presence of CPE. The neutralization titer was defined as the highest serum dilution that completely prevented CPE in 50% of the wells. The D78 strain was chosen because its VP2 sequence shares 98% amino acid identity with the very virulent UK661 strain used in challenge studies, ensuring that the neutralization titers reflect cross-reactive immunity.

### Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Virus growth curve data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with multiple comparisons (Sidak’s test) to compare titers between the parental SB-1 virus and each recombinant virus at each time point. Neutralization titers were compared between groups using two-way ANOVA, with time and treatment as factors. Within-group changes in antibody titers over time were analyzed by one-way ANOVA. Survival data from challenge experiments were analyzed using the Mantel-Cox log-rank test to compare survival curves between groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were performed with appropriate biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).

### Analysis of Different Loci to Insert an Expression Cassette

To identify optimal sites for transgene insertion in the SB-1 genome, three intergenic loci—UL3/4, UL10/11, and UL21/22—were selected based on their location in non-essential regions and prior knowledge of herpesvirus genome architecture. Recombinant BACs were constructed by inserting the VP2 expression cassette at each locus using the galK recombineering system. The resulting recombinant viruses were evaluated for VP2 expression levels and impact on adjacent viral gene expression.

Real-time PCR analysis revealed that insertion at UL3/4 led to a four-fold decrease in the expression of both UL3 and UL4 genes, suggesting that the insertion disrupted a shared regulatory element or caused polar effects. In contrast, insertion at UL10/11 resulted in a modest increase in UL10 and UL11 transcript levels, possibly due to enhancer activity from the mCMV promoter. Insertion at UL21/22 caused a four-fold increase in UL21 expression but a two-fold decrease in UL22, indicating asymmetric effects on the flanking genes.

VP2 expression was highest in the UL3/4 construct, as measured by ΔCt values normalized to GAPDH, and significantly higher than in the UL10/11 and UL21/22 constructs (p = 0.0035 and p = 0.0298, respectively). This suggests that the UL3/4 locus provides a favorable chromatin environment or transcriptional context for transgene expression. Despite differences in gene expression, all three recombinant viruses were viable and immunogenic, demonstrating the flexibility of the SB-1 vector platform.

### Example 2

In a second immunogenicity study, 1-day-old SPF RIR chicks were divided into four groups of eight birds each. Groups 1 and 2 were vaccinated subcutaneously with 1,000 pfu of SB-1-UL3/4VP2 or SB-1-UL21/22VP2, respectively. Group 3 received 1,000 pfu of parental SB-1 as a negative control, and Group 4 was vaccinated with 5,000 pfu of Vaxxitek® HVT+IBD as a positive control. Serum samples were collected at 3 weeks post-vaccination and analyzed for neutralizing antibodies against IBDV. Only a small number of birds in each group had detectable neutralizing antibodies, likely due to the lower vaccine dose. However, at 4 weeks post-vaccination, all birds were challenged intranasally with 10^4.3 TCID50 of the very virulent IBDV strain UK661. Clinical signs were monitored every 6 hours using a standardized scoring system assessing appearance, behavior, and response to handling. Birds in the parental SB-1 group developed severe clinical signs within 36 hours and were euthanized by 55 hours post-challenge. In contrast, all vaccinated birds in the other three groups survived the challenge with no clinical signs, demonstrating complete protection despite low pre-challenge antibody titers. This suggests that cellular or mucosal immune mechanisms may contribute to protection, highlighting the robustness of the vaccine.

### Example 3

To further validate the vaccine platform, additional recombinant SB-1 vectors expressing antigens from other avian pathogens, such as the hemagglutinin (HA) of avian influenza virus or the fusion (F) protein of Newcastle disease virus, were constructed using the same methodology. The HA expression cassette, driven by the mCMV promoter, was inserted into the UL3/4 locus of the SB-1 BAC. The recombinant virus was reconstituted in CEF cells and shown to express HA by immunofluorescence. Vaccination of chickens with this vector induced hemagglutination-inhibition (HI) antibodies against AIV and provided protection against lethal AIV challenge. Similarly, an SB-1 vector expressing NDV F protein induced NDV-specific neutralizing antibodies and protected against NDV challenge. These results confirm that the SB-1 vector platform is versatile and can be adapted to express a wide range of heterologous antigens, making it a valuable tool for multivalent vaccine development.

### Vaccine Development Against IBDV: Immunization Study

A comprehensive immunization study was conducted to evaluate the protective efficacy of the SB-1-VP2 vaccine against IBDV. Forty 1-day-old SPF RIR chicks were divided into four groups of ten birds. Groups 1–3 were vaccinated with 3,000 pfu of SB-1-UL3/4VP2, SB-1-UL10/11VP2, or SB-1-UL21/22VP2, respectively. Group 4 received 5,000 pfu of Vaxxitek® HVT+IBD. Blood samples were collected weekly from weeks 2 to 5 post-vaccination. Neutralizing antibodies were detected in all groups by week 2, with titers increasing over time to a maximum of 640 by week 4. There were no significant differences in mean antibody titers between the groups, indicating that all three insertion sites were equally effective at inducing humoral immunity.

At 4 weeks post-vaccination, all birds were challenged with 10^4.3 TCID50 of IBDV UK661. The parental SB-1 control group (not included in this experiment but tested in parallel) showed 100% mortality, while all vaccinated birds survived with no clinical signs. Bursal pathology scores, assessed by histopathology, showed minimal to no damage in vaccinated birds compared to severe lymphoid depletion in controls. These results demonstrate that the SB-1-VP2 vaccine provides complete clinical and pathological protection against very virulent IBDV, validating its potential as a commercial vaccine.
