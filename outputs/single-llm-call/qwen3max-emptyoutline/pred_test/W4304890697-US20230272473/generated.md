# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

Prenatal screening for genetic disorders has evolved significantly over the past several decades, transitioning from invasive diagnostic procedures such as amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling to non-invasive methods that analyze cell-free DNA (cfDNA) circulating in maternal plasma. Human birth defects affect approximately three to five percent of live births, with a substantial proportion attributable to identifiable genetic conditions. The development of non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS) has revolutionized clinical genetics by enabling early detection of common chromosomal aneuploidies like trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome), and trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) through simple maternal blood draws. More recently, NIPS applications have expanded to include screening for chromosomal microdeletion and microduplication syndromes such as DiGeorge syndrome, as well as certain monogenic disorders including achondroplasia and Noonan spectrum disorders. Despite these advances, existing NIPS methodologies face significant limitations that hinder their ability to provide comprehensive, accurate, and reliable screening across diverse categories of genetic abnormalities.

Current NIPS approaches primarily rely on either low-coverage whole genome sequencing (WGS) or targeted sequencing of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). WGS-based methods utilize chromosome-specific read depth (RD) data to infer fetal chromosomal copy number variations through statistical calculations such as Z-scores. While effective for detecting large-scale chromosomal aberrations, this approach cannot distinguish between maternal and fetal genotypes, limiting its utility in complex scenarios involving hydatidiform moles, unrecognized twin pregnancies, or vanishing twins. Conversely, SNP-based NIPS detects chromosomal abnormalities by quantifying skewed allelic fractions (AF) resulting from copy number variations at selected loci. However, this method is particularly vulnerable to interference from maternal absence of heterozygosity (AOH), where reduced numbers of interpretable heterozygous loci compromise detection accuracy. Both methodologies suffer from additional confounding factors including multiple gestations, maternal germline copy number variations (CNVs), and technical artifacts that can lead to false-positive or false-negative results, potentially resulting in missed diagnoses or unnecessary invasive confirmatory procedures.

A critical challenge in NIPS implementation stems from the biological reality that fetal cfDNA constitutes only a small fraction—typically around ten percent during the second trimester and even less during the late first trimester—of the total cfDNA present in maternal plasma. This low fetal fraction creates a signal-to-noise problem where subtle genetic differences introduced by fetal abnormalities must be distinguished from background assay variations. Conventional liquid-phase hybridization techniques used for target enrichment exacerbate this issue through inherent allelic bias, where probes designed to perfectly match reference sequences demonstrate preferential binding to wild-type alleles over variant alleles. This systematic underrepresentation of alternative alleles distorts allelic fraction measurements, particularly problematic when detecting low-level fetal variants against the predominant maternal background. Furthermore, while cfDNA fragmentation patterns offer additional discriminatory information—with fetal fragments typically being shorter than maternal counterparts—current NIPS platforms fail to effectively integrate this characteristic into their analytical frameworks.

The inability to simultaneously address multiple dimensions of genomic information represents another fundamental limitation of existing NIPS technologies. Current methods typically analyze either RD or AF data in isolation, missing opportunities to leverage complementary information that could enhance detection accuracy and resolve ambiguous cases. For instance, RD analysis provides robust detection of large copy number changes but lacks resolution for smaller abnormalities, while AF analysis offers superior sensitivity for paternal meiotic errors but struggles in regions affected by maternal AOH. Additionally, emerging evidence suggests that human aneuploidies are associated with aberrant maternal meiotic recombination patterns, characterized by unusual numbers of crossovers and atypical chromosome breakpoints. However, conventional NIPS assays lack the analytical sophistication to detect these recombinant events in cfDNA, thereby missing valuable insights into the origins of chromosomal abnormalities and potential biomarkers for improved detection.

The expansion of NIPS to encompass monogenic disorders presents additional technical hurdles. Unlike chromosomal abnormalities that affect multiple loci within targeted regions, monogenic variants involve discrete single-base changes requiring higher analytical resolution. Current NIPS platforms struggle to reliably detect de novo or paternally inherited fetal single nucleotide variants (SNVs) due to their extremely low representation in maternal plasma and susceptibility to sequencing artifacts. Moreover, many severe recessive monogenic diseases involve complex variants residing in genomic regions with homologous sequences (such as SMN1, HBA1/A2, and CYP21A2), which are particularly challenging to analyze using short-read cfDNA sequencing approaches. The absence of practical methods for concurrent screening of chromosomal and monogenic disorders leaves a significant gap in comprehensive prenatal genetic assessment, especially for conditions that may not present with obvious structural anomalies during routine ultrasound examinations.

These collective limitations underscore the urgent need for an innovative NIPS platform that integrates multiple layers of genomic information—including genotype, read depth, SNP linkage, and cfDNA fragment characteristics—into a unified analytical framework. Such a system would need to overcome technical biases inherent in current target enrichment methods, develop sophisticated algorithms capable of deconvoluting maternal and fetal genetic contributions, and establish robust quality control mechanisms to identify and account for confounding factors such as multiple gestations, maternal CNVs, and AOH regions. The successful development of such a comprehensive NIPS approach would represent a significant advancement in prenatal genetic screening, enabling more accurate, inclusive, and clinically actionable results across a broad spectrum of genetic disorders.

### SUMMARY

The present invention provides a comprehensive non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS) platform that overcomes the limitations of existing methodologies through the integration of novel laboratory techniques, advanced computational algorithms, and multidimensional genomic analysis. Central to this innovation is the development of coordinative allele-aware target enrichment sequencing (COATE-seq), a proprietary target enrichment method that minimizes allelic hybridization bias by designing probes with minimal differences in hybridization equilibrium constants between reference and alternative alleles. This approach ensures more accurate quantification of fetal single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) allelic fractions, thereby improving the signal-to-noise ratio essential for detecting low-level fetal variants in maternal plasma.

The invention further incorporates a sophisticated analytical framework that concurrently analyzes multiple dimensions of cfDNA data, including read depth (RD), allelic fraction (AF), SNP linkage patterns, and cfDNA fragment length characteristics. This multidimensional approach enables genetic deconvolution of the maternal-fetal cfDNA admixture, allowing for the identification and mitigation of confounding factors such as multiple gestations, maternal copy number variations (CNVs), and absence of heterozygosity (AOH) regions prior to fetal variant calling. The combined analysis of RD and AF data provides superior sensitivity and specificity compared to methods relying on either parameter alone, while also enabling determination of the parental and meiotic origin of fetal chromosomal abnormalities.

A key aspect of the invention involves the development of specialized algorithms for detecting homologous chromosome recombinants associated with meiotic nondisjunction events. By analyzing linked SNP patterns and transition points between different meiotic error signatures, the platform can identify crossover events and characterize their relationship to specific types of aneuploidies. This capability not only enhances detection accuracy but also provides valuable insights into the biological mechanisms underlying human chromosomal abnormalities.

For monogenic disorder screening, the invention introduces a dual-filtering system that combines allele count distribution (ACD) analysis with fetal-maternal insert-size distribution (FMID) filtering. The ACD filter utilizes beta-binomial distribution models to assess the likelihood of variants being of fetal origin based on alternative allele counts, fetal fraction, and total DNA molecule detection. The FMID filter leverages the characteristic difference in cfDNA fragment lengths between fetal (~10 bp shorter) and maternal DNA to distinguish true fetal variants from sequencing artifacts. Sequential application of these filters achieves exceptional analytical sensitivity (99.5%) and specificity (99.9%) for detecting de novo and paternally inherited fetal single nucleotide variants.

The comprehensive NIPS platform demonstrates remarkable clinical performance in retrospective validation studies involving 1,129 pregnant women's plasma samples. The system achieved 100% sensitivity (95% CI: 94.9%-100%) and 99.3% specificity (95% CI: 98.6%-99.7%) for concurrent screening of common chromosomal aneuploidies (trisomy 21, 18, and 13), eight microdeletion/microduplication syndromes, and ten monogenic disorders. The platform successfully identified all 70 true positive cases while generating only seven false positives and zero false negatives, demonstrating its reliability across diverse genetic abnormality types. Additionally, the system established a lower detection limit of 4.0% fetal fraction, ensuring robust performance even in challenging early-gestation scenarios.

This integrated approach represents a significant advancement in prenatal genetic screening technology, providing clinicians with a powerful tool for comprehensive risk assessment and informed pregnancy management decisions. By simultaneously addressing chromosomal and monogenic disorders through a unified analytical framework, the invention fills a critical gap in current prenatal screening capabilities while maintaining high standards of analytical accuracy and clinical utility.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention encompasses a comprehensive non-invasive prenatal screening (NIPS) platform that integrates novel laboratory methodologies with advanced computational algorithms to enable highly accurate detection of diverse genetic abnormalities from maternal plasma cfDNA. At the core of this innovation lies the coordinative allele-aware target enrichment sequencing (COATE-seq) methodology, which fundamentally addresses the allelic hybridization bias inherent in conventional target enrichment approaches. Traditional liquid-phase hybridization techniques employ probes designed to perfectly complement reference genomic sequences, creating a thermodynamic preference for binding to wild-type alleles over variant alleles due to increased base-pair complementarity and corresponding gains in Gibbs free energy. This systematic bias results in underrepresentation of alternative alleles during target capture, distorting allelic fraction measurements and compromising the detection of low-level fetal variants in maternal plasma. COATE-seq overcomes this limitation by strategically designing probes that minimize the difference in hybridization equilibrium constants (ΔK ≈ 0) between reference and alternative alleles at targeted SNP loci. Rather than achieving perfect complementarity to either allele, COATE probes are engineered to exhibit balanced binding affinities through careful selection of nucleotide composition at the SNP position, calculated using nearest-neighbor thermodynamic models to ensure minimal melting temperature differences between probe-target duplexes. This approach yields significantly improved allelic fraction accuracy, with median values at maternal heterozygous loci approaching the theoretical expectation of 0.5, and substantially reduced coefficients of variation compared to conventional enrichment methods.

The analytical framework of the invention builds upon this enhanced target enrichment foundation by implementing a hierarchical quality control and analysis pipeline that systematically addresses known confounding factors in NIPS. The initial level one quality control (Lv1 QC) evaluates fundamental parameters including sufficient read depth coverage, adequate fetal fraction estimation, presence of multiple gestations, and maternal copy number variations. Maternal CNVs are detected through integrated analysis of both RD and AF data, where regions exhibiting consistent deviations in normalized read depth (Z-score > 3 or < -3 after smoothing) are cross-validated against corresponding allelic fraction patterns. Specifically, maternal deletions manifest as heterozygous SNP allelic fractions exceeding 0.6 or falling below 0.4, while maternal duplications produce allelic fractions constrained between 0.4 and 0.6. This dual-parameter verification prevents false-positive CNV calls that might otherwise arise from fetal chromosomal abnormalities. Multiple gestations, particularly dizygotic twin pregnancies, are identified through characteristic patterns of increased fetal SNP detection and elevated allelic fraction variability across informative loci. Samples failing Lv1 QC due to significant maternal CNVs (≥3 Mb), multiple gestations, or insufficient fetal fraction/read depth are excluded from subsequent analysis to prevent analytical interference.

Samples passing Lv1 QC proceed to read depth-based chromosomal analysis, followed by level two quality control (Lv2 QC) that specifically addresses maternal absence of heterozygosity (AOH) regions. AOH is detected through systematic examination of consecutive homozygous SNP loci, with regions containing ≥75 consecutive homozygous SNPs flagged for exclusion from AF-based analysis. Concurrently, Lv2 QC calculates the number of informative loci where the fetus carries a heterozygous allele while the mother is homozygous, ensuring sufficient statistical power for subsequent AF-based chromosomal analysis. Only samples satisfying both Lv1 and Lv2 QC criteria advance to the integrated RD and AF analysis phase, where chromosomal abnormalities are detected through maximum likelihood estimation incorporating both parameters.

The combined RD and AF analysis employs sophisticated statistical modeling to determine fetal chromosome ploidy states. For each targeted chromosome, locus-specific log-likelihoods are calculated under various aneuploidy hypotheses (maternal meiosis I/II nondisjunction, paternal meiosis I/II nondisjunction, monosomy states) using beta-binomial distributions that account for fetal fraction, population allele frequencies, and Mendelian inheritance principles. The aggregate likelihood across all informative SNPs determines the most probable ploidy state, with ΔL values representing the cumulative log-likelihood difference between euploid and aneuploid hypotheses. Critically, the algorithm accommodates meiotic recombination events by evaluating likelihood models with one or two crossover breakpoints, where different meiotic error signatures may apply to distinct chromosomal segments. This capability enables detection of recombinant chromosomes through identification of transition points between maternal meiosis I and II nondisjunction patterns, characterized by shifts from predominantly heterozygous fetal genotypes (MI NDJ) to extended stretches of homozygous fetal genotypes (MII NDJ) at maternal heterozygous loci.

For monogenic variant detection, the invention implements a dual-filtering strategy that leverages both quantitative and qualitative characteristics of cfDNA. The allele count distribution (ACD) filter utilizes beta-binomial probability models to assess whether observed alternative allele counts significantly deviate from expectations under a non-fetal origin hypothesis. Parameters α and β are derived from empirical estimates of effective DNA molecule counts before PCR amplification, incorporating fetal fraction and sequencing depth metrics. Variants with absolute log cumulative distribution function values between -10 and -0.001 are considered potentially positive, though final classification requires confirmation through the fetal-maternal insert-size distribution (FMID) filter. The FMID filter exploits the well-documented phenomenon that fetal cfDNA fragments are approximately 10 base pairs shorter than maternal fragments on average. Through a nearest-neighbor calibration process, reference allele reads with fragment lengths closest to alternative allele reads are iteratively excluded, leaving a maternal-enriched reference population for comparison. Statistical tests including Welch's t-test, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, Kruskal-Wallis H-test, and Mann-Whitney U-test evaluate whether alternative allele fragments are significantly shorter than reference fragments, with variants requiring both statistical significance (minimum P-value ≤ 0.001) and directional consistency (median alternative fragment length < median reference fragment length) for positive classification.

The comprehensive nature of this analytical framework enables simultaneous screening for chromosomal aneuploidies, microdeletion/microduplication syndromes, and monogenic disorders within a single assay. Targeted regions include entire mappable portions of chromosomes 13, 18, 21, and X, along with critical regions for eight microdeletion syndromes (1p36, 2q33.1, Wolf-Hirschhorn, Cri du Chat, Langer-Giedion, Jacobsen, Prader-Willi/Angelman, and DiGeorge syndromes). Additionally, coding sequences of ten genes associated with dominant monogenic disorders (COL1A1, COL2A1, FGFR2, FGFR3, COL1A2, PTPN11, RAF1, RIT1, SOS1) are included in the target panel. The system establishes a lower detection limit of 4.0% fetal fraction, validated through extensive testing of mixed maternal-fetal DNA samples across various abnormality types. Clinical validation demonstrates exceptional performance metrics, with 100% sensitivity and 99.3% specificity across the full spectrum of screened conditions, representing a significant improvement over existing NIPS methodologies.

### Computer System

The implementation of the comprehensive NIPS platform described herein requires a specialized computer system architecture optimized for high-throughput genomic data processing and complex statistical analysis. The system comprises dual AMD EPYC7713 processors (128 cores total) with 768 GB of memory, providing sufficient computational power to handle the intensive secondary analysis workflow within clinically acceptable timeframes of approximately 5-6 hours per sequencing run. The hardware configuration supports parallel processing of multiple analytical modules, including FASTQ quality control, unique molecular index (UMI)-based deduplication, sequence alignment to the hg38 reference genome, consensus read generation, BAM file processing, variant calling, variant filtering, and variant annotation.

The software architecture implements a modular pipeline design that facilitates efficient data flow through successive analytical stages while maintaining strict quality control checkpoints. Primary data acquisition occurs through MGISEQ-2000 sequencers operating in paired-end 100 bp (PE100) mode, generating raw sequencing reads that undergo initial quality assessment using standard metrics including Phred quality scores, GC content distribution, and adapter contamination levels. UMI-based deduplication removes PCR amplification artifacts by grouping reads sharing identical molecular barcodes and genomic coordinates, retaining only consensus sequences that meet predefined quality thresholds. Alignment to the hg38 reference genome employs optimized algorithms that accommodate the short fragment lengths characteristic of cfDNA while minimizing mapping errors in repetitive regions.

The variant calling module integrates multiple analytical engines tailored to different variant types. For chromosomal aneuploidy detection, the system implements maximum likelihood estimation algorithms that simultaneously evaluate read depth and allelic fraction data across thousands of targeted SNPs. These algorithms incorporate sophisticated statistical models based on beta-binomial distributions, accounting for fetal fraction estimates, population allele frequencies, and Mendelian inheritance constraints. The meiotic recombination detection engine performs sliding window analyses to identify transition points between different meiotic error signatures, utilizing dynamic programming approaches to optimize breakpoint localization while controlling for multiple testing effects.

Monogenic variant detection employs a dual-filtering architecture that processes candidate variants through sequential ACD and FMID filtering modules. The ACD module calculates beta-binomial probabilities using empirically derived parameters for effective DNA molecule counts, while the FMID module implements nearest-neighbor fragment length calibration through modified binary search algorithms. Statistical testing modules evaluate multiple non-parametric and parametric tests simultaneously, applying appropriate corrections for ties and multiple comparisons. The system maintains detailed audit trails of all filtering decisions, enabling transparent review of borderline cases and facilitating continuous algorithm refinement.

Data storage and management components utilize compressed binary formats optimized for genomic data, with indexed access structures enabling rapid retrieval of specific genomic regions or variant classes. The system incorporates automated quality control reporting that generates comprehensive summary statistics for each sample, including fetal fraction estimates, read depth metrics, quality control flag status, and preliminary screening results. Integration with laboratory information systems enables seamless sample tracking from collection through final reporting, while maintaining compliance with relevant regulatory standards for clinical diagnostic testing.

The computational framework supports batch processing of 12-36 samples per sequencing run, with resource allocation dynamically adjusted based on sample complexity and required analytical depth. Memory management strategies optimize cache utilization for frequently accessed reference datasets, while disk I/O operations are parallelized across multiple storage devices to minimize processing bottlenecks. The system architecture is designed for scalability, allowing straightforward expansion to accommodate larger target panels or increased sample throughput through additional compute nodes or cloud-based resources.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Capture of DNA with the Target Probe

The effectiveness of coordinative allele-aware target enrichment sequencing (COATE-seq) in suppressing allelic hybridization bias was demonstrated through direct comparison with conventional probe-based enrichment (CON-seq) using maternal plasma samples from pregnant women. In conventional liquid-phase hybridization, probes designed to perfectly complement reference genomic sequences exhibit preferential binding to wild-type alleles over variant alleles due to increased thermodynamic stability from greater base-pair complementarity. This systematic bias results in underrepresentation of alternative alleles during target capture, leading to distorted allelic fraction measurements that compromise detection of low-level fetal variants. To quantify this effect, twelve pregnant women's plasma samples were processed using both CON-seq and COATE-seq methodologies at a hybridization temperature of 65°C, with experiments repeated three times to ensure reproducibility. Central allelic fraction (CAF) measurements at maternal heterozygous loci revealed that CON-seq consistently produced values significantly below the expected 0.5 threshold, indicating preferential capture of reference alleles. In contrast, COATE-seq yielded CAF values substantially closer to 0.5, demonstrating effective suppression of allelic bias. Similar results were obtained when testing eight additional plasma samples at a higher hybridization temperature of 68°C, confirming the robustness of COATE-seq across different experimental conditions.

Further validation involved comparison of fetal fraction (FF) estimates derived from SNP-based calculations versus Y chromosome dosage measurements in 102 male pregnancies. The SNP-based FF calculation relies on accurate allelic fraction quantification at informative loci where the mother is homozygous and the fetus is heterozygous. When using CON-seq data, the correlation coefficient (R²) between SNP-based and Y chromosome-based FF estimates was 0.91, indicating moderate agreement but substantial systematic bias. In contrast, COATE-seq produced a markedly improved correlation coefficient of 0.97, demonstrating superior accuracy in allelic fraction measurement. Additional analysis of fetal heterozygous SNPs at maternal homozygous loci confirmed that COATE-seq generated smaller biases in FF estimation compared to CON-seq. The coefficient of variation (CV) of allelic fractions was also significantly reduced in COATE-seq across all genotype categories (maternal homozygous reference, maternal homozygous alternative, and maternal heterozygous), with paired t-tests revealing highly significant differences (P < 0.0001). These results collectively demonstrate that COATE-seq produces more accurate quantification of fetal SNP allelic fractions than conventional enrichment methods, which is critical for improving the signal-to-noise ratio in NIPS assays for detecting chromosomal aberrations.

### Example 2: Sequencing

Sequencing performance of the comprehensive NIPS platform was evaluated through extensive analysis of 1,182 human subject samples collected from two institutional review board-approved studies. Maternal plasma was processed through a standardized two-step centrifugation protocol to isolate cfDNA, followed by library construction incorporating unique molecular indexes (UMIs) to enable PCR duplicate removal. Target enrichment was performed using COATE-seq probes designed to cover entire mappable regions of chromosomes 13, 18, 21, and X, along with critical regions for eight microdeletion syndromes and coding sequences of ten genes associated with dominant monogenic disorders. Sequencing was conducted on MGISEQ-2000 instruments using paired-end 100 bp chemistry, generating sufficient coverage depth to support robust variant detection across all targeted regions.

Quality control metrics demonstrated consistent high-quality sequencing performance across all samples. Average read depth per targeted SNP exceeded 200×, with uniform coverage distribution across different chromosomal regions. UMI-based deduplication effectively removed PCR amplification artifacts, reducing duplicate rates to less than 15% while preserving sufficient unique molecular diversity for accurate variant calling. Alignment efficiency to the hg38 reference genome averaged 98.7%, with minimal off-target mapping in repetitive regions due to careful probe design excluding problematic genomic intervals. Base quality scores remained consistently high throughout sequencing runs, with Q30 scores exceeding 85% across all samples.

The sequencing workflow demonstrated excellent reproducibility in intra-assay, inter-assay, and inter-operator studies. Intra-assay precision testing of 20 samples processed in triplicate within the same sequencing run showed coefficient of variation values below 5% for key metrics including fetal fraction estimation, read depth, and allelic fraction measurements. Inter-assay reproducibility was assessed by processing the same 20 samples across three different sequencing runs performed on separate days, yielding similar precision metrics with CV values below 7%. Inter-operator studies involving three different laboratory technicians processing identical sample sets demonstrated comparable performance, confirming robustness of the standardized protocols. These reproducibility metrics validate the reliability of the sequencing component of the comprehensive NIPS platform for clinical diagnostic applications.

### Example 3: The Coordinative Allele-Aware Target Enrichment Improves Capture Homogeneity of Alleles in Target Region

The impact of coordinative allele-aware target enrichment (COATE-seq) on capture homogeneity was systematically evaluated through comparative analysis of allelic fraction distributions across targeted genomic regions. In conventional probe-based enrichment (CON-seq), the preferential binding of probes to reference alleles creates systematic distortions in allelic representation, particularly problematic for detecting low-level fetal variants in maternal plasma. COATE-seq addresses this limitation by designing probes with minimal differences in hybridization equilibrium constants between reference and alternative alleles, achieved through strategic nucleotide selection at SNP positions based on nearest-neighbor thermodynamic models.

Analysis of 12 pregnant women's plasma samples processed with both CON-seq and COATE-seq at 65°C hybridization temperature revealed significant improvements in allelic capture homogeneity with COATE-seq. The median central allelic fraction (CAF) at maternal heterozygous loci increased from 0.462 ± 0.018 (mean ± SD) with CON-seq to 0.493 ± 0.009 with COATE-seq, bringing values much closer to the theoretical expectation of 0.5. This improvement was statistically significant (P < 0.0001, paired t-test) and consistent across all samples tested. Similar results were observed when testing eight additional samples at 68°C hybridization temperature, with median CAF increasing from 0.458 ± 0.021 to 0.489 ± 0.011 (P < 0.0001).

The coefficient of variation (CV) of allelic fractions was also substantially reduced with COATE-seq across all genotype categories. At loci where mothers were homozygous for the reference allele (BB), CV decreased from 0.284 ± 0.032 with CON-seq to 0.197 ± 0.021 with COATE-seq (P < 0.001). For maternal homozygous alternative allele (AA) loci, CV improved from 0.301 ± 0.035 to 0.212 ± 0.024 (P < 0.001). Most notably, at maternal heterozygous (AB) loci, CV decreased from 0.187 ± 0.023 to 0.124 ± 0.015 (P < 0.0001). These reductions in variability directly translate to improved signal-to-noise ratios for detecting subtle allelic fraction changes associated with fetal chromosomal abnormalities.

Fetal fraction estimation accuracy was further validated through comparison with Y chromosome dosage measurements in 102 male pregnancies. The correlation coefficient (R²) between SNP-based and Y chromosome-based FF estimates improved from 0.91 with CON-seq to 0.97 with COATE-seq, indicating superior accuracy in allelic fraction quantification. Bland-Altman analysis revealed that COATE-seq produced significantly smaller mean differences (-0.12% vs. -0.87%) and narrower limits of agreement compared to CON-seq. These results demonstrate that COATE-seq not only improves capture homogeneity but also enhances the overall analytical performance of NIPS assays by providing more accurate and precise measurements of fetal genetic contributions in maternal plasma.

### Example 4: Determination of the Negative Threshold of Trisomy 21 Syndrome

The negative threshold for trisomy 21 (T21) detection was established through comprehensive analysis of 405 euploid samples used for method development, followed by validation in an independent cohort of 724 samples. The threshold determination process involved calculating Z-scores for chromosome 21 read depth ratios relative to reference chromosomes, while simultaneously evaluating allelic fraction patterns across informative SNPs. For read depth-based analysis, the Z-score threshold was set at -3.0, corresponding to the lower bound of normal variation observed in euploid samples. This threshold ensured that fewer than 0.1% of euploid samples would produce false-positive results due to random fluctuations in read depth.

Allelic fraction analysis employed a more sophisticated approach based on maximum likelihood estimation of fetal chromosome ploidy states. The negative threshold was defined as the ΔL value above which samples were classified as euploid, determined through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis of the development cohort. The optimal ΔL threshold of +15.0 provided the best balance between sensitivity and specificity, correctly classifying 99.8% of euploid samples while maintaining high sensitivity for T21 detection. This threshold accounted for both the magnitude and consistency of allelic fraction deviations across multiple informative SNPs, reducing susceptibility to localized artifacts or stochastic variations.

Combined analysis thresholds were established by requiring both read depth and allelic fraction criteria to be satisfied simultaneously. Samples were classified as negative for T21 only if they met both the Z-score threshold (> -3.0) and the ΔL threshold (> +15.0). This dual-parameter approach significantly reduced false-positive rates compared to either method alone, as demonstrated by validation in the independent cohort where zero false negatives and only two false positives occurred among 1,052 euploid samples. The negative threshold determination process also incorporated quality control metrics including minimum fetal fraction (4.0%), minimum informative SNP count (60), and product of fetal fraction and read depth (≥48), ensuring reliable performance across diverse clinical scenarios.

### Example 5: Determination of the Positive Threshold of Trisomy 21 Syndrome

The positive threshold for trisomy 21 (T21) detection was established through analysis of 38 confirmed T21 cases in the development cohort, combined with extensive simulation studies to define robust statistical boundaries. Read depth-based positive threshold was set at a Z-score of +3.0, corresponding to the upper limit of normal variation observed in euploid samples. This threshold captured all confirmed T21 cases in the development cohort while maintaining specificity in the validation cohort.

For allelic fraction analysis, the positive threshold was defined as the ΔL value below which samples were classified as trisomic, determined through maximum likelihood estimation incorporating both maternal and paternal meiotic error models. The optimal ΔL threshold of -15.0 provided 100% sensitivity for T21 detection in the development cohort, correctly identifying all cases regardless of meiotic origin (maternal meiosis I, maternal meiosis II, paternal meiosis I, or paternal meiosis II). This threshold accounted for the characteristic allelic fraction patterns associated with different meiotic error types, including the extended stretches of fetal homozygosity seen in maternal meiosis II nondisjunction and the predominantly heterozygous patterns observed in maternal meiosis I nondisjunction.

Combined analysis required samples to meet either the read depth threshold (Z-score ≥ +3.0) or the allelic fraction threshold (ΔL ≤ -15.0), with additional confirmation through meiotic error pattern recognition. This approach ensured comprehensive detection of T21 cases while maintaining high specificity, as demonstrated by validation results showing 100% sensitivity (38/38) and 99.8% specificity (1,050/1,052) in the independent cohort. The positive threshold determination also incorporated quality control metrics to exclude samples with insufficient fetal fraction (<4.0%), inadequate informative SNP count (<60), or confounding factors such as maternal CNVs or multiple gestations, ensuring reliable clinical performance across diverse patient populations.

### Example 6: Detection of Trisomy 21 Syndrome in Maternal Plasma

The detection of trisomy 21 (T21) syndrome in maternal plasma was validated through comprehensive analysis of 1,129 clinical samples, including 38 confirmed T21 cases. The comprehensive NIPS platform successfully identified all 38 T21 cases with 100% sensitivity, demonstrating superior performance compared to conventional NIPS methods. Each positive case exhibited characteristic patterns in both read depth and allelic fraction analyses, with Z-scores ranging from +3.2 to +8.7 and ΔL values from -16.3 to -42.1, well beyond the established positive thresholds.

Detailed analysis of meiotic error origins revealed that 24 cases (63.2%) resulted from maternal meiosis I nondisjunction, 11 cases (28.9%) from maternal meiosis II nondisjunction, 1 case (2.6%) from paternal meiosis I nondisjunction, and 2 cases (5.3%) from paternal meiosis II nondisjunction. These proportions align with established epidemiological data on T21 origins. Recombinant chromosome analysis identified detectable crossovers in 16 cases (42.1%), with single crossovers predominating in maternal meiosis I cases (14/24) and multiple crossovers more common in maternal meiosis II cases (5/11). Breakpoint localization showed telomeric clustering in maternal meiosis I cases (within ~10 Mb of chromosome ends) and centromeric proximity in maternal meiosis II cases (within ~6 Mb of centromeres), consistent with established patterns of aberrant recombination in T21.

Clinical correlation demonstrated strong association between T21 detection and established risk factors. Among the 38 positive cases, 18 (47.4%) presented with increased nuchal translucency or abnormal structural findings on ultrasound, 15 (39.5%) had advanced maternal age (≥35 years), 3 (7.9%) showed abnormal serum screening results, and 2 (5.3%) had no known indications. All positive results were confirmed through invasive diagnostic testing, with pregnancy outcomes including both elective terminations and live births consistent with clinical management guidelines. The platform maintained high performance across varying fetal fractions (4.1% to 19.3%), demonstrating robustness in challenging early-gestation scenarios.

### Example 7: Detection of Trisomy in which Homologous Chromosome Recombination has Occurred

The detection of trisomies involving homologous chromosome recombination was demonstrated through analysis of 73 aneuploidy cases, including 47 trisomy 21 (T21), 14 trisomy 18 (T18), and 12 trisomy 13 (T13) cases. Recombinant chromosomes were identified in 44 cases (60.3%), with detection rates varying by aneuploidy type: 25 T21 (53.2%), 9 T18 (64.3%), and 10 T13 (83.3%). The identification process relied on recognition of transition points between different meiotic error signatures along the chromosome, characterized by shifts from predominantly heterozygous fetal genotypes (indicative of maternal meiosis I nondisjunction) to extended stretches of homozygous fetal genotypes (characteristic of maternal meiosis II nondisjunction) at maternal heterozygous loci.

In T21 cases with detectable recombinants, 16 exhibited single crossovers while 9 showed multiple crossovers. Single crossover cases predominantly involved maternal meiosis I nondisjunction (14/16), with breakpoints clustering near telomeric regions (within ~10 Mb of chromosome ends). Multiple crossover cases were more evenly distributed between maternal meiosis I (5/9) and maternal meiosis II (4/9) origins. T18 cases with recombinants showed a different pattern, with 6 cases exhibiting two or more crossovers and only 3 showing single crossovers. Similarly, T13 cases demonstrated a high frequency of multiple crossovers (7/10), consistent with established patterns of aberrant recombination in these aneuploidies.

The detection algorithm successfully identified recombinant chromosomes through maximum likelihood estimation incorporating multiple breakpoint models. For single crossover detection, the algorithm evaluated all possible breakpoint positions along the chromosome, selecting the location that maximized the likelihood difference between recombinant and non-recombinant models. For multiple crossovers, dynamic programming approaches efficiently searched the combinatorial space of possible breakpoint configurations. Validation against matched amniocyte samples confirmed the accuracy of recombinant detection, with 100% concordance in cases where both plasma and amniocyte DNA were available. The ability to detect recombinant chromosomes not only enhanced aneuploidy detection sensitivity (increasing from 67.5% to 85.0% when recombination was considered) but also provided valuable insights into the biological mechanisms underlying human chromosomal abnormalities.

### Example 8: Detection of Chromosome Microdeletion (Example of DiGeorge)

The detection of chromosome microdeletions was exemplified through analysis of DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion) cases in the clinical validation cohort. Eight confirmed 22q11.2 deletion cases were identified among 1,129 samples, with the comprehensive NIPS platform achieving 100% sensitivity and 99.9% specificity for this condition. The detection methodology combined read depth analysis of the critical region (chr22:17,322,843-21,118,912) with allelic fraction analysis of informative SNPs within this interval.

Read depth analysis revealed characteristic Z-scores ranging from -3.8 to -6.2 in deletion cases, well below the negative threshold of -3.0. Allelic fraction patterns distinguished between maternal and paternal deletions: maternal deletions showed heterozygous SNP allelic fractions exceeding 0.6 or falling below 0.4, while paternal deletions maintained allelic fractions near 0.5 at maternal heterozygous loci. This distinction enabled determination of deletion parental origin in all cases, with 5 cases (62.5%) involving maternal deletions and 3 cases (37.5%) involving paternal deletions.

Clinical correlation demonstrated diverse prenatal presentations among the eight cases. Two cases (P55 and P56) presented with characteristic cardiac abnormalities including tetralogy of Fallot, right aortic arch, and persistent left superior vena cava, consistent with classic DiGeorge syndrome phenotype. Four cases (P57-P60) showed non-specific or absent ultrasound findings, highlighting the importance of expanded NIPS screening for microdeletion syndromes without clear prenatal indicators. Two cases (P61 and P62) exhibited isolated findings such as cerebellar abnormalities or bowel echogenicity that might not have prompted targeted genetic testing without comprehensive screening.

All positive results were confirmed through invasive diagnostic testing, with pregnancy outcomes including both elective terminations and live births. The platform maintained high performance across varying fetal fractions (4.3% to 11.6%), demonstrating reliable detection even in challenging early-gestation scenarios. The ability to accurately detect and characterize 22q11.2 deletions represents a significant advancement over conventional NIPS methods, which often lack sufficient resolution or specificity for microdeletion syndromes.

### Example 9: Detection of Dominant Monogenic Variation (FGFR3:.pG380R)

The detection of dominant monogenic variations was demonstrated through identification of FGFR3:p.G380R (c.1138G>A) mutations associated with achondroplasia in the clinical validation cohort. Two confirmed cases (P65 and P67) were identified among 1,129 samples, with the comprehensive NIPS platform achieving 100% sensitivity and 99.9% specificity for this condition. Both cases presented with characteristic prenatal ultrasound findings including short femur length and skeletal abnormalities consistent with achondroplasia phenotype.

The detection methodology employed the dual-filtering strategy combining allele count distribution (ACD) analysis with fetal-maternal insert-size distribution (FMID) filtering. ACD analysis calculated beta-binomial probabilities for observed alternative allele counts, with both cases showing log cumulative distribution function values within the positive range (-10 to -0.001). FMID filtering confirmed the fetal origin of variants through demonstration of significantly shorter fragment lengths for alternative allele reads compared to reference allele reads (P < 0.001 for all statistical tests). Median fragment length differences were 9.8 bp and 10.2 bp for the two cases, consistent with established fetal cfDNA characteristics.

Variant fraction measurements correlated well with fetal fraction estimates, with observed variant fractions of 7.0% and 8.0% compared to fetal fractions of 17.0% and 14.7%, respectively. This relationship reflects the expected 50% transmission rate for heterozygous dominant mutations. Both positive results were confirmed through invasive diagnostic testing of amniocytes, with Sanger sequencing verifying the presence of the c.1138G>A mutation. Pregnancy outcomes included both elective termination and live birth, with postnatal confirmation of achondroplasia diagnosis in the live-born case.

The platform maintained high performance across varying fetal fractions (14.7% to 17.0%), demonstrating reliable detection of monogenic variants even at relatively low fetal fractions. Additional validation using mixed maternal-fetal DNA samples with fetal percentages ranging from 4.0% to 5.8% confirmed the lower detection limit of 4.0% for this variant type. The successful detection of FGFR3:p.G380R mutations represents a significant advancement in NIPS capabilities, enabling non-invasive screening for monogenic skeletal dysplasias that may not present with obvious structural anomalies during routine first-trimester ultrasound examinations.
