# DESCRIPTION

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The present invention provides methods and compositions for the diagnosis, prognosis, and risk stratification of chronic kidney disease (CKD) based on the detection and quantification of specific isoforms of versican—namely V0 and V1—in renal tissue. In this first example, the utility of renal versican V0 and V1 expression levels as predictive biomarkers of progressive renal function decline was demonstrated in a cohort of 74 patients diagnosed with various proteinuric nephropathies. Kidney biopsy specimens were obtained during routine clinical evaluation, and total RNA was isolated from cryopreserved tissue sections using standardized protocols. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was employed to measure transcript levels of the four known human versican isoforms: V0, V1, V2, and V3. Of these, only V0 and V1 exhibited consistent and measurable expression across the patient cohort, while V2 was undetectable and V3 showed no statistically significant correlation with any clinical or histopathological parameter.

Patients were classified as either “stable” or “progressive” based on predefined clinical endpoints: specifically, progression was defined as either a doubling of serum creatinine from baseline or the development of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) requiring dialysis or transplantation during longitudinal follow-up. At the time of biopsy, baseline clinical parameters—including age, serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and degree of proteinuria—were recorded. Histopathological assessments were independently performed by a blinded renal pathologist using standardized scoring systems for tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (TAIF), interstitial inflammation (II), and glomerulosclerosis (GS). Statistical analyses revealed that while neither V0 nor V1 expression correlated significantly with age, eGFR, or proteinuria at the time of biopsy, both isoforms showed statistically significant positive correlations with serum creatinine measured at the time of last clinical follow-up (V0: R = 0.337, p = 0.003; V1: R = 0.387, p = 0.001). Notably, when the five patients identified as having acute renal failure (ARF)—defined by a >50% reduction in serum creatinine over follow-up—were excluded from the stable group, the correlation between V0/V1 expression and baseline creatinine became statistically significant, suggesting that versican expression reflects underlying structural and functional renal impairment even at initial presentation.

Furthermore, patients with progressive disease exhibited significantly higher renal expression of both V0 (1.56-fold increase, p = 0.034) and V1 (1.67-fold increase, p = 0.011) compared to those with stable disease. This differential expression persisted after exclusion of ARF cases, albeit with slightly reduced statistical significance for V0 (1.52-fold, p = 0.051), while V1 remained highly significant (1.64-fold, p = 0.018). These findings indicate that elevated renal V0 and V1 mRNA levels are associated with an unfavorable long-term renal prognosis, independent of conventional markers such as proteinuria or baseline eGFR. Importantly, linear regression modeling demonstrated that the inclusion of V1 expression alongside traditional predictors—namely baseline serum creatinine and degree of glomerulosclerosis—significantly improved the model’s ability to predict future serum creatinine levels, as evidenced by an increase in adjusted R² from 0.1619 (model with creatinine and GS alone) to 0.2438 (model including V1). This enhancement in predictive power underscores the potential clinical value of versican isoform quantification as a complementary prognostic tool in the management of CKD.

### Example 2

In a second illustrative embodiment, the cellular and subcellular localization of versican protein within human kidney tissue was investigated using immunohistochemical techniques. Frozen sections from renal biopsies of six patients—three diagnosed with IgA nephropathy (IgAN) and three with class IV lupus nephritis—were subjected to staining with a validated rabbit polyclonal antibody specific for human versican (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-25831). Two of the IgAN patients exhibited severe renal dysfunction and a progressive clinical course, while the remaining four patients maintained stable renal function throughout follow-up. As a control, unaffected renal tissue was obtained from the non-neoplastic margin of a nephrectomy specimen performed for localized renal cell carcinoma.

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed a distinct pattern of versican expression that varied according to disease status and anatomical compartment. In healthy control tissue, versican immunoreactivity was weak and primarily localized to the tubulointerstitial space, with minimal signal detected in glomeruli or vascular structures. In contrast, diseased kidneys displayed markedly enhanced versican staining, particularly within the tubulointerstitium. Glomerular expression was generally faint but detectable, with more pronounced signal observed in sclerotic or damaged glomeruli. Tubular epithelial cells exhibited basolateral membrane-associated versican deposition, consistent with polarized secretion into the interstitial matrix. Critically, areas of advanced interstitial fibrosis demonstrated intense, fibrillar versican immunostaining, co-localizing with regions of extracellular matrix (ECM) expansion. Vascular staining was absent in the intimal layer but robust in the media and adventitia of larger renal arteries, suggesting a role for versican in vascular remodeling during chronic kidney injury.

These spatial expression patterns provide critical insights into the cellular sources and pathological contexts of versican upregulation in CKD. The predominance of tubulointerstitial staining aligns with transcriptomic data indicating high versican expression in proximal tubule cells and interstitial fibroblasts. Moreover, the correlation between versican intensity and fibrotic burden supports its involvement in ECM accumulation—a hallmark of progressive renal disease. The absence of significant glomerular or endothelial expression further refines the understanding of versican’s role, positioning it primarily as a mediator of tubulointerstitial pathology rather than glomerular or vascular injury per se. This compartment-specific expression profile not only validates the molecular findings from qRT-PCR but also offers a histological correlate that may be leveraged in diagnostic pathology workflows, wherein versican immunostaining could serve as an adjunctive marker of fibrotic activity and disease progression risk.

### Discussion

The collective data from Examples 1 and 2 establish versican isoforms V0 and V1 as robust, tissue-based biomarkers of progressive chronic kidney disease. Unlike conventional markers such as proteinuria—which showed no significant association with long-term outcomes in this cohort—or baseline eGFR—which, while predictive, offers limited granularity in early-stage disease—versican expression provides a molecular readout of active pathological processes within the renal parenchyma. The fact that V0 and V1 levels correlate more strongly with future renal function than with concurrent clinical parameters suggests that versican reflects ongoing maladaptive repair mechanisms, fibrogenesis, or inflammatory signaling that precede overt functional decline.

Importantly, the lack of correlation between versican and proteinuria challenges the long-held assumption that protein excretion is a universal surrogate for disease severity across all glomerulopathies. This discrepancy may stem from the heterogeneous etiologies represented in the cohort—including minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, lupus nephritis, and vasculitis—wherein proteinuria may be dissociated from tubulointerstitial damage. Versican, by contrast, appears to integrate signals from both epithelial and stromal compartments, offering a more comprehensive assessment of renal injury.

The immunohistochemical findings further contextualize these molecular observations. The strong versican signal in fibrotic interstitium, coupled with in vitro data showing high basal expression in renal fibroblasts and proximal tubule cells, supports a model wherein versican is secreted by activated fibroblasts and injured tubular cells in response to profibrotic stimuli such as TGF-β1. Although TGF-β1 did not induce versican in tubular cells in vitro, its potent upregulation in fibroblasts suggests that paracrine signaling from injured epithelia may drive stromal versican production in vivo. This dynamic interplay between epithelial stress and fibroblast activation is central to the pathogenesis of CKD progression, and versican serves as a measurable output of this crosstalk.

Moreover, the structural properties of versican—particularly its ability to bind hyaluronan, chemokines, and adhesion molecules—position it not merely as a passive biomarker but as an active participant in disease pathogenesis. By sequestering inflammatory mediators and promoting leukocyte retention, versican may sustain a pro-inflammatory microenvironment that fuels fibrosis. Conversely, emerging evidence from cancer biology suggests that certain versican isoforms can promote epithelial phenotypes, raising the possibility of context-dependent protective roles. Resolving this duality will require further functional studies, but for diagnostic purposes, the net effect of elevated V0/V1 expression is clearly associated with worse outcomes.

Critically, the predictive value of versican was most pronounced in the full cohort, where baseline renal function was heterogeneous and included patients with acute-on-chronic presentations. In this clinically realistic scenario—where clinicians must distinguish transient from progressive injury—versican adds meaningful prognostic information beyond standard metrics. Even after excluding acute renal failure cases, versican retained borderline significance, indicating its relevance across disease spectra. Future prospective validation in larger, multicenter cohorts will be essential to confirm these findings and define clinically actionable thresholds for versican expression.

### Example 3

To further substantiate the biological relevance of versican upregulation in kidney disease, in vitro studies were conducted to characterize cell-type-specific expression patterns and regulatory mechanisms. A panel of human cell lines—including renal proximal tubule epithelial cells (HK-2), kidney fibroblasts (TK-173), skin fibroblasts (SF), prostate and colon epithelial cells, smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and leukocytes—was analyzed for basal expression of versican isoforms V0 and V1 using qRT-PCR. Results demonstrated that HK-2 and SF cells exhibited the highest levels of both isoforms, with TK-173 kidney fibroblasts also showing substantial expression. In stark contrast, versican transcripts were nearly undetectable in endothelial cells, prostate and colon epithelial lines, and smooth muscle cells, with expression levels ranging from 0.01% to 0.3% of those observed in HK-2 cells.

These findings confirm that versican expression in the kidney is predominantly restricted to tubular epithelial cells and interstitial fibroblasts under pathological conditions. The negligible expression in endothelial cells aligns with the immunohistochemical observation of absent intimal staining, reinforcing the notion that versican’s role in CKD is centered on the tubulointerstitium rather than the vasculature or glomerular endothelium. The high expression in skin fibroblasts, while notable, likely reflects a general fibroblast phenotype rather than kidney-specific regulation, underscoring the importance of tissue context.

Subsequent experiments examined the responsiveness of versican isoforms to key profibrotic and proinflammatory mediators. HK-2 and TK-173 cells were serum-starved and then stimulated with angiotensin II (Ang II), platelet-derived growth factor-AB (PDGF-AB), or transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1)—all well-established drivers of renal fibrosis. Treatment with Ang II or PDGF-AB failed to induce any significant changes in V0 or V1 expression in either cell type. However, TGF-β1 elicited a robust ~3.5-fold upregulation of both V0 and V1 in TK-173 fibroblasts, with no corresponding effect in HK-2 tubular cells. This cell-type-specific induction highlights TGF-β1 as a primary regulator of versican in renal fibroblasts, positioning versican as a downstream effector of canonical fibrotic signaling pathways.

This regulatory mechanism has significant implications for therapeutic targeting. Given that TGF-β1 is a master cytokine in CKD progression, its ability to drive versican expression suggests that versican levels may serve as a pharmacodynamic biomarker for antifibrotic therapies aimed at TGF-β signaling. Moreover, the lack of induction in tubular cells implies that epithelial versican may be regulated by alternative pathways—perhaps involving mechanical stress, hypoxia, or protein overload—warranting further investigation. Nonetheless, the fibroblast-specific response to TGF-β1 provides a clear mechanistic link between established drivers of fibrosis and versican upregulation, reinforcing its validity as a disease-relevant biomarker.

### Example 4

The translational relevance of versican as a biomarker of kidney disease progression was further confirmed through studies in three distinct rodent models of proteinuric nephropathy. In the first model, accelerated nephrotoxic serum nephritis (NSN) was induced in C57BL/6J mice, resulting in proliferative glomerulonephritis. Fourteen days post-induction, renal versican mRNA levels were elevated 3.5-fold compared to untreated controls (p = 0.02). In the second model, adriamycin (doxorubicin)-induced nephropathy was established in Lewis rats, which recapitulates features of human minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis. At 21 days, versican expression was increased 1.5-fold (p = 0.03). Most strikingly, in the Passive Heymann Nephritis (PHN) model—a rat model of membranous nephropathy characterized by severe, sustained proteinuria and progressive renal failure—versican mRNA was upregulated 8.0-fold after six months (p < 0.001).

Western blot analysis confirmed that these transcriptional changes translated to the protein level in both the adriamycin and PHN models, demonstrating concordance between mRNA and protein expression. The magnitude of versican induction correlated with disease severity, with the most aggressive model (PHN) exhibiting the greatest upregulation. These findings demonstrate that versican dysregulation is not limited to human disease but is a conserved feature across species and etiologies of proteinuric kidney injury. The consistency of versican upregulation in diverse experimental settings strengthens the argument for its fundamental role in the pathophysiology of CKD progression.

Collectively, these animal model data validate the human observational findings and support the use of versican as a translatable biomarker. The ability to detect versican elevation in preclinical models also opens avenues for interventional studies—for instance, testing whether genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of versican ameliorates fibrosis or functional decline. Such studies would not only elucidate causality but also assess versican’s potential as a therapeutic target. Until then, its consistent upregulation across human and animal disease states firmly establishes versican V0 and V1 as reliable indicators of active, progressive renal injury worthy of clinical implementation as prognostic tools.
