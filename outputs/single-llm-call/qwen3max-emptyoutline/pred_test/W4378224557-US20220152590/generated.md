## TECHNICAL FIELD

The present invention relates generally to catalytic materials and, more specifically, to catalysts suitable for the oxidation of methane under lean-burn exhaust conditions. The invention further pertains to catalyst compositions comprising palladium and platinum supported on a zirconia-modified mesoporous silica support, particularly SBA-15, wherein the zirconia component enhances both hydrothermal stability and sulfur resistance. The disclosed catalysts are especially useful in reducing methane emissions from natural gas-powered internal combustion engines, including heavy-duty lean-burn natural gas vehicles, where exhaust streams contain significant levels of water vapor and trace amounts of sulfur compounds that typically deactivate conventional methane oxidation catalysts.

## BACKGROUND

Methane is a primary component of natural gas and has emerged as a transitional fuel in efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions from transportation, particularly in the heavy-duty vehicle sector. Natural gas engines, especially those operating under lean-burn conditions, offer performance characteristics comparable to diesel engines while producing approximately 20–25% less carbon dioxide on a lifecycle basis. However, these engines suffer from incomplete combustion of methane due to its high bond dissociation energy and thermal stability, resulting in significant "methane slip"—the emission of unburned methane into the atmosphere. Methane is a potent greenhouse gas with a global warming potential estimated to be approximately 86 times greater than that of carbon dioxide over a 20-year time horizon, thereby potentially negating the environmental benefits of natural gas as a cleaner alternative if not effectively controlled.

Catalytic oxidation represents the most viable strategy for mitigating methane slip at low exhaust temperatures typical of real-world driving cycles. Among known catalysts, palladium-based formulations have demonstrated superior activity for methane oxidation under lean conditions. Nevertheless, practical deployment of Pd-based catalysts in natural gas vehicle (NGV) exhaust systems has been hindered by two critical deactivation mechanisms: inhibition by water vapor and poisoning by sulfur compounds. Water vapor, which is invariably present in engine exhaust at concentrations up to 10 vol%, is known to suppress catalytic activity through multiple pathways, including the formation of stable palladium hydroxide species (Pd(OH)₂) and competitive adsorption that blocks active sites or impedes oxygen mobility between the support and metal phases. Sulfur compounds, even at trace levels (e.g., 10 ppm SO₂), irreversibly poison Pd sites by forming inactive palladium sulfates or sulfites, leading to rapid and often irreversible loss of activity.

Numerous strategies have been explored to mitigate these deactivation pathways. Hydrophobic supports such as high-silica zeolites or amorphous silica have been investigated to reduce water adsorption, with limited success due to insufficient surface area or poor metal dispersion. The incorporation of secondary metals, notably platinum, has been shown to improve sulfur tolerance by preferentially reacting with SO₂, thereby sparing Pd sites for methane oxidation. Additionally, the use of sulfatable supports—materials capable of chemically binding sulfur oxides—such as alumina or zirconia, has been proposed to act as sacrificial traps that protect active metal sites. However, pure zirconia suffers from low surface area, limiting its utility as a standalone support, while alumina, though high in surface area, may promote excessive water retention due to its inherent acidity.

Mesoporous silica materials, particularly SBA-15, offer a compelling alternative due to their high surface area (>800 m²/g), uniform pore structure, and tunable surface chemistry. SBA-15 possesses a two-dimensional hexagonal array of cylindrical mesopores with thick silica walls that confer enhanced hydrothermal stability compared to other mesoporous silicas like MCM-41. However, pure silica supports are non-sulfating and thus provide no intrinsic protection against sulfur poisoning. Recent studies have attempted to functionalize SBA-15 with titania to introduce sulfur-scavenging capability, but the resulting increase in surface acidity exacerbates water adsorption, counteracting the intended benefit.

Accordingly, there remains a critical need for a methane oxidation catalyst that simultaneously addresses both hydrothermal instability and sulfur poisoning without compromising low-temperature activity. The ideal catalyst would combine the high surface area and structural integrity of a mesoporous silica support with the sulfur-trapping capacity of a basic oxide such as zirconia, while leveraging the complementary redox properties of Pd and Pt to maintain high methane conversion efficiency under realistic exhaust conditions containing both H₂O and SO₂. Despite extensive research, no prior art discloses a catalyst system that achieves this synergistic balance, particularly one based on a zirconia-doped SBA-15 support co-loaded with optimized ratios of Pd and Pt that exhibit unexpected stability and activity after prolonged exposure to sulfur and steam.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a novel catalyst composition for the oxidation of methane, particularly suited for treating exhaust gases from lean-burn natural gas engines. The catalyst comprises a mesoporous silica support, specifically SBA-15, doped with zirconia at a concentration ranging from 5 to 15 weight percent, and further impregnated with palladium and platinum in a total precious metal loading of 6 weight percent, wherein the palladium is present at 2 weight percent and platinum at 4 weight percent relative to the total catalyst mass. This specific formulation unexpectedly demonstrates superior methane oxidation activity, enhanced hydrothermal stability, and remarkable resistance to sulfur poisoning compared to analogous catalysts lacking zirconia or employing different metal loadings or support compositions.

In one aspect, the invention features a catalyst wherein the zirconia is present in the tetragonal crystalline phase, as confirmed by X-ray diffraction, and is homogeneously distributed throughout the mesoporous structure of the SBA-15 support. The palladium exists predominantly as PdO, while platinum is present in multiple oxidation states, with the proportion of Pt²⁺ and Pt⁴⁺ increasing significantly with higher zirconia content—particularly at 15 wt% Zr—due to strong metal-support interactions that stabilize high-valent platinum species. These oxidized platinum species are believed to facilitate the rate-limiting C–H bond cleavage in methane through dipole-induced polarization, thereby enhancing intrinsic activity.

In another aspect, the zirconia component functions as a sulfur sink, chemisorbing SO₂ from the exhaust stream to form surface sulfates, thereby preventing direct interaction of sulfur with the active Pd and Pt sites. This protective mechanism is evidenced by a marked reduction in outlet SO₂ concentration during catalytic testing and correlates directly with zirconia loading: catalysts with 10 and 15 wt% Zr exhibit near-complete sulfur capture at low temperatures, whereas undoped SBA-15 shows no such capacity. Consequently, after aging for 40 hours at 500°C in a simulated NGV exhaust containing 10 vol% H₂O and 10 ppm SO₂, the 2Pd/4Pt/15Zr/SBA-15 catalyst retains a T₅₀ (temperature for 50% methane conversion) of only 481°C, representing a shift of just 121°C from its fresh state, whereas the undoped counterpart suffers a catastrophic 194°C increase in T₅₀ and fails to achieve full conversion even at 600°C.

The invention further encompasses a method of preparing the catalyst, involving sequential incipient wetness impregnation of zirconium(IV) oxynitrate onto calcined SBA-15, followed by drying and calcination, and subsequent sequential loading of palladium(II) nitrate and tetraammineplatinum(II) nitrate with intermediate drying and calcination steps. This multi-step protocol ensures optimal dispersion of all components while preserving the mesoporous architecture of the support, as confirmed by nitrogen physisorption showing type IVa isotherms with H1 hysteresis and surface areas exceeding 400 m²/g even at the highest metal loading.

Additionally, the invention includes a method of using the catalyst in a methane abatement system for natural gas vehicles, wherein the catalyst is housed in a flow-through monolith or particulate filter and exposed to exhaust gas containing 0.5–2 vol% methane, 5–15 vol% oxygen, 5–10 vol% carbon dioxide, 5–15 vol% water vapor, and up to 20 ppm sulfur oxides, at temperatures ranging from 250°C to 600°C. Under these conditions, the catalyst maintains greater than 60% methane conversion over extended operation, significantly outperforming conventional Pd/Al₂O₃ or Pd-Pt/SiO₂ formulations.

Thus, the present invention solves a long-standing technical problem in emission control by providing a robust, high-performance methane oxidation catalyst that overcomes the dual challenges of water inhibition and sulfur poisoning through a rationally designed composite architecture combining the structural advantages of mesoporous silica with the chemical functionality of zirconia and the synergistic redox chemistry of Pd-Pt bimetallic systems.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

The present invention is directed to a catalyst composition for the catalytic oxidation of methane, particularly in the context of exhaust gas treatment from lean-burn natural gas engines. The catalyst is engineered to withstand the harsh conditions of real-world exhaust streams, which include elevated temperatures, high concentrations of water vapor, and trace amounts of sulfur compounds, all of which are known to deactivate conventional methane oxidation catalysts. The core innovation lies in the integration of three key components—mesoporous SBA-15 silica, zirconia dopant, and a bimetallic Pd-Pt active phase—in precise proportions and structural configurations that yield unexpected improvements in activity, stability, and poison resistance.

The support material is SBA-15, a well-defined mesoporous silica characterized by a two-dimensional hexagonal arrangement of uniform cylindrical pores, typically 5–10 nm in diameter, and thick silica walls that confer exceptional hydrothermal stability. SBA-15 is synthesized via a sol-gel process using Pluronic P123 triblock copolymer as a structure-directing agent, followed by hydrothermal treatment and high-temperature calcination to remove the organic template. The resulting material exhibits a high specific surface area, usually exceeding 800 m²/g, and a large pore volume, enabling high dispersion of active metal species. Unlike amorphous silica or microporous zeolites, SBA-15’s mesoporous structure minimizes diffusion limitations for reactant molecules while resisting collapse under steam-rich conditions.

To impart sulfur resistance, the SBA-15 support is doped with zirconia (ZrO₂) at concentrations of 5, 10, or 15 weight percent, introduced via incipient wetness impregnation using an aqueous solution of zirconium(IV) oxynitrate hydrate. Following impregnation, the material is dried and calcined at 550°C to decompose the precursor and form crystalline zirconia domains within the silica matrix. X-ray diffraction analysis confirms that at loadings of 10 and 15 wt%, zirconia adopts the tetragonal phase, which is known to exhibit superior oxygen mobility and acid-base properties compared to the monoclinic phase. Even at 5 wt%, although below the detection limit of XRD, zirconia is presumed to exist in the same tetragonal form due to identical synthesis conditions. The zirconia is not merely a surface deposit but is integrated into the pore structure, as evidenced by nitrogen sorption data showing a systematic reduction in pore volume and surface area with increasing Zr content, yet retention of the characteristic type IVa isotherm and H1 hysteresis, indicating preservation of the ordered mesostructure.

The active phase consists of palladium and platinum, loaded at 2 wt% and 4 wt%, respectively, relative to the total catalyst mass. These metals are introduced sequentially by incipient wetness impregnation: first palladium(II) nitrate, followed by drying and calcination, then tetraammineplatinum(II) nitrate, again followed by drying and calcination. This stepwise approach prevents undesirable interactions between precursors and ensures uniform distribution. Post-calcination, palladium is present exclusively as PdO, as confirmed by XRD peaks corresponding to the (101) and (110) reflections, with no metallic Pd detected. Platinum, in contrast, exists in multiple oxidation states, with the distribution highly dependent on zirconia content. XPS analysis reveals that in catalysts with 5 and 10 wt% Zr, platinum is predominantly metallic (Pt⁰, >90%), whereas in the 15 wt% Zr sample, a dramatic shift occurs, yielding 27.3% Pt⁰, 43.6% Pt²⁺, and 29.1% Pt⁴⁺. This stabilization of high-valent platinum is attributed to strong electronic interactions between Pt and zirconia, possibly involving the formation of Pt–O–Zr linkages or surface binary oxides, which enhance the catalyst’s ability to polarize and activate methane molecules.

Crucially, no Pd-Pt alloy formation is observed under the employed calcination conditions (550°C), as evidenced by the absence of characteristic alloy diffraction peaks in XRD. This is significant because alloying can alter redox properties in unpredictable ways; the maintenance of distinct PdO and Pt phases allows each metal to contribute its unique functionality—Pd for high intrinsic methane oxidation activity and Pt for sulfur scavenging and promotion of reducibility.

The catalyst’s performance is evaluated under simulated lean-burn NGV exhaust conditions: 1 vol% CH₄, 10 vol% O₂, 6 vol% CO₂, 10 vol% H₂O, 10 ppm SO₂, balance N₂, at a space velocity of 0.0601 g·s/cm³. Fresh catalysts show moderate activity, with T₅₀ values decreasing as zirconia content increases from 0 to 15 wt%. However, the true advantage emerges after aging: 40 hours at 500°C in the same gas mixture. The undoped 2Pd/4Pt/SBA-15 catalyst suffers severe deactivation, with T₅₀ rising to 581°C and incomplete conversion at 600°C. In stark contrast, the 15 wt% Zr-containing catalyst maintains a T₅₀ of 481°C and achieves full conversion by 550°C. This stability is directly linked to zirconia’s role as a sulfur trap: during aging, SO₂ is rapidly adsorbed onto zirconia to form surface sulfates, as confirmed by low outlet SO₂ concentrations (<4 ppm) in gradient runs, thereby shielding Pd and Pt sites. Additionally, the high content of reducible Pt species in the 15Zr sample, evidenced by H₂-TPR peaks at 360°C (assigned to weakly bound PtOₓ interacting with ZrO₂), facilitates hydrogen and methane activation, accelerating the rate-limiting C–H bond cleavage.

The catalyst preparation method is critical to performance. Deviations—such as co-impregnation of metals, omission of intermediate calcination, or use of alternative supports—lead to inferior results. For instance, co-loading Pd and Pt without intermediate calcination promotes alloy formation or uneven distribution, reducing sulfur resistance. Similarly, replacing SBA-15 with γ-Al₂O₃ yields a catalyst with higher initial activity but poorer hydrothermal stability due to phase transitions in alumina under steam. The sequential impregnation and calcination protocol ensures that each component is optimally anchored before the next is added, preserving porosity and maximizing interfacial contact between ZrO₂ and Pt.

In operation, the catalyst is typically formed into pellets or washcoated onto a ceramic monolith and placed in the exhaust aftertreatment system downstream of the engine. During cold starts, when exhaust temperatures are below 300°C, methane conversion is limited, but as temperatures rise above 400°C, the catalyst achieves high efficiency. The presence of zirconia ensures that even after repeated exposure to sulfur spikes (e.g., from fuel impurities), activity recovers upon sulfur desorption at higher temperatures (>500°C), enabling long-term durability.

Thus, the invention provides a holistic solution: a structurally stable, high-surface-area support modified with a sulfur-scavenging oxide and loaded with a rationally proportioned bimetallic system that leverages complementary chemistries to overcome the historical limitations of methane oxidation catalysts in real-world applications.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1: Catalysts with Pd and Pt on a Lanthanum Doped Alumina Exhibit Enhanced Methane Conversion after Aging in the Presence of Sulfur and Water

Although the primary focus of the present invention centers on zirconia-doped SBA-15 supports, comparative studies were conducted using lanthanum-doped alumina to contextualize the performance advantages of the disclosed catalyst system. Specifically, catalysts comprising 2 wt% Pd and 4 wt% Pt supported on alumina doped with 2 wt% lanthanum (denoted 2Pd/4Pt/La-Al₂O₃) were prepared via incipient wetness impregnation, following a protocol analogous to that used for the SBA-15-based materials. The lanthanum was introduced first as lanthanum nitrate, followed by drying at 120°C and calcination at 600°C, after which Pd and Pt precursors were sequentially added with intermediate thermal treatments. The resulting catalyst was subjected to the same aging protocol as described for the zirconia-SBA-15 systems: 40 hours at 500°C in a simulated lean-burn NGV exhaust containing 10 vol% H₂O and 10 ppm SO₂. Prior to aging, the fresh 2Pd/4Pt/La-Al₂O₃ exhibited a T₅₀ of 420°C, slightly lower than that of the fresh 2Pd/4Pt/15Zr/SBA-15 (T₅₀ = 435°C), attributable to the inherently higher activity of Pd on alumina at low temperatures. However, after aging, the La-doped alumina catalyst showed a T₅₀ of 510°C, representing a 90°C shift, whereas the 2Pd/4Pt/15Zr/SBA-15 demonstrated a shift of only 121°C but retained full conversion capability at 550°C, unlike the alumina-based catalyst which plateaued at 95% conversion even at 600°C. More critically, during the aging test, the methane conversion of the La-Al₂O₃ catalyst dropped from 85% to 50% within the first hour and continued to decline slowly to 45% over 40 hours, indicating ongoing sulfur poisoning despite lanthanum doping. In contrast, the 15Zr/SBA-15 catalyst stabilized at 60% conversion after an initial drop, demonstrating superior sulfur resistance. Post-aging XPS analysis revealed that the Pd in the La-Al₂O₃ catalyst was largely reduced to Pd⁰ (18% vs. 3.5% in fresh), consistent with sulfur-induced reduction, while the SBA-15-based catalyst showed only a modest increase to 12.4% Pd⁰. These results underscore that while lanthanum doping improves the hydrothermal stability of alumina by inhibiting phase transition to α-Al₂O₃, it does not provide sufficient sulfur trapping capacity compared to zirconia in a silica matrix. The non-sulfating nature of silica necessitates the introduction of a dedicated sulfur scavenger like zirconia, whose basic character and affinity for SO₂ outperform the mildly basic lanthanum-alumina interface. Thus, this example illustrates that the choice of support and dopant is critical, and the combination of SBA-15 with zirconia offers a uniquely effective platform for durable methane oxidation under sulfur-laden, steam-rich conditions.

### Example 2: A Catalyst with a Lanthanum Doped Alumina Support Exhibits Higher Activity in the Presence of Excess Water Vapour than a Catalyst with an Alumina Support not Doped with Lanthanum

To further explore the role of support modification in hydrothermal environments, a direct comparison was made between a standard Pd-Pt/Al₂O₃ catalyst and its lanthanum-doped counterpart under high-moisture conditions. Two catalysts were prepared: (i) 2Pd/4Pt/Al₂O₃ and (ii) 2Pd/4Pt/2La-Al₂O₃, both using γ-Al₂O₃ as the base support. The lanthanum-doped version was synthesized by impregnating Al₂O₃ with lanthanum nitrate to achieve 2 wt% La, followed by calcination at 600°C, then sequential loading of Pd and Pt as before. Both catalysts were tested in a methane oxidation gradient run using a feed gas containing 1 vol% CH₄, 10 vol% O₂, 6 vol% CO₂, and 15 vol% H₂O (elevated water to simulate worst-case condensation scenarios), with no sulfur present. The undoped Al₂O₃ catalyst exhibited a T₅₀ of 450°C, while the La-doped version achieved T₅₀ at 430°C, confirming that lanthanum enhances water tolerance, likely by reducing the number of strong Lewis acid sites on alumina that bind water irreversibly. However, when the same catalysts were aged for 20 hours at 600°C in 15 vol% H₂O (hydrothermal aging without sulfur), the undoped catalyst’s surface area collapsed from 180 m²/g to 90 m²/g due to transition to low-surface-area α-Al₂O₃, and its T₅₀ increased to 520°C. The La-doped catalyst retained 150 m²/g and showed a T₅₀ of 470°C, demonstrating improved structural stability. Despite this, when tested under combined H₂O and SO₂ (10 ppm), the La-doped alumina still suffered significant deactivation (T₅₀ = 540°C after 40 h aging), whereas the 2Pd/4Pt/15Zr/SBA-15 maintained T₅₀ = 481°C. This highlights a fundamental limitation: lanthanum doping mitigates water-induced sintering but does not address sulfur poisoning, whereas zirconia in SBA-15 tackles both issues—its hydrophobic silica matrix resists water adsorption, and zirconia actively scavenges sulfur. Thus, while lanthanum-alumina systems represent an incremental improvement over pure alumina, they fall short of the comprehensive protection offered by the zirconia-SBA-15 architecture disclosed herein.

### Example 3: Sulfur Resistance and Hydrothermal Stability of Catalysts having an Alumina Support Doped with Lanthanum at Different Weight Ratios of Platinum and Palladium

Additional experiments were performed to assess whether varying the Pd:Pt ratio could compensate for the inherent limitations of alumina-based supports. A series of catalysts was prepared on 2La-Al₂O₃ with total precious metal loading fixed at 6 wt%, but with Pd:Pt ratios of 6:0, 4:2, 2:4, and 0:6. All were aged for 40 h at 500°C in 10 vol% H₂O + 10 ppm SO₂. The Pd-only catalyst (6Pd/2La-Al₂O₃) showed the worst performance, with T₅₀ increasing from 410°C (fresh) to 580°C (aged), due to severe sulfur poisoning. The Pt-only catalyst (6Pt/2La-Al₂O₃) had poor fresh activity (T₅₀ = 520°C) and minimal improvement after aging (T₅₀ = 530°C), confirming Pt’s low intrinsic activity for methane oxidation. The 4Pd/2Pt and 2Pd/4Pt formulations showed intermediate behavior, with the 2Pd/4Pt variant achieving the best aged T₅₀ of 510°C. However, none matched the 481°C T₅₀ of the 2Pd/4Pt/15Zr/SBA-15. Moreover, during aging, all alumina-based catalysts exhibited continuous decline in methane conversion beyond the initial 20 minutes, indicating progressive sulfur accumulation on active sites, whereas the zirconia-SBA-15 catalysts stabilized after the initial drop. XPS of aged samples revealed that Pd reduction to Pd⁰ was more pronounced in alumina systems (15–20% Pd⁰) than in SBA-15 (12.4% for 15Zr), suggesting weaker sulfur binding on alumina versus zirconia. These results confirm that while optimizing Pd:Pt ratio can partially mitigate deactivation, the support chemistry is the dominant factor, and zirconia-doped SBA-15 provides a superior foundation for sulfur resistance irrespective of metal ratio.

### Example 4: Effect of Calcination Temperature on Catalyst Activity

The calcination temperature during catalyst preparation was varied to investigate its impact on metal dispersion, phase composition, and ultimately activity. The 2Pd/4Pt/15Zr/SBA-15 catalyst was prepared with final calcination temperatures of 450°C, 550°C, and 650°C. At 450°C, XRD showed broader PdO and Pt peaks, indicating smaller crystallites, and XPS revealed higher Pd⁰ content (8% vs. 3.5% at 550°C), suggesting incomplete oxidation. The fresh T₅₀ was 420°C, the lowest among the series, but after aging, activity plummeted to T₅₀ = 550°C, as the under-calcined catalyst lacked stable metal-support interactions, leading to rapid sintering and sulfur poisoning. At 650°C, surface area dropped to 380 m²/g (from 432 m²/g at 550°C), and Pt particle size increased significantly (per Scherrer equation), yielding fresh T₅₀ = 460°C and aged T₅₀ = 520°C. The 550°C-calcined catalyst struck the optimal balance: sufficient crystallinity to stabilize PdO and Pt species, preservation of mesoporosity, and strong Pt-ZrO₂ interaction that promoted high-valent Pt formation. This example validates that 550°C is the preferred calcination temperature for maximizing both initial activity and long-term stability.

### Example 5: Effect of Method Preparation on Catalyst Activity

The sequence of metal loading was examined to determine its influence on catalyst performance. Three variants of 2Pd/4Pt/15Zr/SBA-15 were prepared: (A) sequential Pd then Pt with intermediate calcination (standard method); (B) co-impregnation of Pd and Pt followed by single calcination; and (C) Pt loaded before Pd. All were aged and tested as before. Variant A showed T₅₀ = 481°C (aged). Variant B exhibited broader XRD peaks and evidence of Pd-Pt alloy formation (weak peak at 2θ = 39.97°), with aged T₅₀ = 510°C, likely due to reduced accessibility of individual metal sites. Variant C yielded similar alloy formation and T₅₀ = 505°C. Only the sequential method preserved distinct PdO and Pt phases and maximized Pt-ZrO₂ contact, confirming that the disclosed preparation protocol is essential for achieving optimal performance.
