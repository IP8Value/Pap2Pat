# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

Esophageal cancer represents one of the most aggressive and lethal forms of human malignancy, characterized by a persistently poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options. Despite advances in surgical techniques, systemic chemotherapy, and radiation therapy, the five-year survival rate for patients diagnosed with esophageal cancer remains dismally low—typically below 25%—particularly in cases of locally advanced or metastatic disease. The clinical management of esophageal carcinoma is further complicated by its histological heterogeneity, which includes both squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma subtypes, each presenting distinct molecular profiles and responses to treatment. Current standard-of-care regimens for unresectable or medically inoperable esophageal cancer often involve concurrent chemoradiation, combining cytotoxic agents such as cisplatin or 5-fluorouracil with external beam radiotherapy. However, these approaches are frequently associated with significant systemic toxicity, including myelosuppression, nephrotoxicity, gastrointestinal disturbances, and cumulative damage to adjacent normal tissues such as the lungs, heart, and spinal cord. Moreover, intrinsic or acquired resistance to conventional chemotherapeutics severely limits their long-term efficacy, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic agents that can enhance tumor control while minimizing adverse effects on healthy tissues.

In this context, natural products derived from medicinal fungi have emerged as promising sources of bioactive compounds with potential anticancer properties. Among these, *Armillaria mellea*, a basidiomycete fungus traditionally used in East Asian herbal medicine in conjunction with *Gastrodia elata* (Tien-Ma), has garnered scientific interest due to its reported immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and antimicrobial activities. Historically, formulations containing *Armillaria mellea* have been employed to alleviate neurological symptoms such as vertigo, dizziness, headache, limb numbness, and infantile convulsions, suggesting a broad spectrum of biological interactions. Recent phytochemical investigations have led to the isolation and structural characterization of armillaridin (AM), a sesquiterpenoid aromatic ester compound uniquely produced by the mycelium of *Armillaria mellea*. Armillaridin is chemically defined as 3-chloro-6-hydroxy-4-methoxy-2-methyl-,3-formyl-2,2a,4a,5,6,7,7a,7b-octahydro-2a-hydroxy-6,6,7b-trimethyl-1H-cyclobut(e)inden-2-yl ester, and it exhibits notable antibacterial activity. However, prior to the present invention, no evidence existed regarding its potential antineoplastic or radiosensitizing effects, particularly in the context of esophageal cancer.

The development of effective radiosensitizers constitutes a critical frontier in oncology, as such agents can potentiate the cytotoxic effects of ionizing radiation without proportionally increasing damage to surrounding normal tissues. Ideal radiosensitizers should selectively target tumor cells through mechanisms such as cell cycle modulation, inhibition of DNA repair pathways, induction of oxidative stress, or disruption of prosurvival signaling cascades. Notably, cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle are known to be maximally sensitive to radiation-induced DNA damage due to condensed chromatin structure and reduced capacity for homologous recombination repair. Compounds that induce G2/M arrest may therefore serve as effective radiosensitizers by synchronizing tumor cells in this vulnerable phase. Additionally, agents capable of triggering mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis—characterized by loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, cytochrome c release, and caspase activation—offer a complementary strategy to eliminate cancer cells that survive initial radiation exposure. Despite extensive research, few clinically approved radiosensitizers meet the dual criteria of high tumor selectivity and low systemic toxicity, highlighting a significant unmet medical need.

Prior art in the field includes various synthetic and natural compounds evaluated for radiosensitization in esophageal cancer models, such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors, hypoxia-activated prodrugs, and reactive oxygen species (ROS)-modulating agents. However, many of these suffer from pharmacokinetic limitations, off-target effects, or insufficient therapeutic windows. Furthermore, no naturally derived sesquiterpenoid ester—particularly one isolated from *Armillaria mellea*—has been previously disclosed or claimed for use in the treatment of esophageal cancer, either as a monotherapy or in combination with radiotherapy. The absence of prior recognition of armillaridin’s antiproliferative, pro-apoptotic, and radiosensitizing properties against human esophageal carcinoma cells represents a critical gap in the art, which the present invention now addresses comprehensively.

It is therefore an object of the present invention to provide a novel therapeutic application of armillaridin for the treatment of esophageal cancer. Another object is to disclose the unexpected discovery that armillaridin induces apoptosis in esophageal cancer cells via the intrinsic mitochondrial pathway, as evidenced by morphological changes, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, phosphatidylserine externalization, and caspase-3 activation. A further object is to demonstrate that armillaridin causes cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase, thereby sensitizing tumor cells to ionizing radiation. Yet another object is to establish, through rigorous in vitro clonogenic assays and in vivo xenograft models, that armillaridin enhances the efficacy of radiotherapy while exhibiting a favorable safety profile compared to conventional chemotherapeutics like cisplatin. These objectives collectively form the foundation of a new and non-obvious method for improving outcomes in esophageal cancer patients through the administration of armillaridin alone or in synergistic combination with radiation therapy.

## SUMMARY

The present invention provides a method for treating esophageal cancer in a subject in need thereof, comprising administering to said subject a therapeutically effective amount of armillaridin, either as a monotherapy or in combination with ionizing radiation. Armillaridin, a naturally occurring sesquiterpenoid aromatic ester isolated from the mycelium of *Armillaria mellea*, has been unexpectedly discovered to exhibit potent cytotoxic activity against human esophageal cancer cell lines, including both squamous cell carcinoma (e.g., CE81T/VGH and TE-2) and adenocarcinoma (e.g., BE-3 and SKGT-4) subtypes. The invention further encompasses the use of armillaridin as a radiosensitizing agent, wherein pretreatment or co-administration of armillaridin significantly enhances the susceptibility of esophageal tumor cells to radiation-induced cell death, as demonstrated by a sensitizer enhancement ratio (SER) of up to 1.6 in clonogenic survival assays.

In accordance with the invention, armillaridin induces programmed cell death in esophageal cancer cells through the intrinsic apoptotic pathway. This mechanism is characterized by hallmark morphological alterations such as chromatin condensation, plasma membrane blebbing, and formation of apoptotic bodies, as well as biochemical markers including depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane potential, externalization of phosphatidylserine detectable by Annexin V binding, and activation of executioner caspases, particularly caspase-3. The pro-apoptotic effect of armillaridin is concentration- and time-dependent, with half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) ranging from approximately 3.4 to 6.9 micromolar across multiple esophageal cancer cell lines. Importantly, this cytotoxic activity is selective for malignant cells and occurs at concentrations that do not adversely affect normal cellular physiology when administered in vivo.

Additionally, the invention discloses that armillaridin modulates the cell cycle distribution of esophageal cancer cells, leading to a significant accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase and an increase in the sub-G1 (hypoploid) population, indicative of apoptotic DNA fragmentation. Given that cells in the G2/M phase are inherently more sensitive to ionizing radiation due to compromised DNA repair capacity during mitosis, this cell cycle perturbation underlies the radiosensitizing property of armillaridin. In vitro studies confirm that exposure to non-cytotoxic or minimally cytotoxic concentrations of armillaridin (e.g., 0.125 to 0.5 micromolar) prior to irradiation results in a marked reduction in clonogenic survival following radiation doses commonly used in clinical practice (e.g., 2 Gy per fraction), thereby lowering the effective radiation dose required to achieve tumor control.

The therapeutic efficacy and safety of armillaridin have been validated in a murine xenograft model of human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. In this model, intraperitoneal administration of armillaridin at a dose of 80 mg/kg three times per week for twelve doses significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to vehicle-treated controls, without causing significant body weight loss or leukopenia—adverse effects commonly observed with standard chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin. These findings support the clinical translatability of armillaridin as a well-tolerated anticancer agent suitable for integration into multimodal treatment regimens.

Accordingly, the invention provides a pharmaceutical composition comprising armillaridin in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, optionally formulated for parenteral, oral, or intratumoral delivery. The composition may be co-administered with a radiation therapy protocol involving fractionated doses of ionizing radiation, typically in the range of 1.8 to 2.0 Gy per fraction, delivered via linear accelerator using electron or photon beams. The invention also includes a method of enhancing the radiosensitivity of esophageal tumor tissue in a patient undergoing radiotherapy, comprising administering armillaridin prior to, concurrently with, or shortly after each radiation fraction to maximize synergistic tumor cell kill while sparing adjacent normal structures.

In summary, the present invention establishes armillaridin as a novel, naturally derived compound with dual functionality as both a direct antineoplastic agent and a radiosensitizer for esophageal cancer. By leveraging the unique biological properties of this fungal metabolite, the invention offers a promising strategy to improve local tumor control, reduce required radiation doses, mitigate treatment-related toxicity, and ultimately extend survival in patients with this devastating malignancy.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF PREFERRED EMBODIMENTS

The present invention is directed to the therapeutic use of armillaridin—a sesquiterpenoid aromatic ester of defined chemical structure—for the treatment of esophageal cancer, either as a standalone cytotoxic agent or as a radiosensitizer in combination with ionizing radiation. Armillaridin, chemically designated as 3-chloro-6-hydroxy-4-methoxy-2-methyl-,3-formyl-2,2a,4a,5,6,7,7a,7b-octahydro-2a-hydroxy-6,6,7b-trimethyl-1H-cyclobut(e)inden-2-yl ester, is a colorless, acicular crystalline compound originally isolated from the mycelial culture of the medicinal fungus *Armillaria mellea*. While prior literature has documented its antibacterial properties, the inventors have now discovered, for the first time, that armillaridin possesses potent and selective antiproliferative activity against human esophageal carcinoma cells, along with the ability to significantly enhance their sensitivity to radiation therapy.

In preferred embodiments, armillaridin is administered to a human patient diagnosed with esophageal cancer, regardless of histological subtype (squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma), disease stage (localized, locally advanced, or metastatic), or prior treatment history. The compound may be formulated as a sterile pharmaceutical preparation suitable for systemic delivery, most preferably via intraperitoneal or intravenous injection, although oral, subcutaneous, or intratumoral routes are also contemplated depending on formulation stability and bioavailability. The active ingredient is dissolved or suspended in a pharmaceutically acceptable carrier, which may include aqueous buffers (e.g., phosphate-buffered saline), co-solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or ethanol (at concentrations not exceeding 0.1% v/v in final dosing solutions to avoid cytotoxicity), surfactants (e.g., polysorbate 80), or lipid-based nanocarriers (e.g., liposomes or micelles) to improve solubility and tumor targeting. Dosage regimens are tailored based on preclinical data indicating efficacy at doses ranging from 10 to 100 mg/kg in murine models, with a preferred human equivalent dose estimated at 5 to 50 mg/kg administered two to five times per week over a treatment cycle of 3 to 6 weeks.

A central aspect of the invention is the demonstration that armillaridin exerts its anticancer effects primarily through the induction of mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis. In vitro experiments using human esophageal cancer cell lines—including CE81T/VGH and TE-2 (squamous cell carcinoma) and BE-3 and SKGT-4 (adenocarcinoma)—revealed that exposure to armillaridin results in characteristic apoptotic morphology, such as nuclear fragmentation, cytoplasmic shrinkage, and membrane blebbing, observable within 48 to 72 hours of treatment. These phenotypic changes correlate with a concentration-dependent decrease in cell viability, as quantified by MTT assay, yielding IC50 values between 3.4 and 6.9 μM after 72 hours of continuous exposure. Mechanistically, armillaridin triggers early apoptotic events, including the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), detected by reduced fluorescence intensity of the potentiometric dye DiOC6(3) in flow cytometry. This mitochondrial depolarization precedes the externalization of phosphatidylserine on the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, a key marker of apoptosis confirmed by increased Annexin V-FITC binding in the absence of propidium iodide uptake (Annexin V+/PI− population). Furthermore, enzymatic assays demonstrate that armillaridin activates caspase-3, a pivotal executioner protease in the apoptotic cascade, and this activation is completely abrogated by pretreatment with the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK, confirming caspase dependence.

Concurrently, armillaridin disrupts normal cell cycle progression, inducing a significant accumulation of cells in the G2/M phase—as evidenced by flow cytometric DNA content analysis showing an increase from approximately 5.9% in untreated controls to 14.0% in treated cells—and a concomitant rise in the sub-G1 fraction (from 4.4% to 8.7%), reflecting DNA fragmentation associated with late-stage apoptosis. This G2/M arrest is particularly consequential in the context of radiation oncology, as cells in this phase exhibit heightened radiosensitivity due to condensed chromatin architecture and impaired DNA double-strand break repair via homologous recombination. Exploiting this vulnerability, the invention utilizes armillaridin as a radiosensitizer by administering it prior to radiation delivery, thereby synchronizing tumor cells in the radiosensitive G2/M window.

The radiosensitizing efficacy of armillaridin was rigorously evaluated using clonogenic survival assays, the gold standard for measuring reproductive cell death post-irradiation. In these experiments, CE81T/VGH cells were exposed to armillaridin at sublethal concentrations (0.125–0.5 μM) for 24 hours, followed by single-fraction irradiation with 6 MeV electron beams at doses ranging from 0.5 to 4 Gy. Post-irradiation, cells were allowed to form colonies over 10–14 days, and surviving fractions were calculated relative to plating efficiency. Data fitted to the linear-quadratic model revealed that armillaridin reduced the radiation dose required to achieve 37% survival (D0) in a concentration-dependent manner, yielding sensitizer enhancement ratios (SER) of up to 1.6. Notably, a clinically relevant radiation dose of 2 Gy—standard in daily fractionation for esophageal cancer—produced significantly greater cell kill when combined with armillaridin than radiation alone, supporting its potential to lower total prescribed doses and reduce normal tissue complications.

In vivo validation was performed using a xenograft model in which 1 × 10⁶ CE81T/VGH cells were implanted subcutaneously into the gluteal region of immunodeficient nude mice. Once tumors reached approximately 0.5 cm in diameter (~21 days post-implantation), animals were randomized into treatment groups receiving either vehicle control, armillaridin (80 mg/kg intraperitoneally three times weekly for 12 doses), or cisplatin (2.5 mg/kg, positive control). Tumor volumes, measured biweekly using calipers and calculated as 0.5 × (largest diameter) × (smallest diameter)², showed significant growth inhibition in the armillaridin group compared to vehicle, with efficacy comparable to cisplatin but without the associated weight loss or leukopenia observed in the cisplatin cohort. Hematological monitoring via retro-orbital blood sampling confirmed stable white blood cell counts throughout treatment, indicating a favorable safety profile.

These findings collectively establish that armillaridin is not only a direct cytotoxic agent against esophageal cancer but also a potent and safe radiosensitizer. Therefore, in a preferred clinical embodiment, a patient undergoing definitive or palliative radiotherapy for esophageal cancer receives armillaridin intravenously at a dose of 20–40 mg/m², administered 24 hours before each radiation fraction. Radiation is delivered via linear accelerator using 6 MV photons or 6–9 MeV electrons, with daily fractions of 1.8–2.0 Gy to a total dose of 50–60 Gy over 5–6 weeks. This combinatorial approach aims to maximize tumor eradication while minimizing esophagitis, pneumonitis, and cardiotoxicity.

The invention further contemplates the use of armillaridin in neoadjuvant or adjuvant settings, either before surgery to downstage tumors or after resection to eliminate micrometastases. Diagnostic imaging (e.g., PET-CT) and biomarker monitoring (e.g., circulating tumor DNA) may be employed to assess response and guide duration of therapy. Additionally, armillaridin may be co-formulated with other targeted agents (e.g., immune checkpoint inhibitors or PARP inhibitors) to exploit synergistic mechanisms, though such combinations remain within the scope of the core invention centered on armillaridin’s intrinsic antineoplastic and radiosensitizing properties.

### EXAMPLES

**Example 1: Preparation and Characterization of Armillaridin**

Armillaridin was isolated from submerged cultures of *Armillaria mellea* grown in potato dextrose broth for 14 days at 25°C under dark conditions. Mycelia were harvested by filtration, lyophilized, and extracted with methanol. The crude extract was partitioned between ethyl acetate and water, and the organic layer was concentrated under reduced pressure. Purification was achieved by repeated silica gel column chromatography using a gradient of hexane/ethyl acetate, followed by preparative thin-layer chromatography, yielding pure armillaridin as colorless needles. Structural identity was confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (¹H and ¹³C NMR), mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), and comparison with published spectral data. For experimental use, armillaridin was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 10 mM stock solution, stored at −20°C, and diluted in culture medium immediately before use to ensure final DMSO concentration did not exceed 0.1% (v/v).

**Example 2: In Vitro Cytotoxicity Assay**

Human esophageal cancer cell lines CE81T/VGH, TE-2, BE-3, and SKGT-4 were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal calf serum, 10 mM NaHCO₃, and 20 mM HEPES in a humidified 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 10³ cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Armillaridin was added at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 20 μM, and cells were incubated for 24, 48, or 72 hours. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay: 1 mg/mL MTT was added for 4 hours, formazan crystals were dissolved in acidified isopropanol, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm. IC50 values were calculated using GraphPad Prism software, revealing time- and concentration-dependent growth inhibition with IC50s of 6.9 μM (CE81T/VGH, 72 h), 5.2 μM (TE-2), 4.1 μM (BE-3), and 3.4 μM (SKGT-4).

**Example 3: Apoptosis and Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Analysis**

CE81T/VGH cells were treated with 10 μM armillaridin for 72 hours. Morphological changes were visualized by Liu’s staining and light microscopy (1000× magnification), showing chromatin condensation and apoptotic bodies. For mitochondrial membrane potential, cells were stained with 40 nM DiOC6(3) for 15 minutes and analyzed by flow cytometry; a 60% decrease in fluorescence intensity indicated ΔΨm collapse. Annexin V/PI staining was performed using a commercial kit: cells were resuspended in binding buffer with Annexin V-FITC and PI, incubated 15 minutes at room temperature, and analyzed by FACS. Armillaridin increased the Annexin V+/PI− population from 3.2% (control) to 28.7%, confirming early apoptosis. Caspase-3 activity was measured using an AFC-conjugated substrate; armillaridin induced a 4.5-fold increase in fluorescence, which was abolished by Z-VAD-FMK pretreatment.

**Example 4: Cell Cycle Analysis**

CE81T/VGH cells treated with 10 μM armillaridin for 48 hours were fixed in 70% ethanol, stained with propidium iodide/RNase, and analyzed by flow cytometry. ModFit software quantified cell cycle phases: G2/M increased from 5.91 ± 0.43% (control) to 14.04 ± 3.17%, and sub-G1 rose from 4.44 ± 0.01% to 8.72 ± 2.47%, consistent with G2/M arrest and apoptotic DNA fragmentation.

**Example 5: Clonogenic Radiosensitization Assay**

CE81T/VGH cells were pretreated with armillaridin (0, 0.125, 0.25, 0.5 μM) for 24 hours, irradiated with 0–4 Gy (6 MeV electrons, 2.4 Gy/min), and plated at 1000 cells/dish. After 14 days, colonies (>50 cells) were stained with crystal violet and counted. Surviving fractions were normalized to plating efficiency (~37%). Linear-quadratic fitting yielded SER values of 1.2 (0.125 μM), 1.4 (0.25 μM), and 1.6 (0.5 μM) at SF=0.37, demonstrating dose-dependent radiosensitization.

**Example 6: In Vivo Xenograft Study**

Male nude mice (4–5 weeks old) received subcutaneous injections of 1 × 10⁶ CE81T/VGH cells in the right gluteal region. When tumors reached ~0.5 cm diameter (day 21), mice (n=6/group) were treated with vehicle (saline + 0.1% DMSO), armillaridin (80 mg/kg i.p., 3×/week × 4 weeks), or cisplatin (2.5 mg/kg i.p., 2×/week). Tumor volume and body weight were measured every 3 days; blood was collected biweekly for leukocyte counts. Armillaridin reduced tumor growth by 62% vs. control (p<0.01) without weight loss or leukopenia, whereas cisplatin caused 12% weight loss and 40% drop in WBCs.

These examples conclusively demonstrate that armillaridin is a potent, selective, and safe agent for esophageal cancer therapy, with dual mechanisms of direct cytotoxicity and radiosensitization, fulfilling the objectives of the present invention.
