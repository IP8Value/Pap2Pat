# DESCRIPTION

- introduce renal disorders  
Renal disorders represent a broad spectrum of pathological conditions affecting the structure and function of the kidneys, leading to impaired filtration, electrolyte imbalance, fluid retention, and progressive loss of renal reserve. These disorders encompass acute and chronic manifestations, often arising from systemic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune conditions, and inherited syndromes. The clinical burden of renal disease is substantial, with increasing global prevalence, high rates of hospitalization, and significant economic strain on healthcare systems. Early detection and accurate risk stratification remain critical challenges, as many patients present with advanced disease before symptoms become apparent. The kidneys’ remarkable functional redundancy often masks underlying injury until irreversible structural damage has occurred, making timely intervention difficult. Consequently, there is an urgent need for sensitive, specific, and non-invasive biomarkers capable of identifying incipient renal dysfunction prior to the onset of overt clinical decline.

- describe clinical features of nephropathies  
Nephropathies are characterized by a constellation of clinical signs and laboratory abnormalities that reflect underlying structural and functional derangements within the renal parenchyma. Common features include proteinuria, hematuria, hypertension, edema, and progressive decline in glomerular filtration rate. The pattern and severity of these manifestations vary depending on the etiology, whether glomerular, tubulointerstitial, vascular, or congenital in origin. Proteinuria, particularly in the nephrotic range, is a hallmark of podocyte injury and glomerular basement membrane disruption, while tubular dysfunction may manifest as urinary concentration defects, electrolyte wasting, or acid-base disturbances. Histopathological evaluation often reveals glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration, which collectively correlate with long-term prognosis. However, these features are frequently assessed only after significant damage has accumulated, limiting their utility for early therapeutic intervention.

- define acute kidney injury (AKI)  
Acute kidney injury is a sudden and often reversible decline in renal function, typically defined by an abrupt increase in serum creatinine levels, a reduction in urine output, or both, occurring over hours to days. It represents a clinical syndrome rather than a single disease entity and can arise from a variety of insults to the kidney, including ischemia, nephrotoxins, sepsis, or obstruction. The condition is associated with high morbidity and mortality, particularly in critically ill patients, and is frequently complicated by electrolyte disturbances, fluid overload, and metabolic acidosis. Despite advances in supportive care, outcomes remain poor, especially when AKI progresses to require renal replacement therapy. Importantly, even transient episodes of AKI are linked to an increased risk of developing chronic kidney disease and end-stage renal disease in the long term.

- describe causes of AKI  
The etiologies of acute kidney injury are broadly categorized into three pathophysiological groups: pre-renal, intrinsic renal, and post-renal causes. Pre-renal injury results from diminished renal perfusion without direct parenchymal damage and is commonly due to hypovolemia, heart failure, or systemic vasodilation. Intrinsic renal causes involve direct injury to the renal parenchyma and include acute tubular necrosis, glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, and vascular disorders such as thrombotic microangiopathy. Post-renal obstruction arises from mechanical blockage of urinary outflow, often due to nephrolithiasis, prostatic hypertrophy, or neoplastic compression. Each category presents distinct clinical and laboratory features, yet overlap frequently complicates diagnosis, particularly in critically ill patients with multiple comorbidities.

- categorize AKI causes  
The classification of acute kidney injury into pre-renal, intrinsic, and post-renal etiologies provides a clinically useful framework for diagnostic evaluation and management. Pre-renal causes are typically reversible with restoration of perfusion, whereas intrinsic renal injury often requires targeted immunosuppressive or supportive therapies depending on the underlying pathology. Post-renal obstruction is usually amenable to mechanical relief, such as catheterization or surgical intervention. Accurate categorization guides therapeutic decisions, but in practice, mixed etiologies are common, particularly in hospitalized patients with sepsis, multiorgan failure, or chronic kidney disease, where distinguishing between categories becomes challenging without advanced biomarkers.

- list pre-renal causes  
Pre-renal causes of acute kidney injury stem from conditions that reduce renal blood flow without direct parenchymal damage. These include volume depletion due to gastrointestinal losses, excessive diuresis, or hemorrhage; decreased cardiac output from heart failure, myocardial infarction, or arrhythmias; systemic vasodilation induced by sepsis or anaphylaxis; and renal vasoconstriction triggered by nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, or calcineurin inhibitors. Renal hypoperfusion leads to activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and sympathetic nervous system, resulting in glomerular filtration rate decline despite preserved tubular integrity.

- list intrinsic causes  
Intrinsic renal causes of acute kidney injury involve structural damage to the kidney itself and include acute tubular necrosis from ischemia or nephrotoxins such as aminoglycosides, contrast media, or myoglobin; glomerulonephritis secondary to immune complex deposition or anti-glomerular basement membrane antibodies; interstitial nephritis due to drug hypersensitivity or infection; and vascular insults such as renal artery stenosis, thrombosis, or vasculitis. These conditions are often associated with active urinary sediment, proteinuria, hematuria, and elevated urinary biomarkers of tubular injury. Histological examination typically reveals inflammatory infiltrates, tubular epithelial cell necrosis, or glomerular crescent formation, depending on the specific pathology.

- list post-renal causes  
Post-renal causes of acute kidney injury result from obstruction of urine flow downstream from the glomeruli. Common causes include bilateral ureteral obstruction due to stones, retroperitoneal fibrosis, or malignancy; bladder outlet obstruction from benign prostatic hyperplasia, neurogenic bladder, or urethral strictures; and obstructive uropathy secondary to pelvic or abdominal tumors. The obstruction leads to increased intratubular pressure, reduced glomerular filtration, and, if prolonged, secondary parenchymal damage. Clinical signs may include flank pain, palpable bladder distension, or anuria, although obstruction may be asymptomatic in elderly or neurologically impaired patients. Imaging studies are essential for diagnosis and prompt relief of obstruction is critical to prevent irreversible renal injury.

- describe diagnosis of AKI  
The diagnosis of acute kidney injury relies on the detection of a rapid rise in serum creatinine concentration, a decrease in urine output, or both, within a short time frame. Historically, diagnosis has been based on serum creatinine thresholds and urine output criteria, but these measures lack sensitivity in early stages and are confounded by factors such as muscle mass, hydration status, and age. The absence of a specific biomarker for early renal injury has limited the ability to detect subclinical damage before functional impairment becomes evident. Consequently, many patients are diagnosed only after significant and potentially irreversible injury has occurred, delaying therapeutic intervention and worsening outcomes.

- introduce consensus criteria for AKI diagnosis  
Consensus criteria for the diagnosis of acute kidney injury have been developed to standardize definitions across clinical and research settings. The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines define AKI as an increase in serum creatinine by at least 0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or an increase to 1.5 times baseline within seven days, or a urine output of less than 0.5 mL/kg/hour for six consecutive hours. These criteria have improved comparability across studies and facilitated epidemiological tracking, yet they remain reactive rather than predictive, identifying injury only after it has already occurred. The search for biomarkers capable of detecting injury prior to creatinine elevation remains a critical unmet need.

- describe rapid increase in serum creatinine  
A rapid increase in serum creatinine is the most widely used clinical indicator of acute kidney injury, reflecting a decline in glomerular filtration rate. Creatinine, a byproduct of muscle metabolism, is freely filtered by the glomerulus and minimally reabsorbed, making its serum concentration a surrogate marker of renal function. However, its rise lags behind the actual onset of tubular injury due to the time required for accumulation in the bloodstream and the influence of non-renal factors such as muscle mass, diet, and hydration. This delay limits its utility in early detection, particularly in patients with pre-existing chronic kidney disease or low muscle mass, where even modest functional losses may not be reflected in creatinine levels.

- discuss limitations of creatinine as a marker  
Serum creatinine suffers from significant limitations as a biomarker for acute kidney injury. Its production rate varies with age, sex, muscle mass, and nutritional status, leading to interindividual variability in baseline values. It is insensitive to early or subclinical injury, as renal functional reserve compensates for up to 50% loss of nephron mass before creatinine rises. Furthermore, creatinine does not distinguish between pre-renal, intrinsic, or post-renal etiologies, nor does it provide information about the underlying histopathological process. Its delayed kinetics mean that therapeutic opportunities are often missed during the critical early window when interventions might prevent progression to irreversible fibrosis or chronic kidney disease.

- introduce alternative biomarkers for AKI  
To overcome the limitations of serum creatinine, numerous alternative biomarkers have been investigated for their ability to detect renal injury earlier and with greater specificity. These include neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1), interleukin-18, cystatin C, and liver-type fatty acid-binding protein. While several of these show promise in research settings, none have been universally adopted in clinical practice due to variability in assay performance, lack of standardized thresholds, and insufficient validation across diverse patient populations. There remains a critical gap for a biomarker that reliably predicts progression from acute injury to chronic structural damage.

- describe current practice for testing renal disease biomarkers  
Current clinical practice for assessing renal disease relies predominantly on serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, supplemented occasionally by imaging and invasive biopsy. Biomarker testing beyond these parameters is largely confined to research settings or specialized centers, and even then, is rarely used to guide real-time clinical decision-making. The absence of validated, commercially available, and cost-effective assays for novel biomarkers has hindered their integration into routine care. Furthermore, most biomarkers are evaluated in isolation rather than as part of a multiplexed panel, limiting their predictive power and clinical utility.

- identify high-risk patients for AKI  
High-risk patients for acute kidney injury include those with pre-existing chronic kidney disease, advanced age, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, sepsis, or exposure to nephrotoxic agents such as contrast media or certain antibiotics. Critically ill patients in intensive care units, those undergoing major surgery, particularly cardiac or vascular procedures, and individuals with volume depletion or hypotension are also at elevated risk. Identifying these patients early allows for targeted monitoring and preventive strategies, yet current tools lack the sensitivity to detect subclinical injury before functional decline becomes apparent. A reliable biomarker capable of stratifying risk prior to creatinine elevation would significantly enhance clinical management.

- describe chronic kidney disease (CKD)  
Chronic kidney disease is defined as persistent structural or functional abnormalities of the kidneys lasting three months or longer, characterized by a glomerular filtration rate below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or evidence of kidney damage such as proteinuria, hematuria, or abnormal imaging. It is a progressive condition often resulting from uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes, glomerulonephritis, or polycystic kidney disease. CKD is frequently asymptomatic in its early stages, and progression to end-stage renal disease occurs insidiously over years. The disease imposes a substantial public health burden due to its association with cardiovascular morbidity, increased mortality, and the high cost of dialysis and transplantation.

- discuss prevalence of CKD  
Chronic kidney disease affects approximately 10% of the global population, with higher prevalence in older adults, individuals with diabetes or hypertension, and those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. Its incidence is rising in parallel with the global epidemics of obesity and type 2 diabetes. Many patients remain undiagnosed until advanced stages, when therapeutic options are limited and the risk of cardiovascular events is markedly elevated. Screening programs are inconsistently implemented, and the lack of reliable early biomarkers contributes to delayed detection and suboptimal management.

- describe need for reliable histological and serological markers  
The progression of chronic kidney disease is highly variable among individuals, and current clinical parameters such as serum creatinine and proteinuria are inadequate for predicting individual trajectories. Histological features such as interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy are strongly associated with long-term outcomes but require invasive biopsy for assessment, limiting their use for serial monitoring. There is a compelling need for non-invasive, reproducible, and quantifiable serological or molecular markers that reflect underlying pathological processes and can be used to stratify risk, guide therapy, and monitor response over time.

- discuss biomarkers predicting progression of CKD  
Several biomarkers have been proposed to predict the progression of chronic kidney disease, including urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, cystatin C, fibroblast growth factor 23, and various inflammatory cytokines. While some show modest associations with outcomes, none have demonstrated sufficient sensitivity, specificity, or predictive power to replace or augment established clinical parameters in routine practice. The heterogeneity of CKD etiologies further complicates biomarker development, as different pathogenic mechanisms may dominate in distinct patient subgroups. A biomarker capable of capturing the core fibrotic and inflammatory processes common to progressive disease would represent a major advance.

- describe end-stage renal disease (ESRD)  
End-stage renal disease is the final stage of chronic kidney disease, characterized by irreversible loss of renal function requiring renal replacement therapy, either dialysis or transplantation, for survival. Patients with ESRD face high rates of cardiovascular complications, malnutrition, anemia, bone mineral disorders, and reduced quality of life. The transition from advanced CKD to ESRD is often unpredictable, and current clinical tools provide limited guidance on timing or likelihood of progression. Early identification of patients at highest risk for ESRD could enable intensified therapeutic strategies aimed at slowing disease progression and delaying the need for dialysis.

- discuss need for identifying patients with adverse renal prognosis  
Identifying patients with an adverse renal prognosis is essential for optimizing resource allocation, tailoring therapeutic intensity, and enrolling high-risk individuals in clinical trials of novel therapies. Current risk stratification models rely on clinical variables such as age, baseline creatinine, proteinuria, and blood pressure, but these factors lack precision and fail to account for underlying molecular pathways driving fibrosis and inflammation. A biomarker that reflects active tissue remodeling and predicts future functional decline would transform clinical management by enabling personalized, proactive interventions before irreversible damage occurs.

- introduce proteinuria as a risk marker  
Proteinuria has long been recognized as a key risk marker for the progression of chronic kidney disease, reflecting glomerular barrier dysfunction and often correlating with the severity of underlying histopathological changes. While its presence is associated with worse outcomes, proteinuria is neither universally present in progressive disease nor consistently predictive across all etiologies. Some patients with minimal proteinuria experience rapid decline, while others with heavy proteinuria remain stable for years. This variability underscores the need for complementary biomarkers that capture non-proteinuric pathways of injury, such as tubulointerstitial fibrosis and inflammation.

- discuss alternative biomarkers for predicting disease course  
Alternative biomarkers under investigation for predicting the course of chronic kidney disease include markers of tubular injury, extracellular matrix remodeling, oxidative stress, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Among these, versican, a hyaluronan-binding proteoglycan, has emerged as a candidate with strong associations to fibrotic processes and declining renal function. Its expression increases in response to injury and correlates with histological damage in multiple renal pathologies. Unlike traditional markers, versican isoforms may reflect active tissue repair and fibrogenesis, offering a more dynamic and mechanistically relevant indicator of disease progression.

- describe cardiovascular disease (CVD) in patients with CKD  
Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of mortality in patients with chronic kidney disease, with risk increasing as renal function declines. The interplay between renal dysfunction and cardiovascular pathology is complex, involving hypertension, vascular calcification, uremic toxins, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction. Traditional cardiovascular risk factors are insufficient to explain the excess risk observed in CKD populations, suggesting that kidney-specific mechanisms contribute to vascular damage. Biomarkers that reflect both renal and vascular injury could provide a more comprehensive assessment of overall prognosis.

- discuss biomarker candidates for CVD in CKD patients  
Biomarker candidates for cardiovascular disease in the context of chronic kidney disease include markers of vascular stiffness, endothelial activation, myocardial strain, and systemic inflammation. These include B-type natriuretic peptide, troponins, soluble ST2, galectin-3, and osteopontin. However, these markers are often non-specific and influenced by non-renal factors, limiting their utility in this population. There is a need for biomarkers that integrate renal and vascular pathophysiology, providing insight into shared mechanisms of tissue injury and fibrosis.

- introduce molecular biomarker candidates for kidney damage  
Molecular biomarker candidates for kidney damage encompass a wide array of proteins, transcripts, and metabolites derived from injured renal cells. These include urinary and plasma proteins such as NGAL, KIM-1, L-FABP, and clusterin, as well as gene expression signatures derived from renal tissue. While many have shown promise in research, few have been validated for clinical use. The identification of a biomarker that is both specific to renal injury and predictive of long-term outcomes remains a central goal in nephrology.

- discuss clinical significance of biomarkers  
The clinical significance of any biomarker lies in its ability to improve diagnostic accuracy, predict outcomes, guide therapy, and monitor response. A biomarker with high clinical utility must be measurable in accessible biological fluids, exhibit reproducible assay performance, demonstrate strong association with disease progression, and add value beyond existing clinical parameters. The integration of such biomarkers into clinical algorithms has the potential to shift renal care from reactive to predictive, enabling earlier intervention and improved patient outcomes.

- describe gene expression analysis of human kidney cell line  
Gene expression analysis of human kidney cell lines has revealed distinct transcriptional profiles associated with injury, inflammation, and fibrosis. Studies using microarray and RNA sequencing technologies have identified genes upregulated in response to nephrotoxic stimuli, hypoxia, and profibrotic cytokines. Among these, the versican gene (VCAN) was consistently overexpressed in proximal tubular epithelial cells and fibroblasts exposed to transforming growth factor-beta, suggesting a role in extracellular matrix remodeling. These findings provided the foundation for investigating versican isoforms as potential biomarkers of renal injury progression.

- describe gene expression profiles of human proximal tubular epithelial cells  
Human proximal tubular epithelial cells exhibit a unique gene expression profile characterized by high metabolic activity, active transport functions, and responsiveness to inflammatory and fibrotic stimuli. Transcriptomic analyses of these cells from patients with proteinuric nephropathies revealed significant upregulation of versican isoforms V0 and V1, particularly in those with progressive disease. This upregulation was independent of traditional clinical markers and correlated more strongly with future decline in renal function than serum creatinine or proteinuria, suggesting a direct role in disease pathogenesis.

- introduce protein biomarkers associated with AKI and CKD  
Protein biomarkers associated with acute and chronic kidney injury include those derived from tubular cells, glomerular structures, and the interstitial compartment. These biomarkers reflect diverse pathological processes such as cell death, inflammation, and fibrosis. While many have been studied, few have demonstrated consistent predictive value across diverse patient populations. Versican, as a major component of the extracellular matrix, has emerged as a candidate with unique properties, as its isoforms are specifically upregulated in response to renal injury and are detectable in both tissue and biological fluids.

- describe biomarkers indicative of progressive disease  
Biomarkers indicative of progressive kidney disease must reflect ongoing structural remodeling rather than transient injury. Versican isoforms V0 and V1 meet this criterion, as their expression is sustained in the setting of chronic inflammation and fibrosis, correlating with the degree of interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy. Unlike markers of acute tubular necrosis, which normalize with recovery, versican levels remain elevated in patients destined for progressive decline, making them uniquely suited for long-term prognostication.

- introduce versican as an AKI risk factor  
Versican, a large chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan, has been identified as a novel risk factor for the progression of acute kidney injury into chronic kidney disease. Its expression is rapidly induced in renal epithelial cells and fibroblasts following injury, and its isoforms V0 and V1 are preferentially upregulated in patients with persistent renal dysfunction. This induction is not merely a consequence of damage but appears to actively contribute to the fibrotic response, positioning versican as both a marker and a mediator of disease progression.

- describe vascular tumor markers  
Vascular tumor markers are proteins expressed in the vasculature of neoplastic tissues and are often associated with angiogenesis, vascular permeability, and perivascular remodeling. While primarily studied in oncology, some of these markers, including versican, are also expressed in the perivascular space of injured kidneys, particularly in areas of fibrosis and inflammation. This overlap suggests shared molecular pathways between tumorigenesis and fibrotic tissue repair, highlighting versican as a molecule with dual relevance in cancer and renal disease.

- discuss pathogenic role of versican in crescentic glomerulonephritis  
In crescentic glomerulonephritis, versican is prominently expressed in the cellular crescents formed by proliferating parietal epithelial cells and infiltrating monocytes. Its presence within these structures suggests a role in promoting cell adhesion, migration, and extracellular matrix deposition, thereby facilitating the formation of fibrocellular crescents. The accumulation of versican in the glomerular tuft and Bowman’s space may contribute to the obliteration of the urinary space and irreversible loss of filtration capacity, implicating it as a key player in the pathogenesis of rapidly progressive glomerular injury.

- introduce inhibition of versican and antibodies against versican  
Inhibition of versican expression or neutralization of its functional domains has been shown to attenuate fibrotic responses in experimental models of kidney disease. Antibodies targeting the N-terminal domain or glycosaminoglycan attachment regions of versican reduce extracellular matrix accumulation, diminish inflammatory cell infiltration, and preserve renal function. These findings suggest that versican is not merely a passive marker but an active contributor to disease progression, making it a viable therapeutic target and a compelling diagnostic biomarker.

- describe determination of versican isoforms V0 and V1  
The determination of versican isoforms V0 and V1 involves the specific detection of their distinct mRNA transcripts or protein products using molecular and immunological techniques. Isoform-specific primers and probes enable quantitative real-time PCR to distinguish V0 and V1 from other variants such as V2 and V3. Similarly, monoclonal or polyclonal antibodies directed against unique epitopes of V0 and V1 allow for precise immunohistochemical localization and quantification in tissue biopsies or serum samples. This specificity is essential, as other isoforms do not correlate with disease progression and may even have opposing biological effects.

- discuss molecular mapping of distributions of PG-M/versican isoforms  
Molecular mapping of versican isoforms reveals a distinct spatial distribution within the kidney. Isoforms V0 and V1 are predominantly localized to the tubulointerstitial compartment, with strong expression in fibroblasts and at the basolateral membranes of proximal tubular epithelial cells. Minimal expression is observed in healthy glomeruli, but significant upregulation occurs in areas of fibrosis, inflammation, and scarring. This compartment-specific expression pattern underscores the role of versican in interstitial remodeling and distinguishes it from glomerular-specific markers.

- describe increased expression of versican isoforms in tumor cell growth  
Increased expression of versican isoforms V0 and V1 has been documented in a variety of epithelial and mesenchymal tumors, where they promote cell proliferation, migration, and resistance to apoptosis. In renal tumors, versican is overexpressed in the stromal compartment surrounding malignant cells, mirroring its expression in fibrotic kidney disease. This parallel suggests that the molecular mechanisms driving fibrosis in chronic kidney disease share similarities with those involved in tumor stroma formation, reinforcing the biological significance of versican as a mediator of tissue remodeling.

- introduce antibodies against NH2-terminal domain or glycosaminoglycan attachment domain of versican  
Antibodies directed against the N-terminal domain or the glycosaminoglycan attachment domains of versican enable highly specific detection of isoforms V0 and V1, which contain these regions, while excluding other variants. These antibodies facilitate the development of immunoassays for quantifying versican in serum, urine, and tissue samples. Their specificity allows for accurate discrimination between pathologically relevant isoforms and non-contributory variants, enhancing diagnostic precision and enabling the design of targeted therapeutic interventions.

- state goal of the present invention  
The goal of the present invention is to provide a method for the early detection, risk stratification, and prognostic assessment of renal disorders by measuring the expression levels of versican isoforms V0 and V1 in biological samples obtained from patients. This method enables the identification of individuals at high risk for progressive renal disease prior to the onset of significant functional decline, allowing for timely and targeted therapeutic interventions. The invention further provides kits and systems for the reliable, standardized, and reproducible detection of these isoforms in clinical and research settings.

- describe method according to the invention  
The method according to the invention involves the quantitative determination of versican isoforms V0 and V1 in a biological sample obtained from a patient suspected of having or at risk for a renal disorder. The sample may include renal tissue, serum, plasma, or urine. The detection is performed using nucleic acid-based techniques such as quantitative real-time PCR with isoform-specific primers or probes, or protein-based techniques such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, Western blotting, or antibody microarrays employing antibodies specific to the N-terminal or glycosaminoglycan attachment domains of versican V0 and V1. The measured levels are compared to reference values derived from healthy individuals or patients with stable disease to determine the risk of progression.

- introduce specific determination of versican isoforms V0 and V1  
The specific determination of versican isoforms V0 and V1 is achieved through the use of molecular tools designed to distinguish these isoforms from other variants such as V2 and V3. For nucleic acid detection, primers and probes are selected to anneal to unique exon junctions present only in V0 and V1 transcripts. For protein detection, antibodies are generated against epitopes encoded by exons 7 and 8, which are included in V0 and V1 but absent in V2 and V3. This specificity ensures that the measurement reflects only the biologically relevant isoforms associated with renal fibrosis and disease progression.

- introduce VCAN isoforms  
The VCAN gene encodes multiple protein isoforms through alternative splicing of exons 7 and 8, resulting in four major variants: V0, V1, V2, and V3. Of these, V0 and V1 are the predominant isoforms expressed in the kidney during pathological conditions, containing the glycosaminoglycan attachment domains that confer their functional properties. V2 is primarily expressed in neural tissues, and V3 lacks glycosaminoglycan chains and is thought to act as a regulatory isoform. The expression of V0 and V1 is tightly linked to fibrotic processes in the renal interstitium, making them the focus of the present invention.

- describe protein sequence of VCAN V0  
The protein sequence of VCAN V0 comprises the N-terminal globular domain, the glycosaminoglycan attachment region containing both chondroitin sulfate chains, and the C-terminal globular domain. The full-length V0 isoform includes the complete sequence encoded by exons 1–6, 7, 8, and 9–12, resulting in a large proteoglycan with extensive glycosylation. This structure enables V0 to interact with hyaluronan, tenascin, and cell surface receptors such as CD44, facilitating extracellular matrix assembly and cell adhesion during tissue repair and fibrosis.

- describe mRNA sequence of VCAN V0  
The mRNA sequence of VCAN V0 corresponds to the full-length transcript generated by the inclusion of exons 7 and 8 during splicing, resulting in a transcript of approximately 7.5 kilobases. This sequence contains the coding regions for the N-terminal and C-terminal globular domains, as well as the central glycosaminoglycan attachment region. The presence of both exons 7 and 8 distinguishes V0 from V1, which lacks exon 8, and from V2 and V3, which lack either or both of these exons. The specific mRNA sequence of V0 is used as a template for the design of isoform-specific primers and probes in diagnostic assays.

- describe protein sequence of VCAN V1  
The protein sequence of VCAN V1 is identical to V0 except for the absence of exon 8, resulting in a shorter glycosaminoglycan attachment region containing only the chondroitin sulfate chain encoded by exon 7. Despite this truncation, V1 retains the ability to bind hyaluronan and interact with cell surface receptors, contributing to extracellular matrix organization and fibroblast activation. The absence of exon 8 does not impair its pathogenic role in renal fibrosis, and its expression is equally correlated with progressive renal disease as V0.

- define term "VCAN0 and/or VCAN1"  
The term “VCAN0 and/or VCAN1” refers collectively to the two specific isoforms of the versican gene that are associated with progressive renal disease, including both the full-length V0 isoform and the truncated V1 isoform, each defined by the presence of exon 7 and the inclusion or exclusion of exon 8, respectively. This term encompasses any biological sample or measurement in which the expression of either or both isoforms is detected and quantified for the purpose of diagnosing, predicting, or monitoring renal disorders.

- associate VCAN expression with disease progression  
The expression of VCAN0 and VCAN1 is strongly associated with the progression of renal disease, as evidenced by elevated levels in patients with declining glomerular filtration rate, increasing interstitial fibrosis, and poor long-term outcomes. Unlike traditional markers such as serum creatinine or proteinuria, which reflect functional impairment or glomerular permeability, VCAN0 and VCAN1 expression directly correlates with the underlying fibrotic and inflammatory processes driving tissue remodeling. Elevated levels of these isoforms precede significant changes in creatinine and predict future decline with greater accuracy than conventional clinical parameters.

- describe expression of VCAN isoforms in kidney cells  
The expression of VCAN isoforms V0 and V1 is predominantly localized to renal tubular epithelial cells and interstitial fibroblasts, with minimal expression in healthy glomeruli or endothelial cells. In diseased kidneys, expression is markedly upregulated in areas of tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis, where these isoforms contribute to extracellular matrix accumulation and cell-matrix interactions. The expression pattern is consistent across multiple etiologies of chronic kidney disease, including diabetic nephropathy, IgA nephropathy, and lupus nephritis, indicating a common pathogenic pathway.

- describe detection of VCAN in kidney biopsies  
Detection of VCAN isoforms in kidney biopsies is performed using quantitative real-time PCR for mRNA or immunohistochemistry for protein, employing isoform-specific primers and antibodies. In tissue sections, VCAN0 and VCAN1 are visualized as intense staining in the tubulointerstitial compartment, particularly at the basolateral surfaces of tubules and within fibrotic areas. The intensity of staining correlates with the degree of fibrosis and predicts future decline in renal function, providing a histological biomarker that can be integrated into routine diagnostic pathology workflows.

- describe classification of chronic kidney disease  
Chronic kidney disease is classified according to etiology, glomerular filtration rate, and level of albuminuria, as defined by the KDIGO guidelines. However, this classification does not adequately capture the heterogeneity of disease progression. The present invention introduces a molecular subclassification based on the expression levels of VCAN0 and VCAN1, allowing for the identification of patients with high fibrotic activity who are at greatest risk of progression, regardless of their traditional clinical category. This molecular stratification enhances prognostic precision and guides personalized therapeutic approaches.

- describe use of inventive method for early diagnosis  
The inventive method enables early diagnosis of progressive renal disease by detecting elevated levels of VCAN0 and VCAN1 in biological samples prior to the onset of significant functional impairment. This allows for the identification of patients in the preclinical or early clinical stages of fibrosis, when interventions such as renin-angiotensin system blockade, anti-fibrotic agents, or lifestyle modifications may be most effective. Early diagnosis based on this method can prevent or delay the progression to end-stage renal disease, reducing the need for dialysis and transplantation.

- define term "patients"  
The term “patients” refers to individuals who are suspected of having, diagnosed with, or at risk for a renal disorder, including but not limited to chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, diabetic nephropathy, glomerulonephritis, or hypertensive nephrosclerosis. These individuals may be asymptomatic or may present with clinical signs such as proteinuria, hypertension, or reduced glomerular filtration rate. The inventive method is applicable to both pediatric and adult populations and is designed for use in clinical, research, and diagnostic laboratory settings.

- describe method of assessing risk of renal disorder  
The method of assessing risk of a renal disorder involves obtaining a biological sample from a patient, determining the expression level of VCAN0 and/or VCAN1 in the sample, and comparing the measured level to a reference value derived from healthy individuals or patients with stable disease. A level significantly higher than the reference value indicates an elevated risk of progressive renal disease. The method may be performed using nucleic acid or protein detection techniques and may be integrated into a diagnostic algorithm alongside traditional clinical parameters to enhance predictive accuracy.

- describe detection of VCAN isoforms in sample  
Detection of VCAN isoforms in a biological sample is achieved through the use of isoform-specific molecular probes or antibodies that selectively bind to VCAN0 and VCAN1 without cross-reacting with other versican variants. For nucleic acid detection, reverse transcription followed by quantitative PCR with exon-junction-specific primers is employed. For protein detection, immunoassays utilizing monoclonal antibodies directed against the N-terminal or glycosaminoglycan attachment domains are used. These methods allow for precise quantification of the pathologically relevant isoforms in serum, urine, or tissue.

- describe comparison with reference values  
Comparison of the measured expression level of VCAN0 and/or VCAN1 with reference values is performed using statistical thresholds established from large cohorts of healthy individuals and patients with stable renal disease. These reference values are derived from population-based studies and are adjusted for age, sex, and comorbidities. A value exceeding the upper limit of the reference range is indicative of increased risk for progressive renal disease. The comparison may be performed using automated software platforms integrated into diagnostic laboratory systems.

- describe use of microarray hybridization  
Microarray hybridization is employed to simultaneously assess the expression levels of VCAN0 and VCAN1 alongside other renal biomarkers in a single assay. RNA extracted from renal tissue or peripheral blood mononuclear cells is labeled and hybridized to oligonucleotide arrays containing probes specific to the unique exon junctions of VCAN0 and VCAN1. The resulting signal intensity is quantified and normalized to housekeeping genes, providing a high-throughput platform for biomarker profiling in research and clinical validation studies.

- describe use of PCR-based detection methods  
PCR-based detection methods, including quantitative real-time PCR and digital PCR, are used to amplify and quantify the mRNA transcripts of VCAN0 and VCAN1 with high sensitivity and specificity. Isoform-specific primers are designed to span exon-exon junctions unique to each variant, ensuring selective amplification. These methods are highly reproducible, require minimal sample volume, and are compatible with routine clinical laboratory workflows, making them ideal for implementation in diagnostic settings.

- describe gene expression profile  
The gene expression profile associated with progressive renal disease includes a distinct upregulation of VCAN0 and VCAN1 transcripts, along with co-expression of other fibrosis-related genes such as collagen type I, fibronectin, and transforming growth factor-beta. This profile is distinct from that of stable disease or acute injury and provides a molecular signature that can be used to classify patients according to their risk of progression. The profile is detectable in both tissue and circulating cells, enabling non-invasive monitoring.

- describe method for determining risk of renal disorders  
The method for determining risk of renal disorders involves the quantitative measurement of VCAN0 and/or VCAN1 expression in a biological sample, followed by comparison with a validated reference range. The result is interpreted as low, moderate, or high risk based on predefined thresholds. This method may be combined with clinical data such as serum creatinine, eGFR, and proteinuria to generate a composite risk score. The method is applicable to both diagnostic and prognostic purposes and may be used to guide therapeutic decisions and patient monitoring schedules.

- describe use of oligonucleotides for hybridization  
Oligonucleotides designed to be complementary to unique sequences within the VCAN0 and VCAN1 transcripts are used for hybridization in microarray or bead-based platforms. These oligonucleotides are chemically modified to enhance binding specificity and stability, and are immobilized on solid supports such as glass slides or magnetic beads. Hybridization with labeled RNA or cDNA from patient samples allows for the simultaneous detection of multiple isoforms and other biomarkers in a single assay, enabling high-throughput analysis.

- introduce chronic kidney disease  
Chronic kidney disease is a progressive, often asymptomatic condition characterized by structural and functional abnormalities of the kidneys that persist for three months or longer. It is associated with a high burden of morbidity and mortality, particularly due to its strong association with cardiovascular disease and progression to end-stage renal disease. Despite advances in clinical management, a significant proportion of patients experience rapid decline in renal function, and current diagnostic tools lack the sensitivity to predict individual trajectories. There is an urgent need for biomarkers that reflect the underlying pathological processes driving fibrosis and inflammation.

- describe causes and consequences of CKD  
Chronic kidney disease arises from a variety of causes, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and recurrent acute kidney injury. The consequences of CKD include progressive loss of glomerular filtration, electrolyte imbalances, anemia, bone mineral disorders, and increased cardiovascular risk. The disease often progresses silently, with patients remaining undiagnosed until advanced stages, when therapeutic options are limited and the risk of mortality is substantially elevated.

- motivate need for biomarkers for CKD  
The need for reliable biomarkers for chronic kidney disease is driven by the limitations of current diagnostic tools, which rely on serum creatinine and proteinuria—markers that are insensitive to early injury and do not reflect the underlying fibrotic processes. Without a means to identify patients at highest risk for progression, therapeutic interventions are often initiated too late to prevent irreversible damage. A biomarker that accurately predicts disease trajectory would transform clinical care by enabling early, personalized, and proactive management.

- introduce versican as a potential biomarker  
Versican, a hyaluronan-binding proteoglycan, has emerged as a promising biomarker for chronic kidney disease due to its strong association with interstitial fibrosis and progressive renal function decline. Its isoforms V0 and V1 are specifically upregulated in the tubulointerstitial compartment of diseased kidneys and correlate more strongly with long-term outcomes than traditional clinical parameters. This makes versican a compelling candidate for integration into diagnostic and prognostic algorithms.

### Example 1

- describe patient cohort and kidney biopsies  
A cohort of 74 patients with proteinuric nephropathies undergoing diagnostic kidney biopsy was enrolled in this study. Biopsies were obtained as part of routine clinical care, and surplus cryopreserved tissue was stored for research purposes. Diagnoses included focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis, membranous nephropathy, and other glomerular and tubulointerstitial diseases. - outline RNA isolation and real-time PCR methods  
Total RNA was extracted from frozen kidney biopsy tissue using the RNeasy Micro Kit. RNA integrity was confirmed by electrophoresis, and cDNA was synthesized using reverse transcription. These experiments were performed to assess the cellular sources of versican and the regulatory pathways involved in its upregulation.

- present results of versican isoform expression analysis  
Expression of VCAN0 and VCAN1 was significantly higher in patients with progressive disease compared to those with stable disease, with fold-changes of 1.56 and 1.67, respectively. No significant differences were observed for VCAN2 or VCAN3. Expression levels of VCAN0 and VCAN1 correlated with serum creatinine at follow-up but not at baseline, indicating their predictive rather than reactive nature.

- correlate versican isoform expression with eGFR  
A significant inverse correlation was observed between VCAN0 and VCAN1 expression and estimated glomerular filtration rate at follow-up, with correlation coefficients of −0.337 and −0.387, respectively. Patients were classified as stable or progressive based on the occurrence of doubling of serum creatinine or progression to end-stage renal disease during follow-up. Of the 74 patients, 22 progressed, while 52 remained stable. VCAN0 and VCAN1 expression was significantly elevated in the progressive group, with p-values of 0.034 and 0.011, respectively.

- compare versican isoform expression between stable and progressive patients  
The median expression of VCAN0 in the progressive group was 1.56 times higher than in the stable group, and VCAN1 was 1.67 times higher. No significant correlation was found between versican expression and proteinuria at biopsy. Similarly, versican levels did not differ significantly across histological diagnoses. However, when patients with acute kidney injury at biopsy were excluded, VCAN0 correlated with glomerulosclerosis and VCAN1 correlated with tubulointerstitial fibrosis and interstitial inflammation.

- perform linear regression analysis of versican isoform expression and eGFR  
Linear regression analysis revealed that VCAN1 alone explained 14.2% of the variance in serum creatinine at follow-up. When combined with glomerulosclerosis and baseline creatinine, the model explained 24.4% of the variance, demonstrating that versican adds significant predictive value beyond conventional parameters.

The predictive value of VCAN0 and VCAN1 lies in their ability to reflect active fibrotic processes rather than transient injury.
