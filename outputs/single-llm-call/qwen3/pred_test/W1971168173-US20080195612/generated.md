# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND OF THE INVENTION

The field of invention pertains to the computational identification and classification of single-stranded RNA viruses, particularly those belonging to the calicivirus and picornavirus families, which are responsible for a majority of non-bacterial epidemic outbreaks in human and animal populations. These viruses exhibit extraordinary genetic plasticity due to high mutation rates during replication, resulting in a vast and continuously evolving population of distinct strains. Accurate strain recognition is critical for epidemiological surveillance, outbreak tracing, vaccine design, and clinical management, yet remains a persistent challenge due to the lack of standardized, reliable, and scalable methods for strain discrimination. Traditional approaches to strain classification rely heavily on sequence homology comparisons using complete or partial genomic regions, often employing pairwise alignment scores and threshold-based clustering to assign sequences to genogroups or serotypes. However, such methods are inherently limited by their dependence on arbitrary similarity cut-off values that vary across virus families, lack biological interpretability, and fail to account for phylogenetic context. These thresholds are often derived empirically from small datasets and are not generalizable across genera or when partial sequences are analyzed, leading to inconsistent classifications and misassignments. 

Immunoassays and reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) based diagnostics, while widely used, are constrained by their requirement for prior knowledge of antigenic or primer-binding sites, which are frequently altered by mutation, rendering them ineffective against emerging variants. Furthermore, many caliciviruses, such as noroviruses, cannot be cultured in vitro, eliminating the possibility of serological characterization and forcing reliance on computational methods for strain inference. Existing computational tools that utilize sliding-window alignments or global sequence identity metrics suffer from high computational overhead, sensitivity to reference sequence selection, and an inability to resolve subtle phylogenetic distinctions among closely related strains. Recent attempts to automate strain classification have employed machine learning or clustering algorithms, but these methods often lack transparency, require extensive training data, and produce outputs that are difficult to validate biologically. The phylogenetic relationships among caliciviruses and picornaviruses are further complicated by the fact that their capsid proteins—VP1 in caliciviruses and VP1–VP4 in picornaviruses—are the primary targets of host immune responses and thus the most variable regions of the genome. While crystal structures of capsid proteins from select strains are available, these structural insights have not been systematically integrated into strain classification frameworks. 

The challenge of distinguishing subtle sequence variations among strains, particularly when only partial capsid sequences are available, remains unresolved. Homology-based similarity metrics are ill-suited for this task because they treat all positions in the sequence as equally informative, ignoring the fact that only a subset of residues—those that define phylogenetic branching points—are functionally and evolutionarily significant for strain discrimination. Current methods also fail to account for the hierarchical nature of viral evolution, where strain distinctions emerge progressively through nested phylogenetic partitions rather than through global divergence. As the number of available sequences grows exponentially, the computational burden of pairwise comparisons becomes prohibitive, and the risk of misclassification increases due to noise, alignment artifacts, and incomplete taxonomic representation. Although some recent methods have attempted to genotype sequences using consensus-based or motif-driven approaches, they remain confined to specific virus genera, lack cross-family applicability, and do not provide mechanistic insight into the residues that drive strain divergence. A critical gap exists in the ability to automate strain prediction with high accuracy, biological relevance, and computational efficiency across the full spectrum of caliciviruses and picornaviruses, especially when working with partial sequences or in the absence of prior genus knowledge. The need for a robust, scalable, and phylogenetically grounded computational method that can resolve strain identity from both complete and fragmented capsid sequences has remained unmet.

## SUMMARY OF THE INVENTION

The invention comprises a software product that enables the automated identification and classification of strains of single-stranded RNA viruses belonging to the calicivirus and picornavirus families through the use of unique, phylogenetically conserved residue patterns derived from partitioned capsid sequence trees. Unlike conventional methods that rely on global sequence similarity scores, this invention employs a novel residue-wise comparison framework that leverages evolutionary partitions to isolate and compare only those amino acid positions that are diagnostic of branching events within genus-specific phylogenetic trees. The software identifies strains by matching target capsid sequences—whether complete or partial—against precomputed databases of characteristic residues that distinguish between phylogenetic subgroups at each partition level. This approach eliminates the need for arbitrary similarity thresholds and instead relies on the cumulative evidence of multiple residue matches across successive phylogenetic tiers to determine the most probable strain classification. The invention further enables the detection of recombination events and spontaneous mutations by analyzing incongruities in residue patterns across different genomic regions or by identifying isolated residue deviations that do not alter overall tree topology. The computer program product is implemented as a modular, cross-platform software suite with a graphical user interface that accepts FASTA-formatted capsid sequences, automatically selects the appropriate genus database if unknown, and outputs strain assignments with partition-wise residue match details. The invention provides a generalizable, scalable, and biologically interpretable solution for strain identification that is applicable across all known genera of caliciviruses and picornaviruses, significantly improving accuracy, speed, and reliability over existing methods.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF THE INVENTION

The invention is embodied in a software product designed for the automated strain identification of caliciviruses and picornaviruses using capsid protein sequences. The software operates by constructing and querying phylogenetically partitioned databases of characteristic amino acid residues that uniquely define evolutionary branches within each virus genus. These databases are derived from reference capsid sequences aligned and organized into partitioned phylogenetic trees, where each partition corresponds to a specific evolutionary depth and isolates a subset of sequences that share a common ancestral node. The application of this software enables the classification of both complete and partial capsid sequences without requiring prior knowledge of the virus genus, by first determining genus membership through rapid comparison with representative sequences from each genus database, followed by detailed strain assignment through residue matching across successive partitions. The embodiment of the present invention involves the creation of genus-specific databases for all four calicivirus genera—norovirus, sapovirus, lagovirus, and vesivirus—and all nine picornavirus genera—including enterovirus, rhinovirus, hepatovirus, kobuvirus, parechovirus, apthovirus, cardiovirus, erbovirus, and teschovirus—each populated with residue patterns that distinguish sequence groups at ten equally spaced phylogenetic partitions. 

The method is demonstrated by aligning a target capsid sequence with a reference sequence from the appropriate genus database, then systematically comparing each residue position in the target against the characteristic residues stored for each group within each partition, beginning at partition two and proceeding sequentially through partition ten. At each partition, only those groups that are phylogenetically connected to the previously selected group are evaluated, drastically reducing computational complexity. Residue matches are flagged upon confirmation, and ambiguities—such as equal numbers of matches across multiple groups—are preserved for resolution in subsequent partitions, ensuring that no potentially diagnostic residue is prematurely discarded. The program accepts input sequences in FASTA format, allows user selection of reference sequences or automatic genus determination, and provides output detailing the strain assignment, the partition-wise residue matches, and any detected ambiguities or anomalies. The software is implemented in Perl for core computational functions and Java for the graphical user interface, enabling seamless operation on Windows and Linux platforms with X-Windows support for remote execution. The method is validated through extensive testing on over 300 complete and partial capsid sequences from public databases, including noroviruses, polioviruses, coxsackieviruses, echoviruses, and other enteroviruses, achieving 100% accuracy in strain assignment when compared to established classifications and demonstrating robustness even when up to 80% of the sequence is missing.

### Capsid Embodiment of the Invention

The capsid embodiment of the invention centers on the use of amino acid residues within the VP1 subunit of picornaviruses and the single capsid protein of caliciviruses as the primary determinants of strain identity. These residues are selected not for their overall conservation but for their ability to distinguish between phylogenetically distinct clades at each partition level of the genus tree. Databases of these characteristic residues are created by first constructing neighbor-joining phylogenetic trees for each virus genus using complete capsid sequences aligned with the Gonnet-250 distance matrix model. Each tree is divided into ten equally spaced partitions based on maximum evolutionary distance, with each partition representing a distinct level of divergence. Within each partition, sequences are grouped by their shared ancestral node, and consensus residues are determined for each group through multiple sequence alignment. Residues that are conserved within a group but differ between groups are designated as characteristic residues and stored in two-dimensional arrays indexed by genus, partition, group, and residue position. 

Residue-wise comparisons of a target sequence are performed by aligning it to a reference sequence from the appropriate genus database, then scanning each partition sequentially to count matches between the target residues and the characteristic residues of each group. The group with the highest number of matches is selected, and subsequent partitions are restricted to those phylogenetically connected to the selected group. This tree-guided search ensures optimal computational efficiency and prevents the algorithm from exploring irrelevant branches. Characteristic residues are flagged upon match, except in cases of ambiguity, where matches are retained for future comparison. The method allows for the identification of strains from sequences as short as 20% of the full capsid length, and resolves ambiguities through iterative comparison across partitions. For example, in noroviruses, residue 70V in partition P2 reliably distinguishes genogroup GII from GI, while residue 44A in partition P3 confirms GI membership. In enteroviruses, residue 158G in partition P6 distinguishes poliovirus from other enteroviruses. The program constructs partitioned phylogenetic trees for each genus, generates characteristic residues for each node group, stores them in structured databases, and uses these to match target sequences through a hierarchical, residue-by-residue comparison process. The system identifies closest reference strains by accumulating match scores across partitions, accepts the most probable cluster based on cumulative evidence, and flags potential recombination or mutation events when residue patterns conflict across genomic regions. The method is validated using known strains of norovirus and enterovirus, demonstrating accurate strain identification regardless of reference sequence choice, and achieves processing times of approximately five seconds per sequence, even for large datasets containing hundreds of partial and complete sequences.

## CONCLUSIONS

The RECOVIR software package represents a transformative advancement in the computational identification of strains within the calicivirus and picornavirus families. Unlike existing methods that rely on arbitrary sequence similarity thresholds or global alignment scores, RECOVIR implements a phylogenetically grounded, residue-wise comparison framework that leverages the hierarchical structure of viral evolution to identify strains with unprecedented accuracy and speed. The invention enables the classification of both complete and partial capsid sequences without prior genus knowledge, resolves ambiguities through iterative partition analysis, and detects recombination and spontaneous mutations by analyzing incongruities in residue patterns across genomic regions. By encoding strain-defining residues into partitioned databases derived from robust phylogenetic trees, the method eliminates the subjectivity of cut-off values and provides biologically interpretable results that align with evolutionary relationships. The software’s efficiency, scalability, and cross-genus applicability make it uniquely suited for real-time outbreak analysis, surveillance, and diagnostic applications. The method’s reliance on capsid residues—regions under strong immune selection—ensures that the identified strain distinctions are not merely phylogenetic artifacts but reflect functionally relevant evolutionary divergence. RECOVIR thus provides a reliable, automated, and generalizable solution to a long-standing challenge in virology, offering a powerful complement to traditional diagnostic and sequencing approaches.

## Availability and Requirements

The RECOVIR software package is compatible with Windows XP and Linux operating systems. The core computational modules are written in Perl, while the graphical user interface is developed in Java. A functional X-Windows environment, such as Cygwin, is required for remote execution on Linux systems. The software accepts input sequences exclusively in FASTA format and requires no additional proprietary libraries beyond standard Perl and Java runtime environments. The program is distributed with pre-built databases for all four calicivirus genera and all nine picornavirus genera, and includes an update mechanism for incorporating newly characterized sequences. System memory requirements are minimal, with 2 GB of RAM sufficient for processing up to 1,000 sequences in a single batch. The software does not require internet connectivity for strain identification, though database updates may be downloaded via secure file transfer protocols.
