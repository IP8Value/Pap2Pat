# DESCRIPTION

## TECHNICAL FIELD

- relate to monoclonal antibody production

The present invention relates to novel methods for the production of monoclonal antibodies, particularly those targeting post-translationally modified antigens such as phosphorylated peptides, with high specificity and sensitivity. The invention provides a robust, reproducible, and scalable platform for isolating rare antibody-secreting plasma cells directly from immunized animals, enabling the derivation of monoclonal antibodies that recognize epitopes defined by precise molecular modifications. This method overcomes longstanding limitations in conventional hybridoma and single-cell cloning technologies by integrating intracellular antigen staining with controlled fixation, membrane lysis, and mRNA preservation techniques that maintain both antigen-binding integrity and transcriptomic fidelity. The invention is particularly suited for applications requiring antibodies against low-abundance, structurally subtle epitopes, including phospho-specific sites on tumor suppressors, signaling kinases, and other regulatory proteins where conventional immunization strategies fail to yield selective reagents. The disclosed process enables the direct linkage of antibody sequence to functional specificity at the single-cell level, thereby facilitating the rapid generation of high-affinity monoclonal antibodies without reliance on immortalized cell lines or extensive screening pipelines.

## BACKGROUND ART

- introduce antibodies
- motivate need for novel production platform
- limitations of prior art methods
- summarize prior art references

Monoclonal antibodies have become indispensable tools in biomedical research, diagnostics, and therapeutic development due to their exceptional specificity and reproducibility. Historically, monoclonal antibodies have been generated through the fusion of antigen-primed B cells with myeloma cells to form hybridomas, or more recently, through single-cell sorting of live antibody-secreting cells followed by PCR-based amplification of immunoglobulin variable regions. While these approaches have been widely adopted, they suffer from critical limitations when applied to antigens bearing post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation, acetylation, or glycosylation. Conventional methods rely on surface-expressed immunoglobulins for cell identification, yet terminally differentiated plasma cells express minimal surface immunoglobulin, rendering them invisible to standard flow cytometric sorting. Alternative strategies that attempt to detect intracellular immunoglobulins in live cells are compromised by the instability of mRNA during permeabilization, leading to degradation and failure of downstream cDNA synthesis. Furthermore, prior methods lack the ability to subtract cells that bind to unmodified versions of the target antigen, resulting in cross-reactive antibodies that fail to distinguish modified from unmodified forms. Existing protocols also fail to address the covalent crosslinking of RNA to proteins induced by paraformaldehyde fixation, which severely impedes reverse transcription and amplification of full-length immunoglobulin variable genes. These shortcomings have collectively hindered the development of antibodies against phosphorylation-dependent epitopes, despite their critical roles in cellular signaling, disease progression, and therapeutic targeting. The prior art, including methods described in publications such as NPL 4, relies on live-cell staining without fixation, which introduces high background noise, poor signal-to-noise ratios, and inconsistent recovery of mRNA, ultimately resulting in low success rates for generating functional monoclonal antibodies from rare antigen-specific plasma cells.

## SUMMARY OF INVENTION

- motivate need for novel method
- introduce novel method
- summarize advantages of novel method

The limitations of prior art methods in generating monoclonal antibodies against post-translationally modified antigens stem from an inability to simultaneously preserve antigen-binding activity, maintain mRNA integrity, and selectively isolate rare antibody-secreting cells based on precise epitope recognition. These challenges have impeded progress in the development of highly specific reagents for phosphorylation-dependent targets, which are essential for studying dynamic signaling networks and developing targeted therapeutics. To address these shortcomings, the present invention introduces a novel method for the production of monoclonal antibodies that combines reversible chemical fixation, intracellular antigen staining, controlled membrane lysis, and optimized mRNA recovery to enable the isolation and molecular characterization of single antibody-secreting plasma cells with unprecedented fidelity. The method is distinguished by its ability to fix cells in a manner that preserves both intracellular immunoglobulins and their associated mRNA, while permitting subsequent reversal of crosslinks to restore RNA integrity for amplification. This innovation allows for the direct correlation of antibody sequence with antigen-binding specificity, even for epitopes present on less than 0.01% of the total lymphocyte population. The advantages of this approach include the elimination of non-specific binding through subtraction staining, the suppression of mRNA degradation via RNase inhibition throughout the workflow, and the successful amplification of full-length immunoglobulin variable genes from fixed, stained single cells—something previously unattainable. The method enables the rapid, high-throughput generation of monoclonal antibodies with nanomolar affinity against previously inaccessible targets, including phospho-specific epitopes on p53, CHK2, RORγt, and other disease-relevant proteins, without the need for hybridoma cloning or extensive screening.

Given the low abundance of antigen-specific plasma cells in immunized animals and the weak signal strength of labeled antigens compared to surface markers, prior art methods often fail to distinguish true antigen-binding cells from background noise or non-specifically stained populations. The present invention anticipates these difficulties by introducing a multi-step procedure that begins with a controlled fixation step using a reversible crosslinking agent to immobilize intracellular immunoglobulins and associated mRNA without compromising their structural integrity. This is followed by a cell membrane lysis step that permeabilizes the plasma membrane using a mild detergent under optimized conditions to allow entry of labeled antigens while preserving RNA. A staining step then employs fluorescently labeled, chemically synthesized antigens to label only those cells expressing antibodies with specific binding affinity. Subsequent cell isolation is performed using fluorescence-activated cell sorting to physically separate single cells that exhibit high signal for the target antigen and low or absent signal for competing or unmodified antigens. The monoclonal antibody preparation step then proceeds with amplification of immunoglobulin heavy and light chain variable regions, cloning into expression vectors, and transient transfection to produce recombinant antibodies. Optional steps include the addition of reduction agents to minimize disulfide bond interference and the use of phosphatase inhibitors throughout the process to preserve phosphorylation-dependent epitopes. Each step is carefully calibrated to ensure maximal recovery of functional mRNA and minimal loss of antigen-binding specificity, enabling the production of monoclonal antibodies with defined epitope recognition that were previously unobtainable.

## DESCRIPTION OF EMBODIMENT

- describe method for isolating a cell capable of producing a monoclonal antibody
- describe method for producing a monoclonal antibody
- outline steps of the method

The invention provides a comprehensive method for isolating a single cell capable of producing a monoclonal antibody and subsequently producing the antibody itself through molecular cloning and recombinant expression. The method comprises a sequence of interdependent steps designed to preserve both the functional and molecular integrity of rare antibody-secreting plasma cells. The first step involves the fixation of cells derived from immunized animals using a reversible crosslinking reagent such as paraformaldehyde, which stabilizes intracellular immunoglobulins and their associated mRNA without inducing irreversible aggregation. The second step entails the lysis of the cell membrane using a non-ionic detergent at a concentration sufficient to permit antigen penetration while maintaining RNA stability. The third step involves the incubation of fixed and lysed cells with a fluorescently labeled antigen of interest, optionally in the presence of a competing unlabeled antigen to subtract cross-reactive cells. The fourth step is the isolation of single cells exhibiting specific binding to the labeled antigen by fluorescence-activated cell sorting, with stringent gating to exclude cells that bind to non-target antigens. The fifth step includes the immediate collection of sorted single cells into a lysis buffer containing RNase inhibitors and protease K, followed by a heating treatment at 50°C to decrosslink RNA from proteins. The sixth step involves the synthesis of cDNA from the recovered mRNA using homopolymer tailing and reverse transcription, followed by amplification of the immunoglobulin variable regions via 5′ RACE PCR. The seventh step comprises the cloning of amplified heavy and light chain variable genes into mammalian expression vectors, their co-transfection into host cells, and the recovery of secreted monoclonal antibodies from the culture supernatant. The method may further include optional steps such as the addition of phosphatase inhibitors during staining and lysis, the use of RNase-free synthetic peptides as antigens, and the inclusion of a reduction step using dithiothreitol to prevent disulfide-mediated aggregation of immunoglobulins during processing.

The fixing step is the foundational component of the method, designed to immobilize intracellular immunoglobulins and their associated mRNA within the cellular architecture to prevent degradation and loss during subsequent processing. This step begins with the immunization of a non-human animal, such as a guinea pig, rabbit, or rat, with an antigen of interest, which may include chemically synthesized peptides conjugated to carrier proteins such as keyhole limpet hemocyanin. The antigen of interest may be phosphorylated at one or more specific residues, such as threonine 18 of p53 or threonine 68 of CHK2, to elicit antibodies recognizing post-translationally modified epitopes. Immunization is typically performed via intramuscular injection with an adjuvant such as TiterMax Gold, and immune response is verified by measuring serum antibody titers against the target antigen using ELISA. Upon confirmation of immunity, lymph nodes, spleen, or bone marrow are harvested and processed into single-cell suspensions. The cells are then fixed in a solution of 2% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline for a duration of 5 to 15 minutes at 4°C, a condition determined to optimally preserve both immunoglobulin conformation and mRNA integrity. The use of a reversible crosslinking agent is critical, as irreversible fixatives such as glutaraldehyde destroy RNA accessibility and prevent downstream amplification. Paraformaldehyde is preferred due to its ability to form methylene bridges between amino groups that can be reversed under controlled conditions. After fixation, cells are separated from the fixative solution by centrifugation and washed with phosphate-buffered saline to remove excess reagent. The fixation process ensures that intracellular immunoglobulins remain spatially retained and that mRNA molecules remain tethered to their associated proteins, preventing diffusion and degradation. The importance of this step lies in its capacity to lock the antigen-binding state of the antibody at the time of fixation, thereby preserving the functional specificity of the cell’s secreted product. Subsequent decrosslinking of mRNA is achieved through proteolytic digestion with proteinase K at elevated temperatures, which cleaves protein-RNA crosslinks without degrading the nucleic acid backbone. Optimal fixation conditions vary depending on cell type and antigen, and may be adjusted by altering fixation time, temperature, or fixative concentration to maximize recovery of amplifiable RNA while maintaining binding activity. The success of the entire method hinges on the precision of this step, as inadequate fixation leads to RNA loss, while over-fixation impedes decrosslinking and cDNA synthesis.

### (2) Cell Membrane Lysis Step

The cell membrane lysis step is performed to render the plasma membrane permeable to fluorescently labeled antigens while preserving the integrity of intracellular components, particularly mRNA. This step is essential because terminally differentiated plasma cells, which are the primary source of high-affinity monoclonal antibodies, do not express sufficient surface immunoglobulin for detection by conventional flow cytometry. By lysing the membrane, the intracellular immunoglobulin pool becomes accessible to labeled antigens, allowing for the specific labeling of cells based on their antigen-binding capacity. The lysis is achieved by incubating fixed cells in a solution containing a non-ionic detergent such as Triton X-100, NP-40, or digitonin, at concentrations ranging from 0.05% to 0.5% for a duration of 5 to 30 minutes at 4°C. The choice of detergent is critical, as ionic detergents such as SDS disrupt protein structure and degrade RNA, whereas overly mild detergents fail to achieve sufficient permeabilization. The concentration and duration of detergent exposure are optimized empirically for each cell type to ensure complete permeabilization without causing cell fragmentation or RNA release. The lysis buffer may also contain stabilizing agents such as RNase inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors to protect the mRNA and preserve phosphorylation-dependent epitopes. Following lysis, cells are washed to remove excess detergent and resuspended in staining buffer. The purpose of this step is to create a transient, controlled window of accessibility that permits the labeled antigen to bind intracellular immunoglobulins while minimizing exposure of RNA to endogenous RNases. The success of downstream staining and sorting depends entirely on the uniformity and reproducibility of this lysis step, as incomplete permeabilization results in false negatives, while excessive lysis leads to RNA degradation and increased background noise. The step is summarized as a precisely timed, temperature-controlled permeabilization that enables intracellular antigen binding without compromising molecular fidelity.

### (3) Staining Step

- describe purpose of staining step

The staining step is designed to selectively label antibody-secreting cells based on their ability to bind a fluorescently conjugated antigen of interest, thereby enabling their identification and isolation by flow cytometry. The purpose of this step is to visualize and distinguish cells producing antibodies with precise epitope specificity, including those recognizing post-translationally modified forms such as phosphorylated peptides. The cell population, following membrane lysis, is incubated with a labeled antigen, typically a synthetic peptide conjugated to a fluorophore such as DyLight 488 or DyLight 550 via streptavidin-biotin chemistry. The labeled antigen is introduced at a concentration sufficient to saturate binding sites without inducing non-specific interactions, typically in the range of 0.1 to 1.0 μg per 10^6 cells. The reaction is carried out in a buffered solution containing RNase inhibitors, phosphatase inhibitors, and blocking agents to minimize non-specific binding. Incubation occurs for 15 to 30 minutes at 4°C to preserve antigen-antibody complexes and prevent internalization or degradation. To enhance specificity, a subtractive staining strategy is employed, in which cells are simultaneously stained with a fluorescently labeled unmodified version of the antigen to identify and exclude cells that bind indiscriminately to both modified and unmodified forms. For example, cells may be stained with a triphosphorylated p53 peptide labeled with DyLight 488 and an unmodified p53 peptide labeled with DyLight 550, allowing for the identification of cells that bind exclusively to the phosphorylated form. The staining step is concluded by washing the cells to remove unbound antigen and resuspending them in a buffer containing a nuclear stain such as DAPI for viability assessment. The result is a population of cells with distinct fluorescence profiles that reflect their antigen-binding specificity, enabling precise gating and isolation in the subsequent step. The staining step is summarized as a dual-labeling procedure that distinguishes antigen-specific from cross-reactive plasma cells with high resolution.

The cell isolation step is the critical juncture at which antigen-specific plasma cells are physically separated from the heterogeneous population based on their fluorescence signature. Cells are analyzed using a high-resolution fluorescence-activated cell sorter equipped with multiple detection channels to distinguish signals from the labeled antigen, the unmodified competitor antigen, and the anti-immunoglobulin secondary stain. Single cells exhibiting high fluorescence for the target antigen and low or absent fluorescence for the unmodified antigen are gated and sorted into individual wells of a 96-well plate, each containing a lysis buffer supplemented with RNase inhibitors such as RNaseOUT, SUPERase·In, or ribonucleoside vanadyl complexes. The use of RNase inhibitors is mandatory throughout this step to prevent degradation of the fragile mRNA transcripts that encode the antibody variable regions. The sorting is performed under sterile, RNase-free conditions, with the flow cytometer’s fluidics system pre-rinsed with sodium hypochlorite and RNase-free water to eliminate exogenous contamination. The isolation of single cells ensures that each sorted population originates from a single progenitor, enabling the subsequent recovery of monoclonal antibody sequences without clonal ambiguity. The method allows for the identification of cells that bind with high specificity to phosphorylated epitopes, such as pT18-p53 or pT68-CHK2, even when these cells constitute fewer than 0.01% of the total lymphocyte population. By excluding cells that bind to the unmodified antigen, the method eliminates cross-reactive clones that would otherwise dominate downstream screening. The recovered single cells are immediately processed for cDNA synthesis, ensuring that mRNA remains intact and amplifiable. The isolation step thus serves as the gateway to obtaining a diverse repertoire of antigen-specific monoclonal antibodies, each derived from a single cell and each encoding a unique variable region fragment capable of recognizing the target epitope with high fidelity.

The antigen-specific monoclonal antibody preparing step involves the molecular cloning and recombinant expression of the variable regions of the immunoglobulin heavy and light chains derived from the isolated single cells. Following cDNA synthesis, the variable gene fragments are amplified using 5′ RACE PCR with primers specific to the constant regions of immunoglobulin heavy and light chains. The amplified fragments are then cloned into expression vectors using homologous recombination or restriction enzyme-based ligation, ensuring the correct pairing of heavy and light chain genes from the same cell. The resulting constructs are transfected into mammalian host cells such as Expi293 or 293FT cells, where they are expressed and secreted into the culture supernatant. Throughout this step, RNase inhibitors are maintained in all buffers to prevent degradation of residual mRNA or cDNA templates, ensuring the fidelity of gene amplification and cloning. The expressed antibodies are harvested and purified using protein A affinity chromatography, and their binding specificity is confirmed by ELISA, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence against the target antigen. The amino acid sequences of the variable regions are determined by sequencing the cloned genes, and the corresponding nucleotide sequences are deposited for future use. The resulting monoclonal antibodies exhibit high affinity, often in the sub-nanomolar range, and demonstrate exclusive reactivity toward the intended epitope, such as phosphorylated threonine 18 on p53 or threonine 68 on CHK2, without cross-reactivity to unmodified forms. This step transforms the isolated single cell into a defined, reproducible reagent capable of detecting the target modification in complex biological samples, enabling applications in diagnostics, mechanistic studies, and therapeutic development.

OKT10 hybridoma cells, which secrete an antibody against CD38, were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde for 10 minutes at 4°C, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and stained intracellularly with DyLight 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG. Cells were sorted as single entities into wells containing lysis buffer supplemented with RNaseOUT and proteinase K, followed by incubation at 50°C for one hour to reverse crosslinks. cDNA was synthesized using oligo(dT) magnetic beads and homopolymer tailing, and the immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region was amplified by 5′ RACE PCR. Successful amplification was achieved in 92% of single-cell reactions, whereas non-decrosslinked controls yielded no amplification. The recovered sequences matched the known OKT10 V-gene, confirming the fidelity of the method.

## INDUSTRIAL APPLICABILITY

- describe industrial applicability of the present invention

The present invention has broad industrial applicability across biotechnology, pharmaceutical, diagnostic, and academic research sectors. It enables the rapid, high-throughput generation of monoclonal antibodies against previously inaccessible post-translationally modified epitopes, including phosphorylation sites, acetylated lysines, and glycosylated residues, which are central to cancer biology, neurodegenerative disease, and autoimmune disorders. The method eliminates the need for labor-intensive hybridoma screening and reduces the time to obtain functional antibodies from months to days. The resulting antibodies are suitable for use in diagnostic assays, such as immunohistochemistry and ELISA, for detecting disease-specific protein modifications in clinical samples. In therapeutic development, the method facilitates the discovery of antibodies that selectively target activated signaling states in tumors, such as phosphorylated p53 or CHK2, offering potential for precision oncology. The scalability and reproducibility of the method make it ideal for integration into automated platforms for antibody discovery, and its compatibility with synthetic peptides allows for the targeting of novel epitopes without requiring recombinant protein expression. The invention thus represents a transformative advancement in monoclonal antibody technology with direct commercial value in drug development, biomarker discovery, and molecular diagnostics.
