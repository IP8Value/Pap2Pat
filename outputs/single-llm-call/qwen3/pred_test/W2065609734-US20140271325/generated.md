# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

- limitations of sintering techniques

Traditional sintering methods for refractory metals such as tungsten have long been constrained by inherently slow diffusion kinetics and the high temperatures required to achieve full densification. Conventional pressureless sintering of pure tungsten powders typically demands temperatures exceeding 1,100 °C to 1,200 °C, and even at these elevated conditions, the resulting materials often retain significant porosity due to the low self-diffusion coefficients of tungsten atoms in the solid state. The application of external pressure, as in hot isostatic pressing or spark plasma sintering, has been employed to mitigate these limitations, yet such approaches introduce substantial complexity, restrict component geometry, and impose prohibitive costs for large-scale or irregularly shaped parts. Alternative strategies involving liquid phase sintering require the introduction of low-melting-point additives that, while accelerating densification, invariably lead to microstructural coarsening, grain boundary weakening, and loss of the high-temperature mechanical integrity that tungsten is prized for. Solid-state activated sintering, which relies on grain boundary segregation of trace elements to enhance diffusion, has proven ineffective in tungsten systems due to the extreme stability of its body-centered cubic lattice and the inability of conventional additives to form stable, nanoscale interfacial films at moderate temperatures. Furthermore, attempts to utilize nanocrystalline powders alone have failed to deliver dense, fine-grained bulk materials because the high surface energy of nanoscale particles promotes rapid grain growth during heating, thereby negating the intended microstructural benefits. These persistent challenges have hindered the practical fabrication of bulk, fully dense, nanocrystalline tungsten-based alloys without the use of costly, high-pressure, or non-scalable processing techniques.

## SUMMARY

- motivate nanocrystalline alloy

The development of a new class of nanocrystalline alloys has enabled a paradigm shift in the consolidation of refractory metals by leveraging the synergistic interplay between nanoscale grain structure and thermodynamically driven phase separation. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on external pressure or liquid formation, this invention is grounded in the discovery that supersaturated solid solutions composed of nanocrystalline grains can undergo controlled decomposition during heating, resulting in the spontaneous formation of a secondary phase at particle interfaces and neck regions. This phenomenon, termed nanophase separation sintering, dramatically accelerates mass transport without compromising the integrity of the primary matrix. The resulting materials exhibit full density and ultrafine grain structures that were previously unattainable through pressureless sintering, opening new pathways for the production of high-performance components in aerospace, defense, and energy applications where thermal stability, hardness, and radiation resistance are critical.

- introduce sintering method

The method comprises the mechanical alloying of a refractory metal powder with one or more alloying elements to produce a supersaturated solid solution with a nanocrystalline grain structure, followed by pressureless sintering under a controlled atmosphere at temperatures below the melting point of either constituent. The process does not require external pressure, electric pulses, or specialized equipment beyond a conventional furnace. During heating, the supersaturated matrix decomposes, precipitating a secondary phase that preferentially nucleates at particle surfaces and interparticle necks, thereby creating high-diffusivity pathways for mass transport. This mechanism enables rapid densification at temperatures significantly lower than those required for conventional sintering, while simultaneously suppressing grain growth through the pinning effect of the precipitated phase.

- embodiment of sintering method

In a preferred embodiment, tungsten powder with an average particle size of 1 to 5 micrometers is mechanically alloyed with chromium in a high-energy ball mill under an inert atmosphere for a duration sufficient to achieve complete dissolution of chromium into the tungsten lattice, resulting in a supersaturated body-centered cubic solid solution with an average grain size of less than 20 nanometers. The milled powder is then cold-pressed into a green compact at pressures between 200 and 400 MPa and sintered in a reducing atmosphere of argon containing 1 to 5% hydrogen at a constant heating rate of 5 to 20 °C per minute. The sintering temperature is held between 900 °C and 1,500 °C, during which time the chromium precipitates as a crystalline, non-molten phase along particle boundaries, enabling rapid diffusion of tungsten atoms through the chromium-rich interfacial layers.

- embodiment of sintered alloy

The resulting sintered alloy exhibits a relative density exceeding 98%, with an average grain size ranging from 50 to 200 nanometers, depending on the sintering temperature and time. The microstructure consists of a tungsten-rich matrix interspersed with discrete, nanoscale chromium-rich precipitates located predominantly at grain boundaries and interparticle necks. These precipitates are not continuous films but rather discontinuous, interconnected networks that facilitate diffusion without inducing embrittlement. The alloy retains the high melting point, thermal conductivity, and mechanical strength characteristic of tungsten, while achieving a hardness value greater than 8 GPa, comparable to that of fully dense coarse-grained tungsten but with significantly finer grain structure.

- summarize combinations

The invention encompasses combinations of refractory metals with transition metal additives that form supersaturated solid solutions upon mechanical alloying and subsequently precipitate during sintering to enhance densification. These include tungsten with chromium, titanium, or nickel; chromium with nickel; and combinations thereof. The alloying elements are selected such that they exhibit limited equilibrium solubility in the base metal at room temperature but can be supersaturated through mechanical alloying, and upon heating, form a secondary phase with higher diffusivity for the base metal than the base metal’s self-diffusivity. The presence of multiple stabilizing elements, such as titanium in conjunction with chromium, further enhances thermal stability by inhibiting grain growth during sintering and service.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

- introduce nanocrystalline metals

Nanocrystalline metals are defined as polycrystalline materials in which the average grain size is less than 100 nanometers, resulting in a high volume fraction of grain boundaries that dominate the material’s mechanical and diffusion behavior. Unlike nanopowders, which consist of individual nanoscale particles, nanocrystalline metals in this context refer to micron-sized powder particles that are internally composed of numerous nanoscale grains, preserving the handling characteristics of conventional powders while enabling unique sintering phenomena due to the abundance of internal grain boundaries and short-circuit diffusion pathways.

- describe desirable properties

The desirable properties of such materials include high hardness, elevated thermal stability, resistance to creep at high temperatures, and enhanced radiation tolerance, all of which stem from the fine grain structure and the suppression of dislocation motion by grain boundaries. In refractory metals such as tungsten, these properties are particularly valuable for applications involving extreme thermal and mechanical loads, including plasma-facing components in fusion reactors, cutting tools, and high-temperature structural elements.

- present mechanical test data on nanocrystalline Ni—W alloys

Mechanical testing of sintered nanocrystalline tungsten–chromium alloys reveals a Vickers hardness of 8.2 GPa at a load of 500 g, with a fracture toughness exceeding 6 MPa·m¹/². These values surpass those of conventionally sintered tungsten at equivalent densities and are comparable to those of tungsten–heavy alloys produced via liquid phase sintering, but without the presence of ductile binder phases. The hardness remains stable up to 1,000 °C, demonstrating the effectiveness of the precipitated chromium phase in inhibiting grain growth.

- discuss grain size effect on hardness and activation volume

The Hall–Petch relationship governs the increase in hardness with decreasing grain size, and in this system, hardness increases monotonically as grain size decreases below 150 nanometers. Concurrently, the activation volume for plastic deformation, determined through nanoindentation rate-jump tests, decreases with finer grain sizes, indicating a shift in deformation mechanism from dislocation glide to grain boundary sliding and diffusion-mediated processes.

- motivate control over grain size

Precise control over grain size is essential to balance strength and toughness, as excessive grain growth during sintering leads to loss of mechanical performance. The present invention achieves this control through the in situ formation of precipitates that act as grain boundary pinning agents, thereby stabilizing the microstructure during the sintering cycle and subsequent thermal exposure.

- define nanocrystalline materials

Nanocrystalline materials are characterized by a mean grain size less than 100 nanometers, as determined by X-ray diffraction line broadening analysis using the Scherrer equation, confirmed by transmission electron microscopy. These materials exhibit a high density of grain boundaries, which serve as fast diffusion paths and sites for solute segregation, enabling unique kinetic behaviors not observed in coarse-grained counterparts.

- describe grain size regimes

Grain size regimes are categorized as ultrafine (100–500 nm), nanocrystalline (1–100 nm), and ultra-nanocrystalline (<1 nm). The present invention operates within the nanocrystalline regime, where grain boundary diffusion dominates over lattice diffusion, and the thermodynamic driving force for phase separation is maximized due to the high interfacial energy.

- introduce ultra-fine grain and nanocrystalline grain terms

The term “ultra-fine grain” refers to materials with grain sizes between 100 and 500 nanometers, while “nanocrystalline grain” refers to grain sizes below 100 nanometers. Both regimes are accessible through the method described herein, with the final grain size being tunable by adjusting the sintering temperature, time, and composition of the alloying elements.

- describe grain geometry

The grains in the sintered material exhibit a near-equiaxed morphology with minimal elongation, as confirmed by three-dimensional reconstruction from serial sectioning. Grain boundaries are predominantly high-angle, with misorientation angles exceeding 15 degrees, ensuring effective barrier properties against dislocation motion.

- describe particulate shape

The starting powder particles are spherical or irregularly shaped micron-sized aggregates, each composed of a polycrystalline internal structure. The particle size distribution is narrow, with a median diameter between 1 and 5 micrometers, facilitating uniform packing and minimizing void formation during compaction.

- introduce nanocrystalline alloy composition

The nanocrystalline alloy comprises a refractory metal matrix, such as tungsten, molybdenum, or tantalum, alloyed with one or more transition metals including chromium, titanium, nickel, or cobalt, in atomic percentages ranging from 5 to 30 at%. The alloy is prepared as a supersaturated solid solution through mechanical alloying, ensuring homogeneity at the nanoscale.

- describe metal element selection

The selection of alloying elements is based on their limited equilibrium solubility in the base metal at ambient temperature, their ability to form a crystalline secondary phase upon heating, and their capacity to enhance the diffusion rate of the base metal through the precipitated phase. Chromium is preferred for tungsten due to its high solubility for tungsten in the Cr-rich phase and its low self-diffusivity, which ensures that the precipitate acts as a conduit for tungsten transport rather than a mobile phase.

- introduce activator and stabilizer materials

Activator elements, such as chromium and nickel, accelerate sintering by forming high-diffusivity pathways, while stabilizer elements, such as titanium and tantalum, inhibit grain growth by segregating to grain boundaries and reducing boundary mobility. In certain embodiments, a single element, such as titanium, may function as both activator and stabilizer when present in optimal concentrations.

- describe relative density

The sintered material achieves a relative density of at least 95%, with preferred embodiments exceeding 98% and reaching up to 99.5%. This level of densification is attained without the application of external pressure, distinguishing the method from conventional high-pressure sintering techniques.

- define bulk nanocrystalline alloy

A bulk nanocrystalline alloy is defined as a consolidated component with dimensions exceeding 1 cm in at least one direction and possessing a fully dense microstructure with an average grain size less than 200 nanometers throughout its volume, as confirmed by cross-sectional electron microscopy.

- discuss instability of nanocrystalline microstructure

Nanocrystalline microstructures are inherently unstable at elevated temperatures due to the high driving force for grain growth, which is governed by the reduction in total grain boundary energy. Without stabilization, grain growth during sintering leads to coarsening and loss of mechanical properties.

- introduce Gibbs free energy equation

The thermodynamic stability of the nanocrystalline state is governed by the Gibbs free energy equation, ΔG = ΔH – TΔS, where the enthalpy of grain boundary formation is balanced against the entropy of mixing. The addition of solute atoms reduces the grain boundary energy through adsorption, thereby increasing the activation barrier for grain growth.

- discuss grain growth limitation

Grain growth is limited by the segregation of stabilizer elements to grain boundaries, which reduces the boundary mobility and increases the energy barrier for atomic rearrangement. This effect is quantified by the Gibbs adsorption equation, which relates the surface excess of solute to the reduction in interfacial energy.

- introduce nanocrystalline tungsten

Nanocrystalline tungsten, when prepared by mechanical alloying, exhibits a grain size of approximately 10 to 15 nanometers. However, without alloying, such materials undergo rapid grain growth above 800 °C, rendering them unsuitable for pressureless sintering.

- describe localized shearing under high rate loading

Under high strain rate conditions, the nanocrystalline tungsten–chromium alloy exhibits localized shear banding, indicating a transition from homogeneous deformation to strain localization, a behavior consistent with the presence of nanoscale precipitates that impede dislocation motion.

- introduce bottom-up and top-down methodologies

Bottom-up methodologies involve the synthesis of nanoscale powders followed by consolidation, while top-down approaches involve the refinement of bulk materials through severe plastic deformation. The present invention employs a bottom-up approach, enabling scalability and control over composition.

- describe severe plastic deformation techniques

Severe plastic deformation techniques, such as equal-channel angular pressing, are capable of producing nanocrystalline structures but are limited to small sample sizes and geometrically simple shapes, making them impractical for industrial-scale production.

- discuss limitations of SPD techniques

The primary limitations of severe plastic deformation include low throughput, high tooling costs, and the inability to produce complex geometries or large components, which restricts their applicability in manufacturing.

- introduce two-step bottom-up method

The two-step bottom-up method involves (1) mechanical alloying to create a supersaturated nanocrystalline powder and (2) pressureless sintering to consolidate the powder into a dense bulk component. This method eliminates the need for post-sintering treatments and enables the fabrication of net-shape components.

- discuss volume defects in bottom-up method

Volume defects such as residual porosity and inhomogeneous solute distribution are minimized through extended mechanical alloying times and the use of process control agents to prevent cold welding and contamination.

- introduce additive elements for stabilization

Additive elements such as titanium, tantalum, and hafnium are introduced to stabilize the grain structure by segregating to grain boundaries and reducing their mobility. These elements are selected based on their strong binding energy to grain boundaries and their low diffusivity in the matrix.

- discuss ΔHseg importance

The enthalpy of segregation, ΔHseg, is a critical parameter in determining the effectiveness of a stabilizer. Elements with high negative ΔHseg values, such as titanium and tantalum, exhibit strong preferential segregation to grain boundaries, thereby effectively pinning them.

- describe atomic radius mismatch effect

A significant atomic radius mismatch between the base metal and the alloying element enhances lattice strain, which increases the driving force for solute segregation and improves the thermodynamic stability of the nanocrystalline structure.

- introduce segregation strength consideration

Segregation strength is evaluated through thermodynamic modeling and confirmed by atom probe tomography, ensuring that the stabilizer elements remain at grain boundaries during sintering and do not form detrimental intermetallic phases.

- discuss thermal stability determination

Thermal stability is determined by isothermal holding tests at temperatures approaching the sintering temperature, with grain size measured before and after exposure. Materials exhibiting less than 10% grain growth after 1 hour at 1,200 °C are considered thermally stable.

- introduce activated sintering of tungsten

Activated sintering of tungsten traditionally relies on the formation of transient grain boundary films, which are unstable at high temperatures. The present invention circumvents this limitation by forming a stable, nanoscale precipitate phase that enhances diffusion without compromising microstructural integrity.

- describe additive element effect on sintering kinetics

The addition of chromium reduces the sintering activation energy from approximately 550 kJ/mol for pure tungsten to 373 kJ/mol in the W–Cr system, a value closely matching the activation energy for tungsten diffusion in chromium, confirming that the precipitated phase serves as the primary diffusion pathway.

- discuss dislocation climb mechanism

Dislocation climb is not the dominant mechanism in this system, as evidenced by the lack of dislocation tangles in transmission electron microscopy and the absence of strain hardening during sintering.

- introduce electron transfer mechanism

No evidence of electron transfer or electronic effects on diffusion is observed, indicating that the enhanced sintering is purely a result of atomic-scale mass transport through the precipitated phase.

- describe grain boundary diffusion enhancement

Grain boundary diffusion is enhanced by the presence of the precipitated phase, which provides a network of interconnected, high-diffusivity channels that span the particle interfaces, enabling rapid filling of pores.

- discuss sintering kinetics

Sintering kinetics are modeled using the master sintering curve method, which normalizes shrinkage data across multiple heating rates. The collapse of all curves onto a single master curve confirms that the sintering mechanism is consistent and governed by a single rate-limiting process.

- introduce generalized sintering equation

The generalized sintering equation relates the linear shrinkage rate to the applied temperature, time, and activation energy, and is used to predict densification behavior under arbitrary thermal cycles.

- describe instantaneous densification rate

The instantaneous densification rate is highest between 950 °C and 1,100 °C, coinciding with the onset of phase separation, and declines as the pores are filled and the microstructure stabilizes.

- discuss thermodynamic stabilization of tungsten alloys

Thermodynamic stabilization is achieved by selecting alloying elements that reduce the total interfacial energy of the system through segregation, thereby increasing the energy barrier for grain growth and coarsening.

- introduce stabilizer and activator elements

Stabilizer elements include titanium, tantalum, and hafnium; activator elements include chromium, nickel, and cobalt. These elements may be used individually or in combination to optimize sintering kinetics and microstructural stability.

- describe properties of stabilizer element

Stabilizer elements possess high melting points, low diffusivity in the matrix, and strong segregation energy to grain boundaries. Titanium, for example, has a melting point of 1,668 °C and a segregation enthalpy of −120 kJ/mol to tungsten grain boundaries.

- specify amount of stabilizer element

The amount of stabilizer element is maintained between 5 and 20 at%, with a preferred range of 10 to 15 at% to balance grain growth inhibition without compromising density or ductility.

- introduce activator elements

Activator elements are selected for their ability to form a secondary phase with high diffusivity for the base metal. Chromium is preferred for tungsten due to its high solubility for tungsten in the Cr-rich phase and its low melting point relative to tungsten.

- describe properties of activator elements

Activator elements exhibit low self-diffusion coefficients in the base metal but high diffusivity of the base metal within their own phase. Chromium, for instance, has a tungsten diffusivity of 10⁻¹⁴ m²/s at 1,200 °C, compared to 10⁻¹⁷ m²/s for tungsten self-diffusion.

- specify amount of activator elements

The amount of activator element is maintained between 10 and 25 at%, with an optimal range of 15 to 20 at% for maximum densification rate and minimal residual porosity.

- describe selection of activator elements

Selection is based on phase diagram analysis, ensuring that the activator forms a crystalline, non-molten phase at the sintering temperature and that the solubility limit in the base metal is exceeded during mechanical alloying.

- describe role of activator elements in sintering

Activator elements facilitate sintering by forming a network of high-diffusivity pathways at particle interfaces, enabling rapid mass transport and pore elimination without the need for external pressure.

- describe interaction between activator and stabilizer elements

When used together, activator and stabilizer elements interact synergistically: the activator enhances densification, while the stabilizer suppresses grain growth. Titanium, for example, segregates to grain boundaries and also forms a Cr–Ti intermetallic that further pins boundaries.

- specify conditions for activator and stabilizer elements

The activator and stabilizer elements must be added in atomic percentages that ensure supersaturation during mechanical alloying and precipitation during sintering, without forming brittle intermetallic compounds that compromise ductility.

- describe use of single element as both activator and stabilizer

In certain systems, such as tungsten–titanium, titanium functions as both an activator and a stabilizer by forming a Ti-rich phase that enhances tungsten diffusion while simultaneously segregating to grain boundaries and inhibiting coarsening.

- specify metal elements as activator and stabilizer

Metal elements that serve as both activator and stabilizer include titanium, tantalum, and niobium, provided their concentration is sufficient to form a precipitate phase and to segregate to boundaries.

- describe phase diagrams for activator and stabilizer elements

Phase diagrams for the W–Cr, W–Ti, and Cr–Ni systems are analyzed to identify regions of supersaturation, phase separation, and intermetallic formation. The W–Cr system exhibits a eutectoid-like decomposition path that is ideal for nanophase separation sintering.

- analyze interaction between activator and stabilizer elements

The interaction between chromium and titanium in tungsten results in the formation of a Cr–Ti–W ternary phase that precipitates at grain boundaries, enhancing both densification and stabilization simultaneously.

- describe importance of amount of additives

The amount of additives must be carefully balanced: insufficient amounts fail to trigger phase separation or stabilize the microstructure, while excessive amounts lead to the formation of large, brittle intermetallics that degrade mechanical properties.

- illustrate embodiments of activator and stabilizer elements

In one embodiment, a tungsten alloy containing 15 at% chromium and 10 at% titanium is mechanically alloyed and sintered to produce a fully dense material with a grain size of 100 nm and a hardness of 8.5 GPa. In another, a chromium alloy containing 15 at% nickel is sintered to achieve 97% density with a grain size of 150 nm.

- describe phase diagrams for activator and stabilizer elements

Phase diagrams for the W–Cr–Ti ternary system reveal a narrow region of stability for the supersaturated solid solution, which is exploited during mechanical alloying and preserved during sintering through controlled heating rates.

- analyze formation of intermetallic compounds

Intermetallic compounds such as Cr₂Ti and TiW₂ are observed at high additive concentrations but are avoided in preferred embodiments by maintaining additive levels below 20 at% and using rapid sintering cycles to limit diffusion.

- describe liquid phase sintering

Liquid phase sintering is excluded from this invention, as the secondary phase remains solid throughout the sintering cycle, with melting points far exceeding the sintering temperature.

- specify conditions for liquid phase sintering

Liquid phase sintering requires the formation of a molten phase, which is avoided in this invention by selecting elements whose eutectic temperatures exceed 1,800 °C, ensuring that all phases remain solid during processing.

- describe advantages of liquid phase sintering

Although liquid phase sintering offers rapid densification, it results in microstructural coarsening and loss of high-temperature strength, which are avoided in the present invention.

- specify alloy systems for liquid phase sintering

Alloy systems such as W–Ni–Co are commonly used in liquid phase sintering but are not employed in this invention due to their detrimental effect on thermal stability.

- describe microstructural coarsening

Microstructural coarsening is suppressed in the present invention by the presence of nanoscale precipitates that pin grain boundaries, preventing the growth of grains beyond 200 nm even after prolonged sintering.

- specify role of stabilizer in preventing coarsening

The stabilizer element segregates to grain boundaries, reducing their mobility and increasing the energy barrier for atomic rearrangement, thereby preventing coarsening during both sintering and subsequent thermal exposure.

- describe detection of liquid phase sintering

Liquid phase sintering is detected by the presence of a molten phase, which is absent in the present invention as confirmed by differential scanning calorimetry and in situ microscopy.

- illustrate pore filling mechanism

Pore filling occurs through the diffusion of tungsten atoms along chromium-rich interparticle necks, where the chromium phase acts as a high-diffusivity channel, enabling the transport of tungsten to the pore surfaces and subsequent densification.

- introduce sintered nanocrystalline alloys

Sintered nanocrystalline alloys produced by this method exhibit full density, fine grain size, and superior mechanical properties compared to conventionally processed materials, enabling their use in high-temperature structural applications.

- describe production process

The production process involves mechanical alloying of elemental powders in a high-energy ball mill, compaction into green bodies, and pressureless sintering under a reducing atmosphere, with no post-processing required.

- define non-equilibrium phase

A non-equilibrium phase is a supersaturated solid solution formed under non-equilibrium conditions, such as mechanical alloying, in which the solute concentration exceeds the equilibrium solubility limit at ambient temperature.

- describe decomposition of non-equilibrium phase

Upon heating, the non-equilibrium phase decomposes into a matrix phase and a precipitate phase, with the precipitate forming preferentially at interfaces and grain boundaries.

- describe formation of phase rich in second metal material

The phase rich in the second metal material, such as chromium, forms as a crystalline, discontinuous network that connects adjacent particles and provides a high-diffusivity pathway for the base metal.

- describe effect of second metal material on sintering kinetics

The second metal material reduces the sintering activation energy by providing a faster diffusion path than the base metal’s self-diffusion, thereby accelerating densification.

- describe properties of sintered nanocrystalline alloy

The sintered alloy exhibits high hardness, high thermal stability, low porosity, and fine grain size, with mechanical properties superior to those of conventionally sintered materials.

- describe relative density of sintered material

The relative density of the sintered material is at least 95%, with preferred embodiments achieving 98% or higher, as measured by Archimedes’ principle.

- describe grain size of sintered nanocrystalline alloy

The average grain size of the sintered nanocrystalline alloy is between 50 and 200 nanometers, as determined by X-ray diffraction line broadening and confirmed by transmission electron microscopy.

- describe sintering conditions

Sintering is conducted in a reducing atmosphere of argon with 1 to 5% hydrogen at a heating rate of 5 to 20 °C per minute, with a peak temperature between 900 °C and 1,500 °C and a hold time of 30 to 120 minutes.

- describe process for making nanocrystalline particulates

Nanocrystalline particulates are produced by high-energy ball milling of elemental powders in a tungsten carbide mill for 15 to 30 hours, with a ball-to-powder ratio of 5:1 and 1 wt% stearic acid as a process control agent.

- describe mechanical working of powder

Mechanical working involves repeated impact and shear deformation during ball milling, which induces plastic deformation, grain refinement, and solute dissolution.

- describe ball-milling process

The ball-milling process is conducted in a sealed chamber under inert atmosphere, with the powder and milling media subjected to high-velocity collisions that induce severe plastic deformation and homogenize the composition.

- describe calculation of average grain size

Average grain size is calculated from X-ray diffraction peak broadening using the Scherrer equation, corrected for instrumental broadening, and validated by transmission electron microscopy.

- describe XRD patterns

XRD patterns show a single body-centered cubic phase after mechanical alloying, with no detectable peaks for the secondary phase, indicating supersaturation. Upon sintering, additional peaks corresponding to the precipitated phase appear.

- describe ball-milling time

Ball-milling times range from 15 to 30 hours, with longer times ensuring complete dissolution of the alloying element and minimizing residual crystalline impurities.

- describe contamination of tungsten powder

Contamination from the milling media is minimized by using tungsten carbide media with low cobalt content and by verifying impurity levels via energy-dispersive spectroscopy, with total contamination kept below 3 wt%.

- describe grain size of produced nanocrystalline particulates

The produced nanocrystalline particulates exhibit an average grain size of 10 to 20 nanometers, as confirmed by Rietveld refinement of XRD data and direct imaging via TEM.

- describe polycrystalline and single crystalline materials

The powder particles are polycrystalline, composed of numerous nanoscale grains, in contrast to nanopowders, which are typically single crystalline and of nanoscale particle size.

- describe non-equilibrium phase and supersaturated phase

The non-equilibrium phase is synonymous with the supersaturated phase, formed during mechanical alloying, in which the solute concentration exceeds the equilibrium solubility limit at room temperature.

- describe sintering behavior of particulate material

The sintering behavior is characterized by a sharp onset of densification at a temperature significantly lower than the melting point of either component, coinciding with the onset of phase separation.

- describe liquid phase sintering mechanism

Liquid phase sintering is not involved; the secondary phase remains solid throughout the process, and densification occurs through solid-state diffusion along precipitate interfaces.

- introduce master sintering curve

The master sintering curve is a normalized plot of linear shrinkage versus a time–temperature integral, which collapses data from multiple heating rates into a single curve, confirming a consistent sintering mechanism.

- describe integral of instantaneous linear shrinkage rate

The integral of the instantaneous linear shrinkage rate over time and temperature is used to calculate the sintering activation energy, which is found to be 373 kJ/mol for the W–Cr system.

- describe surface energy and atomic volume

Surface energy and atomic volume are used in the derivation of the sintering equation to relate the driving force for densification to the thermodynamic properties of the system.

- describe volume diffusion and grain-boundary diffusion

Volume diffusion is negligible in this system, while grain-boundary diffusion is enhanced by the presence of the precipitated phase, which provides a network of high-diffusivity paths.

- describe rearrangement of equation

The sintering equation is rearranged to isolate the activation energy term, allowing for its determination from the slope of the master sintering curve.

- describe estimation of activation energy

The activation energy is estimated by fitting the master sintering curve to the generalized sintering equation, yielding a value of 373 kJ/mol, which matches the activation energy for tungsten diffusion in chromium, confirming the diffusion mechanism.
