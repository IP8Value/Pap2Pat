## BACKGROUND

Nitric oxide synthases (NOSs) constitute a family of homodimeric enzymes responsible for the biosynthesis of nitric oxide (NO), a critical signaling molecule in mammalian physiology. These enzymes catalyze the oxidation of L-arginine to L-citrulline and nitric oxide through a complex, multi-step electron transfer process that requires the cofactors flavin adenine dinucleotide, flavin mononucleotide, reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate, molecular oxygen, and (6R)-5,6,7,8-tetrahydrobiopterin, along with the heme iron center. Three distinct isoforms of NOS have been identified in mammals: endothelial NOS (eNOS), inducible NOS (iNOS), and neuronal NOS (nNOS), each encoded by separate genes and exhibiting unique patterns of tissue expression, regulation, and physiological function. Endothelial NOS is constitutively expressed in vascular endothelial cells and plays a central role in the maintenance of vascular tone and blood pressure through the continuous, calcium-dependent production of low levels of NO. Inducible NOS is expressed primarily in immune cells following stimulation by inflammatory cytokines and generates large quantities of NO over extended periods to mediate cytotoxic responses against pathogens and tumor cells. Neuronal NOS, in contrast, is predominantly localized within neurons of the central and peripheral nervous systems, where it produces NO in a transient, calcium-calmodulin-dependent manner to modulate synaptic plasticity, neurotransmission, and neurovascular coupling. While NO serves essential roles in normal neurophysiology, its overproduction by nNOS has been mechanistically linked to the pathogenesis of numerous neurodegenerative and neuroinflammatory disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, stroke, and traumatic brain injury. In these conditions, excessive NO contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, protein nitration, DNA damage, and excitotoxic neuronal death. Consequently, selective pharmacological inhibition of nNOS, while sparing the beneficial functions of eNOS and iNOS, represents a compelling therapeutic strategy for the treatment of neurological disorders. However, the development of clinically viable nNOS inhibitors has been significantly hindered by the high degree of structural similarity among the catalytic domains of the three isoforms, particularly within the substrate-binding pocket where L-arginine binds. Early-generation inhibitors, often designed as arginine mimetics, possess high polarity, extensive hydrogen-bonding capacity, and large total polar surface areas, resulting in poor blood-brain barrier penetration and limited oral bioavailability. Moreover, many of these compounds exhibit insufficient selectivity for nNOS over eNOS, leading to undesirable cardiovascular side effects such as hypertension, or over iNOS, compromising immune function. The lack of structural diversity in existing inhibitor scaffolds and the absence of compounds capable of exploiting subtle, isoform-specific differences in the active site architecture have further constrained progress in this field. As a result, there remains a critical unmet need for novel, potent, selective, and brain-penetrant nNOS inhibitors with optimized physicochemical properties suitable for chronic administration in neurological disease settings.

## SUMMARY

Disclosed herein are novel 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinoline derivatives and pharmaceutical compositions thereof, which function as potent and selective inhibitors of neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS). These compounds are characterized by a core 7-phenyl-2-aminoquinoline scaffold substituted at the 4-position of the phenyl ring with an aminomethyl or ether-linked moiety, wherein the substituent is specifically designed to engage isoform-specific residues within the nNOS active site, particularly Asp597 in rat nNOS and Asp602 in human nNOS, which are replaced by asparagine in endothelial NOS. The disclosed compounds exhibit high binding affinity for nNOS, with inhibitory constants (Ki) in the low nanomolar range, and demonstrate exceptional selectivity over endothelial NOS and inducible NOS, with selectivity ratios exceeding 100-fold in many instances. The compounds are further engineered to possess favorable physicochemical properties, including moderate lipophilicity and reduced total polar surface area, which promote blood-brain barrier permeability and central nervous system penetration, as confirmed by parallel artificial membrane permeability assays. The method of using these compounds involves administering an effective amount of a compound of the disclosed formula to a subject in need thereof for the treatment or prevention of a disease or disorder associated with excessive nitric oxide production, including neurodegenerative diseases, ischemic stroke, traumatic brain injury, chronic pain, and psychiatric disorders characterized by glutamatergic excitotoxicity. The disclosed compounds offer significant advantages over prior art inhibitors by virtue of their novel chemical architecture, which enables precise molecular recognition of nNOS-specific structural features, including the Asp/Asn residue difference and the hydrophobic pocket formed by Met336 in nNOS versus Val104 in eNOS. The compounds are defined by a general formula wherein the quinoline ring is substituted at the 7-position with a phenyl group bearing a 4-substituent selected from aminomethyl, alkylamino, heteroalkylamino, cycloalkylmethoxy, arylmethoxy, or heteroarylmethoxy groups, and wherein the phenyl ring may be optionally substituted at the 5-position with a nitrile, halogen, or small alkyl group. The compounds are capable of competitively inhibiting the binding of L-arginine to the oxygenase domain of nNOS by mimicking key interactions of the substrate while simultaneously forming additional stabilizing contacts with isoform-specific residues not present in eNOS or iNOS. Potential applications of the disclosed compounds include the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, stroke, cerebral palsy, neuropathic pain, and depression, where nNOS overactivation contributes to neuronal damage or dysfunction. The disclosed compounds are suitable for formulation into oral, intravenous, intranasal, or transdermal dosage forms and are stable under physiological conditions, with demonstrated resistance to metabolic degradation in human liver microsomes.

X-ray crystallography was performed using sitting drop vapor diffusion with recombinant nNOS and eNOS heme domains. Initial inhibitory and structural data were analyzed by measuring IC₅₀ values and solving crystal structures at resolutions better than 2.0 Å. The X-ray crystal structure of compound 5 bound to rnNOS, hnNOS, and heNOS revealed that the quinoline core forms bifurcated hydrogen bonds with Trp587 and Glu592 in nNOS, while the phenethylamine tail interacts with heme propionate A and H₄B. The binding of compound 5 in heNOS revealed two distinct binding modes, one in the active site and one displacing H₄B. The conformational positioning of the tail amine of compound 5 was optimized by homologation, chain shortening, and isomerization, leading to compound 7, which exhibited enhanced potency and selectivity. The X-ray crystal structures of compound 7 bound to rnNOS and hnNOS showed that the aminomethyl group adopts multiple orientations, suggesting conformational flexibility. The X-ray crystal structures of compound 12 bound to rnNOS and hnNOS revealed that the meta-substituted aminomethyl group forms a direct hydrogen bond with Asp597/Asp602, a residue absent in eNOS, explaining the high selectivity. The binding interactions of compound 12 with heNOS differ significantly, with the amine group oriented toward heme propionate A and no interaction with Asn366. The homologated analogue 13, with an ethylamine tail, showed reduced potency due to failure to reach Asp597/Asp602. The installation of a nitrile at the 5-position of the phenyl ring resulted in a dramatic loss of potency due to steric clash with heme propionate D. The pyridine analogue 15, despite predicted hydrogen bonding, showed reduced potency, likely due to electronic perturbation. Constrained amine analogues 16–18 were synthesized to lock the amine conformation, but did not improve potency. The 4-alkoxy-3-aminomethyl analogues 19–29 were synthesized to exploit interactions with Met336/Met341. Compound 24, with an ethoxy group, restored potency in rnNOS and showed improved binding to Met336. The crystal structure of compound 24 bound to rnNOS showed the ethoxy group occupying a hydrophobic pocket near Met336. In hnNOS, compound 24 exhibited a flipped binding mode with interaction with Asn574. Inhibitors 32 and 33 were designed with pyridinylmethyl ether tails to engage Leu337 in rnNOS and His342 in hnNOS. The hnNOS-32 structure showed weaker binding due to steric repulsion from His342.
