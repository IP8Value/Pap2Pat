# DESCRIPTION

## BACKGROUND

The intestinal barrier serves as a critical interface between the external environment and the internal milieu of the body, maintaining selective permeability to allow absorption of nutrients while preventing the translocation of pathogens, toxins, and undigested antigens. This barrier is composed of a continuous monolayer of epithelial cells interconnected by specialized junctional complexes, including tight junctions, adherens junctions, and septate junctions in invertebrates, all of which are reinforced by a protective mucus layer secreted by goblet cells. Beneath this epithelial lining, a dense network of immune cells, including intraepithelial lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells, actively surveils luminal contents and modulates local inflammatory responses. The integrity of this barrier is dynamically regulated by a combination of genetic, biochemical, and environmental factors, ensuring homeostasis under normal physiological conditions. Disruption of this barrier, characterized by increased paracellular permeability and loss of epithelial cohesion, permits the uncontrolled passage of microbial components such as lipopolysaccharide, bacterial DNA, and dietary antigens into the systemic circulation. Such translocation triggers chronic low-grade inflammation, activation of innate and adaptive immune pathways, and systemic immune dysregulation. The loss of intestinal barrier function is not merely a consequence of disease but is increasingly recognized as a primary driver in the pathogenesis of a broad spectrum of conditions, including metabolic syndrome, autoimmune disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, and age-related decline. Evidence from both human and animal models demonstrates that barrier dysfunction precedes and exacerbates clinical manifestations in conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, type 2 diabetes, and even cognitive impairment. The mechanisms underlying this dysfunction are multifactorial, involving oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, altered gene expression of junctional proteins, and dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. As such, interventions aimed at preserving or restoring intestinal barrier integrity hold significant potential for mitigating disease progression and improving healthspan across diverse pathological contexts.

## SUMMARY

S3QELs are a novel class of small-molecule compounds specifically designed to suppress superoxide generation at the ubiquinone-binding site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III without interfering with normal electron transport or oxidative phosphorylation. These compounds act with high selectivity to reduce mitochondrial reactive oxygen species production at this specific site, thereby interrupting a key upstream driver of oxidative damage and inflammatory signaling in intestinal epithelial cells. Administration of S3QELs to Drosophila melanogaster and Mus musculus fed high-nutrient or high-fat diets significantly reduces intestinal permeability, as measured by the translocation of non-digestible markers such as blue food dye, FITC-dextran, and fecal albumin. In both models, feeding S3QELs correlates with a marked reduction in apoptotic cell number within the intestinal epithelium, preservation of tight junction and mucin gene expression, and extension of median lifespan in flies. The protective effects of S3QELs are not attributable to changes in food intake or caloric restriction, nor do they result from systemic pharmacological activity, as plasma concentrations of S3QELs remain orders of magnitude below their in vitro inhibitory thresholds. Instead, the beneficial effects are mediated locally within enterocytes, where S3QELs directly suppress mitochondrial superoxide production originating from site IIIQo, leading to downstream stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha and reduction of endoplasmic reticulum stress. Treatment refers to any intervention aimed at ameliorating, curing, or managing a pathological condition, including administration of a compound to reduce intestinal permeability. Intestinal permeability refers to the increased passage of molecules, microbes, or antigens across the intestinal epithelium due to disruption of tight junctions, epithelial integrity, or mucus layer integrity.

## DETAILED DESCRIPTION

S3QELs have been demonstrated to protect against intestinal permeability and reduce the number of apoptotic cells in the intestinal epithelium of both Drosophila and mice exposed to high-nutrient or high-fat diets. Administration of S3QELs to mammals fed a high-fat diet results in significant preservation of tight junction protein expression, restoration of mucin secretion, and reduction in fecal and plasma markers of barrier leakage, including FITC-dextran, albumin, and lipopolysaccharide. Observations on superoxide production from complex III in enterocytes reveal that elevated mitochondrial superoxide levels at site IIIQo are directly correlated with increased intestinal permeability, ER stress, and HIF-1α stabilization. The method for treatment or prophylaxis of age-related and/or pathology-associated increase in intestinal barrier permeability involves oral administration of a S3QEL to a subject exhibiting or at risk of developing intestinal hyperpermeability. Embodiments of methods for treatment or prophylaxis of diet-induced intestinal permeability include administration of S3QELs to subjects consuming high-fat, high-sugar, or high-protein diets. Embodiments of methods for treatment or prophylaxis of age-related increases in intestinal permeability include administration of S3QELs to elderly subjects or those exhibiting biomarkers of accelerated aging. Embodiments of methods for treatment or prophylaxis of pathology-associated increase in intestinal barrier permeability include administration to subjects diagnosed with inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, irritable bowel syndrome, obesity, type 2 diabetes, or neurodegenerative disorders. Therapeutic treatment that ameliorates one or more symptoms of a pathology associated with intestinal permeability includes reduction in systemic inflammation, decreased endotoxemia, improved glucose tolerance, and restoration of mucosal architecture.

### Celiac Disease and Ulcerative Colitis (UC)

Intestinal barrier dysfunction is a hallmark feature of celiac disease and ulcerative colitis, characterized by disruption of epithelial tight junctions, increased paracellular permeability, and aberrant immune activation in response to luminal antigens. In celiac disease, exposure to gluten triggers an autoimmune response that leads to villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-15 and interferon-gamma. Impaired epithelial barrier integrity permits translocation of gliadin peptides into the lamina propria, initiating T-cell-mediated destruction of enterocytes. In inflammatory bowel disease, alterations in epithelial tight junctions involve downregulation of occludin, claudins, and zonula occludens-1, accompanied by dislocation of the prion protein and increased susceptibility to bacterial translocation. Increased incidence of apoptotic events in the intestinal epithelium is observed in both conditions, driven by oxidative stress, cytokine toxicity, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6 further exacerbate barrier disruption by inducing cytoskeletal rearrangements and reducing expression of junctional proteins. The etiology of inflammatory bowel disease involves a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and dysbiosis of the gut microbiota. Insufficient mucosal response to bacterial stimuli leads to chronic immune activation and failure to restore epithelial integrity. Intestinal barrier dysfunction in irritable bowel syndrome is associated with increased permeability to macromolecules, structural changes in epithelial cells including microvillous shortening, and altered expression of mucin genes, even in the absence of overt inflammation.

### Parkinson's Disease and Alzheimer's Disease

Brain-gut axis interactions play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease, with emerging evidence implicating intestinal permeability as an early and contributory event. In Parkinson’s disease, gut dysbiosis and increased intestinal permeability precede motor symptoms and are associated with elevated levels of alpha-synuclein aggregation in the enteric nervous system. The innate immune system becomes activated in response to bacterial lipopolysaccharide translocation, triggering neuroinflammation via vagal afferents and systemic cytokine release. Adaptive immune system disturbances include T-cell infiltration into the brain parenchyma and autoantibody formation against neuronal antigens. Changes in gut microbiota composition in neurodegenerative diseases are characterized by reduced microbial diversity and enrichment of proinflammatory species. In Alzheimer’s disease, microbiome-derived lipopolysaccharide has been detected in postmortem brain tissue, where it promotes amyloid-beta aggregation and microglial activation. Circulating endotoxin levels correlate with cognitive decline and neuroinflammatory markers, suggesting that intestinal barrier failure contributes to the progression of neurodegeneration through systemic endotoxemia and chronic neuroinflammation.

### Active Agents

The method involving administration of S3QELs targets the site IIIQo of mitochondrial complex III to suppress superoxide generation without disrupting electron transport or ATP synthesis. The role of site IIIQo electron leak in ROS-mediated signaling and pathologies is well established, as this site generates the majority of mitochondrial superoxide under physiological conditions. High-throughput chemical screening has identified S3QELs as potent and selective inhibitors of this leak, with minimal off-target effects. Illustrative S3QELs include S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3, each bearing distinct chemical scaffolds but converging on the same molecular target. These compounds are commercially available or can be synthesized using established organic chemistry protocols. Screening protocols involve measurement of superoxide production in isolated mitochondria using fluorescent probes or chemiluminescent assays, followed by validation in cellular and in vivo models. Desirable properties of S3QELs include high selectivity for site IIIQo, low cytotoxicity, oral bioavailability, and stability in the gastrointestinal tract.

### Permeability Assays

Permeability assays are employed to quantify the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier in both preclinical and clinical settings. Administration of non-digestible markers such as fluorescent-labeled dextrans, lactulose, mannitol, polyethylene glycols, and fecal albumin allows for assessment of paracellular transport. Fluorescent-labeled dextrans, particularly FITC-dextran, are administered orally and quantified in serum after a defined time interval to determine intestinal translocation. Non-metabolizable oligosaccharides such as lactulose and mannitol are used in the lactulose-mannitol test, where their urinary excretion ratio reflects differential absorption through tight junctions and transcellular pathways. The triple sugar test and multi-sugar test extend this principle by incorporating additional markers such as rhamnose and sucralose to improve diagnostic specificity. PEGs serve as inert, non-absorbable markers with molecular weights tailored to assess varying degrees of barrier disruption. Fecal albumin measurement exploits the presence of serum-derived albumin in stool as a direct indicator of mucosal leakage, leveraging albumin’s resistance to proteolytic degradation in the gut lumen. Intestinal biomarkers such as zonulin, calprotectin, Alpha-1-Antitrypsin, and lipopolysaccharide provide complementary information on barrier integrity, inflammation, and microbial translocation. Zonulin modulates tight junction dynamics and is elevated in conditions of increased permeability. Calprotectin reflects neutrophil infiltration and mucosal inflammation. Alpha-1-Antitrypsin is a protease inhibitor that leaks into the lumen during epithelial damage. Lipopolysaccharide and circulating Endotoxin Core Antibodies indicate systemic endotoxemia. Plasma D-lactate levels, derived from bacterial fermentation, serve as a marker of bacterial overgrowth and barrier compromise.

## EXAMPLES

### Example 1

The intestinal epithelial barrier is essential for maintaining homeostasis by restricting passage of luminal contents while permitting nutrient absorption. Oxidative stress is a key driver of barrier dysfunction, particularly in the context of metabolic disease and aging. Drosophila melanogaster and Mus musculus serve as robust models for studying these processes due to conserved molecular pathways governing epithelial integrity. Dietary yeast extract at elevated concentrations induces intestinal permeability and reduces median lifespan in both fly strains. Intestinal permeability was measured using blue food dye, with a higher percentage of “smurf” flies indicating barrier failure. Correlation analysis revealed that increased permeability strongly predicted reduced lifespan. The balance between enterocyte apoptosis and proliferation was disrupted under high-nutrient conditions, with both processes elevated but apoptosis more strongly linked to barrier dysfunction. Gene expression analysis confirmed upregulation of antimicrobial peptides and septate junction genes, consistent with epithelial stress. Superoxide dismutase and catalase expression increased, indicating oxidative stress. Mitochondrial superoxide production from site IIIQo of complex III was hypothesized as the source. Feeding S3QEL1.2, S3QEL2.2, and S3QEL3 to flies on a high-yeast diet halved intestinal permeability, reduced apoptotic cell number, and extended median lifespan by 10–20%. S3QELs did not affect food consumption or palatability, ruling out caloric restriction. S1QELs, which target complex I, had no effect, confirming specificity. S3QELs reduced expression of inflammatory markers and antioxidant genes, consistent with decreased oxidative stress. Genetic knockdown of Sod1 or Sod2 in enterocytes increased permeability and apoptosis, which was rescued by S3QELs. Dimethyl succinate exacerbated permeability, implicating succinate-driven superoxide production. Hif-1α knockdown improved barrier function and lifespan, while overexpression worsened outcomes. S3QELs were less effective in Hif-1α knockdown flies, suggesting Hif-1α as a downstream mediator. In mice fed a high-fat diet, intestinal permeability increased as measured by plasma FITC-dextran and fecal albumin. Tight junction and mucin gene expression decreased. S3QEL1.2 and S3QEL2.2 restored barrier integrity, reduced ER stress, and preserved mucosal architecture without altering weight gain or food intake. Glucose intolerance persisted, indicating hyperglycemia acts upstream of barrier failure. Experimental design included fly strains w1118 and Canton S, maintained on defined media. Lifespan assays were conducted under controlled conditions. Intestinal permeability was assessed via dye uptake. Apoptosis and proliferation were quantified using Acridine Orange/Ethidium Bromide and Phosphohistone H3 staining. Food consumption was measured by dye dilution. Mouse experiments utilized C57BL/6J males on 60% kcal high-fat diet. FITC-dextran was administered via gavage and measured in plasma. Fecal albumin was quantified by ELISA. Glucose tolerance was assessed by intraperitoneal injection. Body fat was measured by DEXA. RNA was extracted from intestinal tissue and analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR. Statistical analysis employed ANCOVA and Student’s t-tests. Limitations include the inability to directly measure in vivo superoxide levels due to probe artifacts. Application of results supports targeting mitochondrial superoxide at site IIIQo as a therapeutic strategy for intestinal barrier disorders.
